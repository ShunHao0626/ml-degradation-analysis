# A Printable Organic Electron Transport Layer for Low-Temperature-Processed, Hysteresis-Free, and Stable Planar Perovskite Solar Cells

Jinho Lee, Junghwan Kim, Chang-Lyoul Lee, Geunjin Kim, Tae Kyun Kim, Hyungcheol Back, Suhyun Jung, Kilho Yu, Soonil Hong, Seongyu Lee, Seok Kim, Soyeong Jeong, Hongkyu Kang,* and Kwanghee Lee*

Despite recent breakthroughs in power conversion efficiencies (PCEs), which have resulted in PCEs exceeding 22%, perovskite solar cells (PSCs) still face serious drawbacks in terms of their printability, reliability, and stability. The most efficient PSC architecture, which is based on titanium dioxide as an electron transport layer, requires an extremely high-temperature sintering process (  $  \approx500\ ^{\circ}C  $  ), reveals hysterical discrepancies in the device measurement, and suffers from performance degradation under light illumination. These drawbacks hamper the practical development of PSCs fabricated via a printing process on flexible plastic substrates. Herein, an innovative method to fabricate low-temperature-processed, hysteresis-free, and stable PSCs with a large area up to 1 cm  $  ^{2}  $   is demonstrated using a versatile organic nanocomposite that combines an electron acceptor and a surface modifier. This nanocomposite forms an ideal, self-organized electron transport layer (ETL) via a spontaneous vertical phase separation, which leads to hysteresis-free, planar heterojunction PSCs with stabilized PCEs of over 18%. In addition, the organic nanocomposite concept is successfully applied to the printing process, resulting in a PCE of over 17% in PSCs with printed ETLs.

Organic ETLs are promising practical alternatives to   $  TiO_{2}  $   because of their beneficial features, such as a low-temperature process and hysteresis alleviation.  $  ^{[33-41]}  $   Recently, there have been research efforts to develop organic ETLs using polyelectrolyte/organic molecule bilayers  $  ^{[33-35]}  $   and vacuum-processed  $  ^{[36,37]}  $   or doped fullerene.  $  ^{[41]}  $   Although the organic ETLs have been successfully used to replace the   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer, they have disadvantages in terms of film formation because they require a vacuum process to deposit the fullerene, a long annealing time to activate the doping process, and delicate control of the ultrathin thickness (<10 nm) of polyelectrolytes. These disadvantages increase the complexity of the processing steps for the preparation of high-quality films and limit the application of organic ETLs in a continuous printing process. Therefore, developing an ETL that is printable and that simultaneously eliminates the hysteresis effect as a core function has been an important challenge.

Here, we present an innovative method to fabricate efficient and reliable PSCs with a regular structure by introducing self-assembled organic nanocomposites (SAONs) as a new ETL that employs n-type polyelectrolytes and fullerene derivatives. Using a spontaneous vertical phase separation driven by the surface energy difference of the SAON components, we succeeded in forming ideal, self-organized ETLs with dual functionality as an electron acceptor and surface work function modifier. By investigating the photovoltaic performance in combination with electrical and photophysical analysis techniques, we found that efficient charge transfer at the ETL/perovskite interface plays

A schematic illustration of our device architecture and the corresponding energy level diagram are presented in Figure 1a,b. We introduced polyethyleneimine (PEI) as a surface work function modifier and [6,6]-phenyl  $ C_{61} $ -butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) as an electron acceptor, and they were combined into an SAON for a new and efficient ETL.[42,43] The SAON (also referred to as "PEI:PCBM") solutions were prepared by mixing a small amount of the PEI solution with the PCBM solutions without the PEI rigorously inducing a doping effect in the PCBM (Figure S1, Supporting Information). The PEI:PCBM solutions were coated on an indium tin oxide (ITO) cathode via low-temperature solution processing. The PEI:PCBM SAON can function as an efficient ETL via the following two processes: (i) efficient electron transfer from the perovskite to the PCBM top layer due to the strong electron affinity of the PCBM and (ii) efficient electron collection from the PCBM to the ITO cathode by creating an interfacial PEI dipole bottom layer that reduces the work function of the cathode and forms a desirable ohmic contact between the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) level of the PCBM and the reduced work function of the cathode (Figure 1b).

The most remarkable feature of our work is that we introduced a new ETL concept using a vertical self-assembly of organic nanocomposites to fabricate high-efficiency PSCs. In addition to their printability and the elimination of hysteresis, our SAONs offer another important advantage related to the stability of PSCs against self-/photodegradation and photo-oxidation. Because conventional metals (e.g., Al and Ag) are highly unstable in PSC technology, we used stable Au as the top electrode (Figure S20, Supporting Information) to exclude the degradation effects originating from the top metal contacts. A stability test to evaluate the self-degradation of the PSCs was performed in ambient air. As shown in Figure 4a, the control devices with an SAON layer survived for a longer period of time, whereas the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  ETL resulted in the rapid degradation of the PSC performance within a few days. This finding is attributed to the hydrophobicity of the SAONs, which helps inhibit the intrusion of moisture into the perovskite, improving the air stability of the PSC. To gain a deeper understanding of the photodegradation behavior of the PSCs, we also measured the PCE decay profiles of the PSCs in an  $ \mathbf{N}_2 $ -filled glove box under continuous light exposure with an irradiation intensity of  $ 100\mathrm{mWcm}^{-2} $  and a simulated AM 1.5G spectrum. Under light illumination, the PCEs of the reference devices with PEI:ICBA and  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  considerably decreased, whereas a significant enhancement in the photostability was observed for the PSCs with PEI:PCBM, which retained  $ \approx 90\% $  of their initial PCE for up to  $ 60\mathrm{h} $  (Figure 4b). Additionally, by replacing methylammonium iodide by much more stable cesium iodide,[45] we found that PSCs with  $ \mathrm{Cs_{0.05}(CH_3NH_3)_{0.95}PbI_3} $  exhibit prolonged lifetime (Figure S21, Supporting Information). We believe that introduction of the formamidinium cation would be able to further increase the device stability. Little photodegradation was observed to be associated with the degradation of the perovskites because none of the PSCs exhibited changes in the color of the perovskite active layers. These results imply that the invisible accumulation of electrons at the ETL/perovskite interfaces results in the unwanted photodegradation of PSCs. The charge accumulation might become severe, which hinder the formation of stable electric field distribution within the devices, presumably resulting in much faster degradation in the PEI:ICBA-based devices.[46] However, we believe that further studies should be needed to address this issue. Furthermore, we also conducted a stability test to investigate the photo-oxidation of PSCs under humid conditions with continuous light illumination (Figure 4c). We confirmed that the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ -based reference cell exhibited a rapid decrease in the PCE accompanied by a remarkable change in the color of the perovskite active layers, which presumably originated from the well-known photocatalytic effect of  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  that causes perovskite materials to decompose. In contrast, the PCE of the control device displayed a relatively gradual decrease, and there was no change in the color of the perovskite active layers. These results indicated that organic ETLs are photochemically inert, thereby enabling the fabrication of PSCs with long-term stability.

# Experimental Section

Materials and Preparation: All of the materials were used as received. The   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I_{3}  $   was synthesized according to a method previously reported.  $  ^{[23]}  $   A 1.5 M   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   solution was prepared by dissolving the synthesized   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   (Dyesol) and   $  PbI_{2}  $   (Alfa Aesar, 99.9985%) in a molar ratio of 1:1 in γ-butyrolactone/dimethylsulfoxide (7:3 vol%). PEI (Aldrich, 50 wt% in   $  H_{2}O  $  ) was diluted to 0.05 wt% in 2-methoxyethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, anhydrous, 99.8%). All fullerene derivatives (FDs) were purchased from Nano-C. The PCBM and ICBA were dissolved in CB to create a 2 wt% solution. For the PEI:FD nanocomposite, the PEI and FD solutions were mixed at a 1:9 volume ratio. The titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetylacetonate) (75 wt% in isopropanol) was purchased from Aldrich. As HTLs, P3HT (  $  M_{w}=50\ 000\ g\ mol^{-1}  $  ), PCDTBT (  $  M_{w}=38\ 000\ g\ mol^{-1}  $  ), and T1 were purchased from 1-Material. Spiro and PTAA (  $  M_{w}=17\ 500\ g\ mol^{-1}  $  ) were purchased from Lumtec and EM Index, respectively. P3HT and T1 were dissolved in CB to create 1.5 wt% solutions. PCDTBT was dissolved in 1,2-dichlorobenzene (DCB) at 0.5 wt%. The Spiro/CB (70 mg ml  $  ^{-1}  $  ) solution was prepared with 28.8 μL of 4-tert-butylpyridine (tBP), 17.5 μL of Li-bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (Li-TFSI)/acetonitrile (520 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ), and 29 μL of tris(2-(1H-pyrazol-1-yl)-4-tert-butylpyridine)cobalt(III)bis(trifluoromethylsulphonyl) imide/acetonitrile (300 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ). The PTAA/toluene (10 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) solution was prepared with 7.5 μL of Li-TFSI/acetonitrile (170 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) and 7.5 μL of tBP/acetonitrile (1:1 vol%).

Perovskite Solar Cell Fabrication and Measurements: All component layers were spin-coated in an   $  N_{2}  $  -filled glove box. The n-i-p PSCs were fabricated with a structure of ITO/SAON/  $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $  /HTL/MoO  $  _{3}  $  /Ag. The precleaned, ITO-coated glass substrates were treated with UV–ozone before device fabrication. The SAON (PEI:FD) solution was spin-coated at 1200 rpm on the glass/ITO substrate. The resulting film thickness was 50 nm. The samples were dried under vacuum conditions. For fabrication of the   $  TiO_{2}  $   film, 0.15 M titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetylacetonate) in butanol solution was spin-coated at 2000 rpm for 20 s. The resulting film was baked at 125 °C for 5 min and then annealed at 500 °C for 1 h in a furnace. Subsequently, the perovskite precursor solution was spin-coated onto the ITO/SAON and ITO/  $  TiO_{2}  $   substrates via a consecutive three-step spin-coating process at 800, 3000, and 5000 rpm for 20 s. During the spin-coating at 5000 rpm (after 5 s), the substrates were treated using the diethyl ether dropping process (200–400 μL) and then annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. The resulting thickness of the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   film was ≈350 nm. Various HTLs were tested to confirm the validity of the SAON concept. The P3HT, PCDTBT, T1, Spiro, and PTAA solutions were spin-coated onto   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   at 1500, 1500, 1500, 4000, and 3000 rpm, respectively. Finally, MoO  $  _{3}  $   (5 nm)/Ag (100 nm) was deposited via thermal evaporation under a   $  1 \times 10^{-7}  $   Torr vacuum. The Ag electrode area (4.64 mm  $  ^{2}  $  ) defined the active area of the device. In the case of Spiro and PTAA-based PSCs, the samples were kept in ambient air for 1 h before measurement, which increased the device performance due to the oxygen doping of HTLs. The intensity of the solar spectrum obtained from the xenon (Xe) lamp (150 W Oriel) in the solar simulator was calibrated using a calibrated standard silicon solar cell certified by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL). The J-V characteristics of the devices were measured using a Keithley 236 Source Measure Unit (SMU) under air mass 1.5 G global (AM 1.5 G) illumination with a 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $   irradiation intensity. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra were measured using a solar cell spectral response/QE/EQE measurement system (PV Measurements, Inc.).
