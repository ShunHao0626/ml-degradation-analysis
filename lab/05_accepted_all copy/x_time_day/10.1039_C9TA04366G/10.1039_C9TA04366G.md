# Reduced Open-Circuit Voltage Loss for Highly Efficient Low-Bandgap Perovskite Solar Cells via Suppression of Silver Diffusion

Meiyue Liu,  $  ^{1\dagger}  $   Ziming Chen,  $  ^{1\dagger}  $   Yongchao Yang,  $  ^{1}  $   Hin-Lap Yip  $  ^{1,2}  $   and Yong Cao  $  ^{1}  $

$  ^{1}  $   State Key Laboratory of Luminescent Materials and Devices, Institute of Polymer Optoelectronic Materials and Devices, School of Materials Science and Engineering, South China University of Technology, 381 Wushan Road, Guangzhou, 510640, P. R. China.

$  ^{2}  $   Innovation Center for Printed Photovoltaics, South China Institute of Collaborative Innovation, Dongguan 523808, P. R. China

Correspondence: msangusyip@scut.edu.cn

$  ^{\dagger}  $   These authors contributed equally to this work.

Abstract: The development of low bandgap perovskite solar cells is important as the current best perovskite solar cells are not based on light harvesting layer with ideal bandgap and the low bandgap materials can also be used together with large bandgap materials to construct all-perovskite tandem cells with potentially higher power conversion efficiencies. However, low-bandgap perovskite solar cells based on mixed Pb and Sn generally suffer from inferior performance, retarding the further development of tandem perovskite solar cells. One of the main reasons is the relatively high open-circuit voltage loss in low-bandgap perovskite solar cells, which is generally attributed to the high trap density in perovskite film and energy-level mismatching between different functional layers in the device. In this study, we find another mechanism that can cause high open-circuit voltage loss and degrade device performance, which is the silver diffusion towards the perovskite layer. Devices based on a typical inverted structure (indium oxide/PEDOT:PSS/FA $ _{0.5} $ MA $ _{0.5} $ Sn $ _{0.5} $ Pb $ _{0.5} $ I $ _3 $ /PCBM/Ag) suffered from severe silver diffusion that contaminated the whole perovskite layer during the thermal deposition process of the silver electrode. Such contamination increased the trap density and down-shifted the energy level of the FA $ _{0.5} $ MA $ _{0.5} $ Sn $ _{0.5} $ Pb $ _{0.5} $ I $ _3 $  layer, resulting in high open-circuit voltage loss in the low-bandgap perovskite solar cells. Accordingly, we inserted a ZnO layer between the PCBM layer and silver electrode to significantly suppress silver diffusion, which dramatically improved the open-circuit voltage from 0.59 V to 0.79 V. Ultimately, a highly efficient low-bandgap Sn-Pb mixed perovskite solar cell with a power

# Introduction

Because of the promising optoelectronic properties of organic-inorganic hybrid perovskites, solar cell devices based on this class of materials have attracted significant attention and the certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of the best perovskite solar cell (PVSC) has now reached 24.2%.  $  ^{1}  $   Because the PCE is approaching the Shockley-Queisser limit of single-junction cells, the developing speed of single-junction PVSCs has slowed down, and thus a new device structure is needed to promote further development of PVSCs in terms of device efficiencies. A tandem PVSC, which combines a large-bandgap front cell (bandgap of around 1.7-1.9 eV) and a low-bandgap back cell (bandgap of around 1.2 eV), is a device structure that has been proven to have the capacity to break through the Shockley-Queisser limit.  $  ^{2-9}  $   A number of recent efforts have been made to develop large-bandgap PVSCs, and their PCEs have increased to around 18~19%, approaching their theoretical limits.  $  ^{10-12}  $   However, low-bandgap PVSCs still suffer from relatively inferior device performance, which is probably caused by large open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ) loss in the device (typically,   $  V_{oc}  $   loss is defined as   $  E_{g}/q-V_{oc}  $  , where   $  E_{g}  $   and q represent the optical bandgap of the perovskite layer and elementary charge, respectively).  $  ^{13,14}  $   Generally, the high   $  V_{oc}  $   loss has been attributed to two possible causes: first, the high trap density of the perovskite layer, which causes severe trap-assisted recombination; second, undesired energy-level mismatching between the different functional layers in the device.  $  ^{15-17}  $   In recent years, several strategies have been developed to solve these issues, aiming to suppress   $  V_{oc}  $   loss and improve device performance. For instance, in our previous work, a vacuum-assisted thermal annealing process was used to obtain a high-quality MASn  $  _{0.5}  $  Pb  $  _{0.5}  $  I  $  _{x}  $  Cl  $  _{3-x}  $   (MA  $  ^{+}  $   = CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}  $    $  ^{+}  $  ) film with low trap density, which facilitated a higher   $  V_{oc}  $   in the device compared with one based on higher-trap-density film (annealed in a nitrogen atmosphere).  $  ^{18}  $

Furthermore, the incorporation of small molecules of N-(3-aminopropyl)-2-pyrrolidinone in this system can passivate the surface traps of perovskite crystal and stabilize the   $  Sn^{2+}  $   cation in the perovskite lattice, further promoting a smaller   $  V_{oc}  $   loss of 0.48 V with excellent device stability.  $  ^{19}  $   In addition, Tong and coworkers reported that the incorporation of guanidinium thiocyanate in a   $  FA^{+}/MA^{+}  $   (  $  FA^{+} = CH(NH_{2})_{2}^{+}  $  ) mixed low-bandgap perovskite system can passivate the grain boundaries and hence reduce trap-assisted recombination, resulting in an efficient PVSC with a PCE of over 20% and low   $  V_{oc}  $   loss of 0.416 V.  $  ^{20}  $   To the best of our knowledge, this result is the best one achieved for low-bandgap PVSCs to date. In addition, regarding the issue of mismatches in energy levels, interfacial engineering can be used to reduce   $  V_{oc}  $   loss. Recently, Hayase and co-workers used a PCBM/  $  C_{60}  $   spike structure in low-bandgap PVSCs to reduce the interfacial recombination of charge carriers, leading to a reduced   $  V_{oc}  $   loss of 0.45 V and a PCE of 17.6% in the device.  $  ^{21}  $   Li and co-workers inserted an ultrathin organic bulk-heterojunction layer between the PEDOT:PSS and low-bandgap perovskite film to provide better energy-level matching on the anode side, which resulted in a low   $  V_{oc}  $   loss of 0.39 V and a PCE of 18.03%.  $  ^{22}  $

Based on these reports, there is no doubt that the reduction of trap density in perovskite film and the establishment of ideal energy-level alignment at the interface can contribute to lower   $  V_{oc}  $   loss for low-bandgap PVSCs. In addition to these two issues, we have identified another mechanism that governs   $  V_{oc}  $   loss in a low-bandgap PVSC based on an inverted structure (indium oxide (ITO)/poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly (styrene sulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS)/perovskite/PCBM/silver (Ag)): Ag diffusion towards the perovskite layer. In our study, we observed that without proper interfacial modification, Ag could easily diffuse into the perovskite layer and contaminated the whole film, and therefore enhanced the trap density and down-shifted the energy level of perovskite film. To circumvent this problem, we have inserted a thin but dense ZnO layer between the PCBM layer and Ag electrode to suppress Ag diffusion and hence minimized the negative effect of Ag contamination. With the help of this ZnO layer, we obtained a low-bandgap PVSC with a high PCE of 18.1% and low   $  V_{oc}  $   loss of 0.40 V, which is one of the best results for low-bandgap PVSCs to date.

In the organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite family, the lowest-bandgap perovskite (14.2 eV) is composed of a stoichiometrically identical amount of   $  Sn^{2+}  $   and   $  Pb^{2+}  $   in a perovskite lattice (i.e.   $  ASn_{0.5}Pb_{0.5}I_{3}  $  , where A represents a monovalent organic cation).  $  ^{2,8,23,24}  $   In our study, the A-site cation is the mixed organic cation of   $  FA^{+}/MA^{+}  $  , obtained by mixing together identical molar ratios of   $  FASnI_{3}  $   and   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   precursor solution (i.e.   $  FA_{0.5}MA_{0.5}Sn_{0.5}Pb_{0.5}I_{3}  $  ).   $  SnF_{2}  $   and   $  \mathrm{Pb(SCN)_{2}}  $   were also added to the precursor solution to suppress the oxidation of   $  Sn^{2+}  $   and promote better film formation, respectively (details can be seen in the experimental section).  $  ^{25-29}  $   The absorption edge of the low-bandgap   $  FA_{0.5}MA_{0.5}Sn_{0.5}Pb_{0.5}I_{3}  $   film reached around 1040 nm, reflecting its optical bandgap of around 1.19 eV (see Figure S1a). Figure S1b shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the   $  FA_{0.5}MA_{0.5}Sn_{0.5}Pb_{0.5}I_{3}  $   film; the main peak was located at 2θ of 14.06°, along with two small peaks at 24.55° and 28.33°, which can be attributed to diffractions from the (110), (202) and (220) planes in the tetragonal perovskite phase, respectively.  $  ^{30}  $   The XRD pattern shows no peak associated to the precursor materials, indicating that the precursor materials were completely converted to the target perovskite. The morphology of the   $  FA_{0.5}MA_{0.5}Sn_{0.5}Pb_{0.5}I_{3}  $   film on top of the PEDOT:PSS layer was also studied by scanning electron microscope (SEM), as shown in Figure S1c. The polycrystalline perovskite film shows high coverage with crystal sizes of hundreds of nanometres. To characterize the photovoltaic properties of this   $  FA_{0.5}MA_{0.5}Sn_{0.5}Pb_{0.5}I_{3}  $   film, a PVSC based on an inverted structure of ITO/PEDOT:PSS/  $  FA_{0.5}MA_{0.5}Sn_{0.5}Pb_{0.5}I_{3}/PCBM/metal  $   electrode was fabricated, as shown in Figure 1a. Considering that noble metals like Ag, Cu and Au benefit to the stability of devices and the work functions of Ag (-4.26 eV), Cu (-4.65 eV) and Au (-5.10 eV) together, we herein selected Ag as the cathode electrode to provide a better energy-level alignment with PCBM. Figure 1b shows the current density-voltage (J-V) curve of this PVSC, and the photovoltaic parameters are summarized in Table 1. The device obtained a   $  V_{oc}  $  , short-current density (  $  J_{sc}  $  ) and fill factor of 0.59 V, 28.8 mA/cm² and 64.6%, respectively, resulting in a relatively low PCE of 11%.

According to the cross section of the perovskite layer and Pb distribution shown in Figure 1c, the  $ \mathrm{FA}_{0.5}\mathrm{MA}_{0.5}\mathrm{Sn}_{0.5}\mathrm{Pb}_{0.5}\mathrm{I}_3 $  film was uniform with thickness of over  $ 500~\mathrm{nm} $ , which suggests that the film was of high quality. In addition, the PCBM layer completely covered the  $ \mathrm{FA}_{0.5}\mathrm{MA}_{0.5}\mathrm{Sn}_{0.5}\mathrm{Pb}_{0.5}\mathrm{I}_3 $  film and isolated the  $ \mathrm{FA}_{0.5}\mathrm{MA}_{0.5}\mathrm{Sn}_{0.5}\mathrm{Pb}_{0.5}\mathrm{I}_3 $  layer from the Ag electrode. Unfortunately, a strong Ag signal appeared not only in the Ag electrode, but also in the perovskite layer, which suggests that severe Ag diffusion crossing the PCBM layer occurred during the Ag deposition process. A uniform Ag signal could be found in the whole perovskite layer, illustrating that the diffused Ag could migrate easily within the perovskite film. Such Ag diffusion could contaminate the whole perovskite layer and degrade the device properties. Therefore, strategies for suppressing Ag diffusion are needed to achieve better device performance. A similar Ag diffusion phenomenon was also found in other reports, in which PCBM or some other organic layer was used as the buffer layer, and the modification of organic layer or forming two-dimensional perovskite interlayer could inhibit the Ag diffusion.[31-34]

To further evaluate the blocking property of PCBM film, our first attempt is to modulate the thickness of the PCBM layer from 0 nm to 190 nm to evaluate its ability to prevent Ag diffusion. According to Table S1, the   $  V_{oc}  $   of PVSCs increased with the increase of PCBM thickness, which suggests that a thicker PCBM layer indeed provided better suppression of Ag diffusion. However, even when the thickness of the PCBM layer reached 190 nm, which is the maximum thickness we can achieve in our process, there was a relatively high   $  V_{oc}  $   loss of 0.5 V, which indicates that this thick PCBM layer was still not efficient enough to completely block out Ag diffusion. In addition, the absorption competition between the thick PCBM layer and the perovskite absorber also resulted in decreased   $  J_{sc}  $   in the device, which suggests that using a thick PCBM layer is not an ideal solution for achieving a highly efficient low-bandgap PVSC. However, this result strongly illustrates that the   $  V_{oc}  $   loss of the PVSC is dominant by the Ag diffusion towards the perovskite layer, rather than the other factors like the trap states between the PCBM layer and the Ag electrode.

Therefore, our second attempt is to introduce dense inorganic layer as the electron-transport layer (ETL) to provide better suppression of Ag diffusion. Among different inorganic ETLs, ZnO is a suitable candidate because of its high mobility, high transparency and desirable conduction band (CB) of -4.2

# The Chemical State of Diffused Ag

To evaluate how the diffused Ag could affect the property of the perovskite film, it is important to study the chemical state of Ag as it has been reported that Ag in the form of cation  $ (\mathrm{Ag}^{+}) $  could potentially substitute the original monovalent cation (such as  $ \mathrm{Cs}^{+} $ ,  $ \mathrm{MA}^{+} $  or  $ \mathrm{FA}^{+} $ ) in the perovskite lattice and provide

a surface passivation effect to improve the overall quality of the perovskite crystals.  $  ^{39}  $   On the contrary, Ag in the form of   $  Ag^{0}  $   could form Ag metal clusters and therefore act as an electronic quenching site to perovskite. As the diffused Ag may have a positive effect on the perovskite layer, it is necessary to determine the chemical state of diffused Ag in our PVSCs. Therefore, we first fabricated completed devices with ETLs of bare PCBM and PCBM/ZnO, and subsequently dipped the devices into chlorobenzene to peel off the PCBM/Ag layer and PCBM/ZnO/Ag layer, respectively, in order to expose the perovskite film for surface analysis. In this case, we were able to compare these two films with pristine perovskite film to study the impact of diffused Ag. Figure 3a shows the atomic force microscope (AFM) images of pristine perovskite film, the perovskite film with the PCBM/Ag layer peeled off (Film A) and the perovskite film with the PCBM/ZnO/Ag layer peeled off (Film B). It can be seen that small particles appeared in Film A, but no such particle appeared in the pristine perovskite film and Film B. These results combined with the EDS results shown in Figure 1 indicate that the small particles are Ag particles. To further evaluate the chemical state of these Ag particles, XRD and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) studies were performed. Figure 3b shows that the XRD patterns of all the three different films and the peaks corresponded to their (110) planes were all located at   $  2\theta  $   of   $  14.06^{\circ}  $  , which suggests that there was no lattice constant change of the perovskite phase in this case, and we therefore conclude that the diffused Ag was unlikely to incorporate into the perovskite lattice. XPS was used to further study the valence state of the diffused Ag. Figure 3c shows that only one main peak of Ag   $  3d_{5/2}  $   signal presented in Film A, and its location (368.2 eV) supports the existence of Ag metal (  $  Ag^{0}  $  ).  $  ^{32, 39, 40}  $   In addition, we also measured the atomic ratio between Pb and Ag by XPS and the results are shown in Figure S3. It is obvious that the Pb to Ag ratio in Film A was much smaller than that in Film B, consisting well with the EDS data in Figure 1 and also suggesting a severer Ag diffusion in Film A. Consequently, we can conclude that the diffused Ag presented in the form of Ag metal nanoparticles, which can diffuse through the grain boundaries between the perovskite crystals and eventually contaminate the whole perovskite film.

# Trap Density and Energy Level

From our devices results, the outcomes of the Ag diffusion are the significant losses in both   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF, which are strongly related to the trap density and energy level of perovskite thin film.  $  ^{41-43}  $   We therefore investigated whether the diffused Ag nanoparticles had an impact on these two electronic properties of perovskite film. As shown in Figure 4a to 4c, we first evaluated the trap densities of pristine perovskite film, Film A and Film B with an electron-only device configuration using dark J-V curve measurement, and the extracted trap density values were   $  2.91 \times 10^{16}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  ,   $  4.42 \times 10^{16}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $   and   $  3.27 \times 10^{16}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  , respectively. This result illustrates that the Ag contamination in Film A could introduce more trap states in the perovskite film; however, the Film B and pristine perovskite film presented similar trap density. Given that the work function of Ag metal (-4.26 eV) is deeper than the CB of   $  FA_{0.5}MA_{0.5}Sn_{0.5}Pb_{0.5}I_{3}  $   perovskite (-4.0 eV), electron transfer from perovskite to Ag nanoparticles is

10

Journal of Materials Chemistry A

Page 12 of 23

energetically favourable. This electron transfer results in electrons becoming trapped in the Ag nanoparticles, and thus these nanoparticles could act as electron traps. In addition, the overall energy levels of the perovskite films were also evaluated by ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS). The results are shown in Figure S4 and summarized in Figure 4d. The valence bands of the perovskite films became deeper and deeper from pristine perovskite film to Film B to Film A, resulting in the down shift of the corresponding CBs from -4.01 eV to -4.06 eV to -4.17 eV, respectively. We attribute this result to the presence of Ag nanoparticles in the perovskite film, as high-concentration Ag nanoparticles might rearrange the overall CB of the whole perovskite film towards the work function of Ag metal. The down shift of the valance band of the overall perovskite film enlarged the energy offset between the PEDOT:PSS and perovskite layer, causing a larger energy loss at this interface. Therefore, both the enhanced trap density and down-shifted energy levels of perovskite film after Ag contamination could cause increased  $  V_{oc}  $  and  $  FF  $  loss for the whole device.

# Recombination and Extraction in PVSCs

From the standpoint of device properties, the presence of trap states will affect the recombination properties within the device, and therefore result in the losses of   $  V_{oc}  $   and   $  FF.^{45,46}  $  . As radiative recombination is inevitable, non-radiative recombination is the channel we should suppress for a high-performance PVSC.  $  ^{47}  $   In three-dimensional bulk perovskite, non-radiative recombination is dominated by trap-assisted recombination, and a faster extraction of photoexcited charge carriers to the electrodes can also help to suppress recombination within the device.  $  ^{18,48}  $   As discussed earlier, the trap densities and energy levels of perovskite films changed after being contaminated by Ag nanoparticles, which probably influenced the charge recombination and extraction properties in the device. Therefore, transient photovoltage measurement (TPV) and transient photocurrent measurement (TPC) were conducted to study the charge recombination and extraction properties of the devices. TPV was used to measure charge recombination under the   $  V_{oc}  $   condition, and the decay of   $  V_{oc}  $   occurred as the recombination of the charge carriers progressed. Figure 5a shows that the   $  V_{oc}  $   of the device with bare PCBM ETL suffered from a much faster decay compared with the device with PCBM/ZnO ETL, which suggests that severer charge recombination occurred within the device with bare PCBM ETL. In addition, interestingly, according to the TPC study shown in Figure 5b, the devices with bare PCBM and PCBM/ZnO ETLs presented similar charge extraction properties, although the device with PCBM/ZnO ETL still showed slightly faster charge extraction. This result correlates well with the slightly higher   $  J_{sc}  $   and EQE of the device with PCBM/ZnO ETL. In terms of energy level alignment, in the device with bare PCBM ETL, Ag diffusion caused a down shift of the CB of perovskite to -4.17 eV, which established a small barrier for electrons at the interface between the perovskite and PCBM layer (lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of -4.0 eV). Generally, such a barrier could retard electron transfer from perovskite to PCBM, which

Journal of Materials Chemistry A

obviously slows down charge extraction and reduces   $  J_{sc}  $   in the device. However, according to the TPC,   $  J_{sc}  $   and EQE measurement, this barrier had only a small impact on the charge extraction property of the device. We believe this phenomenon resulted from the broad energy state distribution of the LUMO of the PCBM layer, which still allowed the easy transfer of electrons from the perovskite to PCBM layer through the LUMO tail of the PCBM.  $  ^{49-51}  $

To further confirm the dominant recombination process, light-intensity-dependent   $  V_{oc}  $   and light-intensity-dependent   $  J_{sc}  $   measurement were conducted to investigate monomolecular and bimolecular recombination in the whole device. Typically, in light-intensity-dependent   $  V_{oc}  $   studies, the slope of the curve becomes   $  1\ k_{b}T/q  $   (where   $  k_{b}  $   and T represent the Boltzmann constant and temperature, respectively) if bimolecular recombination dominates total recombination, whereas the slope deviates from   $  1\ k_{b}T/q  $   and tends to be   $  2\ k_{b}T/q  $   if monomolecular recombination is involved. As Figure 5c shows, the curve of the device with bare PCBM ETL obtained a slope of   $  1.78\ k_{b}T/q  $ , which was larger than the slope of the device with PCBM/ZnO ETL (  $  1.51\ k_{b}T/q  $  ). This result suggests that the device with bare PCBM ETL suffered from severer monomolecular recombination than the device with PCBM/ZnO ETL. We attribute this result to the higher trap density of Film A compared with Film B, as the Ag nanoparticles can serve as recombination centres for charge carriers. In addition, through a light-intensity-dependent   $  J_{sc}  $   study, we can judge the severity of the bimolecular recombination under the short-circuit condition, as less bimolecular recombination means that the slope of the curve tends to be 1. Therefore, we conclude that a smaller bimolecular recombination occurred in the device with PCBM/ZnO ETL, compared with the device with bare PCBM ETL, because as shown in Figure 5d, the curve of the former had a smaller slope (0.998) than the latter device (0.987). Consequently, these results suggest that the device with PCBM/ZnO ETL underwent less monomolecular and bimolecular recombination, resulting in a reduction of total recombination, slower   $  V_{oc}  $   decay and ultimately smaller   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF losses.

# Conclusion

In this study, we observed the diffusion of Ag to the perovskite layer during the thermal deposition of an Ag electrode when using bare PCBM as the ETL in a PVSC based on an inverted structure. This diffused Ag presented in the form of Ag metal nanoparticles, and contaminated the perovskite layer with two main changes in the properties of the film: first, as the work function of Ag metal is deeper than the CB of perovskite, electron transfer from the CB of the perovskite to the Ag nanoparticles could occur, which meant that the Ag nanoparticles could act as trap states and hence increase the overall trap density of the perovskite layer. Second, both the CB and valence band of perovskite layer down-shifted after being contaminated by Ag nanoparticles. The down shift of the valence band of the perovskite layer could enlarge the energy offset between the perovskite and PEDOT:PSS layers, which resulted in a larger

energy loss at this interface. Both of these changes cause large  $  V_{oc}  $  and  $  FF  $  losses for the device, and thus the device with bare PCBM ETL showed an inferior PCE of  $  11.0\%  $  and a  $  V_{oc}  $  loss as high as  $  0.6\mathrm{V}  $ . Interestingly, a thin layer of ZnO inserted between the PCBM layer and Ag electrode efficiently suppressed Ag diffusion towards the perovskite layer, and hence suppressed the negative impacts of Ag contamination. Finally, with the help of the ZnO layer, we successfully demonstrated a highly efficient low-bandgap  $  \mathrm{FA}_{0.5}\mathrm{MA}_{0.5}\mathrm{Sn}_{0.5}\mathrm{Pb}_{0.5}\mathrm{I}_3  $  PVSC with PCE of  $  18.1\%  $  and low  $  V_{oc}  $  loss of  $  0.4\mathrm{V}  $ , which represents one of best results to date, as summarized in Table S2.

# Experimental Section

Precursor solution preparation: The  $ \mathrm{FASnI}_3 $  precursor solution was prepared by dissolving  $ 372~\mathrm{mg} $  of  $ \mathrm{SnI}_2 $  and  $ 172~\mathrm{mg} $  of formamidinium iodide with  $ 10\mathrm{mol}\% $  (15.6 mg) of  $ \mathrm{SnF}_2 $  in  $ 800~\mu \mathrm{L} $  N,N-dimethylmethanamide (DMF) and  $ 200~\mu \mathrm{L} $  dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO). The  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  precursor solution was prepared by dissolving  $ 461~\mathrm{mg} $   $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and  $ 159~\mathrm{mg} $  methylammonium iodide with  $ 3.5\mathrm{mol}\% $  (11.3 mg)  $ \mathrm{Pb(SCN)}_2 $  in  $ 630~\mu \mathrm{L} $  DMF and  $ 70~\mu \mathrm{L} $  DMSO. The  $ \mathrm{FA}_{0.5}\mathrm{MA}_{0.5}\mathrm{Sn}_{0.5}\mathrm{Pb}_{0.5}\mathrm{I}_3 $  precursor solution was obtained by mixing stoichiometrically identical amounts of  $ \mathrm{FASnI}_3 $  and  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  precursor solutions, followed by one hour of stirring before being spin-coated.

Device fabrication: The patterned ITO-coated glass was subsequently cleaned by sonication in detergent, de-ionised water, acetone and isopropyl alcohol, and then dried at 80 °C in a baking oven, followed by a 4-min oxygen plasma treatment. A 40-nm-thick PEDOT:PSS layer (Clevios P VP AI 4083) was spun cast onto the ITO substrate and dried for 15 min at 150 °C. The   $  FA_{0.5}MA_{0.5}Sn_{0.5}Pb_{0.5}I_{3}  $   precursor solution was then spun cast onto the PEDOT:PSS layer at 5000 rpm for 60 s. An anti-solvent dripping process by chlorobenzene was used during the spin-coating process of the   $  FA_{0.5}MA_{0.5}Sn_{0.5}Pb_{0.5}I_{3}  $   film. Subsequently, the perovskite film was annealed at 100 °C for 10 min in an   $  N_{2}  $  -filled glove box, followed by the deposition of a 70-nm-thick PCBM layer and 40-nm-thick ZnO nanoparticle (purchased from Sigma-Aldrich) layer. Finally, a 100-nm-thick silver electrode was thermally evaporated onto the substrates through a shadow mask (0.04 cm²).

Perovskite film characterisation: The SEM images were obtained by a Zeiss EVO 18 scanning electron

The device's TPC was measured by applying 530-nm laser pulses with a pulse width of 120 fs and a low pulse fluence to the short-circuit devices in the dark. The laser pulses were generated from an optical parametric amplifier (TOPAS-Prime) pumped by a mode-locked Ti:sapphire oscillator seeded regenerative amplifier, with a pulse energy of  $ 1.3\mathrm{mJ} $  at  $ 800\mathrm{nm} $  and a repetition rate of  $ 1\mathrm{KHz} $  (SpectraPhysics Spitfire Ace). The time resolution of the overall measurement was near 1 ns. The light-dependent  $ V_{oc} $  and  $ J_{sc} $  measurements were performed under the  $ J-V $  measurement system, and the light intensity was modulated from 0.1 sun to 1 sun.
