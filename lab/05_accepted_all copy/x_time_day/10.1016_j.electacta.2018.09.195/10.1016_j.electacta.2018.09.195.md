# Abstract

In this paper, a novel one-step solution approach is developed to prepare high quality methylammonium lead trihalide (MAPbI $ _3 $ ) perovskite films. This approach employs methanol as additive, which is added into the perovskite precursor solution. The use of methanol can enhance the absorption of MAPbI $ _3 $  and significantly improve the coverage of MAPbI $ _3 $  on a planar substrate. The fabricated perovskite solar cell (PSC) with methanol exhibits a higher power conversion efficiency (PCE) compared with that without methanol (19.51% vs 16.53%). Furthermore, the power conversion efficiencies of devices with methanol are still high after kept in dark under ambient environment for 30 days. This work provides a method to prepare high quality perovskite films for planar perovskite solar cells with high performance.

Keywords: Methanol additive, High power conversion efficiency, Stability, Planar  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite solar cells

# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs), first reported in 2009, have many advantages including simple fabrication, tunable band gaps, high absorption coefficients, high carrier mobility, long charge carrier diffusion lengths and high power conversion efficiencies (PCEs), thus attracting enormous research interest [1-4]. The rapid progresses in device optimization and materials engineering have further increased the PCEs of PSCs to over  $ 20\% $  in the past few years [5-15].

However, it remains a challenge to control the crystallization of perovskite films and improve the film quality. The crystallization of perovskite films can be affected by many factors, such as ambient temperature, humidity, annealing temperature, solution concentration, precursor composition and selected solvent [16-18]. The crystallization process can be controlled during film deposition, and post annealing process is often used to improve the quality of perovskite films [19, 20].

In fact, many other approaches have also been developed to improve the quality of perovskite thin films. Li et al. reported that the crystallization rate of perovskite films can be effectively controlled by incorporating butylphosphonic acid 4-ammonium chloride additives into the perovskite precursor solution [21]. Han et al. demonstrated that the device prepared by using LiCl as additive had a PCE of around  $ 14.5\% $  [22]. Jen et al. showed that the crystallization rate of perovskites can be controlled by adding 1,8-diiodooctane (DIO) into the precursor solution and a PCE of around  $ 11.8\% $  can be obtained [23]. Liao et al. demonstrated that perovskite

crystallization rate can be controlled by adding some water into the precursor solution and a PCE of around  $ 16.06\% $  was obtained [24]. Zhou et al. found that high quality perovskite polycrystalline films can be obtained by the addition of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_2 $  based solution [6]. Also, the intrinsic defect density of the perovskite polycrystalline film was lower by one order of magnitude than that of without additive, and a high PCE of  $ 20.02\% $  was obtained. The addition of additives seems to be an effective method to obtain high quality perovskite films. However, during this process, extra elements are introduced and they may be difficult to remove, thus resulting in the changes of perovskite components and the properties of carrier transport. Therefore, to prepare high quality perovskite films, more research should be carried out on new additives and the preparation process.

This is a simple and effective method to prepare high efficiency planar PSCs. In this study, to prepare high quality perovskite film on planar titanium dioxide  $ (\mathrm{TiO}_2) $  substrates, methanol was used as additive to be added to the standard  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  precursor solution containing methylammonium iodide (MAI,  $ \mathrm{MA} = \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3 $ ) and lead iodide  $ (\mathrm{PbI}_2) $ . Results show that the prepared perovskite film has improved uniformity and crystallinity and is compact and pinhole-free. The charge carrier extraction at the interface between perovskite layer and charge collection layer is enhanced. Compared with PSCs without methanol (PCE of  $ 16.53\% $ ), PSCs with methanol have a higher PCE ( $ 19.51\% $ ), lower hysteresis and greater air stability [19].

# 2.2 Device preparation

The  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -based precursor solution was prepared by dissolving the mixture of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and MAI (molar ratio of 1:1) in DMF/DMSO mixture solvent (4:1, by volume). Fluorine doped tin oxide (FTO) substrates were cleaned by scrubbing in soap water, sequentially sonicated in deionized water, acetone and isopropanol, and finally dried overnight in a vacuum drying box. These substrates were then subjected to an  $ \mathrm{O}_3 $ /ultraviolet treatment for  $ 15\mathrm{min} $  before use. Compact  $ \mathrm{TiO}_2 $  layers were prepared according to the previous report [25].

The perovskite precursor solution was then spin-coated onto the FTO/TiO $ _2 $  substrate at 1000 rpm for 10 s and then 5000 rpm for 40 s. During the last 25 s of the second spin-coating step, the substrate was treated with chlorobenzene drop-casting to form perovskite film. Then the film was annealed at 110 °C for 5 min. The thickness of MAPbI $ _3 $  layer was about 400 nm, determined by a profilometer (KLA-Tencor D-120). For the hole transport layer, the solution of spiro-OMeTAD was spin-coated onto the perovskite layer in a glove box at 3000 rpm for 30 s. The spiro-OMeTAD solutions were prepared by dissolving spiro-OMeTAD (90 mg), lithium bis(trifluoromethylsulphonyl)imide (18 μL, 500 mg/mL in acetonitrile) and

# 2.3 Devices and materials characterization

The morphologies of perovskite films were characterized by SEM (S4800, Hitachi, Japan). The X-ray diffraction (XRD) of prepared films was performed on a D/MAX-TTRIII with a push plug gobel mirror and a monochromatic Cu Kα ( $ \lambda = 1.5405 $  Å) radiation source. The reflection and transmission analysis was performed with a PE Lambda 650 UV–vis spectrophotometer in the 200–850 nm wavelength range at room temperature. The excitation and emission spectra were measured with a HORIBA Fluorolog-III spectrofluorometer and Lake Shore Cryotronics as the cryogenic refrigeration equipment. Luminescent lifetime curves were determined by a HORIBA Jobin-Yvon Fluorolog-III spectrofluorometer, and diode laser with wavelength of 467 nm was used as the excitation source. The fluorescence decay was acquired with a peak preset of 3000 counts. The decay data was analyzed using Data Analysis Software. The incident photon-to-electron conversion efficiency (IPCE or EQE) was measured by a Newport external quantum measurement system (Model 66920). Current-density versus voltage ( $ J $ - $ V $ ) curves of MAPbI $ _3 $  solar cells were measured using a solar simulator (Keithley 2400) with a source meter 100 mW cm $ ^{-2} $ , AM 1.5 G illumination.  $ J $ - $ V $  curves of solar cells with reverse (from 1.2 V to 0 V) and forward (from 0 V to 1.2 V) scans were recorded with a fixed voltage interval of 10 mV and dwelling time of 100 ms. To investigate the hysteresis issue, photo response behaviors of the PSCs were investigated in both the reverse and forward scanning directions (Fig. 3(c) and (d)). There is a noticeable hysteresis in the  $ J - V $  curve of the device without methanol (Fig. 3(c)). Such inconsistency mainly occurs at the bias near  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $ , resulting in under- (in forward scan) or over- (in reverse scan) estimated FF and PCE. This effect is due to ion motion, charge trapping and ferroelectricity, which leads to the accumulation of charge or specific ions in the vicinity of the contact area to re-shield the electric field and change the internal built-in electric field [28-30]. The hysteresis can be reduced with the addition of methanol (Fig. 3(d)). The hysteresis index (HI) for these devices can be calculated according to the following equation [31, 32].

$$
H I = \frac {J _ {R S} (0 . 8 V _ {o c}) - J _ {F S} (0 . 8 V _ {o c})}{J _ {R S} (0 . 8 V _ {o c})}
$$

The HI for PSCs without methanol is 0.16, whereas the HI for PSCs with methanol is only 0.02. The reduction in hysteresis improves the consistency of  $  J-V  $  curves in the two scanning directions (Table S2). These results suggest that the addition of methanol improves the quality of

perovskite film, reduces defects and suppresses the surface charge trapping, which results in the reduction of hysteresis in PSCs [33].

Further research was conducted to confirm that methanol as an additive can reduce the defects of perovskite polycrystalline film, and to obtain more insights into the photophysical origins of the reduced hysteresis. The irradiation (  $  P_{light}  $  ) dependent   $  V_{oc}  $   characteristics of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   solar cells provide straightforward evidence for the reduced trapping in both the surface and bulk of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   (Fig. S3). It is known that the deviation of slope from kT/q reflects trap-assisted recombination [34,35]. In the present case, the curve for the device without methanol exhibits a slope of 1.89 kT/q, indicating a strong energy loss due to the trap-assisted recombination [36,37]. For the device with methanol, the slope of the curve is decreased to 1.43 kT/q, indicating insignificant trap-assisted recombination, which is in good agreement with the conclusion from Fig. S4. The facts that the perovskite absorber layer formed with the addition of methanol shows a reduced trap-state density and the reduced slope of the curve for device with methanol approaches the unity affirm the suppressed Shockley–Read–Hall recombination with effective trap passivation, which agrees with the PL results. Therefore, methanol is effective in reducing the trap-state density, leading to suppressed J-V hysteresis and improved cell efficiency [34-36].

In addition, methanol can modify the DMSO proportion to a proper value in the intermediate perovskite phase to regulate perovskite formation [37]. The influence of incorporated methanol on perovskite precursor solution was examined using Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Fig. S5 a–d). The peak due to S=O stretching vibration in pure DMSO is observed at 1042 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  . This peak is shifted to 1020 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   when DMSO is mixed with MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  -based precursor solution, and to 1022 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   when DMSO is mixed with MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  -based precursor solution containing methanol. The peak due to C=O stretching vibration of DMF is observed at 1659 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  . It is shifted to 1658 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   when DMF is mixed with MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  -based precursor solution and to 1656 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   when DMF is mixed with MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  -based precursor solution containing methanol. Therefore, methanol can also remove redundant DMSO from the perovskite intermediate phase to accelerate the perovskite formation since DMSO can be slightly dissolved in methanol, which was proved by the FTIR spectra in Fig. S5. Therefore, methanol with an optimum concentration can extract a suitable amount of DMSO from the precursor solution to accelerate the formation of perovskite layer and an appropriate MAI–PbI  $  _{2}  $  –DMSO intermediate phase. Research has shown that a proper intermediate phase is a key to preparing perovskite layer with desirable morphology [39].

The morphologies of the perovskite films without and with (5 vol%) methanol were revealed by SEM (Fig. 4). The perovskite film without methanol exhibits small grains with apparent pin holes and grain boundaries (Fig. 4(a)). These defects can introduce strong energetic disorder, impede charge transport, induce recombination and reduce photovoltaic performance [40, 41]. The addition of methanol can significantly improve the morphology of perovskite film (Fig. 4(b)). The film with methanol has better surface roughness, larger grain size and fewer lateral grain boundaries than that without methanol. The grain size in the vertical direction of the

ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

film with methanol is comparable to the thickness of the film. This suggests that the charge carriers can efficiently transport across the perovskite film and reach the corresponding electrode interfaces before recombination [42]. Therefore, the introduction of methanol to perovskite precursor solution can improve both the surface smoothness and crystallinity of perovskite films, which are two important factors influencing the performance of PSCs [43, 44]. It is well known that the precursor solution can affect the crystallization process during the film formation. A smaller colloidal size can lead to a more uniform distribution of reactants (MAI source and Lead source) and thus facilitate the formation of final perovskite thin film [6]. When methanol is added into the perovskite solution, the small molecules might penetrate into the colloids and then the coordination of methanol to Pb (II) ions interrupts the connection of two adjacent  $ \mathrm{PbI}_{6 - x}\mathrm{M}_x $  octahedra (M represents other ligands such as MA), which in turn interferes the self-assembly process to decrease the cluster size [6]. In addition, similar to EtOH [6], methanol might promote the dissolving-recrystallization process, which benefits the grain growth towards larger grain size. With the addition of methanol, uniform polycrystalline perovskite films with larger grain size and less grain boundaries might be obtained. Therefore, the reduction of defect density to hinder charge recombination within the films can be achieved by the introduction of methanol during film growth, which is similar to the addition of MA or EtOH.

# 4. Conclusions

In this paper, methanol is used as an additive to prepare perovskite films. Adding a proper amount of methanol into perovskite precursor solution can not only improve the morphology and crystallinity of perovskite film, but also improve the optical and electrical properties of perovskite photoactive layer. Charge carrier extraction at the interface between the perovskite layer and charge collection layer is enhanced. The fabricated planar PSCs with methanol has the highest PCE, which is 19.51%. In addition, PSCs with methanol exhibit nearly negligible  $  J-V  $  hysteresis effects. More importantly, the high-quality perovskite films with methanol also have high stability. These results provide a new avenue for obtaining high efficiency, stable PSCs.
