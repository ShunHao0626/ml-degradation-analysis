# Abstract

Phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) has been widely researched as a passivate electron transport layer in planar n-i-p-type perovskite solar cells (PSCs). However, due to the terrible wettability of PCBM, the growth of perfect large-area perovskite films on the electron transport layer treated by PCBM is a large challenge, which limits the commercial application of PSCs. Herein, we incorporate a hydrophilic polymer polyethylene glycol (PEG) into PCBM to ameliorate its wettability. A high-quality perovskite film can be prepared on 2 cm x 2 cm substrate. Hydrogen bonding effects between the PEG-PCBM buffer layer and perovskite layer can further stabilize the electron transport layer/perovskite interface. Based on the improved electron transport and suppressed carrier recombination, a device with an active area of 1.03 cm $  ^{2}  $  achieves an efficiency of 18.25%. In addition, the first-principles calculations indicate that PEG has stronger adsorption ( $  E_{\text{ads}} = -0.37  $ ) toward H $  _{2}  $ O than the MAPbI $  _{3}  $  perovskite ( $  E_{\text{ads}} = -0.25  $ ), which can prevent water molecules from infiltrating perovskite. The unsealed device still maintains 90% of the initial efficiency under the ambient conditions, with 30-40% relative humidity for 22 days. These outstanding properties are attributed to the unique molecular structure and prominent wettability of PEG.

Keywords: Perovskite solar cells; Buffer layer; Wettability; Carrier recombination; Crystallinity;

# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have received wide attention and rapid development in recent years due to their advantages of simple and low-cost preparation processes, long carrier life and high mobility  $  ^{1-5}  $  . Due to constant optimization, PSCs have reached a high power conversion efficiency (PCE) exceeding 25%  $  ^{6}  $  . In a typical PSC, with either a mesoporous structure or a planar structure, a perovskite absorption layer is sandwiched between the electron transport layer (ETL) and the hole transport layer (HTL)  $  ^{7-9}  $  . Different functional layers are stacked together, producing several interfaces. In addition to materials themselves, these interfaces also play important roles in enhancing the performance of PSCs  $  ^{10-11}  $  . Recent studies indicate that the low open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ), obvious current-voltage (I-V) hysteresis and poor stability are related to the defects and trap states at perovskite/HTL or ETL/perovskite interfaces  $  ^{12-16}  $  . In addition, the interface is also important for depositing next layer of film  $  ^{17}  $  . Thus, the rational design and regulation of the interfaces of PSCs are crucial for improving device performance  $  ^{18-19}  $  , and deserve further research.

Passivation techniques are effective approaches to improve the performance of PSCs  $  ^{4, 20-22}  $  . Various materials have been used to passivate defects, such as quantum dots  $  ^{23-25}  $  , polymers  $  ^{26-28}  $  , organic halides  $  ^{29-30}  $   or fullerenes and their derivatives  $  ^{31-33}  $  . Among these materials, polymers, fullerenes and their derivatives have been extensively studied due to their unique properties. For instance, Zhao et al. incorporated a PCBM/PEG network into the perovskite film, which significantly passivated the trap states on grain boundaries. Cho et al. successfully passivate the oxygen defect at ZnO/perovskite interface by incorporating PEG to ZnO nanoparticles, resulting in significantly increased carrier lifetime and photovoltaic properties  $  ^{36}  $  . The perovskite can be connected to PCBM-PEG by hydrogen-bonding interactions, forming excellent interface contact and suppressing interfacial Shockley-Read-Hall recombination. In addition, the PCBM-PEG buffer layers can significantly improve the wettability of perovskite-precursor solution on substrate, so that the perovskite grains with preferential growth orientation can completely cover the substrate, which is conductive to decreasing the trap states of the bulk perovskite. As a result, the optimized devices shown significantly improved photovoltaic performance and stability, achieved PCE of  $ 20.2\% $  for active areas

# Experimental Section

# 2.1. Computational details

The spin polarized density functional theory calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP) software  $ ^{38} $ . The generalized gradient approximation of the Perdew, Burke, and Ernzernhof (PBE) functional  $ ^{39} $  were utilized for the exchange and correlation. The core electrons were replaced by the projector augmented wave (PAW) pseudo-potentials for improving the calculation efficiency  $ ^{40} $ . The wave functions were expanded in plane waves with a cut off energy of 450 eV. The convergence criterion for the electronic self-consistent iteration was set to  $ 10^{-4} $  eV, and the maximum force on each atom was less than 0.05 eV Å $ ^{-1} $ . The Brillouin zone was sampled respectively with a single K point at  $ \Gamma $ -centered and a K point of 7 x 5 x 7 for the slab and bulk models.

# 2.3. Device fabrication

ITO substrate was cleaned by ultrasonic processing with detergent solution, deionized water, acetone and at last with anhydrous ethanol for 15 min, respectively. Final Ozone exposure for 10 min to eliminate the traces of organic residues. SnO₂ colloidal dispersion was diluted by water (1:3 wt), and then spin-coated onto the clean ITO/glass substrates at 3000 rpm for 30 s and repeated for two times, then annealed at 150 °C for 1 h under ambient conditions. After ITO substrate was cooled in air, perovskite film was deposited in the glove box. For Sn/P-based devices, 10 mg mL⁻¹ PCBM precursor solution (in CB) was deposited on SnO₂ substrates with a spinning rate of 4000 rpm for 30 s. For Sn/PP-based devices, we first prepare 10 mg mL⁻¹ PEG (in CB) precursor solution, PCBM-PEG solution was obtained by blending individual PCBM solution and PEG solution. Then PCBM-PEG films were prepared by spin mixed solution at 4000r for 30 s. MAPbI₃ precursor solutions (1.4 M) were prepared in a mixture of DMF and DMSO with a volume ratio of 4:1. PbI₂ and MAI with a molar ratio of 1.1:1. Perovskite film was obtained via a two-step spin-coating process. Spinning speed of the first and second steps were 1000 and 4000 rpm, spinning time were 20 and 45 s, respectively. 120 μL of CB was deposited on the sample at the beginning of the second step. # 4. Conclusion

In short, a PCBM-PEG buffer layer with hydrophilic PEG additive was prepared by a simple solution spin-coating process. Modified perovskite films displayed excellent coverage and high-quality crystal, and the corresponding devices show outstanding photovoltaic performance. In addition, the  $ 1.03\mathrm{cm}^2 $  active area PSCs also exhibited comparable performance. PEG has strong adsorption toward  $ \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} $ , which can protect perovskite from  $ \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} $  in the air. The strong hydrogen-bonding interactions between perovskite and the PCBM-PEG buffer layer cause a stability ETL/perovskite interface. These improvements are attributed to the excellent properties of the PCBM-PEG buffer layer, which include favored furrow formation, good wettability and defect passivation, resulting in a high-quality perovskite film and superior interface contact. This strategy not only facilitates the fabrication of large-area PSCs but also improves device stability, which provides a new approach for the commercialization of PSCs.
