# Efficient Defect Passivation and Charge Extraction with Hexamethylenetetramine Interface Modification for Hole-Transporting Layers-Free   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   Perovskite Solar Cells

Mingran Sun, Jingwei Zhu, Benlin He,* Fan Bu, Junjie Ti, Xinpeng Yao, Haiyan Chen, Yanyan Duan, and Qunwei Tang*

# 1. Introduction

The passivation of defects at perovskite films, surfaces and the promotion of charge extraction across perovskite/carbon back interface are of vital importance to develop the power conversion efficiency (PCE) and stability of carbon-based perovskite solar cells (PSCs) free of hole-transporting layers (HTLs). Herein, an electron donor material with polyamino groups, hexamethylenetetramine (HMTA), is used as an efficient surface modifier for tribrominated all-inorganic perovskite films. The modification with HMTA not only eliminates the defects by forming a bond between N atoms and positively charged ions but also optimizes the energy level structure of the perovskite film and back interface contact. Therefore,   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   films with decreased trap states, extended carrier lifetimes, and enhanced hole mobility are gained, which significantly restrains charge recombination and energy loss as well as facilitates charge extraction and transfer at the perovskite/carbon interface. Finally, an improvement of PCE from 6.75% to 10.08% is obtained for carbon-based HTL-free   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   PSCs without and with HMTA modification, respectively. Furthermore, the HMTA-modified device without encapsulation presents an enhanced long-term moisture and heat stability after being stored in the atmospheric environment with 80% relative humidity (RH) at 25 °C and 20% RH at 85 °C, respectively, due to the reduced defects and the improved hydrophobicity of perovskite film.

whereas the full bromine structure perovskite (CsPbBr $ _3 $ ) exhibits outstanding tolerance toward high humidity and high temperature, which has attracted extensive attention from researchers and been regarded as a promising photoactive layer for stable PSCs. $ ^{[11]} $  Moreover, the classical CsPbBr $ _3 $  PSCs use carbon electrode instead of hole-transporting layers (HTLs) and precious metal electrode to extract holes and collect currents, which further simplifies the manufacturing process, reduces the cost, and improves the stability for giving impetus to the development of PSCs commercialization. Depressingly, the PCE of carbon-based CsPbBr $ _3 $  PSCs is relatively low compared to that of organic-inorganic hybrid and other all-inorganic perovskites-based devices. In addition to the narrow absorption spectrum of CsPbBr $ _3 $  perovskite itself, the charge recombination arising from the defects of perovskite film and the poor carrier extraction at perovskite/carbon back interface is the main factor leading to the dismal efficiency. $ ^{[12–14]} $

For instance, Li et al. creatively deposited chemical modified graphene oxides (rGOs) at the perovskite film surface for defects passivation, and the results demonstrated that rGO, as an effective interlayer, suppressed interface recombination and enhanced hole extraction to remarkably improve PSCs performance.  $  ^{[21]}  $   White et al. reported that the carbonyl (C=O) groups had an excellent passivation effect toward   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ions via exploring the molecular interactions between perovskite and poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA), and the corresponding double-side passivated PSCs by inserting PMMA films at perovskite/charge transport layer interfaces achieved 20.8% efficiency due to significantly reduced nonradiative recombination at perovskite surfaces and grain boundaries.  $  ^{[22]}  $   Zhu et al. successfully demonstrated that bidentate anchoring structure of 2-mercaptopyridine can form chelate with   $  Pb^{2+}  $   at perovskite surface for high-quality film preparation and trap states passivation, based on this function, the planar n-i-p structural   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   PSCs obtained an impressive PCE of 20.28% with the most excellent long-term stability among the similar device architecture.  $  ^{[18]}  $   More recently, several nitrogenous compounds (e.g., 4,4',4'',4'''- (pyrazine-2,3,5,6-tetrayl) tetrakis (N,N-bis(4-methoxyphenyl)aniline), cetyl trimethyl ammonium bromide)  $  ^{[23,24]}  $   have been used to effectively passivate surface defects of perovskite film by coordinating with dangling bonds to suppress charge recombination as well as energy loss and to promote carrier extraction at perovskite/HTLs interface for high-efficiency and stable PSCs.

Inspired by this, an electron donor polyamino material of hexamethylenetetramine (HMTA) with high thermal (boiling point temperature/sublimation: 263 °C) and long-term wet stabilities (storage conditions without humidity control) is here used as a modifier to deposit on the   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   film surface to achieve a suppression of charge recombination. Systematic characterizations demonstrate that the N atoms in HMTA can bond with the undercoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and   $  Cs^{+}  $  to tremendously reduce the surface defects of perovskite film after modification to inhibit charge recombination and energy loss. At the same time, the modification of HMTA can also improve the energy level matching and hole extraction ability of   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   as well as the contact at   $  CsPbBr_{3}/carbon  $   interface to boost back interfacial charge extraction. By optimizing the concentration of HMTA, a maximum PCE as high as 10.08% is obtained for carbon-based   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   PSCs without HTLs, which is 49.33% higher than 6.75% efficiency of the original

device. Furthermore, in the absence of packaging, the device shows excellent wet and thermal stability after being stored in an atmospheric environment with a relative humidity of 80% over 40 days and a high temperature of 85 °C over 200 h, respectively. The results demonstrate that HMTA interface modification can reduce defect states and boost holes extraction for retarded charge recombination, thus providing a promising way to improve the performance of carbon-based HTLs-free CsPbBr  $  _{3}  $  PSCs.

# 2. Results and Discussion

HMTA, a polyamines compound with high thermal and long-term wet stabilities, is selected as an interface modifier to passivate the surface imperfections of perovskite film and improve carrier extraction. The procedure of PSCs preparation and HMTA modification is detailedly shown in the Experimental Section and Figure S1, Supporting Information. According to the defect categories of perovskite film and passivation mechanism, the nitrogen atoms with lone-pair electrons in HMTA can form a coordinate bond with the free   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and   $  Cs^{+}  $  on the perovskite film surface, as shown in Figure 1a, thereby reducing the trap states-induced charge nonradiative recombination for increased current output.  $  ^{[25]}  $   To corroborate the interaction between HMTA and uncoordinated   $  Cs^{+}  $  as well as   $  Pb^{2+}  $   of perovskite, the fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) of pure HMTA,   $  HMTA + CsBr  $ , and   $  HMTA + PbBr_{2}  $   samples is characterized and shown in Figure 1b. By comparison, it is clear that the vibration peaks of the C—N bond for   $  HMTA + PbBr_{2}  $   and   $  HMTA + CsBr  $   are shifted toward low wave number of 1062 and 1058 cm  $  ^{-1}  $ , respectively, from 1066 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   for the pure HMTA sample, which indicates the weakening of the C—N bond arising from the combination of nitrogen atoms in HMTA with cesium and lead ions.  $  ^{[26]}  $   X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) characterization is performed and described in Figure 1c,d and Figure S2, Supporting Information, to further explore the passivation effect of HMTA toward metal ionic defects on   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   film surface. Obviously, the peaks of Cs 3 d and Pb 4f for the HMTA-modified   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   film shift to a lower binding energy direction compared with that for the control one, illustrating a decrease in electron affinity and an increase in electron cloud density, which evidences the cesium and lead atoms would receive a large number of electrons by combination with HMTA.  $  ^{[27]}  $   Combined with the analysis of FTIR and XPS, a conclusion can be drawn that HMTA displays a passivation effect toward surface defects of perovskite film though anchoring the uncoordinated   $  Cs^{+}  $  and   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ions by its electron-donated nitrogen atoms, which is beneficial to the suppression of carrier nonradiative recombination and the promotion of carrier transfer and extraction.

To probe the effect of HMTA interface modification on the defect states density and carrier recombination behavior, steady-state PL and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectra of the control and HMTA-modified   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   films directly deposited on glass substrates are measured. As shown in Figure 1e, all the perovskite films show an obvious emission peak at about 530 nm wavelength, which corresponds to the bandgap of   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $  . Compared with the pristine film, the HMTA-modified   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   films manifest a slight blueshift PL peak, demonstrating an effective passivation of HMTA toward

At the same time, given the inverse relationship between the interfacial charge density and the linear slope of the M-S curves, the larger slope of HMTA-modified devices than that of the original PSC manifests the lower charge density at the former devices interface, that is, the charge extraction in the former devices is effectively promoted due to the reduced energy level barrier and improved contact at   $  CsPbBr_{3}/carbon  $   interface with HMTA modification, which also causes a restrained charge recombination and energy loss. The suppression of charge recombination can be distinctly identified by the dark J-V curve characterizations shown in Figure 4f. The leakage current density of HMTA-modified devices is substantially lower than that of the original   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   PSC, whereas all the devices present a similar current density output in the forward direction, demonstrating a higher rectification ratio and thereby an enhanced charge selectivity of the PSCs after modifying with HMTA,  $  ^{[46]}  $   which is mainly ascribed to the improved hole mobility and energy level matching of HMTA-treated perovskite films and results in an inhibited charge recombination and current loss inside the devices for enhanced   $  J_{SC}  $   and PCE output. Through the analysis of the aforementioned characterizations, it is apparently that the diminished charge recombination arising from the defects passivation, the energy

level matching and the hole mobility promotion after HMTA modification is the root for the enhanced photovoltaic performance of carbon-based HTLs-free   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   PSCs.

The long-term stability of the PSCs in the atmospheric environment is as important as efficiency in evaluating device performance. To deeply inspect the effect of HMTA interface modification on the stability of   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   PSCs, the photovoltaic performance of the unencapsulated devices with and without HMTA treatment stored in air with high humidity of 80% RH at 25 °C and high heat of 85 °C at 20% RH is systematically tested, respectively. From the normalized photovoltaic parameters over time summarized in Figure 5, it can be found that the HMTA-2-modified PSC maintains a higher retention of photovoltaic parameters including PCE (94%),   $  J_{SC}  $   (97%),   $  V_{OC}  $   (99%), and FF (98%) than the original device (PCE: 76%,   $  J_{SC}  $  : 90%,   $  V_{OC}  $  : 93%, and FF: 91%) after 40 days of 80% RH attack in air, indicating an evidently enhanced tolerance to humidity of the device with HMTA treatment. Moreover, as shown in Figure S7, Supporting Information, the higher retentions of initial photovoltaic parameters for HMTA-2-modified solar cell than those for the control device after 200 h aging at 85 °C in air with 20% RH suggest that the introduction of HMTA molecules also effectively improves the long-term thermal stability of   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   PSCs. To probe the mechanism behind the aforementioned phenomena, the contact angle of water droplets on different   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   films is detected and shown in Figure S8, Supporting Information. The larger contact angle (from 60.47° to 81.11°) of the   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   films with HMTA modification than 52.08° of the pristine one implies a higher hydrophobicity of the former films due to the more dense and uniform morphology after HMTA treatment, which is beneficial to prevent the penetration of moisture and therefore the decomposition of perovskite structure to strengthen the device stability.  $  ^{[51]}  $   Furthermore, the reduced defects and the enhanced crystallinity of HMTA-modified   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   films also boost the long tolerance of PSCs toward humid and thermal environment.

# 3. Conclusions

In summary, we have launched that highly efficient and stable carbon-based HTLs-free   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   PSCs are attained employing polyamine compounds, HMTA, as an interface modifier. The nitrogen atoms in HMTA afford lone-pair electrons to bind with the free   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and   $  Cs^{+}  $   ionic imperfections on the perovskite surface, which reduces the nonradiative recombination and prolongs the carrier lifetime to improve charge extraction. In addition, the band structure adjustment of perovskite films due to surface dipole formation after HMTA modification also promotes charge separation and transfer. Consequently, the PCE of HMTA-modified   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   PSCs substantially enhances to 10.08% from 6.75% of the original device. Furthermore, the decreased imperfections and the increased hydrophobicity of the   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   films with HMTA treatment enable an improved long-term stability for PSCs in air with 80% RH at 25 °C and 20% RH at 85 °C. This result introduces a promising interface modification strategy toward high-efficiency and stable HTLs-free   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   PSCs.

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials and Reagents: HMTA (Aladdin Industrial Corporation; purity >99.0%), lead bromide (PbBr₂, Aladdin Industrial Corporation; purity >99.0%), cesium bromide (CsBr, Aladdin Industrial Corporation, purity >99.9%). The materials for preparing TiO₂ paste were purchased from Aladdin Industrial Corporation. Carbon paste was purchased from Shanghai MaterWin New Materials Co. Ltd. All the chemicals were used without further purification.

Preparation of the   $  TiO_{2}  $   Colloid: The   $  TiO_{2}  $   colloid used in this work was prepared by a sol–hydrothermal method. The specific process was as follows: a 10 mL of tetra-n-butyl titanate was slowly added dropwise to 100 mL of deionized water, and stirred at room temperature for 30 min to obtain a white powder. Subsequently, a 10 mL of acetic acid and a 0.8 mL of nitric acid were slowly added dropwise, and stirred at 80 °C for 15 min to form a thick emulsion. Then, a 160 mL of deionized water was added to obtain a light blue transparent liquid. The liquid was transferred to a high-pressure hydrothermal kettle and heated at 200 °C for 12 h, after cooling, a 0.4 g of P25 was added and ultrasound for 30 min. The same hydrothermal reaction was performed again. After cooling to room temperature, a 0.8 g of polyethylene glycol and a 1 mL of op emulsifier (OP) solution were added. Finally, the prepared solution was concentrated.

Assembly of PSCs: The device was completely assembled under air conditions. The fabrication process was a multistep spin-coating technology developed by our group. First, the conductive glass was etched with zinc powder and 2 M HCl to obtain the desired pattern, and then washed thoroughly with deionized water and ethanol. A 0.5 M isopropyl titanate ethanol solution was spin coated on a clean FTO substrate at 7000 rpm for 30 s and calcined in a muffle furnace at 500 °C for 2 h to obtain a dense TiO₂ layer (c-TiO₂). Subsequently, the TiO₂ colloid was spin coated at 2000 rpm for 30 s and calcined at 450 °C for 30 min to prepare a

mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   (m-  $  TiO_{2}  $  ) layer. Finally, the obtained c-  $  TiO_{2}  $  /m-  $  TiO_{2}  $   was immersed in 0.04 M   $  TiCl_{4}  $   solution at 70 °C for 30 min, and then calcined at 450 °C for 30 min after washing with deionized water and ethanol in turn. Next, DMF solution of   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (1M) was spin coated at 2000 rpm for 30 s, and heated on a heating table at 90 °C for 30 min to evaporate the solvent. After cooling, a 0.07 M methanol solution of cesium bromide was spin coated on the   $  PbBr_{2}  $   film at 2000 rpm for 30 s and heated at 250 °C for 5 min, this process was repeated seven times to prepare a   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   film. The isopropyl alcohol solution of HMTA with different concentrations (0.5, 2, 5, and 10 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   denoted HMTA-0.5, HMTA-2, HMTA-5, and HMTA-10, respectively) was spin coated at 2000 rpm for 30 s on the   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   film and placed on a hot plate at 200 °C for 10 min. Ultimately, the modified   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   film was coated with a layer of commercially available carbon paste by knife coating and dried on a hot plate at 90 °C for 30 min to achieve a complete PSC.

Measurements and Characterizations: The surface and cross-sectional morphologies of the perovskite film were observed by field-emission SEM (Nissan Hitachi S-4800). FTIR spectroscopy was characterized by the instrument of NEXUS 670. The perovskite crystal phase was detected by a X-ray diffractometer (Bruker D8 ADVANCE) with Cu Kα (  $  \lambda = 1.5406 \AA  $  ). The light absorption properties of perovskite films were characterized by UV–vis spectrophotometer (Meipuda UV-3200). XPS was tested by RBD upgraded PHI-5000C ESCA system (Perkin Elmer) with Mg K2α (  $  h\nu = 1253.6 \, eV  $  ). The band structure of perovskite films was obtained by UPS with a model of Kratos AXIS ULTRA. TRPL was measured by a Horiba spectrometer with an excitation wavelength of 510 nm. Steady-state PL was characterized by a Fluoro Max-4 spectrometer under 350 nm excitation wavelength. The J–V curve of the device was recorded using the CHI660E electrochemical workstation under AM 1.5 G irradiation (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , Newport, Oriel Class A, 91195 A) calibrated by a standard silicon solar cell.
