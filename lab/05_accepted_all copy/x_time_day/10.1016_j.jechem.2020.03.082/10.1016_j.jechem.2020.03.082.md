# Abstract:

Outdoor environment including moisture, dust, UV, oxygen and thermal stress (repeated heating-cooling) is devastating to perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Here, we demonstrate a new strategy to make fully printed PSCs stable with maximum power output in outdoor environment by coating them with a porous hydrophobic inorganic layer. After coating, the PSCs can maintain superior stability of more than 150 days of outdoor storage,  $ 240\mathrm{h} $  of continuous operation at the maximum power output point in ambient air with relative humidity as high as  $ \sim 80\% $ , and stable operation for more

than 10 h under raining condition. ANSYS simulation shows that the thin and porous nature of the inorganic coating layer offers much better heat dissipation than conventional encapsulation methods using glasses attached by photocurable epoxy. A similar thermal expansion coefficient of the inorganic encapsulation material with the solar cell substrate can also prevent it from cracking after repeated heating-cooling cycles. All of these merits resulted from our encapsulation method endow the perovskite solar cells with the real outdoor working capability.

Keywords: Perovskite solar cells; Inorganic encapsulation; Heat dissipation; Outdoor stability

# 1. Introduction

Hybrid organic-inorganic perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have shown impressive progresses over the past ten years, with power conversion efficiency (PCE) rocketed from ~4% in 2009 to a certified 25.2% in 2019 [1–6]. The rapid and tremendous improvements on the PCE of PSCs stimulate great interests to move this technology a step further towards commercialization. However, the long-term stability remains a daunting challenge to be addressed in PSCs, owing to feasible degradation of the perovskite light absorbers under outdoor circumstance [7,8].

Outdoor environment including moisture, UV, oxygen and temperature can degrade PSCs, among which moisture is most notorious that will rapidly decompose the light absorber   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   into   $  PbI_{2}  $  . Many strategies, from materials to structure, have been applied to improving the PSCs stability [9–13]. Partially replacing A and/or X in the perovskite   $  ABX_{3}  $   structure with other ions is widely researched. In this

context,   $  Cs^{+}  $  ,   $  NH_{2}CHNH_{2}^{+}  $  ,   $  Br^{-}  $  ,   $  Cl^{-}  $   etc. ions have shown great potential [13,14]. Furthermore, Additives such as chloride salt [15,16], small organic molecular [17] have been shown improving the perovskite crystallinity or linked perovskite crystals together, leading to higher resistance to moisture [18]. Engineering   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   contacts to suppress moisture permeation is another popular way to improve PSCs stability [19–21]. Very recently, Yang et al. find an effective way to engineering contact by converting the surface organic Pb to inorganic lead oxysalts [22].

On the aspects of structure engineering strategies, planar, inverted, and fully printed PSCs have been developed. Among them, fully printable structures have achieved great stability improvements   $  [23–26]  $  . But their stability against high humidity and water needs further improvement. Encapsulation PSCs with a moisture barrier layer offer great opportunities to improve the stability. Hydrophobic organics, such as Teflon, poly(ethylene oxide, PEO) and photocurable fluoropolymers were recently investigated, which showed great improvement of the stability   $  [27–34]  $  . However, those organic films may encounter detachment/crack and leakage of lead under outdoor working circumstances, where they face repeated heating-cooling cycles   $  [30,35,36]  $  . Furthermore, organic films are generally thick and densely packed, which are less efficient to dissipate heat out of the cell when the cell works under high-intensity solar illumination   $  [34,35]  $  . Besides, atomic layer deposition (ALD) of alumina has also shown effective in improving the stability of PSCs   $  [37]  $  . However, the ALD technique is expensive and difficult to be scaled up. Hence, finding a facial, lost-cost and scalable strategy to protect PSCs is still highly desirable.

Here, we report a facial and low-cost spray coating method to fabricate a thin porous superhydrophobic inorganic film on PSCs to overcome the cell vulnerability under humid and thermal stress conditions. The PSCs coated with a thin layer of superhydrophobic  $ \mathrm{ZrO_2} $  nanoparticles showed superior stability of more than 150 days of outdoor shelf time,  $ 240\mathrm{h} $  of continuous operation at the maximum power output point in ambient air with humidity as high as  $ \sim 80\% $ , and stable operation for more than  $ 10\mathrm{h} $  under raining condition.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1 Screen-printable pastes preparation

# 2.1.1 Preparation of titania paste

The titania nanoparticles were synthesized according to our previous work [26]. For preparation of the paste,  $ 1\mathrm{g} $  of the as-prepared  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  was dispersed in  $ 20~\mathrm{mL} $  of ethanol in a flask. In another flask,  $ 0.5\mathrm{g} $  of ethyl cellulose was dissolved in  $ 4.5\mathrm{g} $  of ethanol. The solutions in the two flasks were mixed together, and then  $ 5\mathrm{g} $  of terpineol was added. The mixed solution was homogenized with a titanium ultrasonic horn for more than  $ 10\mathrm{h} $ . Finally, ethanol was removed through rotary evaporation to form the paste.

# 2.1.2Preparation of carbon paste

Graphite powder and carbon black with a weight ratio of 3:1 were ground mixed for 30 min. Then 8 g of the mixed powder was added into 30 mL of terpineol under vigorous stirring. After that, 1 g of zirconia nanoparticles (20–40 nm) and 1 g of

hydroxypropyl cellulose were added. The mixtures were ball milled for  $ 10\mathrm{h} $  to obtain the carbon paste.

# 2.1.3 Preparation of   $  ZrO_{2}  $   paste

$ \mathrm{ZrO_2} $  paste was prepared with a similar method of making titania paste, where zirconia nanoparticles (20–40 nm) were used instead of titania nanoparticles.

# 2.2 Air brushing zirconia ink preparation

0.1 g of 1H, 1H, 2H, 2H-perfluorooctyltriethoxysilane was added into 9.9 g of anhydrous isopropanol, and the solution was mechanically stirred for 2 h. To the resulting solution, 0.2 g of zirconia nanoparticles with sizes between 20–40 nm was added. The mixture was sonicated for 4 h to make a homogeneous ink for air brushing.

# 2.3 Cell fabrication

All processes of cell fabrication were conducted in ambient condition.

# 2.3.1 Fully printable cells fabrication

The fully-printed  $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / ZrO_2} $  carbon triple layer PSCs were fabricated on fluorine doped tin oxide (FTO) glass substrates using screen-printing techniques. Firstly, zinc powder and  $ 2\mathrm{M} $  hydrochloric acid were used to pattern fluorine-doped  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  (FTO) substrate. A compact layer of titania was formed by spray pyrolysis of a  $ 20~\mathrm{mM} $  titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetylacetonate) solution at  $ 450^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  and then sintered for 1 h. Subsequently,  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  and  $ \mathrm{ZrO_2} $  pastes were screen printed on the compact titania one after the other and sintered at  $ 500^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 30\mathrm{min} $ . Following, the carbon paste was screen printed on the  $ \mathrm{ZrO_2} $  layer and sintered at  $ 400^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 30\mathrm{min} $ . The cells were

# 2.3.2 Superhydrophobic zirconia coating process

The as-fabricated fully-printable PSCs was placed on a hotplate with the carbon electrode facing up and heated at  $ 120^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for 10 min. Then, the zirconia ink was air brushed onto the carbon electrode for some cycles (9 cycles give  $ 3.73\mu \mathrm{m} $  thickness) to complete the coating. Here, one cycle means brushing the cell from left to right then back to left. Afterwards, the cell was heated for another 10 min at  $ 120^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . For comparison, Ossila encapsulation epoxy was used as glue to attach a cover-glass on top of the carbon electrode to seal the cell. After 1 min exposure to 300 W UV, the sealing process was finished.

# 2.4 Cell characterization

J-V curves were recorded on a Keithley 2400 source meter with an aperture mask of  $ 0.07~\mathrm{cm}^2 $ . Light illumination was performed with Abet 2000 solar simulator. The IPCE spectra were measured using a lock-in amplifier (Stanford Research Systems, SRS 810) with white light channeled from a Newport 300 W Xenon lamp and through a  $ 17\mathrm{Hz} $  mechanical chopper wheel and a monochromator (Oriel Cornerstone 130). One heating-cooling cycle means keeping the PSC in an electric oven at  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 4\mathrm{h} $  and then take it out to cool it down naturally for  $ 4\mathrm{h} $ .

Temperature dependent J-V curves were recorded on a hotplate. The PSCs were heated to desired temperatures for  $ 30\mathrm{min} $  and tested on-site. J-V curves of PSCs as a

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained on a Bruker D8 X-ray powder diffractometer. Film thickness was measured by a KLA-TENCOR surface profiler. # 2.5 Simulation of heat dissipation

The commercially available software ANSYS 15.0 has been used to simulate the temperature at the interface between perovskite solar cell and coating layer. The size for the perovskite solar cell used in simulation is the FTO size which is  $ 12 \times 8 \times 2.2 \mathrm{~mm} $  (length  $ \times $  width  $ \times $  height). The layer thickness is set to  $ 10 \mu \mathrm{m} $  for the porous and nonporous zirconia, and  $ 200 \mu \mathrm{m} $  for the photocurable epoxy attached cover-glass. The physical properties of zirconia and glass (silicon) were adopted for the simulation.

# 3. Results and discussion

To make PSCs stable under outdoor operating conditions, surface functionalized zirconia nanoparticles were applied to encapsulate PSCs to block moisture and water from decomposing the perovskite light absorbers. The surface of zirconia nanoparticles was modified by perfluorooctyltriethoxysilane to make them

Fig. 2(a & b) compare the top surface morphology of the cell with and without   $  ZrO_{2}  $   coating. Both cells display rough surfaces. To show the priority of zirconia, we also compared titania and alumina in our experiments. When titania was used, the hydrophobic property of the surface could only last for about several weeks, mainly because of the high photocatalytic activity of titania. When alumina (50–150 nm in size) was applied, it could not be well dispersed in isopropanol. After spray coating, the alumina film is coagulated, which is less hydrophobic as compared to the zirconia film. SEM images of the alumina surface on the solar cell are shown in Fig. S4.

The stability of the cells was first examined by keeping them outdoor in a transparent box (relative humidity 70–100%) to avoid direct contact with water and allow sunlight illumination. Fig. 4 shows the change of the cell performance as a function of time. It can be seen that with zirconia coating, the cells can maintain very stable performance for more than 150 days storage without any noticeable degradation. On the other hand, the performance of the cells without coating starts to decay after about 40 days of storage. The PCE is decreased by about 50% after 100 days. The decrease of PCE is mainly originated from decrease of   $  J_{sc}  $   due to perovskite decomposition. XRD characterization of the uncoated cell confirms that the perovskite is gradually decomposed into lead iodide during outdoor storage (Fig. S6). If we stored the cells in an electric dry cabinet with 45% relative humidity, the PCE of the cell with hydrophobic coating did not decrease after 400 days (Fig. S7), while the PCE of the cell without coating decreased by 70%. These results show that humidity

To make the stability test even hasher, we directly immersed the PSCs into water. The color of the cell without zirconia coating immediately changed from black to yellow (Fig. S8), indicating rapid perovskite decomposition, which severely damaged the cell and degraded the cell performance. On the contrary, the zirconia-coated PSCs could maintain very stable performances even fully immersed in water (Movie S1). Furthermore, we tested the cell stability by operating it at its maximum power output point under artificial raining condition with full AM 1.5 G illumination (Movie S2, S3). The cell can keep the current output for more than 10 h even in such a harsh circumstance (Fig. S9). To test the outdoor application potential under full weather conditions, the zirconia-coated PSCs were kept outside in a rainy day for 2 h (  $  22^{nd}  $  Dec. 2016, 13:00–15:00 at SCBE NTU), no obvious degradation in cell performance was noticed (Fig. S10). Additionally, it needs to be highlighted that the zirconia

Solar cell performance is largely dependent on operating temperature [45]. Continuous full sunlight illumination and cell operation can increase the cell temperature, even to a temperature higher than  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . Such a high temperature could dramatically lower the PCE of solar cells. The temperature issue becomes more problematic in PSCs, where perovskite encounters phase transition at only around 50  $ {}^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  [46]. Fig. 6(a) displays the temperature dependent cell performance of our PSCs. It can be clearly seen that the cell performance decreases by more than  $ 23\% $  if the cell temperature is increased from room temperature to  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . More severely, repeated overheating could permanently damage the cell. Therefore, it is necessary to quickly dissipate the heat in PSCs during cell operation. Here, our zirconia coating shall offer better heat dissipation capability than the conventional encapsulation methods using photocurable polymers with glass cover, because of its porous nature. To verify this, we performed a theoretical simulation on heat dissipation performance of porous zirconia, non-porous zirconia and glass cover coatings on the perovskite solar cell using ANSYS software (models Fig. S12). The results show that the temperature of a device can decrease from  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  to room temperature in about 2 min with a porous zirconia coating (Fig. S13). But it needs about 3 and 5 min for the device to cool from  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  to room temperature with non-porous zirconia and glass cover coating respectively (Fig. S13). The corresponding temperature distribution after 300 seconds

As shown in Fig. 6(b & c), it can be seen that it only takes less than 1 min for the zirconia coated cell to return back to its initial performance at room temperature, while the cell encapsulated with photocurable polymer and glass needs almost 5 min.

Last but not least, solar cells have to experience repeated heating and cooling cycles during their lifetimes, which makes stringent demands on the encapsulation materials to ensure the cell stability. Zirconia has a similar thermal expansion

coefficient   $  (10\times10^{-6}/\mathrm{K})  $   as glass substrate   $  (9\times10^{-6}\;\mathrm{K}^{-1})  $  , much smaller than that of widely used organics such as poly(ethylene-co-vinylacetate) (  $  \sim150\times10^{-6}\;K^{-1}  $  ), photocurable epoxy (  $  \sim55\times10^{-6}\;K^{-1}  $  ). Accordingly, the inorganic zirconia is expected to have better thermal compatibility than organics when used as the encapsulation material for PSCs. In order to simulate the extreme temperature environment of the cells operating outdoor, we forced the PSCs to undergo repeated heating and cooling cycles (one cycle stands for: 85 °C for 4 h, followed by stay at room temperature for 4 h). We compared the stability of the cells between zirconia coating and photocurable epoxy encapsulation. The test results are summarized in Fig. 6(d). The zirconia-coated PSC can maintain very stable performance after 100 cycles, while the performance of photocurable epoxy encapsulated PSC starts to decay after 40 cycles. All the above stability tests show that our coating strategy can effectively protect the PSCs. Comparing to other coating/encapsulating strategies (Table S2), our strategy has several advantages: 1. The spray-coating method is facial and low-cost, and is compatible with the PSC fabrication process. 2. The expansion coefficient of zirconia is similar to the FTO substrate, which can improve the PSC durability on day and night heating-cooling cycles. 3. The zirconia coating film has good heat dissipation capability because of its porous nature.

# 4. Conclusions

In summary, we have demonstrated a facile and efficient method to stabilize perovskite solar cells under outdoor cell-operating conditions. The porous inorganic coating could efficiently repel water and moisture in ambient air as well as quickly
