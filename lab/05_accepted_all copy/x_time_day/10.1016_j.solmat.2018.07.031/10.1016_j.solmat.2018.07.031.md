# 1. Introduction

In recent years, organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells have stood out and attracted great attention, achieving tremendous and incomparable progresses. The general formula of perovskite is  $ \mathrm{ABX}_3 $ , where A site is usually organic or inorganic monovalent cation, B site is divalent metal ion, and C site is usually halide anion. In theory, the crystal structure of perovskite is cubic phase. Such a highly ordered crystal structure endows perovskite materials many excellent optoelectronic properties such as good light harvesting, large exciton diffusion length, low exciton binding energy, adjustable optical band gap [1-4], greatly broadening the application prospect of perovskite [5,6]. Hybrid perovskite solar cells are expected to be industrialized and become the next generation of thin film solar cells. In the past few years, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of perovskite solar cells has been promoted rapidly from original  $ 3.8 - 22.7\% $  [7-11]. Although the

Herein, 1,6-Diaminohexane Dihydrochloride (1,6-DD) is introduced into perovskite precursors to fabricate the inverted planar perovskite solar cells. By regulating the concentration of 1,6-DD additive, the average power conversion efficiency (PCE) of perovskite solar cells is enhanced by  $ 20\% $ . The champion device achieves a relatively high PCE of  $ 17\% $  and an excellent fill factor of  $ 80.1\% $ . The PCE of the large-area  $ (1\mathrm{cm}^2) $  device also reaches to  $ 13.68\% $ . After exposure to the air for 16 days, the device with 1,6-DD additive still retains above  $ 90\% $  of the initial efficiency, exhibiting good stability. We demonstrate that a small amount of 1,6-DD affects the crystallization dynamic, yielding ideal perovskite film with enhanced crystallinity and enlarged grain size. The two terminal  $ -\mathrm{NH}_3^+ $  groups passivates the vacancy defects at the perovskite crystal surface, suppressing charge recombination and facilitating charge transportation effectively. Meanwhile, adjacent crystal surfaces are linked through the hexane alkyl chain of 1,6-DD molecule, which enhances the interaction between perovskite grains and anchors the microstructure of perovskite to some degree. Hydrophobic hexane alkyl chains also increase the moisture resistance of perovskite film. Thus, an easy and effective way is provided for fabricating efficient and stable perovskite solar cells.

# ABSTRACT

current PCE of perovskite solar cells has surpassed other thin film solar cells [12,13], even comparable to commercial silicon-based solar cells, there is still a big gap in the solar cell lifetime or long-term stability. Therefore, synchronously improving the efficiency and stability is the precondition of commercialization for perovskite solar cells.

The high-quality perovskite film with larger crystals and fewer defects is a priority for high efficiency and stable perovskite solar cells. However, defects often exist in perovskite film. For instance, point defects such as vacancies and interstitials are the common defects found in perovskite   $  [14]  $  . Interstitial defects depend on halide-poor or haliderich conditions   $  [15]  $  . Vacancy defects mostly stem from the loss of organic halide (e.g., methylammonium iodide) during the thermal annealing stage in the perovskite crystallization process. Point defects are responsible for nonradiative recombination in the perovskite active layer. In addition, polycrystalline perovskite film prepared via solution method often has a large number of grain boundaries   $  [16–18]  $  . Usually,

grain boundaries are considered as defect-assisted recombination centers for photo-generated charges, not conductive to charge separation and transportation. The defect concentration at grain boundaries is correlated with grain size. The large-grain perovskite film has a lower concentration of bulk defects and higher mobility than small-grain perovskite film [19]. Also, grain boundaries are susceptible to erosion by moisture, which is detrimental to the stability of perovskite materials and solar cells. Many efforts were made to achieve high-quality perovskite film with larger grain size and fewer defects, such as additive engineering [20], solvent engineering [21], optimization of process technology [19,22] and so on. To improve the photovoltaic performance and stability of perovskite solar cells, it is necessary to reduce point defects, increase crystal size or lessen grain boundaries.

Methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI $ _3 $ ) is the most successful and general perovskite material for photovoltaic applications. But at room temperature, the three-dimensional crystal structure of MAPbI $ _3 $  is distorted, not a real cubic structure in theory, because the methylammonium (MA) cations has polarity properties and can easily rotate within the inorganic octahedra lattice [23,24]. Lattice distortion of perovskite causes non-ohmic hysteresis under an external field, which is not favorable to the device performance as well as the device stability. The stability of perovskite materials, to a great extent, is limited by the weak interaction between A site cation and B or X site ions [25]. Employing Cs $ ^{+} $  or Rb $ ^{+} $  with smaller ionic radius instead of partial MA cation (A site substitution) [26,27], or using smaller Br $ ^{-} $  or SCN $ ^{-} $  to replace larger I $ ^{-} $  (X site substitution) [28,29], both improved the PCE and device stability by enhancing the interaction of A site with X site. In addition, incorporating hydrophobic organic component such as PEA [30], BA [31], or PEI [32] into A site is able to increase the moisture resistance of perovskite film. Although the larger organic component may break the periodic structure of perovskite crystal and reduce the material dimension, causing a decrease in device efficiency. It is always a good way to improve the stability of perovskite solar cells without losing too much efficiency by regulating the additive amount. Besides, anchoring the micro-structure of the crystals is also a reliable strategy to promote the stability of the perovskite film and the devices [33]. Li et al. adopted butylphosphonic acid 4-ammonium chloride (4-ABPACl) as additive to link the neighboring grains and strengthen the microstructure of perovskite material, successfully enhancing the performance and stability of the regular perovskite solar cells based on mesoporous TiO $ _2 $  scaffold [17].

Inspired by Li et al.' report, in this work, we introduced bifunctional 1,6-diaminohexane dihydrochloride (1,6-DD) additive into the methylammonium iodide (MAI) precursor for the first time, using a two-step method to fabricate inverted planar perovskite solar cells. It was found that the 1,6-DD additive slowed down the crystal growth. By regulating the concentration of 1,6-DD, high-quality perovskite film with enlarged grain size and reduced defects was achieved. The two terminal  $ \mathrm{-NH_3^+} $  groups of 1,6-DD passivated the vacancy defects (empty A site) at the perovskite crystal surface and connected adjacent crystal surfaces through hexane alkyl chains. This connection enhanced the interaction between pervoskite grains, modifying and fixing the perovskite's microstructure to some degree. The incorporation of hydrophobic hexane alkyl chains could also increase the moisture resistance of perovskite film. In this paper, the best device from the perovskite with 1,6-DD additive presents a relatively high PCE of  $ 17\% $  and an excellent fill factor (FF) of  $ 80.1\% $ , as well as good stability in ambient. Therefore, our study provided an effective way to obtain perovskite solar cells of high efficiency and brilliant stability.

# 2. Experimental section

# 2.1. Materials and device fabrication

The information of all materials and the preparation of precursor solutions are provided in Supplementary material, also can be found in

our previous report [34,35]. ITO-coated glass with a sheet resistance of   $  10\ \Omega\ sq^{-1}  $   was ultrasonically cleaned by detergent, deionized water in sequence. PEDOT:PSS was spin-coated onto the ITO substrates at 3000 rpm followed by baking at   $  120\ ^{\circ}C  $   for 20 min in the air. The same process parameters of spin-coating are suitable for DMSO-doped PEDOT:PSS. The mixed lead precursor solution was preheated at   $  90\ ^{\circ}C  $   on a hot plate and spin-coated at 6000 rpm onto the ITO/PEDOT:PSS substrates. The ITO/PEDOT:PSS substrates were preheated and kept at   $  40\ ^{\circ}C  $   during the whole spin-coating process, which was realized by using the spin-coater with the function of substrate-preheating. And then the MAI precursor solution with different 1,6-DD content (0 wt%, 0.025 wt%, 0.05 wt%, 0.075 wt%, and 0.1 wt%) was dropping-coated, waiting for 30 s after the liquid spreading evenly, and spin-coated at 6000 rpm followed annealing at   $  90\ ^{\circ}C  $   for 40 min. Then PCBM:BCP blend solution was spin-coated on the perovskite film at 1000 rpm. Finally, Ag metal layer was deposited by thermal evaporation through a shadow mask which determined the cell area of   $  0.1\ cm^{2}  $  . Except for the perovskite preparation process was performed in an argon-filled glove box, almost all solution processes were performed in the air.

# 2.2. Characterization and measurement

X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku D/MAX 2500) with Cu Kα radiation was carried out to record the crystal structure of the perovskite film. Ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy (UV-vis) of perovskite film was observed with a Jasco-4000 spectrophotometer. The film surface morphology of the final perovskite films were investigated by field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi SU-8010), atomic force microscopy and conducting atomic force microscopy (AFM and C-AFM, Bruker INNOVA SPM). The film thickness was recorded by KLA-Tencor AlphaStep D-100 Stylus Profiler. Steady-state photoluminescence spectra (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence of spectra (TRPL) the film were recorded with a Jobin Yvon FluoroLog-3 fluorescence spectrometer to explore the defect passivation and nonradiative recombination. Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) measurements (Zahner-Zennium equipment) were performed in a frequency range from 1 Hz to 500 MHz to investigate the interfacial contact and charge transport. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) characterization were conducted by Thermo Scientific ESCALAB250Xi. Contact angle and surface energy measurements were recorded with Drop Shape Analyzer (Krüss DSA25S). The current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics of the devices were measured in an argon-filled glove box, with a programmed Keithley 2400 sourcemeter under illumination of a Newport Oriel 150 W solar simulator (AM 1.5G, 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ). The light intensity of the solar simulator was calibrated with a solar reference cell (SRC-1000-TC-QZ, VLSI standards, Inc.). All devices were kept in the air without encapsulation.

SEM images of the perovskite films with and without 1,6-DD additive are shown in Figs. 3 and S4 (Supplementary material). All films are comprised of dense grains and present full surface coverage, while the difference mainly lies in the grain size. The average grain size for

perovskite films with various 1,6-DD concentration of 0 wt%, 0.025 wt%, 0.05 wt%, 0.075 wt%, and 0.1 wt% are 190 nm, 297 nm, 432 nm, 336 nm, and 252 nm, respectively. The pristine perovskite film has the maximum crystal size of 398 nm. By contrast, the 1,6-DD additive facilitates the formation of grains bigger than 500 nm, and the largest grain size of the perovskite film containing 0.05 wt% 1,6-DD can achieve 687 nm. However, further increasing the concentration of 1,6-DD leads to too slow crystal growth, which prolongs the complete perovskite crystallization and results in a decrease in crystal size. The root-mean-square roughness (Rq) and peak-to-valley (Rz) of these perovskite films were also determined by AFM, shown in Fig. S5 (Supplementary material). The smoother films with enlarged grain size benefit from the enhanced crystallinity and the preferential crystal growth of (110) face. The ideal film morphology with larger grain size and lower roughness is beneficial to reduce the grain boundaries and defects, contributing to better charge transport and leading to increased Jsc and FF. Therefore, the enhanced photovoltaic performance of the devices with moderate 1,6-DD additive, to a large extent, benefits from the improved film morphology.

Impedance spectroscopy was carried out to further investigate the charge transfer and recombination in devices. The series resistance  $ (R_{\mathrm{sc}}) $  consist of internal resistance of electrodes, as well as charge transfer resistance  $ (R_{CT}) $  at the interface between the perovskite active layer and the charge transport layer. Herein, the difference between the devices based on perovskite films without and with 1,6-DD additive is the  $ R_{CT} $  at the interfaces. Figs. 4c and S6 (Supplementary material) show the Nyquist plots of these devices under dark condition. The high-frequency arc in left is attributed to the interfacial  $ R_{CT} $ . The low-frequency arc in right is assigned to the recombination resistance  $ (R_{rec}) $  at the interface. The  $ R_{CT} $  values of the devices without and with moderate 1,6-DD additive are  $ 338\Omega $  and  $ 279\Omega $ , respectively. The decreased  $ R_{CT} $  reflects a better interfacial contact and facilitates the charge transfer at the interface, greatly benefiting from the improved crystallinity and increased grain size. Moreover, the device with moderate 1,6-DD additive displays a higher  $ R_{rec} $   $ (865\Omega) $  than that without 1,6-DD  $ (153\Omega) $ , indicating the decreased charge recombination at the interface, which is mainly attributed to the reduced defects. Therefore, the moderate 1,6-DD additive endows the device with better interfacial charge transfer and lower charge recombination. The photovoltaic performance of the device is improved accordingly, exhibiting promoted Jsc and FF. However, it should be noted that the conductivity of 1,6-DD is very poor, because its molecule structure contains insulating organic hexane alkyl chains. As the 1,6-DD content rising, more insulating organic alkyl chains are introduced into the perovskite film, and the  $ R_{CT} $  presents an upward tendency (shown in Fig. S6 in Supplementary material). This directly increases the contact resistance at the interface, resulting in a decrease in Jsc, FF and PCE. The EIS results are consistent well with the device performance.

# 3.4. Investigation of device stability

Moreover, the stability of the devices with and without 1,6-DD additive was also investigated. Fig. 5 shows the comparison of degradation characteristics of PCE normalized to the initial values as a function of storage time. All devices without encapsulation were stored in ambient air condition at room temperature and relative humidity of 10–30%, and measured periodically. The PCE of the devices based on pristine perovskite film drops to 43% of its initial value after 16 days. Whereas the PCE of the device with 1,6-DD still retains more than 90%, exhibiting much better stability. The improvement of device stability can be ascribed to two aspects as follows.

# 4. Conclusions

In this work, 1,6-DD, a small organic molecule with two terminal  $ \mathrm{-NH_3^+} $  groups, was introduced into the precursor to fabricate inverted planar perovskite solar cells for the first time. By regulating the concentration of 1,6-DD, the average PCE was improved by  $ 20\% $  compared to the control devices. The champion device based on 1,6-DD additive exhibits a decent PCE of  $ 17.00\% $ , a Jsc of  $ 22.18\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $ , a Voc of  $ 0.96\mathrm{V} $  and an excellent FF of  $ 80.10\% $ . The device with larger active area  $ (1\mathrm{cm}^2) $  can also achieve a decent PCE of  $ 13.68\% $  and a high FF of  $ 78.99\% $ . The Introduction of 1,6-DD additive enhanced the crystallinity and increased the grain size, thus an ideal perovskite film with larger grains, reduced grain boundaries, and smoother surface was obtained. The terminal  $ \mathrm{-NH_3^+} $  groups of 1,6-DD passivated the crystal surface defects effectively, suppressing the nonradiative recombination as well as facilitating the charge transportation. Meanwhile, the hexane alkyl chains of 1,6-DD connected the adjacent crystal surfaces, which enhanced the interaction between different grains and fixed the microstructure of perovskite. The 1,6-DD based perovskite film had a slower decomposition rate. And the device with 1,6-DD additive still retains above  $ 90\% $  of the initial efficiency after exposure to the air for 16 days. Our study provided a promising strategy to improve the photovoltaic performance and stability of the inverted planar perovskite solar cells.
