# Abstract

This study reports on the results obtained after doping the   $  [CH_{3}NH_{3}]_{0.94}[C_{3}N_{2}H_{5}]_{0.06}PbI_{2.6}Cl_{0.4}  $   mixt halide perovskite with europium or antimony   $  (Eu^{3+}/Sb^{3+})  $   at the “B site”. This way two new complex compounds were obtained,   $  [CH_{3}NH_{3}]_{0.94}[C_{3}N_{2}H_{5}]_{0.06}Pb_{1-y}B_{y}I_{2.6}Cl_{0.4}  $   (B= Eu or Sb and y=0-0.05) as perovskite precursor solutions and deposited as thin films. The properties of the perovskite films were investigated by various characterization techniques: X-ray diffraction (XRD), atomic force microscopy (AFM), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), UV-Vis spectroscopy while the photovoltaic parameters were determined by measuring the IV curves of the corresponding solar cells. We find that doping the mixt halide perovskite with very small quantities of Sb improves the quality of the perovskite films and further improves the stability of perovskite solar cells.

Keywords: doping, europium, antimony, imidazolium, hybrid perovskite film, solar cell

# 1. Introduction

Over the past years, hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSC) have been the subject of many studies aiming to understand and improve their performance. The achieved progress was especially possible due to the composition tunability [1], the multitude of materials used as charge transporting layers [2], and the possibilities of optimizing the quality of all the constituent layers by adopting different methods of processing [3],[4]. Amongst the next generation of active materials for solar cells, the hybrid organic-inorganic perovskites with the general formula  $ \mathrm{ABX}_3 $  may find new application based on their versatility in composition and processing, and low effective production cost of devices in the optoelectronics field. Questions concerning their diverse structural chemistry and photovoltaic properties became a new field of research

even though materials with perovskite crystalline structure (ABX $ _3 $ ) are known for a long time (e.g. CaTiO $ _3 $ , BaTiO $ _3 $ , etc.).

Hybrid perovskites have been acknowledged as one of the most exciting and challenging semiconductors for solar energy harvesting. They demonstrated exceptional semiconducting and photoelectric properties, in both mesoporous [5],[6] and planar [7],[8] solar cell architectures. Record power conversion efficiency (PCE) value of  $ 24.2\% $  was achieved in small area PSCs [9]. To explore their properties and charge dynamics, some of the studies were focused on the fabrication of single crystal perovskites [10]. For this purpose, several fabrication routes have been reported such as slow crystallization [11], slow crystallization via cooling of supersaturated solutions [12], injection [13], crystallization by addition of an anti-solvent [11],[14] or, for specific solvents, rapid growth by temperature increase [15]. polycrystalline films used in devices such as solar cells or light emitting diodes [16]. It has been shown that the performance of the devices based on polycrystalline perovskite films can be optimized by changing the composition of all the A, B, or X ionic positions in the perovskite structure. Thus, different organic cations as methylammonium (MA), formamidinium (FA) and imidazolium (IM) can be mixed with inorganic cations (e.g. cesium (Cs) [17], and europium (Eu), tin (Sn), antimony (Sb)) and halogen anions (iodine (I), chlorine (Cl) and bromine (Br)), in certain suitable proportions [18],[19], to ensure the formation of the perovskite structure. Currently, an increasing number of articles identify the main culprit for the instability of perovskite, which is the organic cation, and especially MA [20]. One approach to improve the quality of the perovskite film, and implicitly the stability of the devices, was to intercalate larger organic cations to form 2D perovskite phases that limit the vibration of the organic cation within the crystalline lattice [21],[22].

Besides optimizing the charge selective layers and device geometry, the best solutions for moisture sensitivity and toxicity is the chemical adjustment of perovskite composition. For this purpose, simultaneous doping has been attempted at several positions within the  $ \mathrm{ABX}_3 $  structure [23] and it seems that the general tendency is to use compositions as diverse as possible [4], [16], [24].

In this paper, we focus on investigating the influence of doping with Eu or Sb at the B site of the   $  [CH_{3}NH_{3}]_{0.94}[C_{3}N_{2}H_{5}]_{0.06}PbI_{2.6}Cl_{0.4}  $   mixt halide perovskite films. Eu and Sb were used as dopants due to their comparable ionic radii, thus respecting the Goldschmidt rule [19], and the potential to improve the stability of the solar cells [25]. Moreover, Eu and Sb provide good conversion efficiencies as partial dopants of Pb in perovskite films [23],[26]. We chose an improved formulation for the organic cation in position A, by adding 6% imidazolium, based on the literature reports and some of our previous results, unpublished yet.

The resulted compositions are   $  MA_{0.94}IM_{0.06}Pb_{1-y}Eu_{y}I_{2.6}Cl_{0.4}  $   (y=0-0.05) and   $  MA_{0.94}IM_{0.06}Pb_{1-y}Sb_{y}I_{2.6}Cl_{0.4}  $   (y=0-0.05). The properties of these doped halide perovskites are investigated with respect to the “standard”   $  MA_{0.94}IM_{0.06}PbI_{2.6}Cl_{0.4}  $   composition, used as reference in the present study.

# 2. Experimental

The raw materials (precursors and solvents) used for preparation are: lead iodide (PbI $ _2 $ , Sigma Aldrich 99%), lead chloride (PbCl $ _2 $ , Sigma Aldrich 99.99%), europium nitrate hydrate (Eu(NO $ _3 $ ) $ _3 $ x9H $ _2 $ O, Alfa Aesar, 99.99%), antimony chloride (SbCl $ _3 $ , Sigma Aldrich 99.99%), methylammonium iodide (MAI, Dyesol), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Alfa Aesar 99%), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, Alfa Aesar 99.8%), titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetylacetonate) (Ti(iProp) $ _2 $ AcAc $ _2 $ , Sigma-Aldrich, 75 wt.% in isopropanol), TiO $ _2 $  commercial paste (Solaronix Ti-Nanoxide N/SP), isopropanol (IPA, Sigma Aldrich, 98.5% anhidrous), ethanol (Alfa Aesar, 96% anhidrous).

Imidazolium iodide   $  C_{3}N_{2}H_{5}I  $   (IMI) was synthesized in-house method starting from the commercial imidazole 99% (IM) powder from Alfa Aesar and hydroiodic acid (HI) as follows: into a 3-neck round-bottom flask, a mixture (1:1 molar ratio) of imidazole solution (40 wt.% in absolute ethanol) and hydroiodic acid (HI, Sigma Aldrich 47 wt.% in water) was left to react under stirring at 0 °C and under   $  N_{2}  $   atmosphere, until crystallization. Complete crystallization of   $  C_{3}N_{2}H_{5}I  $   was further achieved by raising the temperature up to room temperature (RT) under   $  N_{2}  $  . The yellowish-white precipitate was washed several times with diethyl ether (DEE, Alfa Aesar, 99.99%), and dried under vacuum at 60 °C overnight. The resulted powder, shown in figure 1.a) is IMI. This result is supported by the X-ray diffractogram depicted in figure 1.b), which shows that the prepared powder presents the specific diffraction lines for IMI at 16.91°, 22.47°, 25.5° in agreement to the diffraction peaks observed by other groups [27].

For the case of Sb doping, the film morphology suffers drastic modifications while increasing the quantity of the dopant (Figures 5e and 5f), in good agreement with the literature data [25]. However, for these samples, the structure is more compact and dense. Since the Sb precursor is dehydrated, the absence of crystallization water leads to a thicker layer, and the perovskite film presents voids only for the sample with Sb over  $ 5\% $ . In general, analyzing SEM (Figure 4) and AFM (Figure 5) images, in the presence of imidazolium and europium/antimony in very small quantities, the perovskite thin film develops in a more compact structure, which is the key for good performance and stable perovskite solar cells. Thus, even if the film looks rough it does not show pinholes or cracks at the surface. The size of the perovskite grains, evaluated from the SEM cross section characterization, varies from  $ \sim 190~\mathrm{nm} $  for the standard to  $ \sim 215~\mathrm{nm} $  for ImEu3 and  $ \sim 150\mathrm{nm} $  for ImSb3.

The presence of IM cation, which has a larger ionic radius than MA induces lattice expansion and the appearance of a new diffraction line at lower  $ 2\theta $  (11.1°) indicating the formation of a new phases,  $ \mathrm{ImPbI}_3 $  (IM) in competition with the perovskite containing MA cation [31]. Low doping concentrations of Eu and Sb do not lead to any shift of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  (110) peak and considering the cationic radius of  $ \mathrm{Sb}^{3+} $  of 76 pm [32] and of  $ \mathrm{Eu}^{3+} $ , 95 pm [33], compared with the 119 pm radius of  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  considerable lattice distortions are, however, expected. The crystallite size of the perovskite layers was determined by measuring the full width at half maximum of the (110)  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  peak and using the Scherrer equation. They revealed the crystallites between 71 to 111 nm with no relation to the concentration of the dopants. This is in agreement with other literature reports that claim the dopants don't have any significant effect on the crystallization of the perovskite [31]. The films also present a secondary phase of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  characterized by the  $ 12.26^{\circ} $  peak, although according to literature the film doped with  $ \mathrm{Eu}^{3+}-1\% $  [34] or  $ \mathrm{Eu}^{2+} $  [35] should display no secondary phase.

The   $  E_{g}  $   values do not change when the Eu is added within the halide perovskite, remaining around 1.63 eV, while Sb addition leads to a detectable blue shift, and the band gap increases slightly from 1.63 eV for standard to 1.645 for ImSb5. Sb exhibits a lower electronegativity (2.05) compared to Pb (2.33). Consequently, Sb–I bonds are stronger and thus reduce the Pb–I binding energy. According to the literature, the band gap of the ABX  $  _{3}  $   perovskite increases as the electronegativity of the B cation decreases. Therefore, the lower electronegativity of Sb  $  ^{3+}  $   may lead to a larger energy gap between the energy states of Sb in the conduction band maximum (CBM) and I in the valence band maximum (VBM) of MA  $  _{0.94}  $  IM  $  _{0.06}  $  Pb  $  _{1-y}  $  Sb  $  _{y}  $  I  $  _{2.6}  $  Cl  $  _{0.4}  $  . As a result, the stronger Sb-I induces an increase on the bandgap of Sb  $  ^{3+}  $   doped perovskite films [37],[38],[25].

As shown, the addition of Eu in the composition of the perovskite active layer seems to be detrimental to the performance of the solar cells. Increasing the Eu concentration from 0 to  $ 5\% $  leads to the decrease of the PCE from  $ 8\% $  to  $ 2\% $  along with the reduction by half of the Jsc. Voc is also negatively influenced by the Eu, to only  $ 0.75\mathrm{V} $  at  $ 5\% $  addition. The average series resistance increases to  $ 35\Omega \cdot \mathrm{cm}^2 $  for the  $ 5\% $  Eu from  $ 13\Omega \cdot \mathrm{cm}^2 $  in the standard cell, indicating a negative effect of the doping onto the semiconductor properties of the perovskite. An expected reduction of the parallel resistance due to the voids present in the active layer observed in the SEM images is confirmed, as well as a decrease of  $ 30\% $  (from 0 to  $ 5\% $  Eu). From figure 8a it is obvious that the cells with higher Eu concentration present a more accentuated hysteresis effect compared with the undoped cells pointing to a higher concentration of defects.

A similar effect has been also observed in the case of Sb doping in the active layer (see figure 8b). Still, it is interesting that the PCE reduction is not so drastic,  $ 3\% $  Sb leading to only a  $ 0.48\% $  decrease of the PCE (that is  $ 6\% $  of the standard cell efficiency) that is caused by a decline of the Voc by  $ 0.011\mathrm{V} $  and of the Jsc by  $ 1.3\mathrm{mA/cm}^2 $ . Comparing further these two cells, we also observe that they present similar series resistance (Rs) and FF, while the parallel resistance (Rsh) decreases by  $ 9\% $ . Considering the AFM and SEM images (see figures 3 and 4) the standard and  $ 3\% $  Sb doped present very similar microstructures. One possible reason for the slight decrease of performance for Sb doped-perovskite is related to the active layer, which in this case contains multiple defects [33],[39]. Increasing the Sb percent leads to decreased efficiency (Table 2) and according to literature, cells with  $ 100\% $  Sb (where  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} $  is totally replaced by  $ \mathrm{Sb^{3+}} $ ) do not exceed  $ 1\% $  PCE [33]. Higher concentration of Sb leads to voids in the perovskite film, similar to the Eu samples.

Stability measurements (Figure 9) after 60 days shelf storage show that in a low humidity environment (<10%), the standard cells retain 75% of initial efficiency. Comparatively, cells with Eu degrade faster, with efficiencies of only 57% to 24% from their initial PCE. However, Sb-containing samples show more interesting behavior. Cells with 3% Sb degrade slower than the standard ones, after 60 days keeping more than 80% of initial PCE. At 5% Sb doping the cells loose half of the initial efficiency. Voids in the perovskite films are one of the causes for this behavior, their presence facilitating water infiltration

in the structure and, as a result, an enhanced deterioration of the solar cells.

Still, considering that devices with  $ 1\% $  Eu doesn't present voids in the perovskite film, morphological defects cannot totally explain the degradation. Europium seems to destabilize the perovskite structure compared with Sb.

# 4. Conclusions

In summary, we report a facile strategy to improve the PSCs stability in air by doping the perovskite with imidazolium and Eu/Sb. The partial replacement of lead with europium does not lead to increased solar cell efficiency but in the case of  $ 3\% $  Sb doping, an increase in stability has been observed. The morphology and microstructure adopted by the europium-doped films lead to rapid cell degradation, which is not desirable in the photovoltaic field. However, partial substitution of Pb with non-toxic elements in small amounts, such as Sb, helps the formation of the perovskite films with good chemical stability with only a small cost in PCE reduction, adopting the triple mixed cation structure for better stability.
