# Enhancement of the Interfacial Connection via Carboxyl-Substituted Perylene as Electron-Transport Layer for Efficient and Stable Perovskite Solar Cells

Hang Zhao, Jia Xu,  $  ^{*}  $   Yi Yang, Zhenzhen Li, Bing Zhang, Xiaolong Liu, Songyuan Dai, and Jianxi Yao  $  ^{*}  $

Carboxyl-substituted perylene (PTCA) has been successfully applied as the electron-transport layer in perovskite solar cells. The large rigid  $ \pi-\pi $  conjugated plane structure in PTCA endows it excellent electronic transmission performance. By the carboxyl groups, PTCA can effectively connect the perovskite layer and FTO, thus reducing the interface barriers induced by weak contact, resulting in a high PCE of  $ 16.09\% $ . In addition, the PTCA-based devices exhibit remarkable stability under illumination in ambient conditions without encapsulation.

In recent years, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of perovskite solar cells (PVSCs) is already above 22%.  $  ^{[1]}  $   Typical sandwich PVSCs devices use perovskites as the active materials intercalated between the electron-transport layer (ETL) and hole-transport layer. It has been shown that the ETL plays an important role in extracting electrons from the active layer to avoid recombination with holes for high PCE PVSCs.  $  ^{[2]}  $   Currently, n-type metal oxides, such as ZnO and   $  TiO_{2}  $  , are used as the ETLs extensively. However, these n-type metallic oxides, especially   $  TiO_{2}  $  , require a high-temperature annealing process above 450 °C to obtain ideal crystallinity for high charge carrier mobility.  $  ^{[3]}  $   To replace the high-temperature ETLs, the organic fullerene derivatives, such as   $  C_{60}  $  ,   $  PC_{61}BM  $  , and   $  PC_{70}BM  $  , are used as effective ETLs in PVSCs. Unfortunately, fullerene derivatives often have poor photostability, are inefficient in preventing erosion of perovskite by water, and are expensive, and the wettability of the precursor solutions of perovskites is also poor.  $  ^{[4]}  $   In this regard, novel non-fullerene perylene-based organic materials are used to replace fullerene derivatives in PVSCs.

Herein, we prepared carboxyl-substituted perylene (PTCA, Figure S1b, Supporting Information) with matching energy level (lowest unoccupied molecular orbital: -4.37 eV, highest occupied molecular orbital: -6.02 eV)  $  ^{[9]}  $  and applied it as the ETL in PVSCs by a low-temperature solution-coating process. A typical n-i-p planar heterojunction PVSC device was constructed with the FTO/PTCA/MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  /spiro-OMeTAD/Au structure (Figure 1a). The carboxyl group was proved to strengthen the interactions among the interfaces of the perovskite film, ETL, and conductive substrate. The device with PTCA exhibited a PCE of 16.09%, among the best values for PDIs-based PVSCs. Moreover, the PTCA-based devices exhibit enhanced stability.

The AFM adhesion force measurement was carried out to quantitatively understand the adhesion force between PTCA and FTO, and between PTCA and perovskite. For comparison,  $  C_{60}  $ -coated probe was used as the contrastive sample and the bare probe was used as the control sample. From the statistical results (Figure 1c), it can be concluded that the adhesion force of PTCA-coated probe on FTO (122 nN) and perovskite (1052 nN) are larger than that of  $  C_{60}  $ -coated probe on FTO (86 nN) and perovskite (696 nN). The larger adhesion force of PTCA-coated probe toward FTO and perovskite was mainly attributed to the interaction of the carboxyl groups of PTCA with FTO and perovskite, while  $  C_{60}  $  does

has this interaction. Moreover, in order to study the enhancement of adhesion in terms of the mechanical stability of the PVSCs, we prepared flexible PVSCs by using a flexible polyethylene terephthalate (PET)/ITO to instead the glass/FTO substrates. PTCA and   $  C_{60}  $  , as two kinds of ETLs in the flexible PVSCs, were respectively deposited on the surface of ITO. The change of the surface morphology of the perovskite films deposited on PTCA and   $  C_{60}  $   after bending experiments was recorded using SEM. Parts of the perovskite film (marked by red circles in Figure S3f, Supporting Information) flaked from the substrate. However, there is little change in the perovskite film on the PET/PTCA under the same treatments (Figure S3g, Supporting Information). These results demonstrate that the total adhesion force among the layers of PET/PTCA/MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   is stronger than that of PET/C  $  _{60}  $  /MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  . The enhanced interfacial adhesion are favorable for strengthening the contact among the interfaces of the perovskite, ETL, and conductive substrate. Furthermore, the interfacial barriers induced by physical contact can be effectively reduced by the chemical interactions.

The PTCA-decorated substrates exhibit a decrease of light transmission in the range 400–600 nm with increasing thickness of the PTCA layer (Figure 1f). Nevertheless, the substrates with PTCA still maintain good visible light transmission. The contact angle of the precursor solution of perovskite on FTO/PTCA is   $  4^{\circ}  $  , which is much smaller than that of the precursor solution on FTO (  $  31^{\circ}  $  ) (Figure S4, Supporting Information). The favorable wettability of the precursor solution on the substrate enhances the homogeneity of the perovskite film. The morphology of the film deposited on bare FTO is very inhomogeneous and the grain size distribution is wide (Figure 1g). In contrast, the perovskite film deposited on the PTCA-based substrate exhibits a much more homogeneous surface, larger crystal grains, and a narrow size distribution (Figure 1h). The cross-sectional SEM images indicate that the thicknesses of the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   films deposited on FTO and the PTCA-decorated FTO substrate are both about 340 nm. However, there are some vacancies among the perovskite grains in the perovskite layer deposited on bare FTO (indicated by red circles in Figure 1g). These vacancies usually lead to a decrease in the PCE owing to electron interfacial barriers. The AFM results show that the surface roughness of the perovskite decreases from 28.9 to 20.9 nm with addition of PTCA (Figure S5, Supporting Information). And according to the results of X-ray diffraction (XRD) test (Figure S6, Supporting Information), the characteristic diffraction peaks of the perovskite based on PTCA exhibit much stronger diffracted intensity than that of perovskite coated on FTO. The preferential crystal growth orientation and a purer phase are beneficial to improve the efficiency of photo-induced carrier extraction from the perovskite layer to the ETL in PVSCs.

The time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) curves (Figure 2a) were fitted with a bi-exponential decay function to describe the process of charge extraction

where   $  \tau_{1}  $   is the rapid decay of the charge transfer from the perovskite to the adjacent layer through the interface and   $  \tau_{2}  $   is the slow recombination in the perovskite layer. According to the fitting data (Table S2, Supporting Information), the fast decay time of the film with PTCA (  $  \tau_{1}=4.41  $   ns) is shorter than that of the perovskite film deposited on bare FTO (  $  \tau_{1}=7.10  $   ns). The average value of   $  \tau(\tau_{\mathrm{avg}})  $   for the PTCA-based film (21.07 ns) is also shorter than that for the device based on bare FTO (37.01 ns). The shorter   $  \tau_{1}  $   and   $  \tau_{avg}  $   values indicate less carrier recombination and faster charge extraction at the interface between the perovskite and the ETL.  $  ^{[4]}  $   From the steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy (Figure S7, Supporting Information), the fluorescence intensity of the PTCA-based device is only half of the device without ETL, which is consistent with the results of TRPL above.

Stability tests were performed by storing the PTCA-based and FTO-based devices without encapsulation under ambient conditions (40–50% relative humidity,   $  \approx25^{\circ}C  $  ). The normalized PCE was plotted as functions of the storage times as shown in Figure 3a. The PCE of the FTO-based device was decreased to 13% after 10 days, while the PCE of PTCA-based devices can maintain 80.7% of the initial PCE after 30 days. Meanwhile, the degradation of films was monitored by XRD (Figure S12, Supporting Information). As shown in Figure 3b, the peak at   $  2\theta=12.71^{\circ}  $   represents the characteristic diffraction peak of (001) plane of   $  PbI_{2}  $   which was the production of the decomposition of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $  . The characteristic diffraction peak of   $  PbI_{2}  $   (  $  12.71^{\circ}  $  ) increased larger in FTO-based perovskite film over 9 days than that of PTCA-based film over 27 days. It is evident that the PVSCs devices exhibit improved stability by introducing PTCA. Degradation of perovskite exposed to   $  PbI_{2}  $   under ambient conditions is inhibited by the tight interconnection of PTCA with the perovskite layer.

To investigate the effect of PTCA on the mechanical stability of the flexible PVSCs, PET/ITO/PTCA/CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ PbI $ _3 $ /Spiro-OMeTAD/Au and PET/ITO/C $ _{60} $ /CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ PbI $ _3 $ /Spiro-OMeTAD/Au after bending experiments were recorded and compared. The PCE of PET/C $ _{60} $ -based device decreased to 52.5% of its initial PCE after 10 times of bending (Table S4, Supporting Information). Contrastively, the PCE of PET/PTCA-based device decreased from 9.61 to 8.37% under the same treatments. The PET/PTCA-based device still keeps 87% of its initial PCE (Table S4, Supporting Information). With increasing the time of bending, it can be found that the PET/C $ _{60} $ -based device has nearly lost its PCE after 50 times of bending (Figure 3c). However, PET/PTCA-based device can still maintain 70–80% of its initial PCE (Figure 3c). As shown in Figure 3d, the deterioration of the  $ V_{oc} $  during the bending experiments is the main culprit of the PCE decay. The separation of the functional layers at the interface could definitely induce the decrease of the  $ V_{oc} $ . Considering the same parts of the flexible PVSCs except the ETLs, such enhanced mechanical stability of the devices could only be attributed to the using of PTCA layers. So, it was proved that PTCA can improve the mechanical stability of the flexible PVSCs.

In summary, low-cost, easily available, low-temperature-processed PTCA has been used as the ETL in PVSCs. The carboxyl group of PTCA can both provide interfacial modification of planar heterojunction-structured PVSCs and enhance the adhesion force of device. The best-performing PVSCs with

PTCA exhibit a PCE of 16.09%. And the PTCA-based devices exhibit good long-term stability and mechanical stability.
