# Postpassivation of   $  Cs_{0.05}(FA_{0.83}MA_{0.17})_{0.95}Pb(I_{0.83}Br_{0.17})_{3}  $   Perovskite Films with Tris(pentafluorophenyl)borane

Jinbiao Jia, Jia Dong, $ ^{*} $  Beibei Shi, Jihuai Wu, Yangqing Wu, and Bingqiang Cao $ ^{*} $

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Passivating defects to suppress recombination is a valid tactic to improve the performance of third-generation perovskite-based solar cells.   $  Pb^{0}  $   is the primary defect in Pb-based perovskites. Here, tris(pentafluorophenyl)borane is inserted between the perovskite and spiro-OMeTAD layer in   $  SnO_{2}  $  -based planar perovskite solar cells. The incorporation of tris(pentafluorophenyl)borane can effectively passivate   $  Pb^{0}  $   defects, decreasing recombination at the surface of the perovskite film. Additionally, the modification with tris(pentafluorophenyl)borane decreases the grain boundaries quantity in the perovskite film, enhancing the transportation capability of carriers. The resulting perovskite solar cell gets a high efficiency of 21.42%. While the reference device without tris(pentafluorophenyl)-borane treatment acquires an efficiency of 19.07%. More importantly, the stability tests manifest that incorporating tris(pentafluorophenyl)borane in perovskite solar cells is conducive to the stability of the device.

KEYWORDS: perovskite solar cell, tris(pentafluorophenyl)borane, interface modification, defects passivation, stability

# 1. INTRODUCTION

Halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been in the spotlight in the photovoltaic field, profiting from their outstanding optoelectronic properties, low-cost fabrication, and simple processing technique.  $  ^{1-4}  $   In the last decade, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs has rapidly enhanced to 25.2 from 3.8%.  $  ^{1,5}  $   Halide perovskite, as a light-absorption layer, is vital for highly efficient PSCs (efficiency   $  >20\%  $  ). However, inherent crystal defects are generated inside a polycrystalline perovskite film during perovskite film fabrication procedure and device operation process.  $  ^{6,7}  $   Although most defects give rise to shallow electronic states in bandgap edges, PSC devices are still confronted with the small number of deep trap states that are in charge of the nonradiative recombination centers in perovskite layers, damaging the optoelectronic properties of the device.  $  ^{8-11}  $   Simultaneously, many unstable factors (moisture, oxygen, and ultraviolet light) also originate from these defects, which greatly attenuates the stability of the photovoltaic device.  $  ^{12}  $   Therefore, the key to efficient and stable PSCs is to reduce or passivate those defects.

Relevant studies reveal that uncoordinated ions, metallic Pb (Pb $  ^{0}  $ ) and iodide (I $  ^{0}  $ ), are the central deep-level trap states lying in perovskite films. $ ^{13,14} $  Also, the report exhibits that Pb $  ^{0}  $  is the primary defect that deteriorates device performance and stability. $ ^{15} $  Approaches, including composition engineering, $ ^{16} $  film optimization engineering, $ ^{17} $  additive engineering, $ ^{7,15,18,19} $  and interfacial modification engineering, $ ^{20} $  have been presented to reduce Pb $  ^{0}  $  defects. Zhong et al. employed organic phosphine redox pair as a Pb $  ^{0}  $  and I $  ^{0}  $  defects stabilizer. The relevant PSC

# 2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

2.1. Materials. Indium tin oxide (ITO)   $  (14\ \Omega\cdot sq^{-1})  $   was obtained from Pilkington. Formamidinum iodide (FAI,   $  \mathrm{HC(NH_{2})_{2}I}  $  ), lead(II) halide, cesium iodide (CsI), and TPFPB were purchased from TCI. Organic solvent, spiro-OMeTAD, and Li-TFSI came from Sigma-Aldrich.

2.2. Solar Cell Fabrication. The controlled PSCs were fabricated with the following structure: ITO/SnO $ _{2} $ /Cs $ _{0.05} $ (FA $ _{0.83} $ MA $ _{0.17} $ ) $ _{0.95} $ Pb-(I $ _{0.83} $ Br $ _{0.17} $ ) $ _{3} $ /Spiro-OMeTAD/Au. The detailed production process can refer to our previous work. $ ^{24} $  For the TPFPB-passivation device, the TPFPB solution in 2-propanol (5 mg·mL $ ^{-1} $ ) was spin-coated on top of the annealed perovskite film (4000 rpm for 20 s) and then heated at 100 °C for 10 min. The subsequent preparation procedure is consistent with that of the controlled PSC device.

2.3. Characterizations and Measurements. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was carried out on a SmartLab. The NICOLET IS 50 was used to perform the Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) characterization was conducted on AXIS SUPRA  $  ^{+}  $  . Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Carl

Zeiss Ultra 55) was used to observe the morphologies. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) and Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) were performed with a Park NX20. The optical properties of perovskite films were analyzed by Lambda1050 of PE and FLS1000 of Edinburg. The PCE of devices (J-V curves) were evaluated with Newport-94043A under the illumination of AM 1.5G. In this work, the voltage scope was 2.0 to -0.1 V for reverse scan and -0.1 to 2.0 V for forward scan. In each scan range, we collected 100 data points. The presweep delay time was 40 ms, yet the dwell time for each data point was controlled at 30 ms. Ultraviolet photoemission spectroscopy (UPS), external quantum efficiency (EQE), and transient photovoltage/photocurrent (TPV/TPC) decay were obtained by the same method as our previous work.  $  ^{24}  $   It should be stated that the devices needed for PCE, EQE, and TPV/TPC measurements are not packaged, and the effective area of the PSC involved in this work was controlled to 0.1 cm  $  ^{2}  $  .

Generally, when defects appear on the surface of perovskite, the carriers will be first used to fill the traps until the traps are filled. Subsequently, the current increases rapidly. At this point, the voltage is the trap-filled limit voltage (  $  V_{TFL}  $  ). As

shown, after TPFPB treatment, the   $  V_{TFL}  $   values decrease to 0.607 from 0.902 V. According to the literature,  $  ^{42}  $   the   $  N_{trap}  $   can be determined by the equation:   $  V_{TFL} = eN_{trap}L^{2}/2\varepsilon\varepsilon_{0}  $  , where e, L,   $  \varepsilon  $  , and   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   are constant. So, the   $  N_{trap}  $   is proportional to   $  V_{TFL}  $  . Obviously, the   $  N_{trap}  $   value of the device with TPFPB treatment is reduced, indicating that TPFPB treatment can significantly passivate the defects. In application, the reduced defect density in perovskite film is not only conducive to high-performance PSC devices but also beneficial to enhance the long-term stability of photoelectronic devices.

To better figure out the charge recombination kinetics in PSCs, Figure 6a,b shows the trend of  $  J_{\mathrm{SC}}  $  and  $  V_{\mathrm{OC}}  $  under different light intensities, respectively. According to the diode characteristic of a solar cell,  $  V_{\mathrm{OC}}  $  can be determined by the equation:  $  V_{\mathrm{OC}} = \frac{\varepsilon kT}{q}\ln (I) + n_0  $ , where  $  \varepsilon, k, T, q,  $  and  $  I  $ , respectively, represent the ideal factor, Boltzmann constant, absolute temperature, elementary charge, and light intensity. $ ^{42} $  In general, the lower value of  $  \varepsilon  $ , ranging from 1 to 2, signifies lower carrier recombination. $ ^{19} $  Compared with the  $  \varepsilon  $  value of the PSC based on pristine perovskite (1.84), the  $  \varepsilon  $  value of TPFPB-treated PSC is 1.24, implying that the introduction of TPFPB can efficaciously suppress charge recombination. Additionally, the charge recombination in perovskite can be understood by the following equation:  $  J_{\mathrm{SC}} \propto I^{\alpha} (\alpha \leq 1)  $ , where  $  I  $  represents light intensity, and  $  \alpha  $  is used to evaluate charge recombination. $ ^{43} $

Hysteresis index (HI) is calculated in terms of the following equation: HI =   $  \frac{J_{\mathrm{R}}(0.8V_{\mathrm{OC}})-J_{\mathrm{F}}(0.8V_{\mathrm{OC}})}{J_{\mathrm{R}}(0.8V_{\mathrm{OC}})}  $  , where   $  J_{R}  $   (  $  0.8V_{oc}  $  ) and   $  J_{F}  $   (  $  0.8V_{oc}  $  ) are the current values when the voltage is 80 percent of   $  V_{OC}  $   in the case of reverse and forward sweep, respectively.  $  ^{52}  $   The TPFPB-passivated device shows a smaller HI value of 0.011, while the HI value of the controlled PSC is 0.045. The results indicate that the TPFPB modification decreases the trap density and ion migration. The capacitance–frequency (C-F) is an effective means of characterization to measure the capacitance at the contact interface of perovskite and HTL. Figure 8c indicates that

the capacitance obviously reduces after the introduction of TPFPB, which is credited with the reduction of ionic migration.  $  ^{39}  $   To obtain the stability of the PSCs, we observed the change trend of efficiency in 20 days, keeping humidity less than 20% during the test. The trends of different devices are given in Figure 8d. The normalized PCE of the pristine PSC remains 47.08% after 20 days. While the trend of the device modified with TPFPB is 81.46%, indicating that incorporating TPFPB is advantageous to the improvement of the device.

# 4. CONCLUSIONS

To better figure out the charge recombination kinetics in PSCs, Figure 6a,b shows the trend of  $  J_{\mathrm{SC}}  $  and  $  V_{\mathrm{OC}}  $  under different light intensities, respectively. According to the diode characteristic of a solar cell,  $  V_{\mathrm{OC}}  $  can be determined by the equation:  $  V_{\mathrm{OC}} = \frac{\varepsilon kT}{q}\ln (I) + n_0  $ , where  $  \varepsilon, k, T, q,  $  and  $  I  $ , respectively, represent the ideal factor, Boltzmann constant, absolute temperature, elementary charge, and light intensity. $ ^{42} $  In general, the lower value of  $  \varepsilon  $ , ranging from 1 to 2, signifies lower carrier recombination. $ ^{19} $  Compared with the  $  \varepsilon  $  value of the PSC based on pristine perovskite (1.84), the  $  \varepsilon  $  value of TPFPB-treated PSC is 1.24, implying that the introduction of TPFPB can efficaciously suppress charge recombination. Additionally, the charge recombination in perovskite can be understood by the following equation:  $  J_{\mathrm{SC}} \propto I^{\alpha} (\alpha \leq 1)  $ , where  $  I  $  represents light intensity, and  $  \alpha  $  is used to evaluate charge recombination. $ ^{43} $
