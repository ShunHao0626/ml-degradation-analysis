Chien-Hung Chiang  $  ^{[b]}  $   and Chun-Guey Wu  $  ^{*[a, b]}  $

The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of the perovskite solar cell is high enough to be commercially viable. The next important issue is the stability of the device. # Introduction

Perovskite solar cells are one of the emerging photovoltaic technologies with an unprecedented high growth rate in the photovoltaics history. High power conversion efficiency (PCE) combined with simple solution processes and cheap starting materials make this technology particularly promising. Nevertheless, to be commercially viable, long-term device stability should be seriously concerned. One can say that without the stability, perovskite solar cells cannot be transferred from the laboratory to industry applications. It is understandable that to properly address the stability issues, the cell must be viewed as a whole system, including the perovskite absorber layer, the charge-transport layers, all buffer layers, the electrodes, and the interfaces between them. There are few review articles  $  ^{[1-4]}  $  that summarize the possible degradation mechanisms occurring in perovskite solar cells and also suggest possible solutions. Undoubtedly, the instability of the perovskite film toward moisture (  $  H_{2}O  $  ) is a main issue  $  ^{[1,5]}  $  for the long-term stability of the solar cell. Furthermore, Pisoni et al.  $  ^{[6]}  $  showed that methylammonium lead triiodide (MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ) perovskite had very

Additionally, Yang  $  ^{[3]}  $  et al. found that Al and Ag, commonly used electrode materials, can react with the perovskite film under humid conditions. In the literature, long-term stability studies are mainly focused on the regular perovskite solar cell architecture, which is generally constructed on a planar metal oxide or mesoscopic metal oxide as the electron-transport layer.  $  ^{[7-10]}  $   The time-dependent degradation of the inverted cell, which has the advantage of a simple architecture and more facile fabrication,  $  ^{[11]}  $   is less studied, although some speculations have been proposed. For example, phenyl-  $  C_{61}  $  -butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM), or other fullerenes, with very high electron affinity are common electron-transport materials for inverted cells, and would be more prone to react with the metal electrode or be damaged by   $  O_{2}/H_{2}O  $  .  $  ^{[12]}  $   Furthermore, the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite film may be destroyed by the hygroscopic acidic poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) polystyrene sulfonate (PEDOT:PSS), which is a common hole-transporting material for the inverted cell.  $  ^{[13]}  $   Although some issues regarding the stability have been mentioned, the intrinsic stability of the perovskite absorber are not yet well-understood. In this article, we investigate the perovskite grain-size related long-term stability of the inverted solar cells.

# Results and discussion

# Controlling the grain size of the perovskite film by post solvent annealing

Solvent-assisted crystal growth has been demonstrated in various crystal systems as a means to dissolve/reconstruct grain boundaries.  $  ^{[14-16]}  $   It was also applied successfully to grow large multi-crystalline grains in perovskite films, and the grain growth mechanism was also proposed.  $  ^{[17-19]}  $   Post solvent annealing (SA) can effectively increase the grain size and reduce pin holes of the perovskite film by grain boundary movement or by increasing the diffusion length of both the MAX and   $  PbX_{2}  $   (X=halogen) components. In this paper, we used the post SA method to control the grain size of the   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   film. Post SA of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   film with dimethylformamide (DMF) was performed with the set-up shown in Figure 1. A perovskite film

Figure 1. Set-up for performing post solvent annealing (SA).

was put in a big Petri dish then covered with a small Petri dish. A few drops of DMF were dripped in the surrounding of the small Petri dish. The whole set up was heated to  $ 60^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  using a hot plate. During heating, DMF molecules vaporized and diffused into the small Petri dish to mend the grain boundary of the film. Full coverage and flat perovskite film is necessary to ensure the success of the SA (i.e., to grow big perovskite grains). The heating (SA) time was then used to manipulate the grain size of the film.

SEM images displayed in Figure 2 reveal that the grain size of perovskite films can be tuned by the SA time. In the first  $ 10\mathrm{min} $ , the grain size of the film increased as the annealing time increased. Nevertheless, when the SA time increased to  $ 20\mathrm{min} $  (Film-20), the size of the grains did not increase further. Instead, lots of pin holes with a few big holes appeared. Certainly the size of the perovskite grains depends not only on the amount and types of solvent used for SA but also on the annealing temperature and heating time. In this paper, we focus on the grain-size (of the perovskite film) related long-term stability of the inverted solar cells based on  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  films. Therefore, SA is only a means to prepare the perovskite film with various grain sizes. # Long-term stability tests

The long-term stability of the cells was tested in two extreme environments: 1) in a glove box having  $ \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} $  content less than  $ 0.1~\mathrm{ppm} $  and  $ \mathrm{O}_2 $  content less than  $ 10~\mathrm{ppm} $ , and 2) under ambient atmosphere with a relative humidity of  $ 30 - 40\% $ . The stability data of D-0 and D-10 under the two environments are illustrated in Figure 6. Cells based on the film with larger grains (D-10) are stable both in the glove box and under ambient atmosphere, whereas devices fabricated using the film containing small grains (D-0) degrade very fast in both environments. It is clear that the grain size of the perovskite film affects substantially the stability of the cells. Therefore, when the long-term

stability of inverted perovskite solar cells is discussed, the morphology (e.g., grain size) of the perovskite film should be mentioned. The long-term stability data also clearly shows that   $  H_{2}O  $   sensitivity of the active film is not the only reason for cell degradation. Even without   $  H_{2}O  $   present (glove box storage, 1 month), the PCE of the cell (D-0) based on small-grained Film-0 decreases by 70% of its original value, whereas when the grain size of the film is large enough (Film-10), the perovskite solar cell (packed with common UV-curing sealant) can be very stable under ambient atmosphere with 30–40% relative humidity. Perovskite films containing small grains have large surface area/more boundaries, and can therefore more reactive, resulting in poor stability of the corresponding cell.

The grain-size related chemical reactivity of the perovskite film was revealed most clearly in the reaction toward  $ \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} $ . Upon standing under ambient atmosphere, D-0 lost device function within 15 days, but the efficiency of D-10 dropped by less than  $ 15\% $  after 1 month. Furthermore, the color of D-10 did not exhibit observable change, whereas the color of the D-0 turns from brown/black to yellow, indicating decomposition of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  to  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and MAI owing to  $ \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} $  contact (see the insert of Figure 6), although both cells were packed similarly with UV-curing sealant. A  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  diffraction peak observed in the GIXRD pattern of the degraded D-0 cell confirmed the decomposition of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  to  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and other chemicals. On the other hand, no  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  diffraction peak was found in the aged D-10 cell. When the films were stored in a glove box for 30 days, the color of Film-10 did not change, but some small yellow spots appeared on Film-0 and the luster of the film also slightly decreased.

The reasons causing the degradation of a perovskite solar cell could be very complicated. The extraction of MAI from   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   by   $  H_{2}O  $   was shown previously.  $  ^{[4,13]}  $   Several authors  $  ^{[26-28]}  $   found that corrosion of the Ag electrode owing to contact with the perovskite through pin holes in the electron- or hole-transporting layers is also a degradation pathway. However, corrosion of the Ag electrode in D-0 and D-10 was not observed in this experiment, probably because the electron-transporting layers in this study were thermally evaporated   $  C_{60}  $   and 2,9-dimethyl-4,7-diphenyl-1,10-phenanthroline (BCP) films, which are much more dense than solution-processed PCBM or Spiro-OMeTAD films.

To understand more about the origin of the grain-size related degradation of the photovoltaic performance, excluding the effects of   $  H_{2}O  $   and   $  O_{2}  $  , variation of the photovoltaic parameters with time for D-0, D-5, and D-10 cells when stored inside the glove box were recorded (Figure 7). The long-term stability data reveals that for all cells the FF decreases more significantly compared to   $  J_{SC}  $   and   $  V_{OC}  $  . The   $  J_{SC}  $   of D-10 shows almost no change upon aging and the change in   $  V_{OC}  $   and FF is also insignificant. On the other hand, the FF of D-0 decreases 40%, and the   $  J_{SC}  $   and   $  V_{OC}  $   each decrease by 30%, upon standing in a glove box for 30 days.

The long-term stability data clearly reveals that the grain size of the perovskite film determines not only the PCE, but also the stability of the cells. If the materials and architectures of the inverted perovskite solar cells are the same, the long-term stability in general is related to the efficiency of the cell. Therefore if a single-crystal perovskite film can be prepared by solution process, solar cells with low fabrication cost, high PCE, and high stability could be achieved.

# Experimental Section

Materials and physicochemical studies: Aqueous dispersion of PEDOT:PSS (1.3–1.7 wt%, H. C. Stark, Baytron P, Al 4083) was obtained from Heraeus Co. Fullerene   $  C_{60}  $   was purchased from Solenne B. V., The Netherlands. PbI  $  _{2}  $   (99.999%) and BCP were purchased from Aldrich Co. All materials were used as received unless otherwise specified. Indium-tin oxide (ITO)-covered glass substrates were purchased from Ruilong Optoelectronic Co., Taiwan and photolithographically patterned in our laboratory with HCI  $  _{(aq)}  $  . MAI was synthesized from CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{2(aq)}  $   and HI  $  _{(aq)}  $   in a method similar to that reported in the literature.  $  ^{[29]}  $   GlXRD data were collected in the range   $  2\theta=5-50^{\circ}  $   on a Bruker powder diffractometer (D8 Discover) using CuKα1 radiation equipped with a 2D detector. UV/Vis absorption spectra were recorded with a Hitachi F-7000 spectrometer. SEM images were taken with a Hitachi S-800 microscope at 15 KV. Samples for SEM study were mounted on a metal stub with a piece of conducting tape then coated with a thin layer of Au film to avoid charging.

Preparation of perovskite films with various grain sizes through post solvent annealing: The grain sizes of the perovskite films were controlled by treating the film with DMF vapor. A heat treated perovskite film was put in a large Petri dish and then covered with a small Petri dish. A small amount of DMF was dripped in the surrounding of the small Petri dish. The big Petri dish was heated at 60°C for various times to control the grain size of the film using a hot plate (see Figure 1).

Device fabrication and photovoltaic performance measurements: PEDOT:PSS was spin-coated on a pretreated patterned ITO substrate at 5000 rpm for 50 s and then annealed at 120°C for 10 min. The MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  film was prepared with anti-solvent washing similar to the literature report.  $  ^{[30]}  $  Equal molar ratio of PbI  $  _{2}  $  and MAI were mixed and dissolved in DMF/DMSO (4:6 v/v) then stirred overnight in a glove box to make the precursor solutions with various concentrations. After passing the solution through a 0.22 μm filter, the perovskite film was formed on PEDOT:PSS-coated ITO substrates by spin-coating. Specifically, in this study, the highest PCE device was based on the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  film fabricated with the concentration of PbI  $  _{2}  $  equal to 1.4 M using the spin program of 1000 rpm for 10 s, then 4500 rpm for 20 s; at the last second, a 50 μL toluene was dripped on the film. The perovskite film was heated at 100°C for 10 min before post SA treatment. After post SA, the PEDOT:PSS/perovskite film was transferred to a vacuum chamber for coating with C  $  _{60}  $ /BCP electron-transporting/blocking layers and Ag electrode (50 nm for C  $  _{60}  $ , 5 nm for BCP, and 100 nm for Ag electrode) to obtain the inverted perovskite solar cell. All the fabrication procedures, except the deposition of PEDOT:PSS, were carried out in a N  $  _{2}  $ -filled glove box. The active area of the cell is 0.5×0.2 cm. The solar cell was covered with a defined mask and the I-V characteristics of the cells were measured using a Keithley 4200 source measuring unit under a simulated AM1.5G light (Wacom solar simulator) at 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $ . External quantum effi

ciency (EQE) or IPCE was measured in air after sealing the device with a silica sealant and measuring immediately. A chopper and lock-in amplifier were used for the phase sensitive detection with QE-R3011 measurement system (Enlitech Inc., Taiwan). The determination of the photovoltaic parameters and the calibration of all measuring facilities are the same as what we reported previously.  $  ^{[31]}  $

Long-term stability testing: The stability test of the cells was performed under two different conditions: 1) stored in a glove box (with   $  H_{2}O<0.1  $   ppm,   $  O_{2}<10  $   ppm), and 2) under ambient atmosphere with the relative humidity of 30–40% with the room light on all the time. The cells for the testing under ambient atmosphere were packed with a UV-curing sealant. The photovoltaic parameters of the cells were measured every 5 days. In between the photovoltaic performance measurements the cells were sitting in the device fabrication laboratory without special protection.

Film Grain-Size Related Log-Term Stability of Inverted Perovskite Solar Cells

Removing boundaries! Inverted perovskite solar cells are fabricated from perovskite films with varied grain size. The films with larger grain size show improved stability under both glove-box-controlled and ambient conditions compared with the films having smaller grain size, suggesting that, the chemical- and thermal-induced production of pin holes at grain boundaries could be the reason for long-term instability of inverted perovskite solar cells.
