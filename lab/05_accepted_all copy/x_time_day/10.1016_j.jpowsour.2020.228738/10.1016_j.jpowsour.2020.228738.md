# ABSTRACT

In recent years, organo-metal halide perovskite and p-i-n planar perovskite solar cells (PSCs) based on it have received tremendous attention due to their high efficiency close to silicon, low temperature process, and ease of cost-effective large-scale manufacturing. However, p-i-n planar PSCs have low fill factor and low current density due to poor interface properties, resulting in low performance. In this work, to solve these problems, we introduce boron nitride (BN) as an interface modifier and demonstrate that the BN incorporated   $  NiO_{x}  $   (NiO:BN) is effectively working as hole transport layer of p-i-n planar PSCs. The NiO:BN hole transport layer (HTL) has a deep energy level of highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and smooth surface compared to pristine   $  NiO_{x}  $ , which provide the better energy level alignment and better interface contact between NiO:BN HTL and perovskite, improving charge extraction and transportation and inhibiting recombination of charge. As a result, NiO:BN based p-i-n planar PSCs represent an improved power conversion efficiency of 20.74% and long-term stability in ambient air for 60 days (maintained over 84% of their initial PCE). Therefore, it can be suggested that the NiO:BN HTL is a promising alternative HTL for high performance p-i-n planar PSCs.

# 1. Introduction

Organo-metal halide perovskite (OMHP) has emerged as an attractive semiconductor material in recent years. In particular, by enabling the production of a light and flexible light-absorption thin film having excellent photoelectric properties through the low-temperature solution process   $  [1-3]  $  , it is accelerating the feasibility of the next-generation solar cells beyond silicon. The efficiency of the perovskite solar cells (PSCs) based on OMHP light-absorption layer has been improved rapidly, it was achieved an efficiency of 25.2% close to the efficiency of the silicon solar cells   $  [4,5]  $  .

The PSC is principally divided into two classes, mesoporous and planar PSCs, depending on their structure   $  [6,7]  $  . More specifically, planar PSC are distinguished by n-i-p and p-i-n structure according to the order of the interface layer, and recently, due to the scalability and stability issues, the research on PSCs with p-i-n structures have been conducted more actively   $  [8–10]  $  . The mesoporous PSC, originated from solid state dye sensitized solar cells, conventionally consisted of mesoporous titanium oxide   $  (m-\mathrm{TiO}_{2})  $   with high-temperature (  $  >450\ ^{\circ}C  $  ) and long-time duration (  $  >2\ h  $  ) sintering, which does not meet the needs of the large-area manufacturing and hinders its market competitiveness   $  [7,11,12]  $  . On the other hand, the planar PSCs have a similar structure with organic bulk heterojunction solar cells composed of multi-layer thin film, including the carrier transport material, which provides the possibility of manufacturing a high-performance large-area PSC with a low temperature process   $  [13–16]  $  . Furthermore, interestingly, p-i-n planar PSCs are reported to have little hysteresis, even if perovskite composition is the same as that used in n-i-p planar PSCs   $  [17]  $  . This suggests that the p-i-n structure is suitable for the realization of high-performance PSCs for commercialization, and that the inherent properties of OMHPs as well as the interfaces between electrode and perovskite layer need to be considered.

Various organic and inorganic p-type semiconductors have been employed as the hole transport materials (HTMs) for p-i-n planar PSCs. PEDOT:PSS, PloyTPD, and PTAA are among the most commonly used organic HTM in p-i-n planar PSCs with ease of process and high efficiency [18–23]. However, the poor stability and high cost of these organic HTM make them not promising in real applications [24]. Such polymer-based organic HTMs tend to agglomeration at an ordinary temperature under continuous light soaking, which can impair device stability, and in general these conjugating polymers are costly due to complex synthesis methods and low yield [25]. Therefore, diverse solution-processable inorganic HTMs have been also researched for p-i-n planar PSCs, such as   $  MoO_{x}  $  ,   $  WO_{x}  $  , CuSCN, rGO, and   $  NiO_{x}  $   [26–31]. In particular, extensive research has focused on   $  NiO_{x}  $   based PSCs. It has been demonstrated that   $  NiO_{x}  $   is one of the most promising hole transport layers (HTLs) for p-i-n planar PSCs.   $  NiO_{x}  $   is known to have a wide band gaps of 3.6–4.0 eV with work function (WF) of up to 5.4 eV, which allow it to use as a p-type interface layer for hole transport of p-i-n planar PSCs [7,32]. However, the photovoltaic performance of PSCs based on solution-processed   $  NiO_{x}  $   as HTL is still not satisfactory because of their low short-circuit current density and fill factor, which is probably associated with the low conductivity of   $  NiO_{x}  $   film, the poor contact with perovskite, and mismatched band alignment between   $  NiO_{x}  $   and perovskite [7,33]. In addition, it is reported that the stability of the device depends largely on the deposition method of   $  NiO_{x}  $   film. For instance, the PSC based on combustion   $  NiO_{x}  $   showed enhanced air-stability compared to devices based on sol-gel   $  NiO_{x}  $   due to improved perovskite/HTL interfaces and improved perovskite film quality [34]. In case of   $  NiO_{x}  $   nanoparticles, chemically pre-synthesized   $  NiO_{x}  $   nanoparticles have excellent crystallinity, but it has been reported that some chemisorption of charged species (  $  H^{+}  $  ,   $  OH^{-}  $  , or other ions) on the surface of the   $  NiO_{x}  $   nanoparticles can lead to degradation of device stability [35].

Several approaches have been attempted to address these problems of  $ \mathrm{NiO_x} $  films. Doping is an effective way to modify the optical and electrical properties of semiconductors, various dopants have been

applied to improve the optoelectrical properties such as transparency, band structure, work function, and conductivity of   $  NiO_{x}  $   [7]. Jen et al. deposited   $  Cu:NiO_{x}  $   films by using spin-coating method and demonstrated that the conductivity was increased by two orders of magnitude, resulting in improved efficiency from 8.94% to 15.40% [36]. After that, the same group employed   $  Cu:NiO_{x}  $   nanoparticles to p-i-n mesoscopic PSCs and reported a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 18.1% with significantly improved stability and negligible hysteresis. Chen et al. reported that   $  Cs  $  -doped   $  NiO_{x}  $   improves conductivity and work function of HTL, resulting in boosted hole extraction and the PCE from 16.04% to 19.35% [37]. Besides, higher conductivity and work function were also observed in the solution-derived   $  NiO_{x}  $   films doped with 1 mol% Fe, which promoted the better charge collection and energy level alignment and enhanced the PCE from 15.41 to 17.57% [38]. Doping, however, can causes significant crystal lattice disorder and defects in the   $  NiO_{x}  $   lattice, which can lead to reduced carrier mobility and parasitic recombination [39,40]. Furthermore, doping clearly complicates manufacturing procedures, shortens the process window, and increases costs; doping is difficult if the size of the dopant atom is too large or smaller than the size of the Ni atom [25,39]. Surface modification is another efficient approach to change the surface characteristics of   $  NiO_{x}  $   thin film, such as work function, wettability and so on [41]. Yang et al. introduced diethanolamine (DEA), self-assembled monolayers (SAMs), as the surface modifier between   $  NiO_{x}  $   film and perovskite and demonstrated that the DEA-modified   $  NiO_{x}  $   produced better perovskite film with improved crystallinity and less pinholes [42]. They also revealed that the formation of a favorable molecular dipole layer improves the hole extraction rate and charge transport rate of the device, significant increasing from 11% to 15% of PCE. Similarly, the 4-bromobenzoic acid, ferrocenedicarboxylic acid, and poly(trial amine) (PTAA) have been also reported to provide both better perovskite films and more efficient hole extraction and transportation, which is due to a reduction in trap-assisted recombination and energy level offset between the   $  NiO_{x}  $   and perovskite [43–45]. More recently, interestingly, Menglin Li et al. reported that thermally reduced graphene oxide (rGO) incorporated   $  NiO_{x}  $   layer (  $  NiO_{x}:rGO  $  ) can effectively improve the film conductivity without sacrificing its high work function, which lead to improved charge extraction by a reduction of interfacial recombination loss and improvement of carrier lifetime in   $  NiO_{x}:rGO  $   based PSCs [25]. The   $  NiO_{x}:rGO  $   based PSC showed a high PCE of 19.1% and FF of 81% and negligible performance degradation over 70 days. This result shows that a transporting layer incorporating rGO having two-dimensional (2D) structure would be a promising alternative to further develop highly efficient and stable PSCs. Further, this result suggests that the HTM incorporating 2D materials would be a promising alternative, and further suggests that by integrating the two HTMs, synergetic effect can be achieved for hole transport capability such as band structure, work function, and conductivity [25,28].

From this perspective, herein, we introduced boron nitride (BN) as an interface modifier to modulate the optoelectrical properties of   $  NiO_{x}  $   HTL more favorably and demonstrated that the BN incorporated   $  NiO_{x}  $   (abbreviated NiO:BN) is effectively working as a novel hole transport layer of p-i-n planar PSCs. The BN has similar structure like graphene and it is also well known that BN has excellent physical and chemical properties including wide band gap (6 eV), chemically stable structure, hydrophobic nature, smooth surface coverage, and deep lying valence band more than 6 eV [46–48]. These features can provide an option to satisfy the key factors for improving efficiency and stability of p-i-n planar PSCs. The NiO:BN hole transport layer showed more higher work function and smoother surface than pristine   $  NiO_{x}  $  , which improved the energy level alignment and interface contact between NiO:BN HTL and perovskite, resulting in improved charge extraction and transportation and suppressed recombination. Optimized NiO:BN based p-i-n planar PSCs showed an improved power conversion efficiency of 20.74% and long-term stability in ambient air for 60 days (maintained over 84% of their initial PCE), which was significantly boosted from the pristine   $  NiO_{x}  $  .

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1. Materials

N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), isopropyl alcohol (IPA), 1,2-dichlorobenzene (DCB), chlorobenzene (CB), and cesium Iodide (CsI) were obtained from the Sigma-Aldrich and used as received without any further modifications. Lead (II) iodide (PbI  $  _{2}  $  ) and lead bromide (PbBr  $  _{2}  $  ) was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry (TCI). Formamidinuim iodide (FAI) and methylammonium bromide (MABr) were obtained from Great Cell Solar. Nickel oxide (NiO  $  _{x}  $  , 2.5 wt % in ethanol) nanoparticles and zinc oxide (ZnO, 2.5 wt% in 2-propanol) nanoparticles suspension were obtained from the Nano Clean Tech. Boron nitride (BN, average particle size: ~70 nm (determined from SSA)) and PC  $  _{61}  $  BM were purchased from Graphene Super market and Nano C, respectively.

# 2.2. Preparation of the NiO:BN dispersion solution

Firstly, to prepare a BN flake dispersion,   $  \sim0.5  $   mg of BN powder was added to 1 mL of IPA, and then sonicated for 3–4 h. BN powder was separated and exfoliated by a high frequency sonication and consequently converted to BN flake. The BN flake was confirmed by the TEM analysis and the size of BN flake ranged from 70 to 200 nm (Fig. S1a). Next, for the preparation of the NiO:BN dispersion solution, we mixed the BN flake dispersion into   $  NiO_{x}  $   nanoparticle dispersion as a percentage of various volumes (1, 2, 3, 4, and 5%, respectively), where the size of   $  NiO_{x}  $   nanoparticles in the dispersion was in the range of 6–10 nm (see Fig. S1b). Finally, the mixed dispersion solution was kept on stirring for 24 h. The prepared NiO:BN dispersion solution was directly used as an HTL for perovskite solar cells.

# 2.3. Solar cell fabrication

The patterned ITO substrates (15 × 15 mm) were firstly cleaned with Mucasol solution, deionized water, ethanol, acetone and iso-propanol under continuous ultra-sonication, followed by drying at room temperature and treated with UV-Ozone cleaning for 30 min. The   $  NiO_{x}  $   and NiO:BN layers were deposited on the cleaned ITO by spin coating the precursor inks at 4000 rpm for 30 s and then annealed at 300 °C for 30 min. The perovskite solution was prepared by dissolving the   $  PbI_{2}  $   (570 mg),   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (80 mg), FAI (19 mg), MABr (12 mg), and CsI (66 mg) in mixed solution of DMF and DMSO (volume ratio 2:1). The perovskite film was deposited by spin-coating the precursor solution onto the   $  NiO_{x}  $   and NiO:BN HTLs with a successive spinning condition of 500 rpm for 5 s and 4500 rpm for 45 s. During this process, a 300 μL of CB was dropped onto the perovskite thin film as an anti-solvent treatment. Then the films were annealed at 100 °C for 30 min. The electron transport layer, PCBM in anhydrous DCB (20 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was spin-coated onto perovskite films at 6000 rpm for 50 s. After this, all samples were annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. Then, ZnO nanoparticles were spin-coated at 1000 rpm for 15 s and 8000 rpm for 40 s and annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. Finally, Ag electrodes (100 nm) were independently deposited to be separated from each other by a thermal evaporator using a finger-shaped mask under a pressure of   $  10^{-6}  $  , where the finger-shaped mask was precisely machined so that the active area of the electrode projected vertically over the ITO was 0.0464 cm  $  ^{2}  $  .

# 2.4. Film and device characterizations

X-ray diffraction pattern was measured with an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Rigaku Ultima) with Cu Kα radiation (  $  \lambda = 0.154  $  nm). Work function and elemental analysis of NiO  $  _{x}  $  and NiO:BN films were studied using ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS, Fischer Scientific)

and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fischer Scientific). The absorption spectra of the   $  NiO_{x}  $   and NiO:BN films were conducted by using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer Lambda 750). The current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics of the devices were analyzed by using a Keithley 2400 source meter and Oriel Sol3A solar simulator with one sun condition (AM 1.5G, 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ). Before the evaluation, the light intensity was confirmed with a standard crystalline Si-cell. The stabilized photocurrent was analyzed at the bias voltage attained from the maximum power point in the typical J-V curve. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) and internal quantum efficiency (IQE) of the solar cell were carried out on a certified incident photon-to-current conversion efficiency (IPCE) measurement system (IQE-200, Oriel Instruments, USA). The topography and surface roughness of   $  NiO_{x}  $   and NiO:BN were characterized by using an atomic force microscope (AFM, XE7 Park Systems). The top view surface morphology of the perovskite films was measured by using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Carl Zeiss-Supra  $  ^{™}  $  -40VP). Room temperature photoluminescence (PL) analysis was achieved by using a spectrofluorometer (Shimadzu Lab solutions RF) with an excitation wavelength of 391 nm. The photo-CELIV, the transient photo voltage (TPV), and transient photocurrent (TPC) were measured with multifunctional organic semiconductor parameter test system (T4000, McScience).

# 3. Results and discussion

In order to change the electrical and chemical properties of the interface between   $  NiO_{x}  $   and perovskite, the NiO:BN layer was synthesized by mixing hexagonal-BN having excellent physical and chemical properties in the   $  NiO_{x}  $   nanoparticles matrix. Detailed synthesis methods are listed in the experimental section, the crystal structure of the BN was confirmed by X-ray diffraction analysis as shown in Fig. S2. First, in order to confirm how BN is incorporated in the NiOx matrix, TEM and EDS analyses were performed on the NiO:BN film. Fig. 1a shows that BN flakes were distributed in the   $  NiO_{x}  $   matrix. In detail, as shown in Fig. S1c, it was observed that the coarse BN flakes (in the range from 70 to 200 nm) was incorporated with small   $  NiO_{x}  $   nanoparticles (in the range from 6 to 10 nm). Further, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping images showed that the incorporated BN well distributed in NiO:BN layer. In the SEM image of Fig. S3 showing a wider range, it was also confirmed that the incorporated BN and   $  NiO_{x}  $   NPs had uniform distribution within the NiO:BN layer. It can be seen that both BN and   $  NiO_{x}  $   NPs can be well dispersed in ethanol and isopropanol mixed solvents and can be uniformly distributed in NiO:BN thin films. To determine the chemical state of the BN and   $  NiO_{x}  $   NPs present in NiO:BN layer, further, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was performed. Fig. 1b, c, d and Fig. S4 show XPS spectra corresponding to Ni, O, B, and N. Generally,   $  Ni2p_{3/2}  $   peak consists of three characteristic peaks: a main peak at   $  \sim854  $   eV, shoulder at   $  \sim856  $   eV, and shake-up satellite peak at   $  \sim862  $   eV [49,50]. For both pristine   $  NiO_{x}  $   and NiO:BN, the main peak typically assigned to   $  Ni^{2+}  $   was similarly confirmed at   $  \sim853.8  $   eV, which represents the characteristics of the standard Ni–O octahedral bonding configuration of the cubic   $  NiO_{x}  $   rock salt [51]. The shoulder (  $  \sim855.5  $   eV) indicating the Ni-vacancy induced   $  Ni^{3+}  $   ions and the shake-up satellite (  $  \sim861.1  $   eV) were also observed similarly [52,53]. In addition, the O1s spectra of pristine   $  NiO_{x}  $   and NiO:BN did not show a difference; the first peak located in   $  \sim528.1  $   eV relates to O–Ni bond of   $  NiO_{x}  $   phase, and the strongest peak located in   $  \sim530.8  $   eV is recognized in the   $  Ni_{2}O_{3}  $   bond [51,54]. This result shows that the chemical state of   $  NiO_{x}  $   NPs is not changed by the incorporated BN, which has also been identified through the chemical state of the unchanged BN. As shown in Fig. 1d and Fig. S4, B1s and N1s peaks were observed only in NiO:BN, changes in their peaks were no observed.

Generally, the change in EQE is mainly dependent on the IQE which further correlated to the charge collection and transport [55,56], thus it can be seen that the improvement of EQE and J $ _{SC} $  of NiO:BN are attributed to enhancements in charge collection and charge transportation. These overall observations indicate that the NiO:BN can provide an improved charge extraction and transportation capability between HTL and perovskite, resulting in improved device performance of p-i-n planar PSCs.

To study why the NiO:BN showed enhanced cell performances, a variety of analyses, such optical transmittance, electrical conductivity, energy level, and surface roughness, associated with photoelectric properties were performed. There is a no significant change in optical transmittance and I-V curves of pristine NiO and NiO:BN thin film as shown in Fig. S6, which indicate that the incorporated BN does not lead to change the optical transmittance and electrical conductivity of NiO thin film. However, the ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) spectra corrected by using gold (Au) as reference showed distinguishable difference as shown in Fig. 3a. The binding energy cut-off of the NiO:BN thin film shifted to lower binding energy, and as a result, it can be seen that the work function (WF) of the NiO:BN was increased than the pristine  $ \mathrm{NiO_x} $ . The valence band maximum (VBM) of NiO:BN was calculated at  $ \sim 5.7\mathrm{eV} $ , which is a downward shift of  $ 0.3\mathrm{eV} $  compared to the pristine  $ \mathrm{NiO_x} $  ( $ \sim 5.4\mathrm{eV} $ ). The NiO:BN showed a better surface roughness than   $  NiO_{x}  $  ; root-means-square roughness (rms) of pristine   $  NiO_{x}  $   and NiO:BN were   $  \sim3.42  $   and   $  \sim2.40  $   nm, respectively. Therefore, the smoother film morphology can also be recognized the main reason of the improved FF and PCE. These observations are better illustrated that the improved performance of the NiO:BN based PSCs may be due to the deeper HOMO level and smoother surface compared to pristine   $  NiO_{x}  $   based PSCs.

Since, the efficiency of PSCs is closely related to the grain size and crystallinity of perovskite, additional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and XRD measurements for the perovskite formed on the pristine   $  NiO_{x}  $   and NiO:BN HTL were performed. Fig. 4a and b shows the surface SEM images of the perovskite layer formed on pristine   $  NiO_{x}  $   and NiO:BN HTL, respectively. The average grain size of perovskite film grown on NiO:BN was observed to be slightly increased compared to the pristine   $  NiO_{x}  $ , as shown in Fig. S7a. This result has also been confirmed to be well consistent with the contact angle (CA) properties of pure   $  NiO_{x}  $   and NiO:BN HTL, as shown in Figs. S7b and c. NiO:BN HTL showed hydrophobic (CA   $  \sim30^{\circ}  $ ) rather than pristine   $  NiO_{x}  $   (CA   $  \sim20^{\circ}  $ ), indicating that the wettability of NiO:BN is low. The lower the wettability of HTL is reported to promote the crystal growth and large grain size of perovskite; the less wettability of HTL reduce the number of the perovskite nucleation sites as well as the surface tension dragging force applying on the perovskite film [67,68]. However, both perovskite films formed on the pristine   $  NiO_{x}  $   and NiO:BN HTL showed similar XRD patterns and the same characteristic peak positions corresponding to triple cation perovskite [69–71], which indicate that both perovskite films have same crystal structure. On the other hand, the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of perovskite formed on the NiO:BN HTL (  $  \sim0.252  $ ) decreased compared to those of pristine   $  NiO_{x}  $   (  $  \sim0.269  $ ), which shows that the perovskite formed on the NiO:BN HTL has better crystallinity and also it is reliable with the large grain size shown in SEM [72]. From the above results, it can be seen that the wettability of   $  NiO_{x}  $   is reduced by BN with a hydrophobic nature, thereby improving grain size and crystallinity of the perovskite and at the same time reduce the grain boundary in perovskite film. Furthermore, it can be seen that the characteristics of charge extraction and transport are improved by the large grain size with high crystallinity and reduction of the grain boundary of the perovskite acting as a recombination site [73].

For better detail investigation on carrier transport properties of HTL, the series resistance (  $  R_{s}  $  ) and shunt resistance (  $  R_{sh}  $  ) were also calculated as shown in Fig. 5a.   $  R_{s}  $   is obtained by calculating 1/slope at the open-circuit voltage point, and   $  R_{sh}  $   is obtained by calculating 1/slope at the short-circuit current point in the representative J-V curve. When the NiO:BN was introduced into HTL, it was observed that the average   $  R_{s}  $   of devices decreased from   $  \sim2.5  $   to   $  \sim1.9\ \Omega\ cm^{2}  $   and the average   $  R_{sh}  $   of devices increased from   $  \sim4599  $   to   $  \sim7192\ \Omega\ cm^{2}  $  . Thus, it is considered that the interface resistance (  $  R_{s}  $  ) and leakage current were decreased by employing the NiO:BN HTL, resulting in the improved FF and   $  V_{OC}  $   at the NiO:BN based PSC [74,75]. Further, this can be attributed to better charge extraction and transportation due to the better energy level alignment, smooth surface, and large grain size of perovskite [76–78]. These results were well agreed by analyzing the dependence of   $  V_{OC}  $   with different light intensities (Fig. 5b). The NiO:BN based PSC maintained   $  \sim85\%  $   of their initial efficiency after 60 days in air with a humidity of 30% to 60%, while the efficiency of the pristine   $  NiO_{x}  $   based PSC was reduced to   $  \sim57\%  $   under the same conditions. Normalized photovoltaic parameters also showed a trend similar to changes in efficiency. These results show that the PSCs with NiO:BN are more stable in ambient air conditions, and this improved stability is believed to be due to the superior quality of the perovskite formed on the NiO:BN HTL. As mentioned above (see Fig. 4 and Fig. S7c), hydrophobic BN reduces the wettability of HTL, which increases the grain size of the perovskite and consequently reduces the density of the grain boundary in the perovskite film. Further, the reduction of the grain boundary density can decrease the diffusion channels for the destructive ions (e.g.,   $  I^{-}  $  ) and molecules (e.g.,   $  O_{2}  $   and   $  H_{2}O  $  ) [90], which could be a major factor that NiO:BN based PSCs have more better long-term stability compared to   $  NiO_{x}  $   based PSCs.
