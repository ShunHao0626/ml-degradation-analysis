# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted growing interest due to an unprecedented increase in power conversion efficiency (PCE), which reached 24.2% within less than ten years (Kojima et al., 2009; Seo et al., 2018; Green et al., 2019). This outstanding advance is related to the remarkable optoelectronic properties of lead halide perovskites, such as strong light absorption and long diffusion length of the photogenerated charge carriers (Ponseca et al., 2014; Stranks et al., 2014). Moreover, processed from small quantities of relatively cheap materials under mild conditions, PSCs are deemed to be a good alternative for low-cost energy production and to be competitive with silicon solar modules (Park et al., 2016; Cai et al., 2016). However, several hurdles have not entirely been overcome, especially the long-term stability of the devices which is crucial for further industrialization of PSC modules. The device stability can be altered by degradation of both materials and interfaces. Since the sensitivity of hybrid perovskites towards moisture is known, many efforts have been devoted to enhance this material's life-time for instance by tuning its composition or processing conditions (Jeon et al., 2015; Ndione et al., 2015; Bi et al., 2016).

# ABSTRACT

We report on the potential of tuning poly(9-vinylcarbazole) (PVK) properties through functionalization for an application as hole transport material (HTM) for perovskite solar cells (PSCs). The synthesized PVK-based polymers were substituted with moieties of interest to improve the solubility, the charge transport properties, or to tune energy levels. Bis(4-methoxyphenyl)amine moieties were found to improve the hole mobility and to increase the HOMO level of the PVK. Therefore, PSCs employing PVK-  $ \left[\mathrm{N}(\mathrm{PhOCH}_3)_2\right]_2 $  as HTM exhibited a best PCE of  $ 16.7\% $ . Compared to spiro-OMeTAD, first studies have shown that PVK-  $ \left[\mathrm{N}(\mathrm{PhOCH}_3)_2\right]_2 $  could extend PSC lifetime.

The HTM is another key material ruling both performance and stability of PSCs. Essential for efficient extraction of holes, it also prevents the hybrid perovskite layer from ambient humidity and degradation. The most studied and effective HTM reported so far is 2,2',7,7'-Tetrakis[N,N-di(4-methoxyphenyl)amino]-9,9'-spirobifluorene known as spiro-OMeTAD. However, due to burdensome synthesis and purification methods, this material is relatively expensive. Moreover, its limited stability makes it unsuitable for industrial scale production (Calió et al., 2016). Therefore, new HTMs, either organic or inorganic, have been developed and investigated to replace spiro-OMeTAD (Calió et al., 2016; Gheno et al., 2016), such as molecular systems composed of spiro-like or carboxylic-based cores (Urieta-Mora et al., 2018), truxene-based core (Guan et al., 2017), nitrogen- or sulfur-containing central scaffolds (Urieta-Mora et al., 2018; Mabrouk et al., 2018) or metallomacrocycles (Urieta-Mora et al., 2018). Some of them include small carbazole-based molecules, like X51 and star-shape derivatives, which have been incorporated into PSCs (Xu et al., 2014; Sung et al., 2014; Benhattab et al., 2018; Nakar et al., 2018). Moreover, several studies have shown the influence of ending groups of small molecules or polymer units on the film forming and the optoelectronic properties of the HTMs. It has been shown for instance that methoxy substituents improved the conductivity of carbazole-based derivatives (Magomedov et al., 2018), or that alkyl-ending chains could be introduced to enhance the solubility and the film forming of thiophene-based HTMs (Zimmermann et al., 2016). On the other hand, bis(4-methoxyphenyl)amine groups present in spiro-OMeTAD have been largely employed to design new HTMs (Zhang et al., 2017; Huang et al., 2016). The triphenylamine groups are reported to bring stability and good charge transport properties to the materials (Zhang et al., 2018).

Herein, we report on the synthesis, the thorough characterization and the application as HTM in PSCs of PVKs functionalized with diphenylamine (PVK-  $  [N(PhOCH_{3})_{2}]_{2}  $  ), tert-butyl (PVK-  $  (tBu)_{2}  $  ) and methoxy (PVK-  $  (OCH_{3})_{2}  $  ) groups. Original post-functionalization routes of PVK were thus established allowing a fine control of the molecular weight and a variety of functionalization. This original method is more opened than conventional polymerization of functional monomers (Xu et al., 2017). Indeed, by contrast to the works reported by Xu et al. our methodology is based on the preparation of well-defined PVK platforms that can be freely post-functionalized (Xu et al., 2017). The new materials were then tested in n-i-p planar and mesoscopic PSCs, and the photovoltaic performances were rationalized in terms of conductivity and electronic properties of HTMs.

# 2.2. Perovskite solar cells

2.2.1.Materials

Zinc powder, hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%), titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetylacetonate) (TiAcAc, 75 wt% in IPA), titanium(IV) chloride (99.9%) and tert-butylpyridine (tBP, 96%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Lead(II) chloride (PbCl₂, ultra dry, 99.999%) was obtained from Alfa Aesar, formamidinium iodide (FAI, > 99%) and methylammonium bromide (MABr, > 98%) from Dyesol, magnesium (II) bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (Mg(TFSI)₂) from TCI, spiro-OMeTAD (99%) from Merck and lithium bis(trifluoro-methanesulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI) from Wako. The 24 nm nano-TiO₂ paste (PST-24NR) was purchased from JPG C&C. Products were all used as received.

2.2.2. n-i-p planar device fabrication

FTO-glass substrates were patterned with zinc powder and HCl. Then, they were subsequently washed through sonication in water and ethanol baths before being treated by UV-ozone cleaner. A 50 nm-  $  TiO_{2}  $  compact layer was deposited from a solution of TiAcAc in ethanol by spray-pyrolysis at 400 °C followed by annealing at the same temperature for 45 min. The substrates were further immersed in a  $  TiCl_{4}  $  solution in ethanol at 70 °C before calcination at 500 °C.  $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $  was synthesized according to a literature method (Etgar et al., 2012).  $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $  and  $  PbCl_{2}  $  (3:1 M ratio) were mixed in anhydrous DMF, deposited by spin coating at 2000 rpm and annealed at 120 °C for 45 min to form a dense perovskite layer of 330 nm. HTM solutions were prepared by dissolving optimal concentration of spiro-OMeTAD (8 wt%), PVK (5.9 wt%), PVK-  $  [N(PhOCH_{3})_{2}]_{2}  $  (4.3 wt%), PVK-(tBu)  $  _{2}  $  (3.7 wt%) and PVK-(OCH  $  _{3}  $ )  $  _{2}  $  (3.1 wt%) in 650 μL of chlorobenzene. LiTFSI and tBP, were necessary mixed to spiro-OMeTAD, PVK-[N(PhOCH  $  _{3}  $ )  $  _{2}  $ ]  $  _{2}  $ , PVK-(tBu)  $  _{2}  $  and PVK-(OCH  $  _{3}  $ )  $  _{2}  $  to enhance hole extraction. Thus, 14 μL of 44 wt% solution of LiTFSI in acetonitrile were added to the spiro-OMeTAD, PVK-(tBu)  $  _{2}  $  and PVK-(OCH  $  _{3}  $ )  $  _{2}  $  solutions, and 7 μL to the PVK-[N(PhOCH  $  _{3}  $ )  $  _{2}  $ ]  $  _{2}  $  solution. 20 μL of tBP were added to all of the solutions. The HTMs were deposited by spin coating at 3000 rpm and let overnight in dry air before finally evaporating an 80-nm thick gold electrode on top of the device. The thicknesses of the HTM layers were about 180 nm, 120 nm, 110 nm and 130 nm for spiro-OMeTAD, PVK-(OCH  $  _{3}  $ )  $  _{2}  $ , PVK-(tBu)  $  _{2}  $  and PVK-[N(PhOCH  $  _{3}  $ )  $  _{2}  $ ]  $  _{2}  $  respectively. In the case of the undoped HTMs, the top electrode was made of 10 nm of MoO  $  _{3}  $  and 80 nm of silver. The device active area was 0.1 cm  $  ^{2}  $ .

2.2.3. n-i-p mesoscopic device fabrication

FTO glass substrates were first laser etched and successively cleaned 10 min in ultrasonic bath in water, detergent, acetone and ethanol, and by excimer UV cleaner. Then, a solution made of TiAcAc mixed with 3 mol.% of LiTFSI and 3 mol.% of Mg(TFSI)  $  _{2}  $  in ethanol was deposited by spray pyrolysis at 430 °C to form a 40-nm-compact-TiO  $  _{2}  $  layer. The

next steps were performed in a dry room with dew point temperature at  $ -30^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . A  $ 150~\mathrm{nm} $  mp- $ \mathrm{TiO}_2 $  layer was made by spin coating a solution of  $ 24\mathrm{-nm - TiO_2} $  paste diluted in ethanol (14 wt%), followed by a 2-step annealing, at  $ 125^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  and  $ 550^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . The layer was then doped with a LiTFSI solution in acetonitrile (0.1 M) deposited by spin coating and annealed at  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  and  $ 430^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . Perovskite precursor solution was made by dissolving MABr,  $ \mathrm{PbBr_2} $ ,  $ \mathrm{PbI_2} $  and FAI (1:1:5.75:5.45 mol) in a mixture of DMSO and DMF (1:4 vol).  $ 5\mathrm{mol.\%} $  of KI, which was previously dissolved in DMSO (1.5 M), were added to the perovskite solution. Anti-solvent spin-coating method was carried out to make a dark, smooth and homogeneous perovskite layer which was annealed at for  $ 160^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . In the final process, a solution of FAI:MABr (5.45:1), FAI or MABr in isopropanol was then deposited by spin-coating on top of the perovskite and rinsed with IPA before being annealed at  $ 80^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 20\mathrm{min} $ . HTM solutions and films were prepared in the same conditions as for the planar devices. Substrates were left overnight in the dry room. Finally, the  $ 80\mathrm{-nm} $  top gold electrode was evaporated. The device active area was  $ 0.181~\mathrm{cm}^2 $ .

2.2.4. Characterizations

UV/Vis absorption spectra were recorded on a Shimadzu UV-3600 spectrometer. Ionization potentials were measured with a Riken Keiki AC-3 photoelectron spectrometer in air. Scanning electron microscopy was performed on a Hitachi SU8000 system at 10 kV. Atomic force microscope (Dimension FatScan, Bruker) was used in tapping mode to characterize HTMs surfaces. I-V measurements were carried out with a solar simulator (CEP-25TF) under AM1.5G solar light with an intensity of   $  100 \, mW \cdot cm^{-2}  $   calibrated by a standard silicon cell (BS-520,S/N235). Reverse scan (from open-circuit to short-circuit) and forward scan (from short-circuit to open-circuit) were recorded at a   $  100 \, mV \cdot s^{-1}  $   scan rate.

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Synthesis

The synthesis of PVK-  $  [N(PhOCH_{3})_{2}]_{2}  $  has required only three steps as depicted in Scheme 1. First a well-defined PVK polymer of  $  7.0\ kg\cdot mol^{-1}  $  was obtained through Reversible Addition Fragmentation Transfer (RAFT) polymerization. Further bromination using N-bromosuccinimide yielded the 3,6-bromo-functionalized PVKs. Finally, 4,4'-dimethoxydiphenylamine substituents were introduced via Pd-catalyzed a Buchwald-Hartwig reaction to give the target polymers PVK-  $  [N(PhOCH_{3})_{2}]_{2}  $  of  $  11.7\ kg\cdot mol^{-1}  $ . On the other hand, PVK-  $  (tBu)_{2}  $  was synthesized from PVK by Friedel-Crafts alkylation, whereas PVK-  $  (OCH_{3})_{2}  $  was prepared from PVK-  $  Br_{2}  $  by direct displacement of bromine (Scheme 1).  $  {}^{1}H  $  NMR and FTIR analyses of all samples show a successful functionalization with the typical spectroscopic features of the different moieties (Figs. S1 and S2). For instance, integration waves of the  $  {}^{1}H  $  NMR spectrum for PVK-  $  [N(PhOCH_{3})_{2}]_{2}  $  are consistent with the introduction of two N,N-di-p-methoxyphenylamine groups (Fig. S1). The functionalization processes were also evidenced by the evolution of the molar masses and the traces obtained by Size Exclusion Chromatography (SEC) (Fig. S3) except for the tBu-functionalized PVK that showed a multimodal distribution (more probably due to interchain coupling during the chemical modification) and a small discrepancy in  $  {}^{1}H  $  NMR Integral values. The macromolecular characteristics of the products are summarized in Table 1. Moreover, it is worth underlining the simple purification procedure used through Söxhlet extraction for PVK-  $  [N(PhOCH_{3})_{2}]_{2}  $ , which facilitates the material production and leads to moderate synthetic costs (Table S1). Due to the polymeric backbone and the nature of the material, it is very easy to get rid of impurities and obtain a pure functionalized polymer.

# 3.2. Properties

Polymer solubility was tested in polar and non-polar solvents at a concentration of  $ 50\mathrm{mg / mL} $  (Table S2). All polymers were soluble in chlorobenzene a suitable solvent to deposit HTMs in PSCs. Besides, the dimethoxyphenylamine moieties of  $ \mathrm{PVK - [N(PhOCH_3)_2]_2} $  improve the solubility of PVK in several polar solvents.

Thermogravimetric analyses (TGA) indicate that the polymers are rather thermally stable under air with a mass loss lower than 10% at 360°C (Fig. S4). Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) evidences a lower glass transition temperature of PVK (203°C) as compared to PVK-  $  [N(PhOCH_{3})_{2}]_{2}  $   (222°C) and PVK-  $  (OCH_{3})_{2}  $   (219°C) (Fig. S5). It is worth mentioning that no transition was observed for PVK-  $  (tBu)_{2}  $ , and neither crystallization nor melting processes were observed for all materials between 0 and 250°C.

The ionization potential and the band gap of the materials were measured from UV photoelectron spectroscopy and UV–vis absorption and emission spectra (Table 1). HOMO energy levels were estimated at -5.8, -5.1, -5.6 and -5.5 eV for PVK, PVK-[N(PhOCH₃)₂]₂, PVK-(tBu)₂ and PVK-(OCH₃)₂ respectively. # 3.3. Photovoltaic properties

3.3.2. n-i-p mesoscopic perovskite solar cells

The potential of the PVK-  $  [N(PhOCH_{3})_{2}]_{2}  $   as HTM was further studied in more efficient, stable and reproducible n-i-p mesoscopic PSCs:FTO/bl-TiO  $  _{2}  $  /mp-TiO  $  _{2}  $  /K  $  ^{+}  $   doped MA  $  _{0.15}  $  FA  $  _{0.85}  $  PbI  $  _{2.55}  $  Br  $  _{0.45}  $  /HTM/Au (Tang et al., 2017). The PVK-  $  [N(PhOCH_{3})_{2}]_{2}  $  -based solar cells were optimized by investigating the effect of the dopant concentration and the thickness on the PV performances. The dopant concentration is noted as x:y,z, where x, y and z correspond to the molar equivalence of the HTM, LiTFSI and tBP respectively. The reference doping ratio of spiro-OMeTAD was 1:0.5:3.3. When increasing dopant concentration, V  $  _{oc}  $   was reduced and J  $  _{sc}  $   and FF increased up to a maximum reached for the 1:1.25:8.25 dopant concentration (Table S4). Thereby, PCE was improved from 11.93 to 14.49%. For the next experiments, dopant formulation was fixed at 1:1.25:8.25. The hole mobility of the doped polymer was   $  2.8 \times 10^{-3} cm^{2}V^{-1}s^{-1}  $  .

The thickness of PVK-  $ \left[\mathrm{N}(\mathrm{PhOCH}_3)_2\right]_2 $  was then varied with the spin-coating parameters and evaluated from the SEM cross section of the PSCs (Fig. S8). These images show the homogeneity of the PVK-[N  $ (\mathrm{PhOCH}_3)_2 $ ] layers, well covering the perovskite without pinholes. Optimal PCE of  $ 14.69\% $  was obtained for the  $ 90\mathrm{-nm} $  thick PVK-N

$ (\mathrm{PhOCH}_3)_2]_2 $  (Table S5).

Despite optimizations of the layer,   $  V_{oc}  $   is still relatively low compared to planar PSCs. The perovskite composition being different, it may locally modify the energy levels of the perovskite or the interactions between the polymer and the perovskite at the interface. The   $  MA_{0.15}FA_{0.85}PbI_{2.55}Br_{0.45}  $   perovskite contains an excess of lead. To make the perovskite/HTM interface less lead rich, solutions of either FAI, MABr or FAI:MABr (0.85:0.15) were deposited on top of the perovskite layer. All of the solutions had the effect to improve the   $  V_{oc}  $ , up to 1.07 V with MABr, and the PCE (Table S6). This may be due to a reduction of recombination at the interface. A best PCE of 16.71% with an acceptable hysteresis was achieved employing FAI:MABr. Using a similar process, spiro-OMeTAD-based PSCs exhibited a best PCE of 17.26% with a lower FF and a higher   $  V_{oc}  $   (Table 3, Fig. 3a).   $  J_{sc}  $   values extracted from EQE measurements are in accordance with those deduced from I to V curves, with a slightly higher   $  J_{sc}  $   in the case of spiro-OMeTAD (Fig. 3b). The maximum power point tracking (MPPT) of the PSCs revealed a better stability in operating conditions and a better photoresponsivity of the PVK-[N(PhOCH  $  _{3}  $ )  $  _{2}  $ ]  $  _{2}  $  -based PSCs (Fig. 3c). In mesoscopic PSCs, PVK-[N(PhOCH  $  _{3}  $ )  $  _{2}  $ ]  $  _{2}  $   demonstrated high efficiencies and confirms its potential to improve the stability of PSCs.

# 4. Conclusion

To summarize, new poly(9-vinylcarbazole)s were synthesized through cost-effective post-functionalization with different moieties. Bis(4-methoxyphenyl)amine ending groups increased the HOMO level the PVK from -5.8 to -5.1 eV and improved the hole mobility over   $  10^{-3}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $  /V·s with the help of dopants. PVK-  $  [N(\mathrm{PhOCH}_{3})_{2}]_{2}  $   HTM led to average photovoltaic performances close to those of spiro-OMeTAD in n-i-p planar devices with a best achievement of 14% PCE and extended lifetime. In n-i-p mesoscopic devices the PCE was enhanced up to 16.7%. More interestingly, the higher quality HTL is suggested to extend the planar device lifetime, and to improve the stability of mesoscopic devices in operating conditions. These results highlight the potentiality of the fine tuning of poly(9-vinylcarbazole) properties through post-functionalization and open new directions to facilitate large-scale production at low cost of high-efficiency PSCs.
