# Improvement of Cs-(FAPbI $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.85} $ (MAPbBr $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.15} $  Quality Via DMSO-Molecule-Control to Increase the Efficiency and Boost the Long-Term Stability of 1 cm $ ^2 $  Sized Planar Perovskite Solar Cells

Xu Liu, Lei Shi, Jialiang Huang, Ziheng Liu, Pengfei Zhang, Jae Sung Yun, Arman Mahboubi Soufiani, Jan Seidel, Kaiwen Sun, Ziv Hameiri, John A. Stride, Yuanfang Zhang, Martin A. Green, Hong Lin,* and Xiaojing Hao*

While interfacial and grain-boundary passivation presently attract enormous research interest for perovskite solar cells (PSCs), the improvement of Cs-(FAPbI $ _3 $ ) $ _X $ (MAPbBr $ _3 $ ) $ _Y $  bulk quality still lacks systematical study, especially for constructing polycrystalline layers in planar configurations. Here, a DMSO-molecule-process for improving the quality of Cs-(FAPbI $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.85} $ (MAPbBr $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.15} $  is developed, where the molar ratio of precursors, the kind of anti-solvents, and speed-time profiles are found critical. The optimized treatment significantly enhanced the crystal orientation, grain size, surface roughness, photoresponse, carrier lifetime, and contact potential difference of absorbers. Cs-(FAPbI $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.85} $ (MAPbBr $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.15} $  absorbers also present superior charge transport, as well as reduced carrier recombination and decreased trap densities via DMSO-molecule-control, enabling performance improvement on both long-term stability and photovoltaic parameters of 1 cm $ ^2 $  PSCs. Champion planar cells demonstrated a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 21.07% (0.159 cm $ ^2 $ ) and PCE of 19.4% (1 cm $ ^2 $ ) with negligible hysteresis. Moreover, 1 cm $ ^2 $  devices retained 90% of initial PCE after aging 50 days in ambient air.

# 1. Introduction

The amount of DMSO is hard to control when using the mixed DMF:DMSO with chlorobenzene anti-solvent for synthesizing Cs-(FAPbI $ _3 $ ) $ _X $ (MAPbBr $ _3 $ ) $ _Y $ , a chlorobenzene wash removes not only DMF but also DMSO from precursor thin films. Assuming DMSO plays a similar role in Cs-(FAPbI $ _3 $ ) $ _X $ (MAPbBr $ _3 $ ) $ _Y $  perovskites in terms of crystal growth, the current process may make crystal growth hardly controllable and reproducible. In fact, few reports systematically study the influence of DMSO-molecule on the quality of Cs-(FAPbI $ _3 $ ) $ _X $ (MAPbBr $ _3 $ ) $ _Y $  absorbers and thus PSCs to date, compared to a lot counterpart research on simple MAPbI $ _3 $  materials. $ ^{[16]} $  As a result, such unexplored field inspired us to identify the key factors of realizing similar DMSO-molecule-control on fabricating Cs-(FAPbI $ _3 $ ) $ _X $ (MAPbBr $ _3 $ ) $ _Y $  perovskites, as well as whether the long-term stability and photovoltaic performance would be enhanced after using optimized DMSO-molecule-control.

In this study, we employ DMSO-molecule-control method via using diethyl ether to efficiently improve the quality of Cs-(FAPbI $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.85} $ (MAPbBr $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.15} $  (Cs-FAMA) absorbers, and thus contributing to the enhanced long-term stability and photovoltaic performance of PSCs. Due to the more complex composition of Cs-FAMA perovskites, the reported molar ratio of precursor components and speed-time profiles for simple MAPbI $ _3 $  are not suitable for Cs-FAMA anymore. Moreover, compared to chlorobenzene, the diethyl ether wash only removes DMF and leaves DMSO molecules in precursor films, enabling the precise control of DMSO-molecule amount. Compared to Cs-FAMA films treated by chlorobenzene, the crystal orientation, grain size, surface roughness, photoresponse, carrier lifetime, and contact potential difference (CPD) of absorbers via DMSO-molecule-control are all significantly improved. Films synthesized by DMSO-molecule-control also present superior charge transport, as well as reduced carrier recombination and decreased trap densities than the counterpart produced by chlorobenzene. Owing to these visible morphological and electronic improvement for perovskite absorbers over large-area size, the long-term stability and photovoltaic parameters of 1 cm $ ^2 $  planar PSCs are both successfully boosted. Hysteresis-free champion planar cells of PCE = 21.07% with V $ _{OC} $  as high as 1.17 V (0.159 cm $ ^2 $ ) and PCE = 19.4% with V $ _{OC} $  as high as 1.13 V (1 cm $ ^2 $ ) were achieved. PSCs via DMSO-molecule-control demonstrated good transit stability. The improved device performance can reach steady-state efficiency in less than 5s upon 1 sun illumination. Moreover, compared to the counterpart by using chlorobenzene, 1 cm $ ^2 $  devices via DMSO-molecule-control presented much better long-term stability, retaining 90% of initial PCE after ageing 50 days in ambient air. These results highlight the importance of drawing some research attention back to the most basic studies about Cs-(FAPbI $ _3 $ ) $ _X $ (MAPbBr $ _3 $ ) $ _Y $  bulk rather than only focusing on ion-doping or incorporating new materials for the improvement of the efficiency and durability of hybrid perovskite devices.

# 2. Results and Discussion

Very recently, we reported that simple inorganic binary alkaline halide salts can efficiently suppress the recombination at the   $  SnO_{2}  $  /perovskite interface.  $  ^{[3]}  $   The resultant low-temperature-processed planar device with the   $  MgF_{2}  $   anti-reflection coating exhibited a PCE of 20.5% and a high   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.13V (0.159 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ), while showing negligible hysteresis.  $  ^{[3]}  $   Therefore, we adopted the same configuration of PSCs (  $  SnO_{2}  $  /KCl/Cs-FAMA perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD/Au) in this work, except for the replacement of FTO substrates with ITO substrates due to the reduced roughness and higher light transmission.

To precisely control the amount of DMSO-molecule within unannealed  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  thin films, diethyl ether was often used as the anti-solvent due to its immiscibility with DMSO-molecule.[16] Moreover, the molar ratio of MAI:  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ : DMSO = 1:1:1 was widely regarded as the optimal dose for forming metastable MAI· $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ . DMSO intermediate and thus annealed  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite. Because the composition of Cs-(FAPbI₃)ₓ(MAPbBr₃)ᵧ perovskites was derived from  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ , the formula of  $ \mathrm{APbX}_3 $  can represent the mixed perovskite as well, where  $ \mathrm{A}^+ = (\mathrm{Cs}^+ + \mathrm{FA}^+ + \mathrm{MA}^+) $  rather than sole  $ \mathrm{MA}^+ $  and  $ \mathrm{X}^- = (\mathrm{I}^- + \mathrm{Br}^-) $  rather than sole  $ \mathrm{I}^- $ . With this in mind, we tried the molar ratio of (CsI + FAI + MAI + MABr):  $ (\mathrm{PbI}_2 + \mathrm{PbBr}_2) $ : DMSO = 1:1:1 at the beginning. Unfortunately, different from  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  films, Cs-FAMA films made from this molar ratio always had visually severe impurity when using diethyl ether as the anti-solvent (Figure S1a,b, Supporting Information), hardly satisfying the prerequisite for making high performance devices. We assumed that the molar ratio of (CsI + FAI + MAI + MABr):  $ (\mathrm{PbI}_2 + \mathrm{PbBr}_2) $ : DMSO may need optimization. As a result, various molar ratios of (CsI + FAI + MAI + MABr):  $ (\mathrm{PbI}_2 + \mathrm{PbBr}_2) $ : DMSO were then tried, and 1:1:3 molar ratio was finally found to be good for fabricating Cs-FAMA films because of the disappearance of visual impurity. However, only changing the molar ratio was not enough. The speed-time profile was found to be another critical factor for making desired perovskite films. Some reported speed-time profiles for planar PSCs[11,13,17,18] were not suitable here anymore, because of the frequent cracking problems (Figure S1c,e, Supporting Information). These differences between  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  and Cs-FAMA may derive from more complicated compositions of mixed perovskites and the corresponding nucleation and crystallization progress. Figure S2, Supporting Information shows the Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR) of un-annealed precursor films treated by different anti-solvents. Stretching vibration of S=O appears at  $ 1015~\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $ , which is well consistent with the value of reported MAI· $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ ·DMSO intermediate. The significantly increased S=O for the diethyl ether-treated film indicates that the DMSO-molecule-control method is better at keeping DMSO in precursor films and thus intermediate complex. A complicated intermediate phase [AX· $ \mathrm{PbX}_2 $ ·3DMSO (A: Cs + FA + MA; X: I + Br)] may form for mixed perovskite recipe, which will be systematically studied in the future. Figure S1d, Supporting Information is the image of the whole perovskite film deposited on ITO/SnO₂/KCl substrate by using the optimal DMSO-molecule-control, showing a mirror-like, crack-free, and visually impurity-free morphology.

The champion   $  0.159 cm^{2}  $   cell showed a   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.17 V, a   $  J_{SC}  $   of   $  23.58 mA cm^{-2}  $  , a FF of 75.65%, and a PCE of 20.84% under reverse scan, and it showed a   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.17 V, a   $  J_{SC}  $   of   $  23.59 mA cm^{-2}  $  , a FF of 76.34%, and a PCE of 21.07% under forward scan. Compared to   $  0.159 cm^{2}  $   PSCs,   $  J_{SC}  $  , and FF of   $  1 cm^{2}  $   PSCs obviously dropped due to the poor lateral transport ability of our ITO electrode. Hysteresis was first observed and widely reported in PSCs with small area sizes. In our previous work, we found that KCl can efficiently suppress the recombination at the   $  SnO_{2}/perovskite  $   interface, and thus eliminate the hysteresis phenomenon in small PSCs. However, we did not exclude the possibility of the impact of bulk defects on hysteresis. Hysteresis should be more obvious for large-area PSCs than small-area counterparts, because of the less efficient charge transport in large cells. Figure 2c shows J-V curves of   $  1 cm^{2}  $   PSCs fabricated by pristine and DMSO-molecule-control methods. To elucidate the hysteresis, a hysteresis index (H-index) was used here: H-index = (PCE  $  _{RS}  $  -PCE  $  _{FS}  $  )/PCE  $  _{RS}  $  , where PCE  $  _{RS}  $   and PCE  $  _{FS}  $   were PCE from reverse and forward scan, respectively. The H-index was reduced from 4.68% for the pristine perovskite to 2.37% for the DMSO-molecule-treated perovskite. Clearly, DMSO-molecule-control provide a leverage on reducing the hysteresis for large-area PSCs. This result confirmed our speculation, suggesting reduced defects in absorbers after DMSO-molecule-control. Moisture and oxygen are known to challenge the stability of PSCs, degrading the quality of perovskite films by attacking the defect sites of perovskite films.  $  ^{[19]}  $   Since DMSO-molecule-control was confirmed to strongly improve the photovoltaic performance of PSCs, especially for   $  V_{OC}  $  . Therefore, it is reasonable to speculate that this route may also improve the long-term stability of PSCs. To verify this,   $  1 cm^{2}  $   Cs-FAMA devices from different treatments were monitored by testing the performance in air (humidity: 50–70%) at room temperature, using an air ambient desiccator (humidity <20%) for storage (Figure 2d). As expected, PSCs made by DMSO-molecule-control presented longer-term stability: they retained 90% of initial PCE over 50 days, whereas pristine cells only retained <80% of initial PCE. This result conclusively confirms that the DMSO-molecule-control can effectively suppress the degradation progress of PSCs in ambient testing conditions. Figure 2e,f show statistical distribution of photovoltaic parameters for   $  1 cm^{2}  $   PSCs. Compared to pristine devices, all photovoltaic parameters of   $  1 cm^{2}  $   PSCs made by DMSO-molecule-control achieved significant improvement, demonstrating the promise of DMSO-molecule-control in up-scaling field.

To systematically investigate the impact of DMSO-molecule-treatment on the quality of perovskite crystals, X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used for characterizing samples. Figure 3a,b are the comparison of x-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of Cs-FAMA perovskite films prepared by pristine and DMSO-molecule-treated methods. From XRD patterns, no systematic peak shifts relative to the pristine film were observed, indicating the identical crystal structure of perovskite. However, we note that the DMSO-molecule-control clearly affected the crystal orientation. The ratio of relative intensity of (100)/(210) diffraction peaks increased from 2.11 for the pristine perovskite to 4.16 for the DMSO-molecule-treated perovskite. The main peak (100) becomes stronger and sharper, where the full-width at half-maximum intensity (FWHM) decreased from  $ 0.16^{\circ} $  for the pristine film to  $ 0.14^{\circ} $  for DMSO-molecule-treated film, reflecting an expansion of the mean size of perovskite crystallites. These results indicate that the DMSO-molecule-control can simultaneously improve perovskite crystallinity and grain size.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images reveal stark surface difference among Cs-FAMA perovskite films prepared by different treatments (Figure 4a,b). For pristine samples, sizes of perovskite grains are mainly between 200 and 500 nm, in good agreement with reported values.  $  ^{[3,10,13]}  $   Cs ions can accelerate the nucleation of the perovskite and thus resulting in smaller crystals.  $  ^{[10]}  $   Interestingly, sizes of Cs-FAMA grains increase when using DMSO-molecule treatment, ranging between 400 and 1000 nm. Clearly, grain size of perovskite is strongly correlated with the DMSO-molecule process. Intermediate phases considerably impact the crystal growth.  $  ^{[11,16]}  $   By using DMSO-molecule-control, it is highly likely that a much better intermediate phase is obtained in unannealed Cs-FAMA films, resulting in an improved crystallization of annealed perovskite and an increased grain size. Figures 4c, 4d, and S4, Supporting Information are cross-sectional SEM and TEM images of the films. The annealed films are both about 550 nm thick, indicating that DMSO-molecule-control does not change the thickness of absorbers. According to SEM results, the sizes of most of the perovskite crystallites from DMSO-molecule-control are commensurate with their thicknesses, and most of the grain boundaries are perpendicular to the substrate for minimizing the grain boundary energy. Therefore, compared to the pristine sample, very few grain boundaries are visible within the plane of the DMSO-molecule-treated planar absorber, which may have implications for the charge-carrier recombination properties and thus the device performance. Figure S5, Supporting Information shows low-magnified surface SEM images of Cs-FAMA perovskite films fabricated by pristine and DMSO-molecule-control methods. There is no difference of PbI  $  _{2}  $   amounts between two samples. DMSO-molecule-control did not impact the compositions of annealed perovskites, well confirming the XRD and EQE results. We further scrutinized the films deposited from different procedures by atomic force microscopy (AFM) as shown in Figure 4e,f. The calculated root mean-squared roughness of the perovskite films made from pristine and DMSO-molecule-treated routes are 22.0 and 12.9 nm, respectively. Clearly, the roughness of films fabricated by DMSO-molecule-control is greatly reduced compared to the pristine counterpart.

To check electronic quality of grains, Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) was performed. KPFM provides spatial mapping of contact potential difference (CPD) in nanoscale using a noncontact atomic force microscope (NC-AFM). CPD originates from the difference in work function between the surfaces of the tip and sample. The work function of the surface of a sample is obtained by measuring the CPD using a tip with known work function. Work function of the sample is often dependent on surface stoichiometry, impurities, defects, and carrier concentration. Note that the measurement is performed in dark and the AFM operating laser wavelength is 1300 nm which is far outside the absorption range of the perovskite film. Figure 6a,b shows CPD images of the pristine and DMSO-molecule-control samples respectively. It is apparent that individual grains are clearly distinguishable with definite grain boundaries, which are 10–18 mV lower in CPD compared to the grain interior for the DMSO-molecule-control sample (Figure 6b). # 3. Conclusion

In summary, DMSO-molecule-control efficiently improved the quality of perovskite absorbers, significantly increasing the PCE and boosting the long-term stability of large-area planar perovskite solar cells. Highly crystallized absorbers with better crystal orientation, larger grain size, fewer grain boundaries, and smoother surface were obtained after using optimized DMSO-molecule-control. This technique also contributed to enhanced photo-response, longer carrier lifetime, and contact potential difference. Moreover, the superior perovskite films made by DMSO-molecule-control demonstrated better charge transport, as well as reduced carrier recombination and decreased trap densities. Therefore, small-area PSCs showed better PCE as high as 21.07% and narrower distributions of photovoltaic parameters, contributing to the successful fabrication of improved large-area (1 cm $  ^{2}  $ ) PSCs of PCE = 19.4% with further reduced hysteresis. Compared to pristine samples, 1 cm $  ^{2}  $  devices via DMSO-molecule-control presented better long-term stability, retaining 90% of initial PCE after aging 50 days in ambient air. We anticipate that this study may draw some attention back to basic studies for improving the stability of Cs-FAMA perovskite materials and the performance of corresponding PV devices.

# 4. Experimental Section

Device Fabrication: The   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETLs were prepared by a simple solution-based process: the solution containing 0.2 M   $  SnCl_{2}  $   in ethanol was spin-coated on clean ITO substrates. The films were subsequently annealed in air at 200°C for 60 min. A 5 mg   $  mL^{-1}  $   KCl aqueous solution was coated on the   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETLs by spinning at 4000 rpm for 20 s, and then annealed at 100°C for 20 min. The optimized 1.2 M   $  Cs_{0.05}(FAPbI_{3})_{0.85}(MAPbBr_{3})_{0.15}  $   perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving the mixture of CsI, FAI, MABr,   $  PbI_{2}  $  ,   $  PbBr_{2}  $  , and DMSO in DMF, where molar ratio of (FAI + MABr + CsI):(  $  PbI_{2}  $   + bBr  $  _{2}  $  ):DMSO was 1:1:3. Compact perovskite absorber layers were deposited on the substrates with a spin-coatin program: first at 2000 rpm for 10 s with acceleration of 200 rpm s  $  ^{-1}  $   and second at 4000 rpm for 20 s with acceleration of 1000 rpm s  $  ^{-1}  $  . For comparison of different anti-solvent, 0.5 mL diethyl ether or 100 μL chlorobenzene was dropped on the spinning substrate during the second spin-coating step at 10 s before the end of the procedure. More detailed time-rotation profiles for the spin-coating are represented in Figure S1, Supporting Information. The films were then transferred on a hotplate and annealed at 100°C for 45 min. After cooling down to room temperature, the hole-transport layer was subsequently deposited on top of the perovskite film by spin coating at 4500 rpm for 30 s using a chlorobenzene solution containing 75 mg Spiro-OMeTAD, 28.5 μL 4-tert-butylpyridine and 17.5 μL of lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide solution (520 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   in acetonitrile). Finally, 100 nm Au contact layer was deposited on top of Spiro-OMeTAD using thermal evaporation.

Device Characterization: The current density-voltage (J-V) measurements were performed using a solar cell I-V testing system from Abet Technologies, Inc. (using class AAA solar simulator) under an illumination power of   $  100 \, mW cm^{-2}  $   with an aperture size of   $  0.159 \, cm^2  $   or   $  1 \, cm^2  $  . The ITO electrode was soldered for J-V measurements. The J-V curves were normally scanned from 1.2 to -0.1 V with a scan rate of   $  0.1 \, V s^{-1}  $  . A Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR, Nicolet 6700, Thermal Scientific) was used to collect FT-IR spectral data for un-annealed precursor films which were deposited on polished Si wafer in the 3000–500   $  cm^{-1}  $   range. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were measured using a PANalytical Xpert Materials Research diffractometer system with a Cu Kα radiation source (  $  \lambda = 0.1541 \, nm  $  ). Secondary electron image was recorded using a field emission scanning electron

microscope (NanoSEM 450). The cross-section TEM specimens were prepared by focused ion beam (FIB). Atomic force microscope (AFM) was performed using Bruker multimode 8 in trapping mode. The microstructure and elemental distribution in each layer of the film were measured by a JEOL F200 with cold field emission gun (FEG) Scanning Transmission Electron Microscope (STEM). The external quantum efficiency (EQE) was measured by QEX10 spectral response system from PV measurements, Inc. KPFM measurements were performed using a gold coated Si tip with  $ 6\mathrm{nm} $  a radius of curvature (HYDRA6R-100NG-10, APPNANO) as the probe in a non-contact mode with an AC voltage of  $ -1 $  to  $ +1\mathrm{V} $ . Photoluminescence (PL) spectra were measured using a back scattering geometry with laser excitation of  $ 405\mathrm{nm} $  and a thermoelectrically cooled Si-CCD detector. Electrical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed in the dark using E4990A Impedance Analyzer, Keysight Technologies.
