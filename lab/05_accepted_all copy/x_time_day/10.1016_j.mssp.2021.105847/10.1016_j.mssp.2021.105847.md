# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have gained tremendous research interest in recent years due to their high conversion efficiency and simple preparation technology   $  [1-4]  $  . Up to now, the certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of organic/inorganic PSCs has been achieved 25.5% in 2020   $  [5]  $  . However, owing to the degradation of their organic components, the poor device stability in moisture, oxygen, and thermal has still not solved yet   $  [6-8]  $  . Besides, the organic hole transport materials (HTMs) become the most significant obstacle to commercial viability due to their high price and instability property   $  [9]  $  . One effective strategy to improve chemical stability is to replace the organic components of the hybrid perovskite with inorganic counterparts   $  [10, 11]  $  .

It is found that carbon-based, all-inorganic PSCs without hole transporting layers (HTLs) and noble metal electrodes have demonstrated to have excellent moisture and thermal stability, combine with a simple fabrication process   $  [12–14]  $  . All inorganic perovskite materials are mainly   $  CsPbI_{x}Br_{3-x}  $  . As x changes from 0 to 1, the bandwidth decreases from 2.3eV (  $  CsPbBr_{3}  $  ) to 1.73eV (  $  CsPbI_{3}  $  ), but the stability of the material decreases   $  [15,16]  $  . Carbon-based, all-inorganic  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have demonstrated to have superior stability and low cost, combine with a simple fabrication process. However, there are two vital factors that impede enhancement of the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs. One is the low energy level alignment matching between perovskite layer and carbon electrode, and the other one is defects of the perovskite film. Here, we introduce  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  QDs as an interface between the  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  layer and carbon electrode to passivate the perovskite bulk film. The surface defects and charge carrier mobility of QDs are also modified using a bidentate chelating ligand, namely 4-mercaptobenzoic acid (4-MBA). By optimizing the energy level alignment at the interface, the hole extraction is promoted, and interfacial recombination is suppressed. Compared to the PSCs without the QDs layer, the QDs-passivated PSC achieved a PCE of  $ 6.69\% $ , leading to an increase of almost  $ 31.43\% $ . Furthermore, the PSCs with QDs passivation had better water resistance. This work provides a promising strategy to enhance both the stability and efficiency of carbon-based PSCs.

$ \mathrm{CsPbI_xBr_{3 - x}} $   $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  has better long-term stability in air condition with an appropriate band gap of  $ 2.05\mathrm{eV} $ , which is suitable for commercial applications [17-19]. At present, the efficiency of carbon-based PSCs with pristine  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  is still very low [20,21]. The possible explanation, resulting from poor PCE, could (1) The low energy level alignment matching between perovskite layer and carbon electrode is not conducive to carrier migration and leads to low  $ \mathrm{J_{sc}} $ . (2) There are defects in the perovskite layer, and the carbon paste is connected with the electron transport layer (ETL), resulting in the reduction of  $ \mathrm{V_{oc}} $ . Therefore, the interface between the perovskite layer and the top electrode is needed to enhance the charge carrier mobility and passivate the defects. Lv et, al [22], introduced a simple substrate pre-heating method to improve the uniformity of the perovskite film. They also used inorganic CuSCN as HTM to improve the efficient hole extraction. The fabricated carbon-based  $ \mathrm{FA}_{0.83}\mathrm{MA}_{0.17}\mathrm{PbI}_{2.49}\mathrm{Br}_{0.51} $  PSC device achieved a high PCE of  $ 14.87\% $ . Cao et, al [23]. fabricated low-temperature paintable carbon electrode-based organic PSCs with CZTS nanoparticles as a HTL. The PCE is  $ 12.53\% $ , and the device has negligible hysteresis and excellent long-time stability. Therefore, interface engineering is an effective method to improve the properties of carbon-based PSCs [24,25]. Nevertheless, the impact of the interface engineering on tuning the

Recently, all-inorganic, low dimensional perovskite quantum dots (QDs) have emerged as a promising interface engineering layer in PSCs due to their tunable energy levels and superior stability   $  [26–28]  $  . Liu et al.   $  [29]  $   introduced   $  \alpha  $  -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   quantum dots (QDs) to passivate the   $  (\mathrm{FAPbI}_{3})_{0.85}(\mathrm{MAPbBr}_{3})_{0.15}  $   film, resulting in the PCE increasing from 15.17% to 18.56% and improving the moisture stability. However, such QDs are unstable because the bonding between the long-chain capping ligands and QDs surface is dynamic, which is undesirable for fast carrier mobility and preparation for high-performance photovoltaic devices   $  [30,31]  $  . Therefore, ligand passivation engineering is also vital if an efficient and stable QDs interface is obtained.

Herein, we introduce bidentate ligand-passivated  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  QDs as an interface layer to enhance both the surface quality of perovskite film and the efficiency of PSCs. The  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  QDs layer can adjust the energy alignment and retard the carrier recombination. We further explore a bidentate ligand, namely 4-mercaptobenzoic acid (4-MBA), to modify the QDs surface. The surface defects and charge carrier mobility of QDs are improved, and also the binding force between QDs surface and ligand becomes stronger. Meanwhile, the QDs-passivated PSC achieves a PCE of  $ 6.69\% $  under one-sun illumination, demonstrating an increase of  $ 31.43\% $  compared with the PSC without the QDs layer. Our results indicate that this is a promising strategy to improve the performance of carbon-based PSCs.

# 2. Experimental section

# 2.1.Materials

CsI (99.98%),   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (99.99%), and dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) were purchased from Polymer Light Technology Inc..   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloidal solution (15% in   $  H_{2}O  $  ) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. Conductive carbon paste was purchased from Shanghai MaterWin New Materials Co., Ltd. Cesium carbonate (  $  Cs_{2}CO_{3}  $  , 99.995%, metal basis), Oleic acid (OA, technical grade 90%), oleylamine (OAm, technical grade 70%), 1-octadecene (ODE, technical grade 90%), methyl acetate (EA,), toluene (TL) and Mercaptobenzoic acid (4-MBA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All of them used as received without further purification.

# 2.2. Synthesis of colloidal   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   QDs

$ \mathrm{Cs_2CO_3} $  (0.407 g), OA (1.2 mL) and ODE (15 mL) were added to a 50 mL three-neck flack. Degassed under vacuum at  $ 120^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 0.5\mathrm{h} $  and then raised to  $ 150^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  under  $ \mathbf{N}_2 $  as Cs-oleate.  $ \mathrm{PbBr_2} $  (0.0404 g),  $ \mathrm{PbI_2} $  (0.1245 g), ODE (10 mL), OA (1 mL) and OAm (1 mL) were loaded in a 25 mL three-neck flack. Degassed under vacuum for  $ 0.5\mathrm{h} $  at  $ 120^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  and then filled with  $ \mathbf{N}_2 $ . After the solid become dissolve completely, the solution was heated to  $ 170^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . Then,  $ 0.8\mathrm{mL} $  Cs-oleate was quickly injected. 5s later, the reaction mixture was cool down through an ice bath. The reaction product was separated by adding  $ 20\mathrm{mL} $  EA and centrifuged at  $ 8000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 10\mathrm{min} $ . The precipitate was redispersed in  $ 2.0\mathrm{mL} $  of TL.

The  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  QDs solution  $ (20\mathrm{mg / mL}) $  was divided into two vials: one was regarded as a control sample, the other one used as a modified sample. In order to enabled the passivation process carry out adequately, the saturation solution concentration of 4-MBA was prepared by adding  $ 10\mathrm{mg} $  of 4-MBA powder into modified sample. As 4-MBA has a tiny solubility in the non-polar solvent, the concentration of 4-MBA in TL is controlled by its limited solubility. The mixture was completely mixed for  $ 2\mathrm{h} $  at room temperature. After treatment, the sample was separated by centrifugation for  $ 10\mathrm{min} $  at  $ 8000~\mathrm{rpm} $  to remove the excess 4-MBA powder, and the supernatant was diluted in TL with different concentrations.

# 2.3. Device fabrication

FTO glasses  $ (2\times 2\mathrm{cm}^2 $  in size) were cleaned with deionized water, acetone, isopropanol, and ethanol in an ultrasonic cleaner. The substrate was then treated in oxygen plasma for  $ 10\mathrm{min} $  to further clean the substrate surface.

The   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloid precursor was diluted by   $  H_{2}O  $   to 2.67% (v/v) and was spin-coated onto ITO/glass substrates at 2000 rpm for 30 s, and then baked on a hot plate in ambient air at 150 °C for 30 min. Oxygen plasma treatment for 10min also needed to improve the hydrophilicity of the substrate.

The perovskite precursor suitable for one-step processing was prepared by adding 440 mg PbBr $ _2 $  and 312 mg CsI into 1 mL DMSO, and then stirred at 60 °C until completely dissolved. The CsPbIBr $ _2 $  perovskite thin film was then prepared by spin coating at 800 rpm for 5 s, and 2800 rpm for 45 s onto the SnO $ _2 $  layer. The obtained film was placed for 5 min, followed by thermal annealing at 280 °C for 10 min. Then the CsPbI $ _2 $ Br QDs solution was spin-coated onto the above CsPbIBr $ _2 $  perovskite layer at 600 rpm for 6 s, and 3200 rpm for 45 s followed by annealing at 280 °C for 5min. Finally, commercial carbon paste was deposited by the doctor-blading method, which was followed by a heat treatment at 100 °C for 15 min on a hot plate in a dry nitrogen atmosphere.

All solutions used were filtered through a  $ 0.45\mu \mathrm{m} $  pore PTFE filter before use and stored in the glove box.

# 2.4. Measurement and characterization

The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were characterized by Tecnai G2 F20 U-TWIN at 300 kV. The atomic force microscope (AFM) images were acquired by Dimension 3100 Veeco. The Surface morphologies (SEM) of the films were obtained by S-4800 at a 5 kV accelerating voltage. Absorbance spectra (UV–vis) were acquired by PerkinElmer UV WinLab Lambda 35. F-7000 FL The photoluminescence spectra (PL) were measured by Spectrophotometer. The time-resolved PL spectra (TRPL) were obtained by Edinburgh Instruments FLS920 fluorescence spectrometer. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were characterized using EscaLab Xi+ with helium gas admitted employing the HeI (21.22 eV) emission line. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded using Rigaku D/MAX 2500 using Cu K radiation. Fourier transform infrared spectra (FTIR) were performed on Nicolet 8700 spectrometer. The J-V curves of the devices were measured by Enlitech SS-F7-3A under AM 1.5G (100 mW/cm²) illumination. A black cardboard mask with a window area of 0.20 cm² was clipped onto the glass side to define the active area of the cell. The External quantum efficiency spectra (EQE) were measured with Enlitech QE-R containing a xenon lamp, monochromator, Si detector, and dual-channel power meter.

# 3. Results and discussion

4-MBA (Fig. S1) has been regarded as a bidentate ligand in chelation for its strong coordination capability. Both the carboxyl and thiol of 4-MBA can coordinate with   $  Pb^{2+}  $  . In comparison with the single carboxylic ligand, the bidentate chelating ligand can separately bind to two surface-exposed   $  Pb^{2+}  $  , thus improving binding strength and reducing the density of the defects.

To explore the influence of 4-MBA on the morphology features and crystal structure of  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  QDs, TEM and XRD were carried out. Fig. 1 (a and b) shows the TEM images of pristine QDs and modified QDs. Both QDs exhibit cubic shape, with an average size of 10.95 and  $ 11.17\mathrm{nm} $  (Figure S2, Supporting Information). and high-resolution TEM reveals that QDs have a lattice distance of  $ 0.60\mathrm{nm} $ , matching well the spacing of the (100) crystal planes in the cubic  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  crystal structure. The XRD patterns of QDs with modification belong to the cubic phase of  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $ . (Figure S3), suggesting that the 4-MBA do not incorporate into the crystal structure of  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  QDs.

The measured absorption spectra and PL intensity QDs are shown in Fig. 1(c and d). There is no significant change in the absorption spectrum for both QDs. However, the PL intensity of modified QDs is obviously enhanced, which can be attributed to the decrease of the defects on QDs surface after the ligand exchange.

In order to characterize the surface ligands, the FTIR spectroscopy of QDs were measured, as shown in Fig. 2 (a). The peaks at   $  2923 \, cm^{-1}  $  ,   $  1461 \, cm^{-1}  $   and   $  1384 \, cm^{-1}  $   represent the C–H stretching and bending, while the peak at   $  1602 \, cm^{-1}  $   is ascribed to the N–H stretching, and the peak at   $  1735 \, cm^{-1}  $   represent to the COO- stretching. These results demonstrate that OA and OLA ligands exist on the surface of QDs. One additional peak at   $  2348 \, cm^{-1}  $   could be observed for modified QDs, consistent with the –SH bond of a 4-MBA molecule, indicating the existence of –SH ligands on the surface of QDs. We also used XPS spectra to further investigate the binding condition of ligands on QDs surface. As shown in Fig. 2 (b), there is no S signal for the pristine QDs, but the S 2p peak appears for the modified QDs (167.2 eV), which is attributed to the existence of 4-MBA. Fig. 2 (c) shows the peaks of Pb 4f (137.0 eV and 141.9 eV) shift slightly to lower binding energies by about 0.2 eV after 4-MBA treatment, which can be attributed to the increasing of electrons surrounding under-coordinated Pb atoms. These results verify the larger binding strength of the ligands of 4-MBA with Pb on the surface of the modified QDs.

Defects can act as pathways for the transmission of oxygen and water molecules in ambient atmosphere. Besides, moisture and oxygen also serve as the ion transport channel, leading to the degradation of the QDs. The photograph obviously exhibits the enhancement in the environmental stability of the modified QDs (Fig. 2 (d)). The modified QDs film with fewer defects still shows a bright color after  $ 10\mathrm{h} $  in air condition. In contrast, the pristine QDs film turns colorless due to the serious aggregate growth of QDs, caused by the penetration of  $ \mathrm{O}_2 $  and  $ \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} $ . The TEM images of degradation QDs are shown in Figure S4.

We fabricated all inorganic PSCs with a normal structure (substrate/FTO/SnO $ _2 $ /CsPbIBr $ _2 $ /CsPbI $ _2 $ Br QDs/Carbon, as illustrated in Fig. 3 (a)). The SnO $ _2 $  solution deposited on an FTO electrode was made as the ETL, the CsPbIBr $ _2 $  film was prepared by one-step deposition, the interfacial layer was fabricated by spin-coating CsPbI $ _2 $ Br QDs on the top of the perovskite layer, and carbon was used both as HTL and counter electrode. Moreover, the energy level diagram of the device structure is presented in Fig. 3 (b), determined from Figure S5. The QDs layer can form a favorable energy level alignment at the CsPbIBr $ _2 $ /carbon interface, which can obviously improve the efficiency of hole extraction from perovskite film into carbon electrode, thus contributing to the lower recombination of electrons and holes.

XRD of the   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   films was measured to identify the change in the crystal structure. As is shown in Figure S6. The peaks at 14.9°, 21.2°, 30.1° and 37.7° are assigned to the (100), (110), (200), and (211) planes of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $  , which is well-indexed to the standard cubic   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   phase. It is clear that perovskite film with QDs treatment have inconspicuous grain boundaries and more uniform morphology than the pristine perovskite film. It can be speculated that the QDs can penetrate into the bulk of the perovskite film through the pinholes and cracks, leading to a lower density of trap states. We further took the AFM to examine the surface roughness of the perovskite film. As shown in Fig. 4 (b), the root mean square roughness value of the  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  film passivated by modified QDs is  $ 26.8\mathrm{nm} $ , compared to the pure perovskite film of  $ 30.7\mathrm{nm} $ . It demonstrates the deposition of the QDs can cover the uneven surface of perovskite film, thus decreasing its roughness.

Moreover, the water contact angle of QDs-passivated perovskite film is also obviously increased, from 54.6° to 81.0°, indicating the excellent ability of water resistance. The possible mechanism, could (1) 4-MBA ligands attached to the QDs surface can passive trap defects and impeding the water adsorbing onto the QDs surface. (2) QDs can penetrate into grain boundaries of perovskite film and decrease the traps and pinholes at the grain surface, thus effectively preventing the water from diffusing into the perovskite film.

Fig. 5 (a) shows absorption spectra of perovskite film with and without QDs passivation. The  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  film absorbs from the UV light through the visible spectrum to  $ 589~\mathrm{nm} $ , while the perovskite film with  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  QDs shows significantly increased absorption, particularly in the wavelength range from 590 to  $ 600~\mathrm{nm} $ . Urbach energy  $ (\mathrm{E_U}) $  for both perovskite films was calculated based on UV (Fig. 5 (b)). It is noteworthy that the  $ \mathrm{E_U} $  of perovskite was decreased from  $ 0.477\mathrm{eV} $  to  $ 0.472\mathrm{eV} $  after the modification of QDs, which could be attributed to the reduction of defect densities.

The PL spectrum for perovskite film and QDs-passivated perovskite film were also analyzed. As shown in Fig. 5 (c), perovskite film with QDs displayed higher PL intensity, certificating lower surface defects. QDs can act as a hole acceptor, leading to a reduced quenching and suppressed the non-radiative recombination process of the  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  film. The TRPL measurement of the perovskite film with and without QDs-passivated were further investigated (Fig. 5 (d)). It is apparently evident that the perovskite film with QDs exhibits longer decay (1.34 ns) compared to the perovskite film without QDs (0.75 ns) (Table S1). These results indicated that the perovskite film with QDs treatment has a better ability to suppress the recombination process than the pure perovskite film, which is consistent with the PL spectra.

With the concentration of modified QDs increased from 0 to  $ 15\mathrm{mg / mL} $ , the PCE of the PSC shows first increasing and then decreasing. There are too many nanocrystals aggregate on the surface of  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  bulk film when the concentration of QDs reaches  $ 15\mathrm{mg / mL} $ , which enhances the roughness of the  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  film (Figure S7). It is noted that the parameters of QDs-passivated PSC are still very low due to the various defects that exist on the surface and grain boundaries of the perovskite film. In future research, the new multitype defect passivation methods, the synthesis technology of core-shell QDs, the mechanism of perovskite crystallization, and the technique to prepare density carbon electrode could be necessarily investigated for enhancing the performance of inorganic PSCs.

The long-term stability of the PSCs under different conditions was

also investigated. As is shown in Fig. 6 (c), The unencapsulated PSC devices with and without QDs passivation exhibited almost 10–15% PCE degradation after 14 days in the   $  N_{2}  $   atmosphere. However, when the devices stored at relative humidity of 75% in ambient condition, the PCE of the device covered with QDs dropped by 55% of the initial value. In comparison, the control device decreased by nearly 90% of its initial efficiency in 60 min (Fig. 6 (d)). It is obviously that the device with QDs had better stability against moisture. The modified QDs could passivate the pinholes and defects at the boundaries and surfaces of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   film, which prevented the transmission of oxygen and water molecules into the perovskite film, leading to the suppression of ion migration.

# 4. Conclusions

In conclusion, we introduced   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   QDs as an interface to passivate the   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   bulk film. The surface defects and charge carrier mobility of QDs were enhanced by bidentate ligand modification. It was found that the optimized energy level alignment at the interface of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}/CsPbI_{2}Br  $   QDs resulted in the promoted hole extraction. The QDs-passivated PSC achieved a PCE of 6.69%, leading to an increase of almost 31.43% compared with the PSCs without QDs layer. Furthermore, the PSCs with the QDs passivation had better water resistance. Our results provided a promising strategy to improve both the efficiency and stability of carbon-based PSCs.
