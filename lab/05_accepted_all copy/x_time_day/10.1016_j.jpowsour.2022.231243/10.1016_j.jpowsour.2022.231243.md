# GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

# ABSTRACT

Because morphology and defect of organic-inorganic halide perovskite film is very sensitive to moisture and oxygen, it is important to develop humidity tolerant air-processes that produce high quality film with minimal defects for the successful commercialization of perovskite solar cells (PeSCs). As an attempt to optimize perovskite film in the air, we develop a cyclic quaternary ammonium chloride  $ (N $ -(2-hydroxyethyl)- $ N $ -methylpyrrolidinium chloride, M-P chloride) with a pentagonal ring and hydroxyl functional group as an effective additive. The Cl $ ^{-} $ anions in M-P chloride increase the crystallinity and the grain size by retarding nucleation and growth rate. After crystal growth, bulky M-P chloride diffuses out and remains on the grain boundaries and surface of the perovskite, and thus passivates both types of defects. Furthermore, since the M-P chloride may act as a moisture absorber, the intermediate phase of the perovskite can be protected from the moisture. As a result, this strategy can not only modulate the crystallinity and film morphology but also passivate the defects, leading to enhanced efficiency and stability, regardless of humidity. In particular, even at high humidity of  $ \sim 77\% $ , PeSC with M-P chloride exhibits  $ 18.20\% $  power conversion efficiency (PCE) without any hysteresis behavior.

# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic halide perovskite has been considered as one of the most promising light absorbing materials in the next generation solar cell technology because of their broad tunability and superior optical and electrical properties such as large absorption coefficient, controllable direct band gap, low exciton binding energy, high charge carrier mobility, and long charge carrier diffusion length   $  [1-3]  $  . Since the first report on PeSCs by Miyasaka in 2009, PCE has dramatically increased from 3.8% to 25.5% through the design of perovskite composition, optimization of film morphology and device architecture, and control of various defects   $  [4-7]  $  . Because the overall device performance extremely depends on the film manufacturing process, tremendous efforts have been devoted to obtain high quality pinhole-free perovskite film with less grain boundaries and large grain size   $  [8-10]  $  . Control of intermediate phase and crystallization rate by using co-solvent and additives, two-step deposition process, and antisolvent-induced precipitation method have shown improved perovskite quality and device performance   $  [11-15]  $  .

On one hand, like other ionic materials, perovskite materials have considerable either negatively charged defects such as under-coordinated I $ ^{-} $ , Pb-I anti site defects, and methylammonium (MA $ ^{+} $ ) vacancies or positively charged defects such as undercoordinated Pb $ ^{2+} $  ions and I vacancies in the bulk, grain boundaries, and surface of the perovskite film [16]. These defects result in charge carrier recombination and serious hysteresis phenomena, which adversely affect the device performance. In addition, water and oxygen can easily penetrate into defects to accelerate the decomposition of perovskite film, inducing poor long-term stability of device [17–19]. Various passivation methods based on coordinate bonding and ionic bonding have been tried to de-activate defects [16]. Lewis acid such as PCBM acting as an electron acceptor passivates negatively charged defects by forming a coordination with halide ions [20,21]. Lewis base such as thiophene or pyridine containing N, S, and O functionalities can combine with under-coordinated Pb $ ^{2+} $  ions to passivate positively charged defects [22–24]. Metal ions (e.g. Na $ ^{+} $  and K $ ^{+} $ ) or cationic organic molecules with ammonium functional groups (-NH $ ^{3+} $ ) passivate negatively charged defects through ionic bonding [25–27]. The Cl $ ^{-} $ , excess iodide, and anionic carboxylate groups (-COO $ ^{-} $ ) effectively passivate positively charged defects through ionic bonding [28]. Most reports on passivation primarily aim at passivating one type of defects, either positively- or negatively charged defects. More recently, Xiaopeng Zheng et al. first reported that choline chloride, containing positively charged quaternary ammonium and negatively charged chloride, can passivate two types of charged defects simultaneously [29]. Besides this, migration of I- ions having low activation energy causes an undesirable reaction between perovskite layer and interfacial layer or metal electrode, which promotes degradation of PeSCs [30,31]. Therefore, the suppression of ion migration is also an important issue in terms of long-term stability. Several groups reported that the strong binding between hydroxyl group and iodide ion is possible to effectively suppress the migration of ions in perovskite layer to obtain more stable and efficient PeSCs [32,33].

Although high efficiency has been achieved by optimizing film formation and passivation techniques, most of above mentioned approaches were carried out in oxygen- and moisture-free nitrogen filled glove boxes due to perovskite's inherent sensitivity to oxygen, heat and moisture. In order to realize practical application of PeSCs, it is important to develop a process suitable for producing defect and pin-hole free perovskite films in the ambient conditions   $  [34–36]  $  . In this study, we demonstrate an effective strategy to passivate both types of defects as well as to produce high quality pin-hole free film with large grain size in air-processed PeSCs using N-(2-hydroxyethyl)-N-methylpyrrolidinium chloride (M-P chloride. When M-P chloride is added to perovskite precursor, the Cl  $  ^{-}  $   anions in M-P chloride may induce retarding of nucleation and growth rate during perovskite film formation. Here, the M-P chloride is not incorporated into the perovskite lattice due to its bulk

structure and diffuses out to the grain boundaries. After thermal annealing, bulky M-P chloride still remains on the grain boundaries and surface of the perovskite, and thus passivates defects and blocks oxygen and moisture from the outside. At that same time, the strong hydrogen bond between hydroxyl group in M-P chloride and iodide ion of perovskite precursor can inhibit the iodide ion migration and prevent film degradation. In addition, since the M-P chloride can act as a moisture absorber, the intermediate phase of the perovskite precursor can be protected from the moisture. As a result, this strategy can not only modulate the crystallinity and film morphology but also passivate the defects, leading to enhanced efficiency and stability, regardless of humidity.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1. N-(2-hydroxyethyl)-N-methylpyrrolidinium chloride (M-P chloride) preparation

3.81 g of N-methylpyrrolidine (Alfa Aesar, 99%) and 3.00 g of 2-chloroethanol (Sigma Aldrich, 99%) were added to 10 mL of acetonitrile (MeCN, SAMCHUN, 99.5%) and refluxed for 24 h. After cooling the mixture at atmospheric condition, the MeCN was removed through an evaporator. To remove residual N-methylpyrrolidine, the salt is dissolved in MeOH (SAMCHUN, 99.5%), precipitated in diethyl ether (SAMCHUN, 99%) and filtered. After drying in a vacuum oven, 3.8 g of M-P chloride was obtained as a white solid. The product is very soluble in water and also able to absorb moisture quickly.

# 2.2. Device fabrication

ITO/glass substrates (AMG–Tech) were cleaned with detergent and in an ultrasonic bath of acetone and 2-propanol for 10 min. After drying in an100 °C oven, substrates were treated with UV ozone plasma for 20 min to improve wettability. NiO precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 0.0248 g nickel acetate tetrahydrate (Sigma Aldrich, 98%) in 1 mL ethanol (Alfa Aesar) mixed with 6 vol% ethanolamine (Sigma-Aldrich). This solution was spin-coated onto a prepared ITO glass substrate at 3000 rpm for 40 s, followed by annealing at 350 °C for 30 min. For the preparation of perovskite layer with M-P chloride,   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $   (MAI, Greatcell Solar) and lead iodide (  $  PbI_{2}  $  , 99.999%, TCI) with a 1:1 M ratio were dissolved in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, anhydrous, 99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich) with the concentration of 45 wt % and then small amount of synthesized M-P chloride (10 mg/mL) were added. This solution was deposited onto a NiO layer by spin coating at 5000 rpm for 45 s with 5 s of ramping speed. 5 s after the speed reached at 5000 rpm, 0.7 mL of anti-solvent (toluene:ethyl acetate mixture with a 1:1 vol ratio) was dropped to control morphology. Spin coated perovskite film was thermally annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. For comparison, a reference perovskite solution without M-P chloride was also fabricated under the same condition. All the processes have been carried out in the ambient conditions, temperature of ~21 °C and humidity from 40% to 78%. Next, PCBM dissolved in chlorobenzene (20 mg/mL) were spin coated onto the perovskite films at 1000 rpm for 60 s. Bathocuproine (BCP 3 nm, 0.5 Å/s)/silver (Ag 80 nm, 1.0 Å/s) top electrodes were thermally evaporated with an area of 0.0464 cm² an under high vacuum at a pressure of 10⁻⁷ Torr.

# 2.3. Characterization

The J-V curves of PeSCs were measured by Keithley 2400 equipment under standard solar irradiation (AM 1.5 G 1sun condition). The light intensity was calibrated by neutral density (ND) filter wheel from Newport. After the J-V measurement was completed, the PCBM layer and the metal top electrode were removed using chlorobenzene to investigate the relationship between the morphology of the perovskite and performance. # 3. Result and discussion

As mentioned, because the crystallinity and surface morphology of the perovskite film are very sensitive to moisture in the air, it is important to develop humidity tolerant processes that produce a high quality film with minimal defects to accelerate commercialization of PeSCs. To optimize perovskite film in the air, we developed a bulky cyclic ammonium chloride with pentagonal ring structure as an effective additive, which expected to be more stable physically, chemically, and electrochemically than simple linear structures. Fig. 1 shows the molecular structure of M-P chloride used in this study and detailed understanding on the formation of perovskite layer with/without M-P chloride. Among various device structures such as mesostructured  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ -based device and planar heterojunction devices including n-i-p and p-i-n structure, p-i-n structured planar heterojunction PeSCs consisting of indium tin oxide (ITO)/NiO HTL/CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ PbI $ _3 $  perovskite layer/[6, 6]-phenyl- $ \mathrm{C}_{61} $  butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM)/BCP-Ag have been studied. In case of typical  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  perovskite layer starting with MAI and  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ ,  $ \mathrm{MA}^+ $  diffused into the  $ [\mathrm{Pb}_6]^{4-} $  octahedral layer to form the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  phase [37,38]. perovskite precursor, a rich chloride intermediate phase is formed by  $ \mathrm{Cl^-} $  in M-P chloride, causing the crystallization kinetics to change from the precursor-perovskite to the precursor-intermediate phase [39-41]. As a result, a high-quality film having a large grain size and excellent crystallinity can be obtained by retarding nucleation and crystal growth rate.

Because bulky M-P chloride cannot incorporate into the perovskite lattice during crystal growth from intermediate phase, M-P chloride diffuses out and remains on the perovskite surface and grain boundaries after crystal growth. The M-P chloride is ionic salt and it does not show boiling phase transition. Hence, its vapor pressure would be very negligible and vaporization is not possible at ambient condition. To confirm thermal stability information of M-P chloride, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was carried out. As shown in Fig. S1, the M-P chloride showed their thermal decompositions at  $ \sim 270^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . In other words, M-P chloride has no melting transition before the thermal decomposition. Hence, we conclude that M-P chloride remains on the final perovskite film after thermal annealing at  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 10\mathrm{min} $ . Thus, passivating the defect at grain boundary and surface of completed perovskite film by M-P chloride can be expected. As shown in Fig. 1, quaternary ammonium and  $ \mathrm{Cl^-} $  anions in M-P chloride can effectively passivate both positively- and negatively-charged defects simultaneously. Negatively charged defects such as undercoordinated I-, Pb-I anti-site, and  $ \mathrm{MA^{+}} $  vacancies can be passivate by quaternary ammonium ion  $ (-\mathrm{NR}_{4}^{+}) $  and positively charged defects such as undercoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  and  $ \mathrm{I^{-}} $  vacancies can be passivated by chloride anions  $ (\mathrm{Cl^-}) $  and also hydroxyl groups (-OH). At the same time, hydrogen bond of hydroxyl groups (-OH) in M-P chloride with iodide ion can effectively inhibit the migration of iodide ions and thus reduced hysteresis and improved stability of device can be expected. Moreover, because M-P chloride can act as a moisture absorbent, which can easily absorb water from the external environment and block contact with the moisture during the perovskite film manufacturing process, pinhole-free uniform

In addition, bulky M-P chloride, which is more resistant to high moisture, diffuses out and remains at grain boundaries or surface to block external moisture and prevent decomposition of the perovskite film.

# 4. Conclusions

In order to produce high quality pin-hole free film with large grain size and passivate both types of defects in the air-processed perovskite layer,  $ N $ -(2-hydroxyethyl)- $ N $ -methylpyrrolidinium chloride (M-P chloride) with bulky pentagonal ring has been applied. The  $ \mathrm{Cl}^- $  anions in M-P chloride can increase the crystallinity and grain size by retarding the nucleation and growth rate. The M-P chloride is not incorporated into the perovskite lattice due to its bulk structure during film formation and diffuses out to the grain boundaries or surfaces. After thermal annealing, bulky M-P chloride still remains on the grain boundaries and surface of the perovskite, and thus passivates defects and blocks moisture from the outside. Furthermore, since the M-P chloride can act as a moisture absorber, the intermediate phase of the perovskite precursor can be protected from the moisture.

This strategy significantly enhanced PCE up to  $ \sim 19.53\% $ , comparing to reference cells showing  $ 16.25\% $ . In particular, even at high humidity of  $ \sim 77\% $ , PeSC with M-P chloride exhibited PCE of  $ 18.20\% $  without any hysteresis behavior. M-P chloride based PeSCs showed superior stability, maintaining more than  $ 80\% $  of its initial PCE for 8 days. As a result, this strategy can not only modulate the crystallinity and film morphology but also passivate the defects, resulting in enhanced efficiency and stability, regardless of humidity.
