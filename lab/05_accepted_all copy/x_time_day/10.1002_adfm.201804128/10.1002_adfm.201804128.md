# Efficient and Stable Nonfullerene-Graded Heterojunction Inverted Perovskite Solar Cells with Inorganic   $  Ga_{2}O_{3}  $   Tunneling Protective Nanolayer

Junjie Ma, Meijuan Zheng, Cong Chen, Ziqiang Zhu, Xiaolu Zheng, Zhiliang Chen, Yaxiong Guo, Chang Liu,* Yanfa Yan,* and Guojia Fang*

Inverted planar perovskite solar cells (PSCs) exhibit advantages in terms of simple device fabrication, good reproducibility, and less hysteresis. However, there still remain challenges to passivate the trap states within the perovskite film and reduce interface instability arising from penetration of moisture and the diffusion of metal electrode. In this work, perovskite passivation is reported using gradually distributed nonfullerene electron-rich Lewis bases small molecule IDIC (a  $ \pi $ -conjugated Lewis base: indacenodithiophene end-capped with 1.1-dicyanomethylene-3-indanone), which is realized through an antisolvent dropping process. Additionally, an inorganic wide band gap  $ \mathrm{Ga}_2\mathrm{O}_3 $  is introduced as a tunneling nanolayer into interface between the metal electrode and electron transport layer that effectively passivate interface and mitigate moisture and metal ion diffusion. These treatments enable the fabrication of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $ -based inverted PSCs with excellent power conversion efficiencies up to  $ 19.86\% $  as well as enhanced ambient stability. This work provides new strategies to improve the performance and stability for the inverted PSCs.

It was reported that defects at interfaces and grain boundaries (GBs) of perovskite films give rise to the charge-recombination loss, which is mainly responsible for the low performance of PSCs.  $  ^{[5,12]}  $   Moreover, these defects can make the device prone to instability caused by ion

migration, heat, and moisture.  $  ^{[6,7]}  $   Passivating these defects in perovskite plays a key role for improving the performance of PSCs. Fullerene has shown passivation effects for perovskite thin films. For example, perovskite–fullerene bulk solar cells showed improved the photoelectron collection and reduced recombination loss.  $  ^{[8]}  $   Recently, nonfullerene acceptors as the alternative to fullerene acceptors have attracted increasing attention in both polymer and perovskite solar cells. Zhan and co-workers demonstrated that nonfullerene acceptors present good light harvesting capability, high mobility, facile energy level modulation, and good stability in polymer solar cells.  $  ^{[9]}  $   Zhou and co-workers employed nonfullerene IDIC (a   $  \pi  $  -conjugated Lewis base: indacenodithiophene end-capped with 1.1-dicyanomethylene-3-indanone) as electron transport material in n-i-p PSCs and demonstrated that IDIC can facilitate electron extraction due to its high carrier mobility.  $  ^{[10]}  $   Huang and co-workers introduced IDIC as an interlayer between the perovskite and   $  C_{60}  $   that realized trap passivation on the perovskite surface.  $  ^{[11]}  $   At the same time, they demonstrated a longer   $  \pi-\pi  $   stacking coherence length than [6,6]-phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM), which is expected to give rise to a small energy disorder of electronic states. However, few studies have aimed at fabricating nonfullerene-graded-heterojunction planar PSCs based on p-i-n structure.

# 1. Introduction

Organic–inorganic metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have gained great attention in recent years due to their

impressive photovoltaic performance.  $  ^{[1]}  $   The record power conversion efficiency (PCE) is rapidly rising and now reached over 22%, which is attributed to the intriguing optoelectronic properties of perovskite films such as the super long carrier diffusion length and high optical absorption coefficient.  $  ^{[2]}  $   The p–i–n planar PSCs show many advantages, such as simple device fabrication process, good reproducibility, and small hysteresis. However, their record PCE still falls behind the PSCs based on n–i–p structure, particularly for the methylammonium lead trihalide (MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  )-based PSCs.  $  ^{[3,4]}  $

# 2.2. Passivation Effect of Electron-Rich Lewis Bases on Perovskites through Graded Heterojunction

Undercoordinated Pb ions are frequently formed on the surface and at GBs due to the low stability of perovskite, where the loss of iodine leads to vacancy sites causing trap states.  $  ^{[5]}  $   These defect states which induce charge recombination constitute a major energy loss and then deteriorate the device performance. Passivating these trap states is critical to the enhancement of device performance. These trap states in the perovskite with lots of positive charge centers behave like Lewis acid, which would then create favorable conditions for coordination or bond formation with electron-rich Lewis bases. IDIC as electron-rich Lewis bases which possesses electron-withdrawing groups of carbonyl (C=O) and cyano (C≡N) and thiophene can effectively passivate the Lewis acid traps.  $  ^{[11]}  $   The molecular structure of IDIC is depicted in Figure 2b. As shown in Figure 2a, trap states with positive charges are neutralized via coordination bonding with IDIC. It is worth noting that the trap state passivation can occur not only at surface but also at GBs within the whole perovskite as the IDIC can diffuse through the films with a gradient distribution. An optical image of IDIC dissolved in ethyl acetate solution with a concentration of 1 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   is displayed in Figure 2c, indicative of a good solubility of IDIC in ethyl acetate.

To understand the effect of IDIC on device performance, photovoltaic parameters were measured after the treatment with various concentrations of IDIC for a device structure of fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)/Li-NiO/MAPbI $ _3 $ -IDIC/PCBM/BCP/Ag. Box plots of the ten devices performance parameters with different concentration of IDIC in the EA and the corresponding detailed parameters are summarized in Figure S6 and Table S2 in the Supporting Information, respectively. It is clear that the performance shows a significant increase with the use of IDIC comparing with that of control group.

# 2.3. Role of   $  Ga_{2}O_{3}  $   Tunneling Nanolayer in the Charge Carrier Transportation and Recombination Suppression

Interfacial recombination of photogenerated carriers is one of the major loss factors that deteriorate the device performance.   $  Ga_{2}O_{3}  $   with the fundamental properties like allowing the efficient electrons tunneling injection can be considered

for the selection of an effective blocking layer.  $  ^{[27]}  $   To investigate the effect of inserting   $  Ga_{2}O_{3}  $   tunneling nanolayer on photovoltaic performance, we fabricated devices with a structure of FTO/Li-NiO/MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  /PCBM/BCP/Ga  $  _{2}  $  O  $  _{3}  $  /Ag. The box plots and corresponding photovoltaic parameters of samples that deposited with different cycles of   $  Ga_{2}O_{3}  $   film are shown in Figure S9 and Table S3 in the Supporting Information. As compared to the control group, not only the PCE, but also the   $  J_{sc}  $   and FF, are improved after the insertion of   $  Ga_{2}O_{3}  $   film. To elucidate the carrier transport and recombination process, we examined the electrical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) for the devices with or without   $  Ga_{2}O_{3}  $   film.  $  ^{[28]}  $   As shown in Figure 4a, the interfacial recombination resistance (  $  R_{rec}  $  ) of the PSCs can be calculated from the radius of the semicircle in the low-frequency range. Incomplete semicircle in the high-frequency range is ascribed to be contact resistance (  $  R_{co}  $  ).  $  ^{[35]}  $   The enlarge region in high-frequency range can be found in Figure S10 in the Supporting Information. The parameters of   $  R_{rec}  $   and   $  R_{co}  $   from EIS measured are shown in Table S4 in the Supporting Information. Obviously, the PSCs with   $  Ga_{2}O_{3}  $   exhibit higher   $  R_{rec}  $   and lower   $  R_{co}  $   indicating that more efficient charge transfer and lower recombination rate at the interface. The EIS analysis supports that   $  Ga_{2}O_{3}  $   can be a good tunneling layer with a wide band gap characteristic to enhance charge carrier transport and suppress the interfacial charge carrier recombination reaction. These results also are observed in the dark J-V curves (Figure 4b). Enhanced injection current at a large voltage bias for the device with   $  Ga_{2}O_{3}  $   suggests the faster electron extraction from perovskite to electrode that avoids the carrier accumulation at the electrode interface. According to the literature reports,  $  ^{[3]}  $   poor electron injection and extraction are originated from the big band offset barrier between the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital of the ETL and the Fermi level of the high-work function metal. Meanwhile, in the reverse bias region, the device with   $  Ga_{2}O_{3}  $   film possesses smaller leakage current, confirming that the   $  Ga_{2}O_{3}  $   film can suppress the leakage pathway and retard the carrier recombination between the interface of ETL and metal electrode.

To gain further insight into the blocking effects of   $  Ga_{2}O_{3}  $   layer over the metal electrode, we conducted J-V measurements of the electron only devices with structures of FTO/  $  SnO_{2}/BCP/Ag  $   and FTO/  $  SnO_{2}/BCP/Ga_{2}O_{3}/Ag  $  . From Figure 4c, we can see that both devices present Ohmic-type contact with linear J-V curve. The device with   $  Ga_{2}O_{3}  $   layer has higher curve slope corresponding to the smaller contact resistance and smaller barrier for electrons injection. This observation suggests that   $  Ga_{2}O_{3}  $   as n-type semiconductor can form ideal Ohmic-type contact to metal, which could be mainly derived by the tunneling effect and good film quality with less surface traps. This result is in accordance with the EIS and dark J-V data, which is supposed to be responsible for the decreased   $  R_{co}  $   and improved injection current.

# 2.4. The Combined Effect of IDIC and   $  Ga_{2}O_{3}  $   on Photovoltaic Performance and Stability

An efficiency toward 20% was obtained after optimization via the combination of IDIC passivation and   $  Ga_{2}O_{3}  $   interface engineering. The best-performing device exhibited a PCE of 19.86% (19.67%) measured under reverse (forward) voltage scan, which

is among the highest for p–i–n inverted PSCs based on MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  system with one-step deposition method.  $  ^{[30]}  $  The corresponding J–V curves and detailed photovoltaic parameters are depicted in Figure 5a and Table 1. The incident photon-to-current conversion efficiency (IPCE) measurements were conducted to confirm the superior photoelectric superior properties. As shown in the Figure 5b, the IPCE values exhibited a strong response in the entire photoresponse range from 300 to 800 nm. The integrated current density from the IPCE spectrum is 21.4 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $ , which is well consistent with the measured  $  J_{sc}  $  value within a 5% mismatch. The corresponding steady-state output is performed at the point of maximum power voltage under continuous AM1.5G illumination for 200 s. A high steady-state current density of 20.81 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  and a steady-state efficiency of 19.76% are achieved. Device long-term stability is an important metric for assessing the performance of PSCs. There are mainly two processes hamper the long-term stability behavior of the p–i–n PSCs. Generally, it is consensus that perovskite itself is prone to degradation in the presence of a humid environment. Moreover, the corrosion of metal electrodes like Ag has also been identified to be a critical issue would drag down the performance of the PSCs, leading to an increase in leakage current.  $  ^{[31]}  $

Defect-state passivations are realized for both the surface and the GBs of the perovskite with graded IDIC distribution. The hydrophobicity of perovskite is also enhanced after IDIC modified. # 3. Experimental Section

Materials: FTO glass substrates with sheet resistance of 14 Ω sq  $  ^{-1}  $  were purchased from Asahi Glass (Japan). All the chemicals were purchased and used without further purification. CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}  $  I and PbI  $  _{2}  $  were obtained from TCI. PCBM was purchased from NANO-C. The IDIC was purchased from Derthon Optoelectronic Materials Science Technology Co., Ltd. The molecule IDIC was synthesized by one-step simple Knoevenagel condensation.  $  ^{[36]}  $  Nickel(II) acetylacetonate was obtained from Alfa Aesar. Li-TFSI was purchased from Aladdin reagent. EA, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) were supplied from Aldrich. Tris(dimethylamino)gallium (99.99%, TDMA-Ga, from Suzhou Fu Na Electronic Technology Co., Ltd) was used as the Ga precursor and pure H  $  _{2}  $  O was used as oxidizer for ALD deposition. Others were from Sinopharm Group.

Device Fabrication—The Hole Transporting Layer Preparation: FTO substrates were cleaned using ultrasonication in deionized water, acetone, and ethanol and then dried with an   $  N_{2}  $   blow gun. Li-doped NiO was deposited onto FTO substrates by a modified sprayed pyrolysis method. The precursor solution contained 307.2 mg nickel acetylacetonate in 20 mL acetonitrile/ethanol (with 95:5 volume ratio) solution. Then Li-TFSI, predissolved in acetonitrile as a stock solution was added to precursor solution at the metal ion concentration ratio of 1%. The substrates were placed on a hotplate at the temperature of 450 °C during spraying pyrolysis process within 5 min and followed by annealing at 450 °C for 1 h in air.

Device Fabrication—The Perovskite Absorber Preparation: The perovskite absorber was prepared according to the literature.  $  ^{[22]}  $   MAI and PbI  $  _{2}  $   (1:1, mol:mol) were dissolved in the mixed anhydrous DMF:DMSO 4:1(v:v) with a concentration of 1.1 M. To prepare the perovskitenonfullerenegraded heterojunction perovskite, the IDIC was predissolved in the dripping solvent of EA varying the concentration from 0.015 to 0.06 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  . The perovskite solution was spin coated on substrates in a 3500 rpm for 40 s. 300 μL EA (with or without IDIC) was fast dripped onto the above film at the 10th s of the spinning. The substrates were then annealed at 100 °C for 10 min to form perovskite film.

Device Fabrication—The Electron Transporting Layer Preparation: 20 mg PCBM dissolved in 1 mL chlorobenzene was spin coated on the perovskite film at 2000 rpm for 30 s and then baked at 80 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, an ethanol solution of BCP (0.5 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was spin coated on the top of PCBM layer. ALD was carried out to form the Ga  $  _{2}  $  O  $  _{3}  $   layer. Nitrogen was used as a carrier gas to carry Ga and H  $  _{2}  $  O sources. Ga  $  _{2}  $  (NMe  $  _{2}  $  )  $  _{6}  $   and H  $  _{2}  $  O were used to deposit Ga  $  _{2}  $  O  $  _{3}  $   thin films with a growth rate of 0.1 nm per cycle at 120 °C. A typical ALD cycle consisted of 100 ms of the Ga  $  _{2}  $  (NMe  $  _{2}  $  )  $  _{6}  $   pulse time, followed by 5 s of N  $  _{2}  $   purge time, then 700 ms of H  $  _{2}  $  O pulse time, and 5 s of N  $  _{2}  $   purge time. The thickness of the Ga  $  _{2}  $  O  $  _{3}  $   could be tuned by the deposition cycles and the entire deposition time is no longer than 5 min.

Device Fabrication—The Metal Electrode Preparation: A silver electrode with thickness of 60–80 nm was deposited by thermal evaporation.
