# ABSTRACT

Morphological engineering plays a very important role to the performance of perovskite solar cells. In this study, 3,4-dihydroxybenzhydrazide employs as an additive in the perovskite precursor to control the crystallization kinetics. It is found that the doping of 3,4-dihydroxybenzhydrazide led to increase of grain size and decrease of grain boundaries, both of which facilitate charge transportation and suppress charge recombination within the

# 1. Introduction

Owing to the excellent optoelectronic properties of organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite, the perovskite solar cells (Pero-SCs) have shown great potentials as the next generation of photovoltaic technology [1-5]. Like the other photovoltaic devices, the performance of Pero-SCs relies very much on the morphology of the photoactive layers. The film morphologies including film uniformities, film coverage, and crystal size can be effectively improved by annealing in the presence of additives [6, 7]. Besides, it's known that the majority of the charge recombination and perovskite degradation occur at the grain boundaries and interfaces [8-12]. Therefore, the enhanced film qualities of perovskite by utilizing additives are expected to facilitate charge transportation, suppress charge recombination and improve the stability of the devices. As known, additives can promote the development of high performance Pero-SCs. Up to now, additives reported in the literature can be divided into several categories: polymers [13-16], fullerenes [17-19], metal halide salts [20,21], organic halide salts [22-24], solvent [25-27], inorganic acid [28,29], nanoparticles [30,31] and so on [32,33].

Among them, Lewis acid or base that can interact with perovskite (e.g. coordination between C=O or S=O and   $  Pb^{2+}  $  ), are widely applied to control the perovskite crystallization or defect passivation. For example, Namyoung Ahn et al. firstly introduced DMSO into the solution of perovskite precursor, where adducts of   $  PbI_{2}\cdot DMSO  $   and   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I\cdot PbI_{2}\cdot DMSO  $   due to interaction between S=O and Pb / MA were generated. The formation of intermediate product slowed down the crystallization, and thus led to high-quality perovskite films and consequently a high performance of the corresponding devic [35]. Zhifang Wu et al. reported a cationic additive, 2-(6-bromo-1,3-dioxo-1H-benzo[de]isoquinolin-2(3H)-yl)ethan-1-ammonium iodide (2-NAM), which possesses a planar aromatic group and C=O groups. This molecule can effectively decrease the crystallization rate, and thus increasing the grain size and consequently reducing the charge recombination within the perovskite films [34]. In addition, the hysteresis behavior of the corresponding Pero-SC was suppressed, and the stability was improved. Our results indicate that upon addition of DOBD, the grain size of the crystals became bigger and the grain boundaries got less after annealing. # 2. Results and discussion

The p-i-n type Pero-SCs with the configuration of ITO/PEDOT:PSS/perovskite/C $ _{60} $ /BCP/Al were fabricated. The details of the device fabrication were presented in the experimental section. The procedure to prepare perovskite films was schematically illustrated in Scheme. 1. Briefly, solution 2 was quickly dropped on the Pbl $ _2 $  surface without stopping spinning of the substrates. Annealing of the substrates at 100 °C for 3 min led to the formation of the crystallized perovskite. It is worth to note that an interesting phenomenon happened during preparation of solution neat Pbl21. As shown in the up row of Fig. 1, the DMF solution of neat Pbl $ _2 $  was opaque even after being stirred at 70 °C for 12 h. This should be attributed to the relatively poor solubility of Pbl $ _2 $  in DMF.

The results indicate that addition of DOBD has very little effect on the final perovskite, and it is very possible that interaction between DOBD and  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  has been replaced by forming perovskite structures. As shown in Fig.4b, the diffraction peaks located at  $ 14.08^{\circ} $  and  $ 28.45^{\circ} $  should be assigned to [110] and [220] crystal planes of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_{3 - x}\mathrm{Cl}_x $  perovskite [46].

The FWHM of the peaks were first decreased and then increased, a minimum width was obtained when the concentration of DOBD was  $ 3\mathrm{mg~mL}^{-1} $ . Both the peak intensity and width of the XRD pattern indicate that  $ 3\mathrm{mg~mL}^{-1} $  of DOBD dopant should be the best in terms of crystallinity and grain size. These results imply that the crystallinity of the films should be different, and the films doped with  $ 3\mathrm{mg~mL}^{-1} $  DOBD may lead to a higher performance.

The effect of DOBD on the morphological change of perovskite films were investigated by SEM. As shown in Fig. 4c, the perovskite film without DOBD additives consists of many small crystals. Upon addition of DOBD, the grain size apparently increased, and it reaches to a maximum as the concentration of DOBD was  $ 3\mathrm{mg~mL}^{-1} $ , and further increasing the concentration did not cause increase of the size. The maximum average diameter was approximately 500 nm. And according to the Scherrer equation, the maximum average diameter was  $ 34 \pm 3\mathrm{nm} $  when the concentration of DOBD was  $ 3\mathrm{mg~mL}^{-1} $ . The grain size was increased comparing to the  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  nanoparticles doped with DOBD, which is also calculated with Scherrer equation. Due to the increase of the gain size, the boundaries per unit area were correspondingly decreased. # 3. Conclusions

In conclusion, we introduce an additive DOBD containing Lewis base C=O group into perovskite to control the crystallization behavior. With the addition of DOBD, the perovskite presented large grain size and few grain boundaries, which were beneficial to facilitate charge transportation and suppress charge recombination. The PCE of DOBD doped-Pero-SC skyrocketed to 17.58% with huge enhanced   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.06 V,   $  J_{sc}  $   of 21.40 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   and FF of 79.1%, which was up to 21.5% higher than that without DOBD (14.47%). The conspicuous improvement of PCE mainly resulted from the enhanced   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF. Besides, the DOBD doped-Pero-SCs were much more stable after storing in

# 4. Experimental section

# 4.1 Materials

ITO-coated glass (10 Ω sq  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was purchased from CSG Holding Co., Ltd. PEDOT:PSS (Clevios P VP AI 4083) was acquired from Nichem Co., and the possible deposition was removed by running through a syringe filter with typical pinholes of 0.45 μm before use. Methylammonium iodide (MAI) was prepared according to the literature [56]. Methylammonium chloride (MACl) was purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Crop. PbI  $  _{2}  $  (99.999%) and 3,4-dihydroxybenzhydrazide (DOBD) were both acquired from Alfa Aesar. C  $  _{60}  $  and BCP were purchased from Puyang Yongxin Fullerene Technology Co., Ltd. and Alfa Aesar, respectively.

# 4.2 Device fabrication

All devices were fabricated on ITO-coated glass slides with size of  $ 1.5\mathrm{cm}\times 1.5\mathrm{cm} $ . The ITO slides were washed in detergent, deionized water, acetone, ethanol and isopropanol for 20 min twice each solvent under ultrasonification. Then the slides were dried in nitrogen flow, followed by treating with UV-ozone for 20 min. PEDOT:PSS was spin-coated on the cleaned ITO slides at 5000 rpm for 40 s, and the slides were annealed at  $ 150^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  in air for 15 min.

The perovskite films were prepared by a two-step deposition method.   $  PbI_{2}  $   was dissolved in N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%, J&K) with the

concentration of 460 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  and stirred at 70 °C for 12 h. DOBD was added into DMF with different concentrations of 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  to form a series of PbI  $  _{2}  $  /DOBD mixture solutions. These series of solutions are noted as solution 1. The solution of MAI and MACI was prepared by mixing MAI and MACI in isopropanol (IPA, J&K) with the concentration of 50 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  and 5 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $ , respectively, and stirred at 70 °C for 12 h. This solution is noted as solution 2. The above solution was filtrated with a polyvinyl difluoride (PVDF) filter with typical pore size of 0.45 μm before use. The PbI  $  _{2}  $  solution was firstly spin-coated on the above prepared PEDOT:PSS surface at 4500 rpm for 45 s, and then approximately 45 mL of mixed solution of MAI and MACI was dropped onto the spinning substrate in 20 s. The substrate was then heated at 100 °C for 3 min, obtaining a resulting perovskite film of ~ 320 nm. Finally, the perovskite films were capped with C  $  _{60}  $  (~ 30 nm), BCP (~ 8 nm) and Al (~ 80 nm) by thermal evaporation under vacuum of 1.0 × 10  $  ^{-5}  $  pa with a shadow mask covered on the slides to define the active area (0.0757 cm  $  ^{2}  $ ). The stabilities of Pero-SCs were tested with samples (without encapsulation) stored in glove box filled with nitrogen atmosphere for 35 days.

# 4.3 Characterization

The surface morphologies of perovskite films were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, S-8010, Hitachi) with applied acceleration voltage of  $ 5\mathrm{kV} $ . The crystallinities of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and perovskite films were collected by X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns performed on a diffractometer (D2 PHASER, Bruker). The UV-vis absorption spectra were measured using a UV-visible

The current density-voltage (J-V) curves and steady-state efficiencies of Pero-SCs were measured on a Keithley 2400 source meter unite under simulated air mass 1.5 global (AM 1.5G) solar irradiation at  $ 100\mathrm{mWcm}^{-2} $  (SAN_EI ELECTRIC, XEC-300M2).
