# $ \left[\mathrm{NH}_{3} \left(\mathrm{CH}_{2}\right)_{6} \mathrm{NH}_{3}\right] \mathrm{PbI}_{4} $  as Dion-Jacobson Phase Bifunctional Capping Layer for 2D/3D Perovskite Solar Cells with High Efficiency and Excellent UV Stability

Yanping Lv  $  ^{ab}  $  , Hongru Ma  $  ^{a}  $  , Yanfeng Yin  $  ^{c}  $  , Qingshun Dong  $  ^{a}  $  , Weidong Zhao  $  ^{a}  $  , Shengye Jin  $  ^{c}  $   and Yantao Shi  $  ^{*a}  $

Currently, three-dimensional (3D) organic-inorganic hybrid metallic halide perovskites are emerging as optoelectronic materials, but they demonstrate poor stability against heat and moisture and are vulnerable to oxidation and photodegradation upon exposure to ultraviolet (UV) light. Herein, for the first time, 1,6-diaminohexane ammonium (HDAD  $  ^{+}  $  ) is demonstrated to be a unique cation spacer to prepare a two-dimensional Dion-Jacobson phase (DJP) perovskite that is ultimately used to construct a 2D/3D stacking structure. Compared to 3D perovskites and Ruddlesden-Popper phase (RPP)-2D perovskites, the DJP-2D perovskite demonstrates superior UV stability and can efficiently protect the 3D layer underneath from being damaged by the UV light. Moreover, the DJP-2D perovskite can also act as an interfacial layer to passivate the defects located on top of the 3D perovskite, resulting in a notably increased photoluminescence lifetime and suppression of interfacial charge recombination. As a result, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) is enhanced from 19.22% (3D) to 20.31% (2D/3D) and is accompanied by improved moisture and thermal stabilities.

# Introduction

In recent years, organic-inorganic metal halide perovskites have been increasingly studied as emerging photoelectric materials due to their low cost, facile solution processibility  $  ^{1}  $  and exceptional optoelectronic properties, including an excellent light absorption,  $  ^{2,3}  $  a low exciton binding energy,  $  ^{4}  $  a tunable bandgap,  $  ^{5}  $  and an ultralong carrier diffusion length  $  ^{3,6}  $ , which enables use in various types of highly efficient photoelectric devices. The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have jumped from approximately 10% (in 2012)  $  ^{7}  $  to over 25% (in 2019),  $  ^{8}  $  which is comparable to that for mature commercially available PV technologies. The practical application and commercialization of PSCs, however, are being substantially inhibited by the intrinsic instability of three-dimensional (3D) perovskites whose structures are prone to be destroyed upon exposure to heat,  $  ^{9,10}  $  moisture,  $  ^{11,12}  $  ultraviolet (UV) light  $  ^{13,14}  $  and oxygen.  $  ^{15,16}  $  Consisting of inorganic sheets sandwiched by organic spacer layers, two-dimensional (2D) perovskites feature a layered structure generally represented by the formula  $  (\mathrm{A}')_{\mathrm{m}}\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{n}-1}\mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{n}}\mathrm{X}_{3\mathrm{n}+1}  $ , where the spacer cation can be

monovalent (m = 2 for the Ruddlesden-Popper phase (RPP)) or divalent (m = 1 for the Dion-Jacobson phase (DJP)).  $  ^{17-19}  $   Relative to their 3D counterparts, 2D perovskites, in particular those with   $  n \leq 2  $  , demonstrate excellent moisture resistance due to the hydrophobic nature of the organic spacer cations.  $  ^{20-22}  $   When serving as photoactive layers in PSCs, however, pure 2D perovskites are greatly limited because of their larger exciton binding energy and indirect and wider bandgap (>2.3 eV) compared to those of 3D perovskites.  $  ^{23-25}  $   The combination of 2D and 3D perovskites into a well-defined stacking structure has been validated as a promising strategy to simultaneously resolve the instability issue and improve the photovoltaic performance of PSCs.

The 2D perovskites in 2D/3D stacking structures reported so far are mostly RPPs with monovalent organic ammonium as the spacer cations, such as phenethylammonium (PEA  $  ^{+}  $  ),  $  ^{26-29}  $  butylammonium (BA  $  ^{+}  $  ),  $  ^{30-33}  $  and hexylammonium (HA  $  ^{+}  $  )  $  ^{34}  $ . PSCs with 2D/3D stacking structures containing RPPs demonstrated excellent moisture and thermal stabilities compared to those of their 3D counterparts, and the PCE was also notably improved due to suppressed interfacial charge recombination. For example, in 2017, Snaith and coworkers  $  ^{33}  $  reported high PCEs exceeding 20% and a stability of over 1000 hr in air for the BA  $  ^{+}  $  cation-derived 2D/3D device. Moreover, in addition to the observed good moisture and thermal stabilities, Shihe Yang et al  $  ^{28}  $  demonstrated a (MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  -PEA  $  _{2}  $  Pb  $  _{2}  $  I  $  _{4}  $ ) graded 2D/3D structure enabling a continuous upshift in the energy level, which finally resulted in a high open-circuit voltage (V  $  _{OC}  $ ) of 1.17 V as well as an efficiency enhancement. Very recently, HA  $  ^{+}  $  was also used to establish a 2D/3D stacking structure, endowing the device

Therefore, in addition to humidity and heat stabilities, UV stability is also an important concern when evaluating the practical long-term durability of PSCs. To improve the UV stability,   $  Sb_{2}S_{3}^{35}  $  ,   $  CsBr^{36}  $   or a silane coupling agent  $  ^{14}  $   were previously used as interfacial layers (usually between the electron transport layer and perovskite) to prevent UV light from damaging the perovskite layer, thus improving the UV stability. In this work, 1,6-diaminohexane ammonium iodide (HDADI) was introduced to prepare the DJP-2D perovskite capping layer in a 2D/3D stacking structure. # Results and discussion

The 3D perovskite in our 2D/3D stacking structure had a mixed composition for both cations and halides, namely,   $  Cs_{0.05}(MA_{0.17}FA_{0.83})_{0.95}Pb(I_{0.83}Br_{0.17})_{3}  $  , which was fabricated by one-step spin-coating with an anti-solvent method. When preparing the 2D/3D perovskite stacking structure, the HDADI isopropanol solution with a concentration of 2 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   was spin coated on the as-prepared 3D perovskite surface, and the obtained film is referred to as 3D+HDADI. Figure 1a illustrates the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the 3D and 3D+HDADI perovskite film samples, which were deposited on the SnO  $  _{2}  $  /FTO substrates. For the 3D sample, typical diffraction peaks from the 3D perovskite at 14.3°, 20.2°, 24.7°, 28.6° and 32.1° appeared. For the 3D perovskite, the water contact angle was  $ 55.26^{\circ} $ . However, the 2D/3D perovskite stacking structure exhibited a larger water contact angle of  $ 63.38^{\circ} $ . To further evaluate the moisture stability of the 2D/3D stacking structure based on HDADI, XRD analysis was performed to observe the degradation process of the two perovskite films, as illustrated in Figure S3 in the supporting information. Two perovskite films were directly exposed to ambient conditions. The hysteresis factor of the DJP-2D/3D-based PSCs was 0.109, which is much smaller than that of 3D PSCs (0.138). The dramatically reduced photocurrent hysteresis can be attributed to the defect passivation from the newly formed DJP-2D perovskite capping layer on the 3D perovskite surface. Figure 5c shows the internal photo-to-current efficiency (IPCE) spectra of the two devices. The integrated photocurrent of the two devices was almost identical, which is consistent with the  $  J_{sc}  $  values determined by the  $  J-V  $  curves. A steady-state output measurement at the maximum output power point was also performed to demonstrate the reproducibility of the performance. As depicted in Figure 5d, for the DJP-2D/3D derived device, the output power remained steady over 120 s with a stabilized  $  J_{sc}  $  of 21.27 mA cm $  ^{2}  $  and PCE of 19.31%, which is higher than that of the 3D-based device. This result demonstrates that the DJP-2D/3D-based device performed better than the 3D device, which is believed to be a result of reduced trap states and non-radiative recombination at the interfaces between the perovskite and spiro-OMeTAD layers.

Finally, we assessed the long-term stability of the non-encapsulated devices based on 3D and DJP-2D/3D perovskite films. The stability in the ambient environment was determined and is illustrated in Figure 6a, where the related PSCs were stored in an environment with a relative humidity of approximately 55%–75% and a temperature of approximately 25–40 °C. As a result, the DJP-2D/3D perovskite-based device maintained 80% of the initial PCE after 60 days, while the PCE of the control device decreased gradually to 40%. The excellent stability in the ambient environment was ascribed to the DJP-2D perovskite capping layer

on the 3D perovskite surface, which was demonstrated to be more hydrophobic in the water contact angle tests. Furthermore, the thermal stability of the two devices was determined and is illustrated in Figure 6b. After continuous heating for  $ 350\mathrm{hr} $ , the PCE of the 3D device gradually decreased to  $ 40\% $  of the initial PCE, while the device based on the DJP-2D/3D film still maintained  $ 60\% $  of its initial value. In general, the thermal stability was attributed to ion migration, which always occurs in perovskites with defects. As the foregoing state, the DJP-2D perovskite played a role in passivating surface defects on the 3D perovskite. Therefore, compared to the characteristics of the 3D perovskite, the DJP-2D/3D counterpart contained fewer trap states; thus, the probability of ion migration was greatly decreased, leading to an improved thermal stability.

# Conclusions

In this work, 1,6-diaminohexane ammonium iodide (HDADI) was introduced to prepare a DJP-2D perovskite capping layer in a 2D/3D stacking structure for the first time. The HDADI-based DJP-2D perovskite with  $  n = 1  $  demonstrated superior UV stability to a 3D perovskite and an RPP 2D perovskite involved

# Experimental

Device Fabrication: FTO was cleaned with hellmanex, and then washed with deionized water, isopropanol and ethanol successively. After dried, FTO was treated with UVO for 30 min. The SnO₂ electron-accepting layer was fabricated by spin coating the 0.1 M precursor solution (22.565 mg SnCl₂·2H₂O dissolved in 1 ml isopropanol) at 2000 rpm for 30 s on the FTO substrate. The ultimate film is obtained after annealing at 180 °C for 1 hr. For preparing the 3D perovskite precursor solution, 18.2 mg Csl, 606.7 mg PbI₂, 77.1 mg PbBr₂, 204.6 mg FAI and 23.5 mg CH₃NH₃Br were dissolved in 1 ml solvent containing DMF and DMSO (4:1, volume ratio). After stirring for 12 hr at room temperature, it was spin-coated following two-steps: 1000 rpm for 10 s (a speed of 100 rpm/s) and then 6000 rpm for 30 s (a speed of 6000 rpm/s). Chlorobenzene was poured at 15 s before the finish. The ultimate film was obtained after annealing at 100 °C for 60 min. For fabricating 2D perovskite layer, 2 mg HDADI is dissolved in 1mL isopropanol. The obtained solution was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 30 s. For the preparation of the hole transport layer (HTL), the corresponding precursor solution was obtained as follows: 72.3 mg Spiro-OMeTAD was dissolved in 1 ml chlorobenzene, and then 17.5 μL lithium bis-(trifluoromethylsulphonyl) imide (520 mg mL-1) in acetonitrile solution, 29 μL 4-tert-butylpyridine and 20 μL FK 209 Co(III) TFSI salt (300 mg mL-1) in acetonitrile solution were added into it. After stirring for 4 h, it was spin-coated at 3000 rpm for 30 s. At last, Ag was evaporated onto the HTL layer thermally forming the total device.

Characterizations: The X-ray diffraction patterns were recorded by (XRD, D/MAX-2400, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (γ = 0.1541 nm). The GIWAXS measurements of perovskite films were performed at the BL16B1 beamline of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility using a Pilatus 2M detector and X-ray with a wavelength of 1.24 Å. Absorbance spectra measurement was performed via a UV–vis spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer Lambda 950). Micromorphology was characterized via scanning electron microscopy (SEM, FEI QUANTA 450). Fluorescence spectra (PL) were excited by a pulse laser (PIXEA-CU-1, AUREA, France) with the wavelength of 406 nm. The IPCE spectra were measured by a monochromatic Xenon lamp (Bunkouki CEP-2000SRR). The contact angle was obtained using an optical CA meter (OCA20,

Dataphysics). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was collected via an electrochemical workstation (Zennium Zahner, Germany). The current density-voltage  $ (J - V) $  curves were measured under simulated AM 1.5 G (Solar3A, Newport,  $ 100\mathrm{mWcm}^{-2} $ ) with a Keithley 2450 source meter.
