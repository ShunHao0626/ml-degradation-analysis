# Efficient Perovskite Solar Cells Achieved using the 2-Methoxyethanol Additive: Morphology and Composition Control of Intermediate Film

Liuxing Guo, Yi Chen, Ge Wang, Yiqiu Xia, Dongxu Luo, Zihan Zhu, Chen Wang,  $  ^{*}  $  Wei Dong, and Shanpeng Wen  $  ^{*}  $

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Achieving a highly crystallized perovskite active layer with a dense, flat surface and a large grain size is very significant for constructing efficient perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Regarding this, the solvent engineering plays an important role in optimizing perovskite crystallinity and the final device performance. 2-Methoxyethanol (2-ME) is recently reported to be a good processing solvent of a high-quality perovskite film. However, in a conventional one-step fabrication method, little attempt is made to introduce 2-ME as a solvent additive into the  $ N,N $ -dimethylformamide/dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)-based perovskite precursor and study its influences on the quality of the resulting perovskite film. In this report, we demonstrate that 2-ME can be a feasible additive to modify the crystallinity of the perovskite active layer. Samples prepared in the presence of 2-ME show larger grains and less grain boundaries repeatedly. 2-ME also rationalizes DMSO in the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{I} - \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  -DMSO intermediate film effectively, thus guaranteeing a flat and pin-hole-free film at the same time.

Lower levels of traps and trap-assisted recombination are confirmed owing to the morphology improvement. The 2-ME-optimized planar devices yield a higher average power conversion efficiency and a better stability under an ambient atmosphere. This work may provide an alternative method to control the perovskite crystal growth and prepare PSCs with a higher performance and stability.

KEYWORDS: additives, intermediate phase, crystallization control, traps, perovskite solar cells

# INTRODUCTION

Lead halide perovskite is one of the hottest research topics in recent years because of their solution processability, tunable band gaps, long charge diffusion length, large absorption coefficients, and high degree of defect tolerance.  $  ^{1-4}  $   Because of these superior photoelectric properties a fastest power conversion efficiency (PCE) has been achieved using perovskite solar cells (PSCs) in the photovoltaic processes The PCE is increased from 3.8%  $  ^{5}  $   in the early stage to over 25%  $  ^{6}  $   in less than a decade. This already rivals those of the conventional inorganic photovoltaic materials such as Si, CdTe, CuInGaSe, and GaAs. The device stability has also been substantially prolonged from several minutes in the early stage to several days or months.  $  ^{7}  $

The rapid enhancement in PCE and stability of a PSC device has been achieved with continuous advancements in materials and a fundamental understanding of the unique photoelectric properties of the perovskite. One of our main tasks is to achieve a highly qualified perovskite active layer with less density of grain boundaries (GBs) and pin-holes. # RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Figure 1a depicts the device configuration used in this work [fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)/SnO $ _2 $ /MAPbI $ _3 $ /spiro-OMeTAD/Ag]. The perovskite active layers are obtained using a one-step antisolvent method (Figure 1c) and the SnO $ _2 $  is used as the electron collection layer and spiro-OMeTAD is used as the hole collection layer. The perovskite active layers are prepared as follows, PbI $ _2 $  and CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ I (in a 1:1 molar ratio) are dissolved in a mixed solvent of 2-ME, DMF, and DMSO. The volume ratio of 2-ME + DMF and DMSO is fixed at 4:1. Then, DMF/2-ME 10:0, 9:1,and 8:2, 7:3 v/v is denoted as 0, 10, 20, and 30 vol %. The precursor solution is spin coated onto SnO $ _2 $ . After spinning for a certain time, a mixture of chlorobenzene and acetonitrile is dropped onto the film to promote the formation of an intermediate phase. $ ^{23} $  The film is then annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. Figure 1b shows scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (cross-sectional) of the 2-ME modified devices. It can be seen that the solar cell shows clear interfaces among layers and every functional layer can be distinguished. The thickness of SnO $ _2 $ , MAPbI $ _3 $ , spiro-OMeTAD and Ag layer is 27, 529, 176, and 101 nm, respectively.

Figure 2 shows the SEM and atomic force microscopy (AFM) images of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  films fabricated using different ratios of 2-ME. For the sample without the 2-ME additive, the size of the perovskite grain varies a lot and there was a large

density of GBs (Figure 2a). These may induce more structural defects, hindering charge transport, aggravating recombination, and thus leading to bad photovoltaic performance.  $  ^{24}  $   It should be noted that the samples prepared in the presence of 2-ME show an obviously larger grain size and less GBs. The average grain size increases to about 400 nm when adding 20 vol % 2-ME. When we increase the 2-ME volume ratio over 20 vol %, the perovskite grain size changes little but the surface becomes blurred, suggesting a rough surface and decreased film quality. AFM measurements are also conducted, as shown in Figure 2b. Root-mean-square surface roughness (  $  R_{q}  $  ) values are 6.05, 6.19, 5.16, and 6.68 nm for 0-, 10-, 20-, and 30 vol %-based sample, respectively. This morphology feature is basically in line with the SEM results, further confirming that the 20 vol % 2-ME optimized film shows an enhanced grain size, fewer GBs, and a smoother surface than the control film. The morphology evolution indicates that 2-ME is an efficient additive to manipulate the perovskite crystallization process. The ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption spectra (Figure S1) show that the 2-ME-optimized perovskite exhibits improved light absorption in the region of 500–800 nm. It is well known that in a one-step method, the morphology and composition of the intermediate film are important to achieve an improved perovskite film quality.  $  ^{25}  $   An interesting result is that 2-ME shows influences on both aspects. We measure the SEM images of the intermediate films fabricated under different conditions, as shown in Figure S2. We can see that the conventional intermediate film is composed of plenty of short rods, which is consistent with the reported results.  $  ^{26}  $   In the presence of 2-ME, a different morphology is observed that small nanorods fuse into larger-sized regional areas. We also conduct the X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurement to study the influences of 2-ME addition on perovskite crystallinity, as shown in Figure 3a. Similar XRD patterns are observed for the control and 2-ME-modified perovskite films. The peaks located at 14.1, 28.5, and 31.9° are attributed to the (110), (220), and (310) crystal planes, respectively. These are the characteristics of the orthorhombic phase.  $  ^{27}  $   2-ME introduction does not cause the phase change. However, the narrowest full width at half-maximum value and the largest peak intensity of the (110) peak is observed under the 20 vol % 2-ME condition (Figure S3). These results are in line with the SEM and AFM results. To investigate the roles of 2-ME during the growth of perovskite crystals, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis is carried out, and the results obtained are shown in Figure 3b. For the control sample, we observe peaks located at around 1011 and 3190 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  . This can be assigned to S=O (from DMSO) and N-H (from MA) stretching vibration, respectively, also indicating the MAI-PbI  $  _{2}  $  -DMSO intermediate forms.  $  ^{28}  $   It can be seen that the position and intensity of these peaks change as we introduce 2-ME. The N-H stretching peak shifts from 3190 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   to a lower wavenumber of 3184 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   for 10 vol % 2-ME, 3180 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   for 20 vol % 2-ME, and 3178 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   for 30 vol % 2-ME addition. The peak position of the S=O stretching vibration moves in the opposite direction to a higher wavenumber (see Figure S4). Furthermore, we calculate the intensity ratio of the S=O peak to N-H peak, which allows us to study the DMSO amount within the film. This ratio decreases from 1.99 for the control film to 1.66 for 10 vol % 2-ME-, 1.59 for 20 vol % 2-ME-, and 1.17 for 30 vol % 2-ME-based films. This phenomenon indicates that 2-ME can help to extract a certain amount of DMSO during the antisolvent process. To our knowledge, this can reduce the pin-holes and small grains caused by DMSO evaporation.  $  ^{29}  $   Less DMSO in the intermediate film also promotes the rapid nucleation,  $  ^{30,31}  $   thus decreasing the density of seeds. This is also a prerequisite for growing a dense and uniform perovskite film with a larger grain size. In order to understand how the 2-ME additive probably accelerates DMSO extraction during the antisolvent process, we also investigate the interaction between solvents in the perovskite solution using the FTIR tool. Here, we gradually

increase the DMSO amount in DMF and 2-Me solvents and investigate the dynamic process, as shown in Figure 3c,d. It can be seen from Figure 3c that the pristine DMF (black curve) shows a sharp peak located at around 1660 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  , which can be assigned to the stretching vibration of C=O.  $  ^{32}  $   This peak slightly blue-shifts by 9 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  , which probably originates from decreased self-association of DMF after dilution.  $  ^{33}  $   There is no obvious interaction between DMF and DMSO. As shown in Figure 3d, the pristine 2-ME (black curve) shows a broad peak located at around 3400 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  , which is assigned to the stretching vibration of hydroxyl.  $  ^{34}  $   Upon increasing the DMSO amount, this peak obviously red-shifts by over 56 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  , indicating there is a significant interaction between 2-ME and DMSO. According to the reported literature, we deduce that it is a hydrogen bond interaction.  $  ^{35}  $   This interaction may potentially weaken the interaction between DMSO and other components (such as PbI  $  _{2}  $  ). Hence, after introducing 2-ME, DMSO may be more easily extracted using an antisolvent during the antisolvent process, leading to a reduced DMSO amount in the intermediate film to some degree.

Before concluding, we will make a general comment on how the 2-ME additive controls the perovskite crystallization process, thus improving film quality and the final device performance. Introducing more volatile 2-ME into the precursor solution accelerates the evaporation rate of the solvent thus promoting a fast nucleation process. In this scenario, the intermediate film is composed of island-shaped regional nuclei, which provide the conditions for growing into larger perovskite grains. Besides, interaction between 2-ME and DMSO helps to extract a certain amount of DMSO during the antisolvent process, confirmed using the FTIR results. This avoids the pinholes caused by DMSO evaporation. Less DMSO also means accelerated conversion from the perovskite intermediate phase to the perovskite phase, which is expected to be conducive to obtain a high-quality perovskite.  $  ^{39,52}  $   PSC device fabricated from the optimized perovskite film shows less density of traps and reduced trap-assisted charge recombination. Correspondingly, the devices afford improved   $  V_{oc}  $   from 1080 to 1102 mV. Following this route, we may be able to screen more efficient additives like 2-ME to control the perovskite crystallization process.

# CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, we have showed that 2-ME can be effectively added into the perovskite precursor to control the crystallization process, thus improving the quality of the final perovskite film. It turns out that adding appropriate amount of 2-ME in precursor solution can not only affect nucleation but also facilitate extraction of DMSO from the intermediate film. This results in the formation of pin-hole free film with larger perovskite grains. Utilizing this strategy, we can fabricate hysteresis-free PSCs with an average efficiency over 18.5%, which is significantly higher than those of the devices without 2-ME. Moreover, the 2-ME-based devices display superior long-time storage stability when aged under ambient atmosphere. The improved photoelectric properties including lower trap density, suppressed trap-assisted nonradiative recombination losses, and beneficial charge transport behaviors are responsible for the observed device performance enhancement. These findings in our work may provide a new avenue for the construction of high-performance PSC devices.

The SEM images were obtained by using a FEI MAGELLAN 400 scanning electron microscope. The AFM images were obtained by using a Veeco Dimension 3100 in tapping mode. The UV–vis absorption spectra were obtained by using an ultraviolet–visible spectrophotometer-1700. The PL spectra were recorded using a SHIMADZU RF3600 spectrophotometer. The FT-IR spectra were obtained using the IS50 Fourier transform infrared spectrometer. TRPL measurement was carried out using IHR320 with a laser of 450 nm. The current density–voltage (J-V) characteristics of the fabricated devices were measured using a Keithley 2601 source meter under Air Mass 1.5 Global (AM 1.5 G) with an Oriel 300 W solar simulator intensity of 100 mW/cm². The light intensity was measured using a photometer (International light, IL1400) and corrected by a standard silicon solar cell. The best J-V curves as well as the J-V hysteresis are recorded at a scanning rate of 46 mV/s. The EQE spectra were obtained using Crowntech Q-Test Station 1000 AD. We also fabricated the electron-only devices with a configuration of indium tin oxide/SnO₂/MAPbI₃/PC₆₁BM/C₆₀/BCP/Ag.

Device Fabrication. The structure of PSCs in this work is FTO/  $  SnO_{2}/CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}/spiro-OMeTAD/Ag  $  . First, the FTO-coated glass substrates were cleaned using a detergent, then ultrasonicated in deionized water, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol for 15 min, respectively, at room temperature. The substrates were dried under a stream of   $  N_{2}  $  , and were treated in ultraviolet ozone for 10 min before spin coating the   $  SnO_{2}  $   precursor solution. A   $  SnO_{2}  $   film was prepared by spin coating the   $  SnO_{2}  $   precursor solution (filtered through a 0.45   $  \mu  $  m MCE filter) onto the FTO substrate at 3000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 180 °C for 1 h in the muffle furnace. The substrates were transferred into a glovebox with oxygen and moisture concentrations <0.1 ppm. The spin-coating procedure was performed at 1000 rpm for 10 s then at 5000 rpm for 30 s. Then, 100   $  \mu  $  L mixed solution of chlorobenzene and acetonitrile was poured rapidly onto the substrates 20 s before the end of spin-coating to induce fast crystallization. Thereafter, the samples were annealed on a hot plate at 100 °C for 10 min. After the samples were left to cool down to room temperature, spiro-OMeTAD was then sequentially deposited by spin coating at 4000 rpm for 30 s. Next, the substrates are placed in an oxygen-filled box for 3–4 h to ensure sufficient oxidation of the spiro-OMeTAD films. Finally, silver electrodes with a thickness of 90 nm were deposited on the top of the HTM layer at a vacuum level of   $  10^{-7}  $   Pa through a shadow mask. Before testing, the cells are covered using a metal mask in order to limit the active area to be 0.064 cm  $  ^{2}  $  .
