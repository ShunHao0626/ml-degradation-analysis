Abstract: Low-cost carbon-based perovskite solar cells (C-PSCs) without hole-transport-layer (HTL) and metal contact are highly promising for marketing. However, lower efficiency than conventional PSCs and instability during the penetration of moisture through the porous carbon electrode as well as the incoming of the UV light from the glass side of the device remain as challenges. Herein, a multifunctional triple-layer system containing  $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / SiO_2 / CeO_2} $  porous nanomaterials is numerically simulated and experimentally employed on the glass side of the HTL-free C-PSCs. This strategy is designed to increase cell efficiency by enhancing the anti-reflective feature and long-term stability via the UV light blocking and superhydrophobic properties introduced to the surface. Furthermore, this system is durable against environmental pollutants owing to the photocatalytic self-cleaning effect of  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ . We also used a superhydrophobic carbon back contact to sandwich perovskite active layer between two superhydrophobic surfaces and further humidity resilience of the device for the first time. # 1. Introduction

These years, many researchers have concentrated on Perovskite solar cells owing to their rapid progress in conversion efficiency (from 3.8% to 25.2%) and excellent photovoltaic performance [1-5]. However, one of the fundamental challenges in PSCs is their instability against various environmental conditions, predominantly ultra-violet (UV) light, water or humidity exposure, and temperature [6-10]. Besides, the presence of organic HTLs and metal electrodes in the structure of conventional PSCs (e.g., FTO/mesoporous TiO $ _2 $ /perovskite/hole transport layer (HTL)/metal) is a reason for their low stability [11,12]. Most organic HTLs are unstable and prone to metal and halide ion migrations, resulting in PSC degradation [11,12]. In recent years, carbon-based PSCs (C-PSCs) have made it possible to manufacture cells without HTL and metal contact, although it has resulted in lower efficiency than conventional PSCs. In the HTL-free C-PSCs, the photogenerated holes are directly extracted by the carbon electrode. A large difference between the Fermi-level of carbon and the VB level of the perovskite would slow down the hole transfer. However, the charge separation rate is lower at the perovskite/carbon interface than that at the perovskite/HTL interface of conventional PSCs. Thus, HTL-free C-PSCs have higher charge recombination, lower Voc, and therefore lower efficiency. For more details, energy band diagrams and charge kinetic behaviors of the conventional HTL-based PSCs and HTL-free C-PSCs are shown in Figure S1. The first highly promising C-PSC was introduced with 6.6% efficiency [13], and now it has reached 16.37% [14]. These HTL free PSCs have a high potential for commercialization because carbon materials are low-cost, stable, intrinsically hydrophobic, inert to ion migration, and an excellent alternative for vacuum-processed metal electrodes [15]. However, the possibility of perovskite degradation and the instability of the device during the penetration of moisture through the porous carbon film as well as the incoming of the UV light from the glass side of the device (the input light side) remain as challenges.

Concerning the poor stability in the face of the water molecule, through which the perovskite material shows a tendency to hydrolyze toward the precursors, some strategies have been proposed to prevent the perovskite collapsing in the conventional PSCs   $  [6,18,27,28,19-26]  $  . Incorporating a permeation barrier layer below or above the hole transport layer (HTL)   $  [19,20]  $  , the employment of the hydrophobic HTLs   $  [21-23]  $  , applying a transparent superhydrophobic film including polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) arrays on the glass-side of the PSC device (incoming light side)   $  [6,24]  $   and complete encapsulating the perovskite photovoltaic devices   $  [25,26]  $   were among the most important efforts. Although there have been some achievements, in most instances, deposition processes are complex and expensive. Besides, the durable outdoor stability of some materials is low and, even in some cases, may decrease the photovoltaic performance of the devices.

As far as instability upon UV light exposure is concerned, Leijtens et al. demonstrated that the   $  TiO_{2}  $   component assists perovskite photocatalytic degradation at the presence of UV irradiation [8]. Several studies have addressed this issue by introducing a layer outside or inside the PSC [4,8,37,38,29–36]: Utilizing some material as a replacement for mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   such as   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $  ,   $  SnO_{2}  $  ,   $  BaSnO_{3}  $   [8,29,30], inserting an interlayer (  $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $  ,   $  Sb_{2}S_{3}  $  , CsBr, and   $  SnO_{2}  $  ) at the perovskite/  $  TiO_{2}  $   interface [31–33], and employing fluorescent downconversion materials such as YVO4:  $  Eu^{3+}  $  ,   $  SrAl_{2}O_{4}:Eu^{2+}  $  ,   $  Dy^{3+}  $  , and Au@  $  Y_{2}O_{3}:Eu^{3+}  $   outside the device, which absorb UV light (  $  \lambda\leq400~nm  $  ) and emit visible light [4,35,36]. Nevertheless, there are drawbacks to these approaches, such as decreasing transparency of device substrate for the layers placed outside and reducing carrier transportation for those inside the device [4,37,38].

Increasing stability while maintaining efficiency is a challenge facing PSCs. Coating the surface of the glass substrate with UV absorbent layers is a common strategy to prevent the perovskite from being degraded by UV light entered into the solar cell structure. In addition to being UV-absorbent, this coating structure must also have anti-reflective, hydrophobic, and photocatalytic self-cleaning properties. Most UV absorbers have a refractive index higher than glass, which increases the light reflectance from the surface. This, in turn, reduces the input light into the active layer and light-harvesting efficiency. To date, no solution has been presented to encompass all aspects.

In this work, a multifunctional triple-layer coating (MTLC) composed of  $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / SiO_2 / CeO_2} $  is designed and simulated based on the refractive index grating technique. In this structure,  $ \mathrm{CeO_2} $ ,  $ \mathrm{SiO_2} $ , and  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  act as a UV absorber, anti-reflector, and photocatalyst, respectively. Since the used materials have refractive indexes higher than the glass substrate (this causes high reflectance from the surface), the porosity-making technique is applied to achieve a low effective refractive index. Further, the coating is optimized (in terms of the absorbent layer material and the thickness of the layers) to maximize the visible light passing through the active layer, and UV light absorption using Finite-Difference Time-Domain (FDTD) simulation. The optimized coating is experimentally deposited onto the glass side of the device substrate (FTO  $ 7\Omega/\mathrm{sq} $ ). The obtained coating is functionalized by covalently grafting of PDMS brushes to induce superhydrophobic properties to the surface. This multifunctional designed coating exhibit several properties simultaneously, such as high visible light transparency, highly efficient absorption in the whole UV range, photocatalytic activity, and durable superhydrophobic properties to stabilize and increase the performance of the C-PSC device. The photocatalytic activity can lead to coating recovery to its initial properties by degrading the oily stains and organic pollutants under UV irradiation. This strategy is presented for the first time and provides a device with superior stability and record efficiency of  $ 16.60\% $  for HTL-free C-PSCs. Moreover, a superhydrophobic carbon back contact is introduced to further enhance the stability of the device for the first time.

# 2. Results and discussion

The transmission spectrum clearly shows the UV Blocker and the anti-reflective features of the provided structure. In the simulation process, at first, to absorb UV light, TiO $ _2 $ , CeO $ _2 $ , and ZnO layers with a thickness of 100 nm and a porosity of 50-55% (to achieve a low effective refractive index) were added to a one-side glass substrate (Figure S2a.). The porous CeO $ _2 $  layer absorbs more UV light than other UV-absorbing layers at the same thicknesses, while providing the least manipulation and increased reflection in the visible spectral region. Therefore, by selecting the CeO $ _2 $  layer as the UV-absorbing layer, the layer thickness was optimized for maximum UV absorption and minimal increase in visible light reflection. The results show 600 nm as the optimal thickness at which the transmission of light tends to zero at around a wavelength of 340 nm (Figure S2b.). Figure 1b illustrates the transmittance spectra of the one-side glass substrate in comparison to the coated substrate with 600 nm thick porous CeO $ _2 $  layer. To reduce the reflection of visible light, the SiO $ _2 $  porous layer was added as an anti-reflective material on the CeO $ _2 $  layer.

With a single anti-reflective layer, the light reflection at normal incidence is minimized when the refractive index of the anti-reflective layer is  $  n_{AR} = \sqrt{n_{Sub}}  $ , where  $  n_{Sub}  $  is the refractive index of the substrate, and this is while its optical thickness is considered to be equal to a quarter of the wavelength[39]. Accordingly, SiO $ _2 $  porous layer with a porosity of about 60%, which provides an effective refractive index of 1.21 at a wavelength of 600 nm, is a suitable candidate as an anti-reflective layer to cover the CeO $ _2 $  porous layer (effective refractive index of 1.62) and the glass substrate (refractive index of 1.49). The porous SiO $ _2 $  layer thickness was optimized for maximum visible light transmission. As the thickness increased from 50 to 200 nm, the transmission spectrum

# 3. Conclusions

A multifunctional coating composed of  $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / SiO_2 / CeO_2} $  was designed and simulated by the refractive index grating technique. This triple-layer coating system was optimized regarding the absorbent layer material and the thickness of the layers to maximize the visible light passing through the cell substrate, and UV light absorption using FDTD simulation. The optimized coating was experimentally deposited onto the glass side of the device substrate (FTO,  $ 7\Omega \mathrm{sq}^{-1} $ ) and functionalized by PDMS to induce superhydrophobic properties to the surface. A long-term stable HTL-free carbon-based PSC with a  $ 16.60\% $  PCE record was achieved in the PDMS- $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / SiO_2 / CeO_2 / Glass/meso - TiO_2 / MAPbI_3 / superhydrophobic} $  carbon configuration. Generally, using the multifunctional coating,  $ Jsc $  increased from 21.44 to  $ 23.82\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $  thanks to the anti-reflective feature of MTLC. This superiority led to an increase in light-harvesting efficiency and,

# 4. Experimental Section

# 4.1. Numerical calculations

The transmission and reflection spectra have been calculated using finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations. Commercial software of OptiFDTD was used to perform FDTD calculations. A simulation box size of  $ 0.4 \times 0.4 \times 2 \mu \mathrm{m} $  with periodic boundary conditions on in-plane directions and perfectly matched-layer (PML) conditions along the z-direction were used. The simulation volume was gridded with  $ 2 \mathrm{~nm} $  mesh. A plane wave light source propagated along Z direction with  $ 300 - 800 \mathrm{~nm} $  wavelength range was incident on the coated sides of the glass substrate. The reflection and transmission spectra were directly calculated with a frequency-domain transmission monitor placed above the plane wave light source and inside the substrate, respectively. Refractive indexes of materials were taken from the literature [1-5]. The deformed spherical nanoparticles with size distribution taken from experimental work have been used to design porous layers. These nanoparticles, which can overlap with each other, are randomly distributed in the volume of the desired layer. In this case, the part of the light that is scattered during the interaction with the porous layer is considered. This design provides an accurate simulation with the least difference between simulation and experimental results.

Preparation of Porous   $  CeO_{2}  $   Under Layer: a ceria sol and the related film was prepared based on the literature   $  [6,7]  $  . In this case,   $  CeCl_{3}.7H_{2}O  $   (Sigma-Aldrich, 573.4 mg) was added to a solution including P123 triblock copolymer (Sigma-Aldrich, 1250 mg), ethanol (Merck, 12.5 mL), 2-methoxypropanol (Sigma-Aldrich, 12.5 mL), and glacial acetic acid (Merck, 0.658 mL). After three days, the   $  CeO_{2}  $   sol was coated on the glass side of the FTO substrate by dip coating in a relative humidity of 50% and a withdrawal rate of 50 mm min  $  ^{-1}  $  . The coated film was directly transferred to an oven at 150 °C for 1 h. Multiple dip coating was used to achieve the desired thickness. Finally, the sample was heated to 300 °C with a ramp rate of 1 °C min  $  ^{-1}  $   for 6 h.

Preparation of Porous  $ \mathrm{SiO}_2 $  Middle Layer: First, Pluronic P123 (0.5 g) was dissolved in ethanol (12 mL) and HCl (3.2 mL, 0.05 M). A certain amount of tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, Merck) was added to the P123 solution to achieve a copolymer to silica mass ratio of  $ 1.7^{[51]} $ . Then, the  $ \mathrm{SiO}_2 $  sol was coated on the  $ \mathrm{CeO}_2 $  film via dip coating in a relative humidity of  $ 10\% $  and a withdrawal rate of  $ 60~\mathrm{mm~min^{-1}} $ . The as-made film was calcined at  $ 350^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  in air for  $ 30\mathrm{min} $  utilizing a temperature ramp of  $ 2^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\mathrm{min}^{-1} $ .

Preparation of Porous   $  TiO_{2}  $   Up Layer:   $  TiO_{2}  $   up layer was prepared from a fresh solution containing   $  TiCl_{4}/P123/PPG/EtOH/H_{2}O  $   in respective molar proportions of 1:0.002:0.06:160:42, dip-coated in a withdrawal rate of 100 mm min  $  ^{-1}  $   at 40 °C, and relative humidity of 10%. Finally, the as-formed triple-layer system was annealed at 500 °C for 5 min to ensure the crystallization of the   $  TiO_{2}  $   top layer  $  ^{[52]}  $  .

PDMS Grafting onto the   $  TiO_{2}  $   Photocatalyst Film: The triple-layer coatings were covered by trimethylsiloxy-terminated PDMS (Mw: 5970) and irradiated by UV-A light (wavelength: 315–380 nm, intensity: 5 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) for 45 min. After illumination, free PDMS molecules were thoroughly rinsed with tetrahydrofuran (THF) and toluene   $  [41]  $  .

Perovskite Precursor Solution and Deposition: First, a solution of   $  PbI_{2}  $   (TCI, 1.4 M) was prepared into DMSO/DMF (1:9) at 80 °C. Then, the   $  PbI_{2}  $   solution was deposited into   $  TiO_{2}  $   scaffolds maintained at 80 °C by spin-coating at 2000 rpm for 20 s. To convert the   $  PbI_{2}  $   Film to   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $ ,   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $   solution (1 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was made in a mixed solvent of isopropanol/cyclohexane (1:9), and then,   $  PbI_{2}  $   films were immersed into the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $   solution for 12 h. Finally, the as-prepared methylammonium lead triiodide (MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ) films were heated at 100 °C for 15 min [56,57].

Preparation of the Superhydrophobic Carbon Electrode: A commercial carbon paste composed of carbon black and graphite was used for preparing the carbon back electrode. The carbon paste was painted on the perovskite film followed by spraying an ethanol solution of 1H, 1H,2H,2H-perfluorodecyltriethoxysilane (Sigma-Aldrich, 5% v/v) on the paste and heating at 100 °C for 1 h.

A UV–vis spectrophotometer (JASCO V-670, USA) was used to measure the optical characteristics of the coated films.
