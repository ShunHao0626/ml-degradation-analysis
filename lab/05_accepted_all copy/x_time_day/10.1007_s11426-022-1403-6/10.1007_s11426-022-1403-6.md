# Multifunctional indaceno[1,2-b:5,6-b']dithiophene chloride molecule for stable high-efficiency perovskite solar cells

The additive strategy has emerged as an effective approach to improving the performance of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Herein, a small acceptor-donor-acceptor type molecule indaceno[1,2-b:5,6-b']dithiophene chloride (IDT-Cl) is designed and synthesized to advance both the efficiency and stability of  $ \mathrm{FA}_{0.85}\mathrm{MA}_{0.15}\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  PSCs. Within the IDT-Cl molecule, the S-group with high electron density promotes chemical bonding with the lead cations in the perovskite, resulting in enlarged grain size and smoother surface topography of the perovskite absorber. In addition, the undercoordinated lead ions in the perovskite layer may be passivated by the carbonyl group in the 5-chloroindolin-2-one unit, thereby reducing the number of nonradiative recombination centers. Meanwhile, the IDT-Cl adjusts the energy level mismatch between the perovskite and two adjacent carrier transport layers, leading to easy charge collection. By the multifunctional effect of the IDT-Cl molecule, the modified device yields a high power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 24.46\% $ ,  $ 8.8\% $  higher than that of the control PSC  $ (22.48\%) $ . More importantly, the hydrophobic alkyl side-chain of the IDT-Cl molecule further ensures enhanced humidity stability of the perovskite film and environmental, thermal, and light stabilities of the PSC devices.

# 1 Introduction

The organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cell (PSC) is recognized as the most promising next-generation photovoltaic technology because of its high absorption coefficient across the visible spectrum, small exciton binding energy [1],

efficient bipolar charge transport [2], high carrier mobility [3], long charge-carrier diffusion length and lifetime [4]. Since its invention by Miyasaka et al. [5], its efficiency has soared to 25.7% in just a decade [6–10], making it the highest efficiency thin-film solar cell.

leading to reduced carrier lifetime and device performance [11]. Therefore, a few strategies, including precursor selection [12], solvent engineering [13], functional additive introduction [14,15], and post-treatment [16,17] have been used to enlarge the grain size, improve crystalline quality, and reduce surface and bulk defect densities. Among them, the additive strategy has been proven to be effective to control the surface morphology and element distribution within the perovskite film, resulting in improved solar cell efficiency. For this purpose, fullerene is often used as an acceptor for its appropriate energy level, high electron mobility, and defect passivation ability [18–23]. Unfortunately, it also has obvious weaknesses, such as its limited energy-level tunability, thermal- and photo-chemical instability, and difficulty in purification [24]. Therefore, to overcome the above-mentioned shortcomings, non-fullerene organic semiconductors are introduced as alternative acceptors into the PSCs [25,26]. Their observed immediate benefits include: (1) the energy level can be adjusted to match those of the perovskite materials for effective charge extraction [27]; (2) the optical absorption in the near-infrared spectrum may be used to compensate for the perovskite absorber [24]; (3) there are a variety of functional groups that can be designed in non-fullerene molecules to enhance their defect passivation [28].

Based on these advantages, various n-type organic semiconductor acceptors with donor-acceptor structures have been specially designed for various photovoltaic devices. In addition, the selection rule of such donor-acceptor molecules generally reflects simple synthetic procedures, easily tuned electronic/optical properties, and rigid backbone for reduced reorganization energy that benefits charge conduction. Recently, indaceno[1,2-b:5,6-b']dithiophene (IDT) core module-related acceptor materials were tested as hole-transporting materials (HTMs) [29,30]. The polymers with the IDT group exhibit an excellent ability to extract and transport holes because their coplanar structure is conducive to intrachain interaction and charge transfer [31]. Generally, both ends of the fused ring of the IDT building block contain high electron-density groups, which play a role in interacting with undercoordinated metal ions. In addition, the extended sides of IDT arms are often connected with Lewis-base-type functional units, including carbonyl and thiocarbonyl groups, which further interact with undercoordinated metal cations in the perovskite layer, to reduce the number of defect sites and improve the quality of the perovskite film [32,33]. More practically, the polymers usually need side-chain assistance to promote their solubility [34], especially when introduced into perovskite precursor solution. It is known that the solubility of synthetic IDT can be enhanced by introducing an alkyl chain so that the prepared films are hydrophobic. Based on the above design route, IDT polymer has been used as a passivator at the interface between the electron transport

layer (ETL) and the perovskite absorber [35]. Meanwhile, the donor and acceptor parts of IDT can simultaneously passivate under-coordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and halide dangling bonds, and   $  \pi  $  -conjugated systems are incorporated into the donor-acceptor molecules to keep the molecular planarity and promote the electron delocalization through overlapping   $  \pi  $  -orbitals.

In this study, a non-fullerene derivative molecule with an IDT core and a 5-chloroindolin-2-one side chain unit, termed IDT-Cl, is designed and synthesized for organic-inorganic hybrid  $ \mathrm{FA}_{0.85}\mathrm{MA}_{0.15}\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  perovskite-based photovoltaic devices. The IDT-Cl molecule, with its high electron-density S, promotes crystallinity, enlarges the grain size, and smooths the surface topography of the perovskite absorber. The undercoordinated lead atoms or the iodine vacancies in the perovskite layer are passivated by the carbonyl group in the 5-chloroindolin-2-one. It is found that the molecule shows improved energy-level match with the perovskite layer, resulting in beneficial carrier transport. By the multifunctional effect of the IDT-Cl molecule, the modified device yields a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 24.46\% $ , significantly higher than that of the control PSC  $ (22.48\%) $  without it. Moreover, the hydrophobic alkyl side chain of the IDT-Cl molecule further ensures the humidity stability of the perovskite film and the environmental stability of the device.

# 2 Experimental

# 2.1 Materials

Lead(II) iodide (PbI $ _2 $ , 99.99%), formamidinium iodide (FAI, ≥99.5%), and methylammonium iodide (MAI, ≥99.5%) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. (China).  $ N,N $ -dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%) and dimethylsulphoxide (DMSO, 99.9%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biological Technology Co. Ltd. (China). 4-tert-butylpyridine ( $ t $ -BP), chlorobenzene (CB), and lithium bis (trifluoromethylsulfonyl) imide (Li-TFSI) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). The synthesis process of the IDT-Cl molecule is described in the experiment part below.

# 2.2 Preparation of perovskite precursor solutions

175.4 mg FAI, 553.2 mg PbI $ _2 $ , and 28.6 mg MAI were dissolved in a polarized mixture of dimethylsulfoxide and dimethylformamide with a volume ratio of 4:1. The perovskite precursor solutions containing IDT-Cl with molecular concentration gradient of 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mg were prepared, respectively. Through the systematic evaluation of perovskite film and its device performance, 1 mg IDT-Cl additive was determined as the optimal concentration and adopted for the analysis in this study. # 2.3 Device fabrication

The fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) substrates (2.5 cm × 2.5 cm) were successively washed with ethanol, acetone, isopropyl alcohol, and ethanol in an ultrasonic bath for 30 min, and then blown dry with a nitrogen gun and treated with ultraviolet (UV)-ozone for 15 min to remove moisture and organic residues on the surface of the substrates. The   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer was prepared by chemical bath deposition at 70 °C for 1 h. The substrates were annealed in air at 200 °C for 30 min and then treated in UV-ozone for 15 min before spin coating. The perovskite films were prepared by the one-step spin coating method. The perovskite precursor solutions without and with IDT-Cl were spin-coated for 10 s at 1,000 r min  $  ^{-1}  $   and 40 s at 3,000 r min  $  ^{-1}  $  , where 1 mL diethyl ether was dropped on the rotating substrate 5 s after spinning. And then the spin-coated perovskite films were annealed on a hot plate at 150 °C for 15 min. The annealed perovskite films were washed with 120 μL isopropanol by spin-coating at 5,000 r min  $  ^{-1}  $   for 30 s. The spiro-MeOTAD layer was formed on the perovskite layer by spin-coating 50 μL stock solution comprising 90 mg spiro-MeOTAD in 1 mL CB, including 36 μL t-BP and 22 μL Li-TFSI solution (520 mg Li-TFSI in 1 mL of acetonitrile (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.8%)) at 5,000 r min  $  ^{-1}  $   for 30 s. Finally, a 100 nm Au electrode was deposited on top of the spiro-MeOTAD layer using the thermal evaporator. The active area of the PSCs was 0.09 cm  $  ^{2}  $  .

# 2.4 Device characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were performed using a Bruker D8 GADDS Diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (Germany). The surface morphologies of the perovskite films and cross-sectional images of the PSCs were visually detected using a field-emission SEM (HITACHI, SU-8020, Japan). Fourier transformed infrared (FTIR) spectra were measured with a Bruker Vertex 70 (Germany). Contact angle measurements were carried out on an OCA20 instrument (Data-physics). The atomic force microscopy (AFM) images were obtained using a Veeco NanoScope IV with a silicon cantilever. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was implemented using a photoelectron spectrometer (ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The band structures of the perovskite films were evaluated using ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS, ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher, USA). Absorption spectra of perovskite films were characterized on a UV-visible (UV-vis) near-infrared (NIR) spectrophotometer (Ocean Optics Instrument, USA). Photoluminescence (PL, excitation at

(532 nm) spectra were studied using an Edinburgh Instruments Ltd. FLS980 spectrometer (UK) under 510 nm wavelength excitation, and time-resolved PL (TRPL) spectra were measured using a PicoQuant FluoQuant 300 (Germany). Current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics of the PSCs were measured using a solar simulator (AM 1.5G, SAN-EIELECTRIC XES-40S2-CE, Japan) calibrated using a standard silicon solar cell with a delay time of 10 ms, and start and end voltages from -0.1 to 1.2 V for the forward scan and 1.2 to -0.1 V for the reverse scan. All devices were tested in the reverse and forward scan directions following standard procedures at a rate of 10 mV s  $  ^{-1}  $  . External quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra of the PSCs were measured using a Q Test Station 500TI system (Crowntech, Inc. USA). The monochromatic light intensity was calibrated using a reference silicon detector. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurement was carried out by Zahner electrochemical workstation (IM6ex, Germany).

# 2.5 Synthesis of IDT-Cl

A mixture of IDT (0.308 g, 0.4 mmol), β-alanine (0.006 g, 0.07 mmol), and 5-chloroindolin-2-one (0.335 g, 2.0 mmol) was dissolved in a mixed solvent of 1,2-dichloroethane (50 mL) and methanol (25 mL). The reaction mixture was stirred and refluxed overnight. The resulting mixture was extracted with dichloromethane, washed with water, and dried over anhydrous MgSO₄. To visualize the impact of the IDT-Cl additive on the surface morphology of the perovskite absorber, top-view scanning electron microscopy (SEM) is performed (Figure 1b, c). After adding the IDT-Cl additive, the perovskite film becomes denser and the grain size is remarkably enlarged compared with that of the control sample. The statistical distribution of perovskite crystalline grain size indicates that the average value remarkably increases from  $ 0.49\mu \mathrm{m} $  for the pristine film to  $ 1.02\mu \mathrm{m} $  for the perovskite film containing IDT-Cl, suggesting that IDT-Cl influences the nucleation behavior of perovskite and boosts the enlargement of grains. The increase in grain size will reduce the area of grain boundaries, thereby reducing recombination centers in the grain boundaries or at the film surface. The morphological

changes after the introduction of IDT-Cl are tracked by AFM measurements (Figure 1d, e). The root-mean-square roughness of the perovskite surface significantly decreases from  $ 25.4\mathrm{nm} $  for the control sample to  $ 15.7\mathrm{nm} $  for the perovskite film with IDT-Cl doping, suggesting that the additive contributes to the generation of a smooth and dense film. In addition, the hydrophobic properties of the perovskite film surfaces are studied through water contact angle tests (Figure 1f, g). Due to the existence of IDT-Cl additive, the contact angle of the treated perovskite film substantially increases, probably because of the moisture-proofing shielding effect of the hydrophobic alkyl side chain in the molecule, improving the hydrophobic performance of the perovskite film [36].

The effect of the IDT-Cl molecule on the crystallization of the perovskite layer is evaluated by XRD measurements (Figure 2a). Compared with the pristine sample, the XRD peak position presents no obvious change after adding the IDT-Cl additive, indicating that IDT-Cl molecules do not induce a change in the perovskite crystal lattice [37]; however, the high intensity and the narrow full-width at half maximum (FWHM) of the observed diffraction peak demonstrate the improvement of crystallization quality and the enlargement of crystal size of the IDT-Cl-based perovskite film (Figure S6), in line with the SEM results.

To clarify the surface-coordination relationship of IDT-Cl additive and the elements in the perovskite, XPS measurements are performed. As presented in Figure 3a, two main peaks with saturated coordination of  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+}4\mathrm{f}^{7/2} $  and  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+}4\mathrm{f}^{5/2} $  located at 138.39 and  $ 143.27\mathrm{eV} $ , respectively, are observed in the control sample. In addition, two small peaks attached to the  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  peaks appear at 136.58 and  $ 141.46\mathrm{eV} $ , which are assigned to under-coordinated defects of metal  $ \mathrm{Pb}^0 $ . The existence of metal lead clusters will lead to the formation of deep defect energy levels, and then directly capture carriers in the perovskite layer [44]. After introducing the IDT-Cl molecule, the two main peaks of  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  exhibit an obvious shift in the low binding energy direction. Generally, when an atom has a high electron density, the binding energy of the core electron shifts to a lower level [27,45,46]. This binding energy shift behavior implies that there are strong electronic interactions between IDT-Cl and  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $ . Moreover, the  $ \mathrm{Pb}^0 $  defects existing in the control perovskite film completely vanish in the treated film, which may be due to the coordination effect between the  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  cation and the  $ \mathrm{C=O} $  on the amide in the 5-chloroindolin-2-one group. Such passivation effect and reduction of non-radiative recombination promote the performance of the perovskite film and photovoltaic devices. A change also occurs in the I 3d peak (Figure 3b), which shifts towards higher binding energy in the IDT-Cl-modified perovskite film, also demonstrating that an interaction exists between IDT-Cl and iodine-through hydrogen bonding that passivates iodine-related defects in the perovskite film. It is reported that the beneficial configuration of N-H and  $ \mathrm{C=O} $  in the molecule contributes to the bonding of  $ \mathrm{C=O} $  with antisite lead defects, greatly passivating the defect states of the perovskite absorber [47]. Figure 3c illustrates the XPS O 1s spectra of the pristine and IDT-Cl-treated perovskite films. IDT-Cl molecules are further confirmed to exist in the perovskite absorber because the peak at  $ 532.00\mathrm{eV} $  is related to the  $ \mathrm{C=O} $  group in the IDT-Cl molecule. Moreover, the coordination effect of IDT-Cl is proved by the intensity weakening of the peak at  $ 533.16\mathrm{eV} $ , which originates from adsorbed oxygen and moisture from the environment [48], suggesting that the IDT-Cl additive can promote the environmental stability of the perovskite film and even PSC devices. Figure 3d shows the N 1s spectra of

the perovskite films without and with IDT-Cl additive. In the control sample, and N 1s peak at  $ 400.8\mathrm{eV} $  is detected, which stems from the protonated amines  $ (\mathrm{NH}_3^+) $  in organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite. For the modified perovskite film, the peak shifts by  $ 0.2\mathrm{eV} $  in the high binding energy direction, demonstrating that the protonated amines are apt to combine with the  $ \mathrm{PbI}_3^- $ , which assists the controlling precursor solution nucleation and grain growth. Figure S10 shows the Cl 2p and S 2p spectra of the perovskite films without and with IDT-Cl additive. With doping, the existence of S and Cl peaks can be further confirmed, indicating that the IDT-Cl additive is successfully incorporated into the perovskite film. To further confirm the exact position of IDT-Cl molecules in perovskite films, an XPS depth profile is conducted to track the distribution of characteristic element S in IDT-Cl (Figure S11). The results demonstrate that the amount of S on the perovskite surface is slightly higher than that in the perovskite bulk, and the element S is evenly distributed in perovskite films regardless of the detection depth. Moreover, the energy-sidpersive spectrometry (EDS) line scanning results of cross-sectional perovskite absorber also indicate that the element S almost uniformly penetrate the perovskite layer (Figure S12). However, the introduction of IDT-Cl hardly changes the lattice constant of perovskite. Therefore, it is believed that IDT-Cl molecules are distributed at the grain boundary of perovskite grains, which is beneficial to the growth of perovskite grains and the passivation of defects at the grain boundary. The previous literature confirms that the incorporation of an appropriate amount of additive containing Cl into perovskite precursor solution successfully induces the formation of perovskite intermediates without thermal assistance [49], which significantly improves the vertical growth orientation, grain size, and phase crystallinity of the perovskite film and increases the carrier diffusion length [50]. In addition, the interaction between Cl atoms in the perovskite absorber and  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  ETL adjusts the interface electronic structure, promotes the interface coupling, and matches the conduction band energy level of  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  film [51].

To further explore the formed coordination bonds between IDT-Cl and perovskite film, pure IDT-Cl solid and IDT-Cl-modified perovskite films are analyzed by FTIR (Figure 3e). Many groups have studied the coordination bonds between compounds containing carbonyl and lead ions and discussed the C=O unit's passivation of lone pairs on the under-coordinated lead cation. Here, in the IDT-Cl sample, the stretching vibration related to the C=O bonds is found at   $  1,686 \, cm^{-1}  $   (Figure 3f). When the IDT-Cl is introduced into the perovskite film, the C=O vibration shifts to   $  1,710 \, cm^{-1}  $  , suggesting that a strong interaction between the IDT-Cl additive and the perovskite precursor solution is produced. # 4 Conclusions

In summary, IDT-Cl with multifunctional groups is designed and synthesized to efficiently inactivate the defects in the perovskite absorber. The incorporation of IDT-Cl as an additive contributes to the improvement of device performance and stability for  $ \mathrm{FA}_{0.85}\mathrm{MA}_{0.15}\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  PSCs. The designed IDT-Cl molecule containing high electron-density S promotes

crystallinity, enlarges the grain size, and smooths the surface topography of the perovskite absorber. The strong coordination interaction between the carbonyl group in the 5-chloroindolin-2-one unit and the undercoordinated lead ions in the perovskite absorber plays a crucial role in passivating defects and reducing the density of nonradiative recombination centers. Thanks to the high crystalline quality of the resulting perovskite absorber and appropriate energy-level matching between the perovskite and adjacent layers, the PCE of the IDT-Cl-modified device is boosted to 24.46%, as compared with the efficiency of the control PSC (22.48%). Correspondingly, the IDT-Cl containing hydrophobic alkyl side chains improves the humidity stability of the perovskite film and the environmental, thermal, and light stabilities of the device.
