# Bifunctional Interfacial Modification Engineering with Biomimetic Perfluoro-Copolymer-Enabled High-Efficiency and Moisture-Resistant Perovskite Solar Cells

Qian Ye,  $  ^{*,\#}  $   He Yang,  $  ^{\#}  $   Jin Zhang, Meijuan Long, Pengfei Guo, and Hongqiang Wang  $  ^{*}  $

Cite This: https://dx.doi.org/10.1021/acsaelm.0c00808

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: In recent years, interfacial technology encompassing attributes such as defect passivation and stability enhancement of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has captivated the solar research fraternity with their power conversion efficiency (PCE) being as high as 25.5%. In accordance with the theory of compounding two functional groups integrally into a single structure under relatively moderate formation conditions, this paper presents an efficacious interfacial passivation modification strategy for PSCs with well-controlled morphologies and passivated charged surface states using a bidentate ligand perfluorocarbon copolymer (PFOMA). The strategy involves self-assembly of biomimetic PFOMA on a perovskite surface due to the coordination binding between the catecholic OH groups of PFOMA and Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  . The time-resolved photoluminescence measurements demonstrated that the effective passivation of catecholic OH groups benefits from the inhibition of the photogenerated carrier recombination. Furthermore, the long fluorocarbon alkyl chains of PFOMA bestow the perovskite surface with higher resistance against

moisture. Hence, the resulting PFOMA-modified PSC can accomplish an above-average efficiency of about 20.66% (with an average PCE of 19.71%) with a notable increment in the open-circuit voltage of about 40 mV compared to the control voltage (1.12 mV). Particularly, the PFOMA-modified PSC exhibits a remarkable enhancement in the moisture stability factor under a relative humidity of   $  70 \pm 5\%  $  , retaining   $  >90\%  $   of their initial efficacies along with considerably upgraded device performance (an average PCE of 19.71% and a champion device PCE of 20.66% were achieved) and an enhanced moisture stability factor even under a relative humidity of   $  70 \pm 5\%  $  . The highly proficient passivation strategy demonstrated in this work can help the future exploration of this function-oriented synthesis strategy and can pave the path for the universal application of PSCs employing the modification of polymers and more organic species.

KEYWORDS: perfluoro-copolymer, interfacial engineering, defect passivation, perovskite solar cells, moisture stability

# 1. INTRODUCTION

The perovskite families typically known to have a crystal structure similar to that of calcium titanium oxide, especially the metal–organic hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs), are reckoned to be the most promising photovoltaic candidates  $  ^{1,2}  $  for the next-generation solar energy applications, attributable to their large light absorption coefficient,  $  ^{3}  $  effective charge carrier transport,  $  ^{4}  $  intrinsic long carrier recombination lifetime,  $  ^{5}  $  and a relatively low reproduction cost.  $  ^{6}  $  Predominantly, a planar PSC device comprises of two charge transporting layers (an electron transport layer and a hole transport layer, abbreviated as ETL and HTL below, respectively) interposed by a light absorber film—perovskite film and two electrodes placed outward with their interfacial layer in between. Recently, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs has been recorded to have soared appreciably to about 25.5%,  $  ^{7}  $  deemed to be analogous to that of the crystalline silicon and thin-film solar cells. However, the hybrid perovskite structure tends to undergo a hydrolysis reaction in the presence of

Meanwhile, acute carrier trapping triggered by critical level defects often leads to nonradiative recombination loss, which further curtails the carrier lifetime producing a decreased open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) and fill factor (FF).  $  ^{[11]}  $   In addition, the loss is further intensified by the radiative recombination of electrons and holes by means of the direct contact of ETL and HTL at pinholes at the perovskite surface and in the GBs.

Several recent studies have provided methodically comprehensive analyses of the principles for the pinhole and defect formation process. The antisite   $  I^{-}  $  tends to bond with the neighboring   $  Pb^{2+}  $ , with an uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $  outstanding on the surface, while an   $  MA^{+}  $  vacancy may develop during the evaporation process of MAI in the annealing stage.  $  ^{12}  $  Yin et al. proposed that uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $  may possibly be a dominant defect for recombination on the perovskite surface,  $  ^{13}  $  while the   $  MA^{+}  $  vacancies are reckoned to trigger the film degradation.  $  ^{12}  $  Most recently, Niu et al. revealed that commonly the over-crystallization of the lead iodide (  $  PbI_{2}  $  ) compound is associated with pinholes and nonuniform film coverage.  $  ^{14}  $

The perovskite photovoltaic system inherently possesses another shortcoming called the hysteresis phenomenon, which suggests that the PCE measured at the forward bias may fall behind the reverse bias or that they simply do not coincide. Carrier trapping occurring at the surface defects appears to be the primary genesis of hysteresis.  $  ^{15-17}  $   The hole carriers trapped by the charged defects at the interface of the perovskite and the HTL tend to accumulate at the interface,  $  ^{18}  $   thus holding back the effective charge extraction by hole-

transporting materials. In addition, it is highly likely that the organic as well as the halide ions in the perovskite film undergo migration at GBs, which is owing to their relatively low activation energies, causing polarization of the light absorber layer and charge accumulation at interfaces nearby, resulting in not only hysteresis but also accelerated degradation of the perovskite under the conditions of thermal and light stress.  $  ^{18,19}  $   Therefore, it is a matter of paramount importance to develop an interfacial modification method that is capable of passivating the defects at both the surface and the GBs of the perovskite film, which proves competent in blocking the ion migration as well as the water and oxygen invasion.

It has been broadly established that the polymer and other hydrophobic organic species emerge as being highly competent in PSC interfacial engineering. Jiang et al. doped the perovskite film with a p-type semiconductor polymer J71 in order to attain a selective carrier shift as well as augmented device stability and demonstrated that the polymer dopant need not possess a matching energy level with that of the perovskite substance for efficacious passivation of the grain surface.  $  ^{20}  $   Yang et al. fabricated highly potent, inexpensive, and stable devices utilizing perovskite films reformed and passivated by functional polymers, such as poly(4-vinylpyridine).  $  ^{21}  $   Grätzel's group demonstrated that the hydrophobic species 1-adamantylamine and its halide derivatives are impactful agents for perovskite/HTL modification and can achieve outstanding device efficiency with good moisture and operational stability via readily antisolvent or solution methods.  $  ^{22,23}  $   Several other polymer molecules, like polystyrene  $  ^{24}  $   or poly(ethylene oxide),  $  ^{25}  $   were also employed to function as a defect passivation/water screening interlayer between the perovskite and the ETL/HTL.  $  ^{26,27}  $

However, in most cases, the polymer dopants tend to be utilized as additives or interlayers and exhibit relative single function attributable to the constraint devised by the simple molecule structure. It will be further beneficial if the polymer-based interfacial technology effectuates defect passivation, morphology enhancement, and water screening in an integrated manner simultaneously. According to recent investigations,  $  ^{28,29}  $  the dominant vacancy for nonradiative recombination—Pb  $  ^{2+}  $ , with an empty 6p orbital, by nature forms a Lewis acid, which can be coordinated and passivated via Lewis base ligands, like amino or hydroxyl. Herein, we have synthesized a perfluorocarbon copolymer (PFOMA, as shown in Figure 1c) and dissolved it in an antisolvent chlorobenzene as a perovskite-HTL interface-modifying agent. The PFOMA molecule contains a hydrophobic fluorine-carbon (C–F) chain as well as a catecholic OH group (Ph-OH), which forms a chelate with uncoordinated Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  by means of the donor atom O, and hence, considerably passivates the surface defects at the interface of the perovskite and HTL. Additionally, the ion migration and humidity or oxygen-induced device degeneracy seems to be incredibly suppressed.

Consequently, we fabricated FTO/TiO $ _2 $ /Cs $ _{0.05} $ FA $ _{0.81} $ MA $ _{0.14} $ PbI $ _{2.55} $ Br $ _{0.45} $ -PFOMA/spiro-OMeTAD/Au structured devices—as shown in Figure 1a—with the average PCE up-surging from about 18.10% (control group) to 19.71% (optimized concentration group) and a champion device exceeding an efficacy of 20.66% under reverse scanning, along with commendable moisture resistance. Furthermore, the theoretical mechanism of this interfacial modifying agent was demonstrated using various characterizations and it appears inevitable that this rather unsophisticated implementation of polymer engineering can pave the way for universal commercialization of modified PSCs and numerous other photovoltaic devices.

# 2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

2.1. Materials. All of the referred materials were purchased generally from Sigma-Aldrich and directly used as initially received unless stated otherwise. Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass square substrates (1.5 cm × 1.5 cm) were purchased from Pilkington, 2,2',7,7'-Tetrakis[N,N-di(4-methoxyphenyl)amino]-9,9'-spiro bifluorene (spiro-OMeTAD, ≥99.8% purity), 4-tert-butylpyridine (tBP, ≥99.9% purity), and lithium-bis (trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (Li-TFSI, ≥99.9% purity) were obtained from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp.

2.2. Synthesis of PFOMA. A total of 0.94 g of FOMA (2 mmol), 44 mg of DOPAMA (0.2 mmol), and 3.6 mg of AIBN (0.022 mmol) were dissolved in 20 mL of acetonitrile. The obtained solution was heated and stirred at 75 °C under nitrogen and kept stirring for 24 h. The reaction mixture was cooled at room temperature, and the precipitate was then collected by filtration, repeatedly washed with methanol for purification, and desiccated under reduced pressure to obtain the copolymer PFOMA (Figure S1). For preparing PFOMA-chlorobenzene (PFOMA-CB) antisolvent, an appropriate amount of PFOMA in chlorobenzene was dissolved and then filtered with a 220 nm organic filter to obtain certain concentrations of the antisolvent.

2.3. PFOMA-Modified Device Fabrication. A  $ \sim 50 $  nm-thick  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  compact layer was prepared on FTO using the chemical bath deposition method. A concentration of  $ 0.06\mathrm{M} $  CsI,  $ 0.97\mathrm{M} $  FAI,  $ 0.97\mathrm{M} $   $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ ,  $ 0.17\mathrm{M} $  MABr, and  $ 0.17\mathrm{M} $   $ \mathrm{PbBr}_2 $  were mixed and stirred in a mixture of (4:1 V/V) DMF and DMSO and then heated at  $ 55^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 2\mathrm{h} $ . The resulting solution was coated onto the  $ \mathrm{FTO / TiO_2} $  substrate via a two-step spin-coating process at 1000 and  $ 4000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for 10 and  $ 30~s $ , respectively. During the second step,  $ 200~\mu \mathrm{L} $  of chlorobenzene with different concentrations of PFOMA (0.05, 0.10, and  $ 0.15\mathrm{mg}/ $  mL) was immediately poured onto the spinning substrate. The

substrate was put onto a hotplate for 60 min at a constant temperature of 100 °C, forming a   $  Cs_{0.05}FA_{0.81}MA_{0.14}PbI_{2.55}Br_{0.45}  $  -PFOMA thin film, and then the HTM solution was spin-coated onto the as-obtained perovskite-PFOMA film at 4000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, an 80 nm-thick Au electrode was physically deposited onto the HTL via thermal evaporation.

2.4. Characterizations and Measurements. For the current density–voltage (J–V) characteristics measurement, the simulated AM 1.5G (100 mW/cm  $  ^{2}  $  ) irradiation was generated using a solar simulator (Oriel 67005, 150 W Solar Simulator) with a xenon lamp. A Keithley 2420 Source Meter was used for the J–V measurement. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were conducted using an Axis Supra (Kratos). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded using a PANalytical X'pert PRO equipped with a diffraction beam monochromator, with a conventional cobalt target X-ray tube set to 40 kV and 30 mA. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the samples were obtained using a field-emission SEM (FEI Verios G4). Conducting-atomic force microscopy (c-AFM) measurements for the samples were performed using an Asylum Research MFP-3D Origin AFM. IPCE (Incident Photon-to-Current Efficiency) spectra were obtained from the external quantum efficiency curves characterized with a Newport QE measurement kit. The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra were collected by applying a pulse laser as the optical excitation source (excitation wavelength: 470 nm, Horiba Fluorolog FL-3), and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) experiments were performed by excitation at 470 nm simultaneously. Moisture stability measurements of the PSC devices without encapsulation were performed in a constant temperature and humidity incubator in the dark. PCEs of the devices were periodically measured under AM 1.5 G simulated sunlight in ambient air.

# 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The process of synthesizing PFOMA and the preparation method of PFOMA-CB solutions is illustrated in the Experimental Section. Essentially, the PFOMA agent was synthesized by a free radical copolymerization and thereafter dissolved in chlorobenzene with various concentrations in the order of 0.05, 0.10, and  $ 0.15\mathrm{mg / mL} $ , respectively. During the second stage of the two-step spin-coating,  $ 200~\mu \mathrm{L} $  of the PFOMA-CB solution was effused onto the substrate brushed with the perovskite solution. The final molecule and the device structure are shown in Figure 1a-c. Techniques like XPS were employed to reveal the existence of PFOMA on the surface area of the perovskite. As shown in Figure 1d,e, the characteristic peaks of O 1s and F 1s were observed at around 532 and  $ 689\mathrm{eV} $ , respectively, in the PFOMA spectra, while the control group spectra displayed no evidence of these two featured elements. We further etched the perovskite film by 10 nm beneath the surface and employed XPS to investigate the distribution of PFOMA within the perovskite interior. As illustrated in Figure S4, the PFOMA-modified group films showed the characteristic peak of F 1s (around  $ 689\mathrm{eV} $ ) on both surface and sub  $ 10\mathrm{nm} $  regions, which means that the PFOMA molecule introduced by the antisolvent can penetrate throughout the whole perovskite layer. Furthermore, XRD analysis was undertaken to validate the distribution of PFOMA in the perovskite films. Figure S7 shows the comparison between the XRD patterns of perovskite film with or without PFOMA modification in the Supporting Information. As shown in Figure S7c, both control group films and modified device films illustrate strong diffraction peaks for the tetragonal phase perovskite at approximately 14.2, 28.4, and  $ 31.8^{\circ} $ , while no evident peak position shift or relative intensity change was observed after the modification, indicating that both the films exhibit good crystallinity. The passivation effect of the bidentate ligand PFOMA was further advocated by the dark I-V curve measurement and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), as shown in Figure 5c,d. The dark current curves represent the resistance of electron transport from the perovskite layer to HTL, which acts as a reverse pathway to the working conditions of the photocurrent. As presented in the bottom half of Figure 5c, the PFOMA-treated device measured in the dark moves to a higher bias voltage compared to the control group device, proving that the shunt impedance has improved after modification. reflects the response to the low-frequency disturbance, which is associated with the combined impedance for electron and hole transport at the interfaces. The device after modification demonstrates appreciably larger impedance for electrons at the PFOMA-treated interface, suggesting a reduced chance of carrier recombination.

Considering the above measurements, an in-depth analysis of the PFOMA function mechanism was conducted. As illustrated in Figure 4h, the PFOMA formed at the perovskite and HTL interface could transform into a potential barrier capable of obstructing the electron transport from the ETL and the perovskite, whereas the hole injection to the HTL remains unaffected, even though the large-concentration dielectric PFOMA would impede the flow of both the carriers (electrons and holes). As a result, it appears that the photoelectric performance was highly influenced by low-concentration addition of PFOMA, curtailing both carrier recombination and charge accumulation.

Noticeably, the bidentate ligand PFOMA implements two essential functions for device improvement: both chelation-assisted passivation for charged defects and moisture inhibition from an outward surface via its hydrophobic fluorocarbon chain, which are presumed to greatly enhance the device durability against water and oxygen invasion. The moisture durability performance enhancement can be interpreted as the following mechanism. The PFOMA with long fluorocarbon chains at the perovskite and spiro-OMeTAD interface isolated the moisture-vulnerable part of the PSC device from direct contact with water; and the defect passivation effect of PFOMA curbed the chance of water and oxygen degradation of the perovskite film as defects like MA  $  ^{+}  $  could breed combination sites for water, oxygen, and perovskite.  $  ^{12}  $  This mechanism is further verified by XRD tracking of the perovskite film composition. On recording the same after 30 days, the discrepancy appeared even larger, indicating that the modified device exhibits evidently superior water repellency than that of the pristine device without the key-role PFOMA.

As mentioned above, the chelant PFOMA is synthesized by radical copolymerization with two monomers: DOPAMA with two phenolic hydroxyls and FOMA with a long C–F chain. Apparently, this modification agent implements a bifunctional effect to PSC devices due to its function-oriented structure: two phenolic hydroxyls chelate with the uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   for the purpose of defect passivation, while the C–F chain is positioned outward to inhibit moisture and oxygen invasion. This innovative functional group-combination strategy led to a remarkable improvement in device performance. Particularly, this function-oriented strategy for synthesis does not seem to be limited to the perovskite–HTL interface modification or combining defect passivation and water repellency. Photostability of a PSC device emerging from the susceptible nature of   $  TiO_{2}  $   to UV illumination is another key issue for the application of an unencapsulated device, and it has been reported that sinapoyl malate—an ester derivative of sinapic acid—employed to moderate the   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer can effectively promote the UV stability of a PSC device.  $  ^{34}  $   Therefore, it is an added advantage to combine this featured organic species with other electrode/ETL or ETL/perovskite interface modifying organic species through copolymerization while maintaining both the functional groups.
