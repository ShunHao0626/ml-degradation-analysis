# Abstract

A fused-ring electron acceptor based on indacenodithiophene (IDIC) was used to replace   $  TiO_{2}  $   and work as an electron transport layer in the planar n-i-p perovskite solar cells. IDIC improves perovskite crystallinity and film quality due to its hydrophobicity and incompatible wetting surface. IDIC facilitates electron extraction and transport due to its high mobility and suitable energy levels matched with perovskite. IDIC reduces charge recombination in the devices due to trap passivation at perovskite surface. The IDIC-based devices exhibit a champion power conversion efficiency of 19.1%, which is higher than that of the   $  TiO_{2}  $  -based devices (17.4%). Moreover, the device stability is significantly improved by IDIC.

# Introduction

Organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite materials based on methylammonium lead halides (CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ PbX $ _3 $ , X = Cl $ ^- $ , Br $ ^- $ , I $ ^- $ ) exhibit strong and broad absorption in visible region, ambipolar charge transport, long charge diffusion length and lifetime, and solution processability. Over the past few years, power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have encouragingly boosted from 3.8% to 22.1%. PSCs is regarded as a potential alternative to the conventional silicon-based photovoltaics. $ ^{1-4} $  PSCs typically have a sandwich structure of perovskite layer, electron transport layer (ETL) and hole transport layer (HTL) with conventional n-i-p and inverted p-i-n configurations. $ ^5 $

For n-i-p PSCs,   $  TiO_{2}  $   is the most commonly used ETL.  $  ^{2,3,6}  $   However, the   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETL usually requires high temperature sintering/annealing to improve crystallinity and charge carrier mobility.  $  ^{1,6-8}  $   This complicated preparation process is not compatible with the production of large-area devices based on flexible plastic substrate. Meanwhile, UV sensitivity of   $  TiO_{2}  $   is harmful to the stability of the devices.  $  ^{9-11}  $   Therefore, it's necessary to replace or modify the   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer.  $  ^{12-14}  $   On the other hand, organic semiconductors can be solution processed at room temperature and is used as an ETL in PSCs. For example, fullerene derivatives have already been introduced into n-i-p PSCs as the ETL, which exhibited PCEs of 12-19%.  $  ^{15-21}  $   Nevertheless, fullerene derivatives suffer from some disadvantages, such as limited energy level variability, photochemical instability and high cost. Recently, several non-fullerene organic semiconductors have been used as the ETL in n-i-p PSCs, which exhibited PCEs of 16-17%.  $  ^{22-24}  $

There have been three reports on FREA application in PSCs as interfacial layer to modify the surface of perovskite  $  ^{33, 34}  $   or TiO  $  _{2}  $   films.  $  ^{35}  $

In this work, we report the first example of using FREA as an ETL in the n-i-p PSCs to replace   $  TiO_{2}  $  . Our previous work demonstrated that IDIC (Fig. 1a) exhibited excellent performance in OSCs.  $  ^{36-38}  $   Since IDIC has good solution processability, high electron mobility of   $  1.1 \times 10^{-3}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $   V  $  ^{-1}  $   s  $  ^{-1}  $   and suitable LUMO energy level matched with the conduction band of perovskite layer, we used it as ETL in the n-i-p PSCs and compared with   $  TiO_{2}  $  -based devices. Devices based on the IDIC ETL showed an champion PCE of 19.1%, superior to that of the devices based on   $  TiO_{2}  $   (17.4%). The PCE of 19.1% is the highest value reported for n-i-p PSCs based on non-fullerene ETL (the reported best was 17.66%)  $  ^{22}  $   and rivals fullerene-based n-i-p PSCs (the reported best was 19.1%).  $  ^{19}  $   Furthermore, the stability and hysteresis of IDIC-based devices has been significantly improved against the   $  TiO_{2}  $  -based devices.

The LUMO (-3.9 eV) and HOMO (-5.7 eV) energy levels of IDIC are calculated from cyclic voltammetry measurement.  $  ^{36}  $   The LUMO level of IDIC is same as the conduction band (-3.9 eV) of   $  TiO_{2}  $   and well matches the conduction band of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}(Cl)  $   (-3.9 eV), indicating a favorable electron transport from   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}(Cl)  $   to IDIC (Fig. 1c). Meanwhile, the HOMO level of IDIC is much deeper than the valence band of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}(Cl)  $   (-5.4 eV), which benefits the hole blocking.

UV-visible absorption intensity of perovskite films on the   $  TiO_{2}  $   and IDIC substrates is same (Fig. 2a). The crystal structures of the perovskite films were studied by X-ray diffraction (XRD). As shown in Fig. 2b, both perovskite films on the   $  TiO_{2}  $   and IDIC substrates show intense diffraction peaks at 14.2° and 28.3°, assigned to the (110) and (220) plane of tetragonal phase perovskite, respectively, in accordance with the previous work.  $  ^{39}  $   However, the diffraction peaks of the perovskite film on the IDIC substrate are much stronger than those on the   $  TiO_{2}  $   substrate, indicating the improvement in the crystallinity of the perovskite film, which is important for high performance devices.  $  ^{40}  $   The top-view SEM images of the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}(Cl)  $   film on   $  TiO_{2}  $   and IDIC substrates are shown in Fig. 2c and d. High-quality and homogeneous perovskite film with larger crystalline domains is formed on IDIC substrates, which is beneficial to enhancing the device performance.  $  ^{41}  $   To explain this, we investigated the wettability of different ETL substrates. The water contact angles of   $  TiO_{2}  $   and IDIC are 41.2° and 83.5°, respectively (Fig. S3). Apparently, compared with   $  TiO_{2}  $ , the incompatible wetting surface of IDIC substrate to perovskite will provide a weaker surface tension and dragging force for perovskite precursor solution to form larger perovskite crystalline domains.  $  ^{42}  $

To investigate the charge extraction, we measured the steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved PL spectra of glass/TiO $ _2 $ /CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ PbI $ _3 $ (Cl) and glass/IDIC/CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ PbI $ _3 $ (Cl) (Fig. 3a and Fig. 3b). The PL intensity of the perovskite film on glass/IDIC substrate is much weaker than that of the perovskite film on glass/TiO $ _2 $  substrate, indicating more efficient charge transfer at the interface of IDIC/perovskite. Meanwhile, a blue-shift of PL peak from 779 to 773 nm was observed for the perovskite film on glass/IDIC substrate relative to the perovskite film on glass/TiO $ _2 $  substrate, which reveals that IDIC has the ability to fill and passivate the trap states on the surface of perovskite film leading to the recovery of the bandgap and blue-shifts of PL peak. $ ^{33,43} $  The time-resolved PL decay was measured with the peak emission at 780 nm as shown in Fig. 3b. Fitting the data with two-component exponential decay (here, the longer lifetime was used for comparison) yields the lifetime of carriers. The PL lifetime of the perovskite film on glass/TiO $ _2 $  substrate is 98 ns, while the PL lifetime of the perovskite film on glass/IDIC substrate is substantially shortened to 34 ns, suggesting a faster electron extraction from the perovskite film to the organic ETL.

To further explore the charge carrier behaviors in entire devices based on different ETLs, we carried out the transient photovoltage (TPV) and photocurrent (TPC) measurements, which are widely used to study the charge recombination and transfer dynamics of solar cells respectively.  $  ^{43-45}  $   The TPV and TPC measurements are performed under dark condition, excited by a 532 nm pulse laser with certain intensity, and the data are fitted by bi-exponential decay function. As shown in Fig. 4a, the IDIC based devices exhibit a decay time of photovoltage (0.15 ms), which is longer than that of the control devices based on   $  TiO_{2}  $   (0.13 ms). The prolonged lifetime suggests that less carrier recombination takes place in the devices with IDIC. From the TPC measurement (Fig. 4b), the IDIC

The photovoltaic parameters of the modified devices based on IDIC ETL with different concentrations are summarized in Table S1. The optimal IDIC concentration is 4 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  . The device performances of PSCs with different ETLs under simulated solar illumination of AM1.5G 100 mW/cm  $  ^{2}  $   are shown in Fig. 5a and Fig. 5b. The IDIC based devices exhibit a best PCE of 19.1% with a short circuit current density (  $  J_{SC}  $  ) of 23.0 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , an open circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) of 1.08 V, and a fill factor (FF) of 77.0%, which is higher than the TiO  $  _{2}  $   based devices with a maximum PCE of 17.4%,   $  J_{SC}  $   of 22.2 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ,   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.05 V and FF of 74.1% (Table 1). The IDIC based devices show reduced hysteresis with the reverse and forward scan PCEs of 19.1% and 16.9%, respectively, while the TiO  $  _{2}  $   based devices exhibit a hysteresis with the reverse and forward scan PCEs of 17.4% and 14.2%, respectively. Fig. 5c shows the external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra of the devices. The IDIC based PSCs show stronger photocurrent response from 300 to 800 nm than the TiO  $  _{2}  $   based devices, which is consistent with the results in   $  J_{SC}  $  . Meanwhile, the integrated   $  J_{SC}  $   from the EQE spectra matches the measured   $  J_{SC}  $   well (Table 1). Fig. S4a shows the steady-state current density and power output measured at the maximum power point of the PSCs with different ETLs. The device with IDIC ETL exhibits a stabilized efficiency of 16.9%, superior to that of the device with TiO  $  _{2}  $   ETL (15.5%). Both stabilized efficiencies match well with those from J-V measurement. Moreover, the IDIC based PSCs show good reproducibility in the PCE histogram of 20 devices (Fig. S4b).

The stability tests of PSCs with different ETLs were performed under ambient conditions (humidity about 40% and temperature of 25 °C) without encapsulation, and the stability curves are shown in Fig. 5d. The PCEs of the devices based on TiO₂ ETL remain about 30% of their initial values over 7 days, while the devices based on IDIC ETL remain about 85% of their initial PCEs during the same test period. The devices based on IDIC ETL exhibit much better stability than the TiO₂ based devices. According to the XRD and SEM measurements of perovskite films grown on different substrates, larger crystalline domains with better crystallinity of perovskite film on IDIC substrate was observed. The high-quality and compact perovskite film can prevent moisture from penetrating into the interior of perovskite layer, suppress decomposition of the perovskite material and improve device stability.

# Conclusions

We demonstrate the first example of using FREA as an ETL to replace  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  in planar n-i-p PSCs. PSCs based on IDIC show a champion PCE of  $ 19.1\% $ , higher than that of the devices based on  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  (17.4%). The PCE of  $ 19.1\% $  is the highest value reported for n-i-p PSCs based on organic ETL. XRD and SEM measurements confirm that IDIC ETL is beneficial to perovskite crystalline grain growth due to its hydrophobicity and incompatible wetting surface, enhancing perovskite crystallinity and film quality. Steady-state and time-resolved PL spectra as well as TPC measurement reveal that the IDIC ETL facilitates efficient electron extraction and transport from perovskite layer to ETL due to its high mobility and suitable energy levels matched with perovskite. TPV measurement suggests that less charge recombination takes place in the IDIC based devices. Benefited from the high-quality

This work demonstrates that FREAs can be the promising alternative to  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  as the ETL in planar n-i-p PSCs to realize simple preparation, high efficiency and excellent stability.

# Experimental

# Materials

The molecule IDIC was synthesized by one-step simple Knoevenagel condensation, according to our previously published procedure.  $  ^{36}  $   Unless stated otherwise, all reagents were obtained commercially and were used without further purification. PbI  $  _{2}  $   (99.999%), N,N-dimethylformamide (99.99%), isopropanol (99.99%) and chlorobenzene (99.9%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Aminomethane (CP) and ethanol (AC) were purchased from Beijing Chemical Works. Hydrogen iodide (57%) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. Spiro-OMeTAD was purchased from Luminescence Technology Corp..

# Device fabrication

PSCs were fabricated with a structure of ITO/ETL/CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ PbI $ _3 $ (Cl)/spiro-OMeTAD/Au. The indium tin oxide (ITO) glass was pre-cleaned in an ultrasonic bath of detergent water, ultrapure water, acetone and isopropanol successively. For reference devices based on TiO $ _2 $ , a TiO $ _2 $  nanoparticle suspension in ethanol (5.3 mg mL $ ^{-1} $ ) with an appropriate amount of titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetylacetonate) was spin coated onto an ITO glass substrate and annealed at 150 °C for 30 min in air to form an ETL (40 nm thick). Next, the substrates were transferred into a

9

Journal of Materials Chemistry A

Page 10 of 19

nitrogen-filled glove box. For the IDIC-based devices, the ITO glass was treated with ultraviolet ozone for 20 min and transferred into a nitrogen-filled glove box. The IDIC solution (4 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  in chloroform) was subsequently spin-coated onto the ITO substrates with 4000 rpm for 60 s and annealed at 70 °C for 10 min. Then a solution of PbI  $  _{2}  $  (dissolved in DMF, 450 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $ ) was spin-coated on TiO  $  _{2}  $  and IDIC substrates at 3000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 70 °C for 20 min. The mixture of CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}  $  I (50 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $ ) and CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}  $  Cl (5 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $ ) dissolved in isopropanol was spin-coated onto the dried PbI  $  _{2}  $  layer at 3000 rpm for 30 s. The obtained films were annealed at 150 °C for 15 min in air. The doped spiro-OMeTAD was spin-coated at 3000 rpm for 30 s at a concentration of 80 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  with the addition of 35 μL Li-TFSI/acetonitrile (260 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $ ) and 28 μL 4-tert-butylpyridine. Finally, an 80 nm gold layer was deposited on the top of spiro-OMeTAD through shadow masks via thermal evaporation under high vacuum (~2 × 10  $  ^{-6}  $  Torr).

# Measurements

The current density–voltage   $  (J-V)  $   characteristics of photovoltaic devices were obtained using a Keithley 2400 source-measure unit under simulated sunlight from an Oriel 300 solar simulator, and the light intensity was calibrated using a KG-5 Si diode. The effective area of each cell was 0.102 cm² defined by masks for all the photovoltaic devices discussed in this work. The EQE spectrum was measured using a Solar Cell Spectral Response Measurement System QE-R3011 (Enlitech Co., Ltd). The light intensity at each wavelength was calibrated using a standard single crystal Si photovoltaic cell. The UV-visible absorption spectra were measured using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (Agilent 8453). AFM images were obtained by a Multimode 8 scanning probe microscopy (Bruker). The top-view and cross-sectional SEM images were measured using a scanning electron microscope

(Hitachi S4800). The XRD spectra were obtained from a D/MAX 2400 diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (Rigaku). Steady-state PL and time-resolved transient-state PL were measured by FLS980 (Edinburgh Instruments Ltd) with an excitation at 470 nm. Transient photovoltage (TPV) and photocurrent (TPC) measurements was performed with a self-designed system excited by a 532 nm (Brio, 20 Hz, 4 ns) pulse laser. A digital oscilloscope (Tektronix, DPO 7104) was used to record the photocurrent or photovoltage decay process with a sampling resistor of 50 Ω or 1 MΩ, respectively. Static contact angles were measured on a Dataphysics OCA25 contact-angle system at ambient temperature (the test liquid is water). The thickness of the IDIC layer was measured using Bruker Dektak-XT. All the measurements of the solar cells were performed under ambient atmosphere at room temperature without encapsulation.
