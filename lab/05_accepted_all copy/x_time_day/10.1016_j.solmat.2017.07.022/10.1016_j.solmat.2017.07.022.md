# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with alkylammonium lead halides like   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   for the light absorption enhancement have attracted considerable attention because of their low fabrication cost, high power conversion efficiency (PCE), high light absorption coefficient and long charge diffusion length [1–7]. The morphology of the perovskite layer is the key factor for high photovoltaic performance, and it is quite sensitive to the experimental conditions. Various methods have been developed for controlling the crystal morphology [6,8–10]. Right now, over 20% certified efficiency has been achieved [11,12]. Among them, the planar PSCs with poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS) as the hole transport material and [6,6]-phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) as the electron transport material attract much attention due to their good reproducibility and hysteresis free nature [13–15].

To date, one major problem during the perovskite thin film formation is the film quality which is controlled by nucleation and crystal growth behaviors of the perovskite material. The photovoltaic performance of planar PSCs strongly depends on the perovskite layer. Optimal perovskite morphology can maximize the light absorption as well as charge generation and transport   $  [16–19]  $  . # ABSTRACT

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted much attention due to their high power conversion efficiency (PCE) and low cost fabrication. Since the PCE is critically related to the perovskite crystal morphology and quality, controlling the perovskite crystal formation is becoming more important. In this work, we chose PDDA polyelectrolyte as the crystal capping agent to modulate the perovskite thin film formation. The PDDA capping agent significantly enhanced the short-circuit current  $ (J_{\mathrm{sc}}) $  and fill factor (FF) of the corresponding PSCs, which produced an optimal PCE of  $ 14.1\% $  compared to  $ 12.5\% $  of control device without the modifying agent. The effect of PDDA agent is examined by various techniques. It was found that improved surface morphology, enhanced crystallinity, and decreased grain boundary related defects are contributed to the performance and stability enhancement.

Since the thin film formation critically depends on the solution concentration, precursor composition, solvent choice, interface energy, and deposition temperature,   $  [4,18,24,25]  $   it is expected to control the thin film crystallization during the thin film deposition and annealing.

It is well known that the perovskite thin films have large surface area and many grain boundaries, which makes them unstable towards the moisture from thermodynamic aspects   $  [26]  $  . It is considered to be favourable to apply chemical engineering approach to reduce this effect, since from chemical aspect, the perovskite grains could form hydrogen bonding or coordinate bond with other compounds   $  [27,28]  $  . It is expected that proper chemical modifications of the perovskite precursor could improve the device efficiency and stability.

Previously, Masi et. al optimized the perovskite film morphology and crystallinity by implanting the perovskite into polymers to form nanocomposite. In this method, the polymer could act as the template for controlling the perovskite crystal domain growth. However, the efficiency obtained is only around 3%, much lower than the literature reported [29]. Recently, Li et al. reported that phosphonic acid ammonium additive can act as a crosslinker between neighbouring grains in the perovskite structure through strong hydrogen bonding of -PO(OH)  $  _{2}  $  and -NH  $  _{3}^{+}  $  terminal groups to the perovskite surface [27]. Meantime, it would not change the crystal structure since the organic cation is much larger, and it is not possible to occupy the lattice during crystal growth.

# 2. Experimental part

# 2.1. Materials and chemicals

Patterned ITO glass substrates (10 Ω/□) were supplied by NSG group. PEDOT:PSS (Clevios P VP Al 4083) was purchased from Heraeus Holding GmbH. Methylammonium iodide (MAI) was obtained from Dyesol Ltd. [6,6]-phenyl-C $  _{61}  $ -butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) was bought from Nano-C Inc. Other materials, including lead (II) iodide (PbI $  _{2}  $ , 99% purity), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, anhydrous, ≥ 99.9% purity), γ-butyrolactone (GBL, ≥ 99% purity), LiF (≥ 99% purity), chlorobenzene (anhydrous, 99.8% purity), were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich. PDDA was freeze dried before use. All materials are used without further purification.

# 2.2. Fabrication and characterization of perovsktie solar cells

ITO glass substrates were cleaned sequentially in detergent, deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol by sonication for 20 min. After drying under   $  N_{2}  $   stream, substrates were further treated with UV-ozone for 15 min. A PEDOT:PSS layer with a thickness of   $  \sim40  $   nm was prepared by spin coating on ITO substrates at 7000 rpm for 1 min and subsequently annealed at 140 °C for 15 min in air. The substrates with PEDOT:PSS were then transferred into a glovebox filled with highly pure   $  N_{2}  $  . The perovskite layer was formed by spin coating a solution consisting 1.4 M PbI  $  _{2}  $   and 1.4 M MAI and different amounts of PDDA in cosolvent of DMSO:GBL (3:7 vol. ratio) at 1000 rpm for 20 s, and then at 4000 rpm for 60 s. After 45 s of the start of the spin coating, 200   $  \mu  $  l toluene was dripped. Then, they were annealed at 100 °C for 20 min. The thickness of the perovskite thin films was around 300 nm. The PCBM layer with a thickness of about 55 nm was deposited by spin coating a chlorobenzene solution of 20 mg/mL PCBM at 2000 rpm for 40 s. After that, 0.5 mg/mL BCP/IPA was spin coated at 2000 rpm onto the PCBM layer. The devices were completed by 100 nm-thick Ag thermal deposition in a vacuum of   $  <1\times10^{-6}  $   Torr. Each device had an area of 0.12 cm  $  ^{2}  $  . The photovoltaic stability of PSCs was investigated by storing the unencapsulated devices in   $  N_{2}  $   cabinet conditions (10% RH) for one week. The photovoltaic performance of the PSCs was tested in air with a computer-programmed Keithley 2400 source/meter and a Newport's Oriel class A solar simulator, which simulated the AM1.5 sunlight with energy density of 100 mW/cm  $  ^{2}  $   and was certified to the JIS C 8912 standard. Incident photon-to-current conversion efficiencies (IPCEs) of PSCs were measured with a 300W Xenon Lamp (Oriel 6258) and a Cornerstone 260 Oriel 74125 monochromator.

# 3. Results and discussion

In order to investigate the film crystallinity and microstructure change of perovskite polyelectrolyte composites, X-ray diffraction (XRD) studies were performed. As shown in Fig. 1, the XRD spectra of MAPbI $ _3 $  thin films showed strong diffraction peaks around  $ 14.2^{\circ} $ ,  $ 28.5^{\circ} $ , and  $ 31.9^{\circ} $ , which can be assigned to (110), (220), and (310) crystal planes of tetragonal perovskite phase, respectively, indicating a highly crystalline nature with (110) plane preferentially oriented [14]. For the composites with  $ 0.05\mathrm{mg/mL} $  PDDA, the diffraction intensity was enhanced significantly, indicating enhanced crystallinity and a more ordered crystal structure formed by PDDA doping and crosslink effect between neighbouring grains. The crystal sizes of the perovskite films could be estimated by the Scherrer equation, and the values are slightly increased from pristine perovskite to perovskite-PDDA grains.

The absorption characteristics of the thin films with different PDDA contents were also studied by UV–vis spectroscopy (Fig. 2). The pristine perovskite thin film has strong absorption from the visible range down to the near-infrared wavelength range with the absorption onset at ca. 780–790 nm (optical band gap   $  \approx  $   1.58 eV). Compared to pristine perovskite, the 0.05 mg/mL PDDA capped perovskite showed slight enhancement in absorption due to increased thin film quality. The absorption decreased at higher ratio of PDDA due to lower perovskite content formation caused by higher ratio of PDDA existed.

The PDDA capped perovskite based devices were also examined by incident photo-electron conversion efficiency (IPCE) measurements. It is known that the IPCE spectra could provide the information on the components primarily responsible for converting absorbed photos to the collected electrons and holes. As shown in Fig. 5d, the IPCE data of the pristine perovskite based device showed enhanced efficiency of photon to electron conversion. It could be observed that the IPCE of PDDA-perovskite device increases up to  $ 80\% $  from 400 to  $ 780~\mathrm{nm} $ . The integrated current densities from IPCE were  $ 18.89\mathrm{mA/cm^2} $ ,  $ 19.58\mathrm{mA}/\mathrm{cm}^2 $ ,  $ 18.60\mathrm{mA/cm^2} $ , and  $ 16.32\mathrm{mA/cm^2} $ , respectively for devices based on pristine, 0.05-PDDA, 0.1-PDDA, and 0.5-PDDA perovskite, respectively. The observed increase in IPCE efficiency is attributed to the capping effect which decreases the charge recombination and improves charge transport.

The hysteresis in the  $ J - V $  curves of PSCs was also studied. It has been reported that the perovskite solar cells in some cases may show severe hysteresis and affect the device performance [34]. As shown in Fig. 6 and Fig. S2,  $ J - V $  curves for the PSCs were recorded along the forward and reverse directions with a step of  $ 10\mathrm{mV} $  and a delay time of  $ 20~\mathrm{ms} $  (or  $ 1\mathrm{s} $ ) under AM 1.5G illumination. The  $ J - V $  curves of the forward and reverse scans are well coincident for both devices indicating that the device parameters and efficiency are almost the same regardless of the scan rate. Hence, the hysteresis is minimal and can be neglected in these devices. The steady state output at maximum power point was also measured and shown in Fig. S3. The device showed relatively stable current density and PCE output, indicating the measurement is reliable. Meanwhile, the current density and PCE are consistent with the  $ J - V $  measured values.

Due to the intrinsic structure of metal halide perovskite, the perovskite film is sensitive to moisture, and the devices degrade rapidly in humidity environment. However, for real applications of perovskite photovoltaic cells, the reasonable operational stability is needed under atmospheric conditions. It was found that capping perovskite film with polymer or metal oxide can enhance the stability and retard the decomposition   $  [35,36]  $  . In order to investigate the stability of the perovskite cells with PDDA additive, we tested the devices by storing the unencapsulated devices for one week at room temperature in   $  N_{2}  $   cabinet with humidity level of 10%RH, and the device was tested in air (60% RH) without encapsulation. As shown in Fig. 7, the   $  J_{sc}  $   and FF values suffer a substantial decrease during the aging process, resulting in a PCE decay of the pristine device from 12.5% to 6.5% over the time, whereas a PCE of 9.7% of the PDDA capped device sustaining during the same aging period. Since only perovskite layers are different, the improved stability could be explained by the modification of the perovskite grains by PDDA, which prevents the water accessing into perovskite   $  [37,38]  $  . Thus, the degradation process is retarded.

To investigate the photoconversion processes associated with the PDDA incorporation in the perovskite films, steady-state photoluminance (PL) and time-resolved PL were carried out on both thin films. The steady-state PL was performed on the quartz substrates, and shown in Fig. 8a. The PDDA-perovskite thin film exhibited a larger intensity of PL emission compared to the pristine counterpart. Thus, the non-radiative recombination originating from the defects is reduced, resulting in an increased PL intensity [39,40]. The photo-excited carrier kinetics was investigated by time-resolved PL measurement based on perovskite films prepared on glass substrates. Detailed information about the thin films measurement, and fitting methodology can be found in the experimental part. Perovskite thin films are known to be subject to two-carrier recombination under high fluence which results in a fast PL decay. The time-resolved PL measurement was performed under a low energy density  $ (0.2\mu \mathrm{J} / \mathrm{cm}^2) $  to minimize the second order decay. The PL decay and bi-exponentially fitted curves of these two thin films are shown in Fig. 8b. The fitted parameters are summarized in Table 2, and the average PL lifetimes for perovskite thin film prepared from PDDA-perovskite were calculated to be  $ 14.53~\mathrm{ns} $ , much higher than the pristine perovskite thin film (8.61 ns). Based on above results, the PDDA capped perovskite thin films displayed higher steady-state PL intensity and a slower PL decay rate, indicating that the perovskite  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  with PDDA additive has a high electron transport rate and a low carrier recombination rate compared to the pristine perovskite. The time-resolved PL study implies that  $ \mathrm{PDDA - MAPbI}_3 $  also possesses a longer charge diffusion length.

# 4. Conclusions

In summary, we have successfully achieved perovskite-polyelectrolyte composites as the active layer in planar heterojunction solar cell. The polyelectrolytes can play a template effect for perovskite growth. The composite perovskite exhibited smaller surface roughness and better photovoltaic performance due to decreased grain boundaries related defects and improved surface morphology. The increased carrier lifetime and improved surface morphology were responsible for the efficiency and stability enhancement.
