# ARTICLEINFO

Spinel Nickel cobaltite oxide (NiCo $ _2 $ O $ _4 $ ) have received great interest due to its usage in several industrial applications. The motivation of the present study is to explore the usefulness of spinel Nickel cobaltite oxide as inorganic charge transporting layer for standard perovskite solar cells (PSCs). This is the first demonstration of successful use of NiCo $ _2 $ O $ _4 $  nanoparticles as hole transporting layer (HTL) in standard PSCs with the triple cation perovskite material. The synthesis of nanoparticles was done using a facile chemical precipitation method without the use of surfactant. The synthesized nanoparticles were characterized by the various techniques like X-ray diffraction (XRD), X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM), and UV–vis spectroscopy (UV–vis). The co-ordination of Ni in Co $ _3 $ O $ _4 $  matrix, as well as M $ ^{+n} $ –O bond lengths, were confirmed by the XAS studies. The standard mesoporous PSCs were fabricated by spin-coating a thin layer of NiCo $ _2 $ O $ _4 $  (120 nm), and fabricated PSCs show an esteemed power conversion efficiency (PCE) of > 14% (under standard illumination conditions) and long-term stability (under ambient condition RH = 30–40%) as compared to the spiro-based PSCs. To improve the device performance further we also fabricated the PSCs using the interfacial hole transporting layer, presenting a PCE of > 16% with almost negligible hysteresis that is comparable to the normal standard PSCs based on spiro-OMeTAD as HTL. The performance of PSCs was further analysed by Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS), Photoluminescence (PL), Time-resolved Photoluminescence (TrPL) studies. The results showed the reduced recombination resistance in the PSCs using NiCo $ _2 $ O $ _4 $  as well as interfacial layer. These outcomes indicate the effectiveness of NiCo $ _2 $ O $ _4 $  interlayer for stable and highly efficient perovskite solar cells.

# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) based on organometal trihalide materials have engrossed enormous attention for photovoltaic applications (Green et al., 2014; Xing et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2012), due to its outstanding optical and electronic properties such as high absorption coefficient (De Wolf et al., 2014), long carrier diffusion lengths (Takahashi et al., 2013; Wehrenfennig et al., 2014), long carrier mobility and unique defect physics. (Yin et al., 2014) As a result, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs rapidly enhanced from  $ 3.8\% $  to  $ 22.1\% $  (Kojima et al., 2009) through the advancement made in

These downsides of organic HTMs hamper the widespread development of PSCs in the future. Therefore, less work has been reported on the PSCs containing inorganic charge transport layers with favorable band offsets to address the stability and cost issues.

The Inorganic hole transporting materials (metal oxides) have a wide range of chemical, physical and optoelectronic properties. These materials also have low cost as compared to organic charge transporting layers. According to their elemental composition, inorganic HTLs are classified as binary or ternary metal oxides. The ternary metal oxides possess a complex structure and offer an additional range of optical and electrical properties that can be tunable (Paudel et al., 2011; Depla et al., 2008) for photovoltaics applications (Marco et al., 2001). Recently, a ternary metal oxide i.e.  $ \mathrm{CuCrO_2} $  has been reported as HTL in PSCs giving a power conversion efficiency of  $ 17.19\% $  (Qin et al., 2017). Similarly,  $ \mathrm{CuGaO_2} $  nanoplatelets with  $ 50 - 75\mathrm{nm} $  in diameter were synthesized by a microwave-assisted method. The PSCs with  $ 345~\mathrm{nm} $  thick  $ \mathrm{CuGaO_2} $  as HTL have attained a power conversion efficiency of  $ 18.51\% $  (Zhang et al., 2017). The  $ \mathrm{Co_{1 - y}Cu_yO_x} $  thin film was demonstrated as HTL in PSCS via a high-cost magnetron sputtering method, presenting a comparatively low PCE of  $ 10\% $  (Huang et al., 2017). However, inorganic HTLs have been used in inverted devices and their utilization in normal devices is challenging due to the thermal processing of the oxides which degrades the underneath perovskite absorber (Qiu et al., 2017). Here we report the utilization of oxide HTL processable at low temperature and successfully implemented in normal PSCs.

Spinel   $  Ni_{x}Co_{3-x}O_{4}  $   is a p-type, mixed-valence oxide with Ni occupying octahedral sites and Co distributed over both octahedral and tetrahedral sites. The conductivity of   $  Ni_{x}Co_{3-x}O_{4}  $   is 500 S/cm (Owings et al., 2005) that has been reported as the highest value for transparent conducting oxide (TCO) (Windisch et al., 2001) so far. Due to its high conductivity and transparency   $  Ni_{x}Co_{3-x}O_{4}  $   has been used in the photodetector, (Hu et al., 2011) oxygen reduction, (Angelo et al., 1991) electrode catalysis, (Cui et al., 2008) battery applications (Alcántara et al., 2002) and in organic photovoltaics. (Steirer et al., 2011) Unlike the NiOx which demand an application of a very thin layer to avoid a reduction in PSCs performance due to resistive loss, the higher conductivity of   $  Ni_{x}Co_{3-x}O_{4}  $   must enable the use of thicker layers further amenable for scalable device production. Recently it has been applied as HTL in PSCs with inverted architecture achieving the PCE of 15–18%. (Lee et al., 2019; Ioakeimidis et al., 2019; Ouyang et al., 2018; Papadas et al., 2018) However, it is still not applied as HTL in PSCs with triple cation perovskite (  $  Cs_{0.10}(FA_{0.4}MA_{0.6})_{0.9}PbI_{2.8}Br_{0.2}  $  ) to replace spiro-OMeTAD to achieve good stability and efficiency.

In this work, we present the first demonstration of the use of   $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $   nanoparticles as an efficient hole transport layer for normal (non-inverted) PSCs application. We use the normal structure PSC with the direct deposition of   $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $   nanoparticles film on the surface of perovskite film through a low-temperature solution processed method. The   $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $   nanoparticles exhibit a uniform and dense morphology, which greatly improves the stability of the PSC. Furthermore, by introducing the spiro-OMeTAD interfacial layer between   $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $   and Au electrode, the device performance was further improved from 14% to >16% with good stability at a relative humidity (RH) of 30–40%. The role of the interfacial layer was explored through electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and Time-resolved photoluminescence (TrPL).

# 2. Results and discussion

# 2.1. Structural characterization of  $ \mathrm{NiCo_2O_4} $  nanoparticles and films

Different methods have been reported for the synthesis of   $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $   nanoparticles such as solution processed (Cui et al., 2008; Yu et al., 2014; Zhang and Lou, 2013), spray pyrolysis, sputtering (Windisch

et al., 2001; Nkeng et al., 1996; Tsai et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2015), and Pulsed-laser deposition (Silwal et al., 2012; Iliev et al., 2013), and it has been observed that the optoelectronic properties of the resulting nanoparticles depend profoundly on the processing methods employed. Similarly, NiCo $ _{2} $ O $ _{4} $  including nanowires (Zou et al., 2013), nanoflakes (Mondal et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2013), nanotubes (Li et al., 2013) and hollow spheres (Yu et al., 2014; Shen et al., 2015; Li et al., 2015) have been synthesized successfully. However, the challenge associated with the above synthetic routes is an agglomeration of nanoparticles. This unwanted particle growth process was controlled using organic surfactant as already reported in the literature (Yu et al., 2014; Shen et al., 2015; Li et al., 2015). However, the organic surfactants used in synthesis are expensive, environmentally unfriendly, (Galović et al., 2015) and also affecting the phase purity of synthesized nanoparticles. Henceforth, for the practical application, it is essential to solve these problems by developing a synthetic route without the use of organic surfactants. In the present study, NiCo $ _{2} $ O $ _{4} $  nanoparticles were synthesized using the modified chemical precipitation method already reported in the literature (Shakir et al., 2013) without the use of surfactant or template.

The XRD pattern of synthesized nanoparticles is shown in Fig. 1(a). The well-defined diffraction peaks positioned at 31.1°, 36.6°, 44.6°, 55.5°, 59.1°, and 64.9° confirm the formation of cubic phase of   $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $   nanoparticles. These values are consistent with the standard JCPDS file (#20-0781). All the diffraction peaks are matching well with the (2 2 0), (3 1 1), (4 0 0), (4 2 2), (5 1 1), and (4 4 0) reflection planes of the spinel   $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $   nanoparticles. No diffraction peak related to the NiO, CoO,   $  Co_{3}O_{4}  $  , or their hydroxide were detected in XRD pattern. The spinel structure of   $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $   is presented in Fig. 1(b) which shows that the Ni is distributed over the octahedral sites, while Co is present both at tetrahedral and octahedral sites.

The geometric statistical diameter of the nanoparticles is measured by Scherrer equation  $  \mathrm{D} = 0.94\lambda /\beta \cos \theta  $ . The average crystallite sizes of synthesized nanoparticles are approximately  $  9.11~\mathrm{nm}  $ . Hence the chemical precipitation method proves to be viable for preparation of small size nanoparticles. The calculated crystallite sizes and lattice parameters are shown in Table S1. The lattice parameter a of the cubic lattice  $  \mathrm{NiCo_2O_4}  $  as determined from the observed d spacing for the crystal plane (2 2 0), and (3 1 1) at the 20 value of 31.1, and 36.6 is to be  $  0.8117~\mathrm{nm}  $ . The results are consistent with the standard value of  $  0.8110~\mathrm{nm}  $  for spinel  $  \mathrm{NiCo_2O_4}  $  (JCPDS # 20-0781).

The FE-SEM analysis was conducted to examine the morphology and microstructure of synthesized nanoparticles as depicted in Fig. 1(c, d). From SEM images it is clear that the shape of particles is almost spherical and are slightly agglomerated. Due to the agglomeration the nanoparticles are looking slightly bigger, However, very careful examination of SEM images showed that the average particle size is less than  $ 50~\mathrm{nm} $  clear from the Fig. 1(c, d). The composition of synthesized nanoparticles was further confirmed by the Energy dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) as presented in Fig. S1(a). Cobalt (Co), Nickel (Ni) and Oxygen (O) elements were detected a shown in Fig. S1(a), and the contents of Ni, Co were determined to be 18.02 and  $ 36.88\% $  respectively. The Ni to Co atomic ratio obtained from EDS is very close to 1:2. This ratio in accordance with the stoichiometric ratio of Co to Ni in  $ \mathrm{NiCo_2O_4} $ . Furthermore, the elemental mapping of  $ \mathrm{NiCo_2O_4} $  nanoparticles confirms the presence of Ni, O, and Co in the sample (Fig. S1(c-e)).

The X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) was conducted to get an insight on the oxidation states of Ni and Co metal, as well as to get an idea about M–O (metal-oxygen) and M–M (metal-metal) bond. The first shell analysis of EXAFS data was conducted to find the relationship between the atomic distances of Ni and Co ions to the nearest neighbors. The fitted data is presented in (Fig. 2(c, d)). The Ni K-edge EXAFS were fitted according to the crystallographic model proposed by Knop et al. i.e. considering the first coordination shell of six oxygen atom surrounding the central Ni atom and two further Co and Ni shell (composed of six atoms each) at a longer distance. The result of such a Fit is shown in Fig. 2(c) and in Table 1. Considering that typical  $ \mathrm{Ni}^{2+}-\mathrm{O} $  and  $ \mathrm{Ni}^{3+}-\mathrm{O} $  distances in octahedral coordination are around  $ 2.05-2.09\AA $  and  $ 1.95-1.98\AA $  respectively, the fit of EXAFS data is consistent with the XANES observation where it indicates the existence of  $ \mathrm{Ni}^{3+} $ . The fit gives a bond distance of  $ 1.942\AA $  for the tetrahedral  $ \mathrm{Co}^{2+}-\mathrm{O} $  bond, and  $ 1.831\AA $  for the octahedral  $ \mathrm{Co}^{3+}-\mathrm{O} $  bond. These values are consistent with the values reported for  $ \mathrm{Co}^{2+} $  and  $ \mathrm{Co}^{3+} $  bonded to oxygen, one being at tetrahedral and two at octahedral sites respectively (c.f.  $ \mathrm{Co}^{2+}\mathrm{O}_4 \approx 1.956\AA $  &  $ \mathrm{Co}^{3+}\mathrm{O}_6 \approx 1.903\AA $ ).

The samples were then heated at 70 °C for 20 min. The spin-coated films were characterized using FE-SEM, and UV–Vis to investigate any change in the microstructure of nanoparticles during the dispersion in 1-butanol, and thermal annealing at 70 °C to form the film.

The SEM micrograph of the spin-coated films is presented in Fig. S2(a-d). As preferred for perovskite solar cells fabrication, films are dense, uniform and crack-free when the concentration of   $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $   is 3–10% with full surface coverage. However, with further increasing concentration from 10% to 15% cracks appear in the film, although surface coverage is good. This might be due to the higher concentration of   $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $   nanoparticles in the sample.

# 2.2. Process optimization and device results

The schematics diagram showing the step by step device fabrication process is presented in Fig. 4(a). The detailed process is given in the experimental section. Briefly, a thin layer of c-  $  TiO_{2}  $  was spray coated on FTO coated glass substrate followed by the spin coating of m-  $  TiO_{2}  $ . The m-  $  TiO_{2}  $  layer was annealed at 500 °C for 2 h in the air. The perovskite layer (  $  Cs_{0.10}(FA_{0.4}MA_{0.6})_{0.9}PbI_{2.8}Br_{0.2}  $  ) (Shukla et al., 2017) was deposited by single step method using chlorobenzene as an antisolvent followed by annealing at 100 °C for 1 h under nitrogen environment in the glove box. Following the deposition of perovskite layer, a thin layer of  $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $  was spin coated on the top of the perovskite layer. The thermal annealing of  $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $  layer was done at 70 °C for 30 min inside the glove box. Finally, a 100 nm gold is evaporated thermally in a vacuum chamber as a back contact on the top of the  $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $  film to complete the device stack.

The schematic diagram for standard PSCs with the Au as the top electrode is shown in Fig. 4(b). The device architecture consists of glass/FTO/C-TiO $ _2 $ /m-TiO $ _2 $ /Perovskite/HTL/Au. The energy level diagram for given architecture is presented in Fig. 4(c) using the work functions of NiCo $ _2 $ O $ _4 $  as measured in this work using Photo-electron Spectroscopy in the air (PESA, Fig. S5) which confirms the p-type nature of the NiCo $ _2 $ O $ _4 $  film. The possible path for the propagation of photo-generated charge carriers is also depicted in Fig. 4(c). The valence band position of NiCo $ _2 $ O $ _4 $  is approximately  $ -5.5 $  eV, which is closer to the valence band position of perovskite ( $ -5.45 $  eV) suggesting that the efficient hole extraction from perovskite and suitable Ohmic contact was achieved at the interface.

# 2.3. Interface engineering of  $ \mathrm{NiCo_2O_4} $  based PSCS using hybrid HTL

The solar cells using   $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $   as HTL exhibits the lower efficiencies than devices using Spiro-OMeTAD (with and without dopants) as HTL. The limited efficiency may be due to the charge recombination processes induced by surface defects states that believed to be originated due to the hydroxyl groups (Manders et al., 2013) present on the surface of   $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $   (Manders et al., 2013; You et al., 2016). The transition metal oxides usually contain surface hydroxyl group that provides surface dipole. Due to the presence of surface dipole, these metal oxides can be modified by developing the covalent bond with organic molecules (surface modifier). This modification with surface modifier also alters the work function of metal oxide, a factor that seems to be important for hole collection from perovskite absorber. Therefore, as a proof of the concept, we have also explored the effect of surface modification of   $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $   on device performance. Thus, the surface of 5%   $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $   film was modified with the very thin layer of spiro-OMeTAD (without dopants). The thin layer of spiro-OMeTAD was spin coated on the surface of the   $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $   layer to form hybrid HTL. The resultant J-V curve of champion device is demonstrated in Fig. 7(a) and Table 3.

# 2.4. Analysis of device performance

To gain physical insights into the charge-extraction property of different HTLs films (spiro-OMeTAD,   $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $  , and   $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}/spiro-OMeTAD  $  ) affecting their corresponding photovoltaics performance, we commenced the different HTLs interface with perovskite using steady-state Photoluminescence (PL), Time-resolved Photoluminescence (TrPL), and Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS).

The steady-state PL was conducted to check the charge extraction from the perovskite layer by different HTLs as presented in Fig. 7(c). The Perovskite (PSK) film gave a characteristic PL peak around  $ 760~\mathrm{nm} $  in the absence of any charge extraction layer. However, the PL intensity of perovskite decreases significantly when it is in contact with hole collection layers. The PL intensity of perovskite decreases in order i.e.  $ \mathrm{NiCo_2O_4} \approx $  spiro-OMeTAD (without dopant)  $ >\mathrm{NiCo_2O_4} $ /spiro-OMeTAD (without dopant)  $ >\mathrm{spiro-OMeTAD} $  (with dopant), demonstrating the charge extraction efficiency in reverse order. The highest hole extraction is observed in the hybrid film, while it is comparable in

wo materials, i.e,  $ \mathrm{NiCo_2O_4} $  and spiro-OMeTAD (without dopant).

To further assess the charge extraction by different HTLs, the time-resolved PL decay of film was conducted as shown in Fig. 7(d). The charge carrier life-time was obtained by fitting the decay curve with a bi-exponential decay function. Perovskite film exhibited a longer carrier life-time of 4.5 ns in the absence of any hole collecting layer. However, PL lifetime decreases significantly to 1.07 and 1.06 ns for films containing spiro-OMeTAD and   $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $   layers, respectively. In the case of hybrid film, the average decay time of 0.94 ns is obtained. A shorter PL lifetime specifies that the more efficient charge transfer was attained at perovskite/  $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $  /spiro interface, which also explained the improved PCE of devices containing hybrid HTLs.

# 4. Experimental section

# 4.1. Synthesis of nanoparticles

Briefly, 10.0 mmol of   $  \mathrm{Co(NO_{3})_{2}\cdot6H_{2}O}  $   and 5.0 mmol of Ni(  $  NO_{3}  $  )  $  _{2}\cdot6H_{2}O  $   was dissolved in 50.0 mL of deionized water followed by constant stirring for 2 h to get homogeneous precursor solution. Then, 10 mL of ammonium hydroxide (  $  NH_{4}OH  $  ) was added to this solution dropwise under continuous stirring. The resultant precipitates were kept for stirring for 1 h at room temperature. After one hour the precipitates were centrifuged and washed with ethanol and deionized water to remove residual impurities. The precipitate was further dried at 100 °C for 12 h followed by grinding. The obtained black powder was finally transformed into the desired   $  NiCo_{2}O_{4}  $   phase by annealing at 300 °C. The suspension by prepared by dispersing nanoparticles in 1-butanol (1, 3, 5, 10, 15% w/v) followed by ultrasonication for 4 h at room temperature. The NiCo2o4 films was prepared by spin coating the suspension at spin speed of 3000, 4000, and 5000 rpm for 30 s followed by heating at 70 °C for 20 min.

# 4.2. Device fabrication

The fabrication process for perovskite solar cells is the same as already reported in the literature (Shukla et al., 2017). Briefly, the cleaning of FTO (FTO, ~14 ohm per square, 2.2 mm thick) was done with decon soap, De-ionized water, and ethanol. The thin compact TiO  $  _{2}  $  layer was deposited through spray pyrolysis using titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetylacetonate) (75 wt% in isopropyl alcohol (IPA)) and absolute ethanol in the ratio of 1:9 by volume followed by annealing at 500 °C for 30 min. To fill up the pin holes in c-TiO  $  _{2}  $  layer, the substrates were dipped in 100 mM TiCl  $  _{4}  $  bath at 70 °C for 30 min, followed by annealing at 500 °C for 30 min. Afterward, a 150 nm thick layer of mesoporous TiO  $  _{2}  $  was spin coated on the top of c-TiO  $  _{2}  $  layer at spin speed 4000 rpm for 30 s. Furthermore, annealing of these substrates was done at 500 °C for 30 min to improve the crystallinity of TiO  $  _{2}  $ . The triple cation perovskite solution was prepared according to the protocol already reported in the literature, with the composition of Cs  $  _{0.10}  $  (FA  $  _{0.4}  $  MA  $  _{0.6}  $  )  $  _{0.9}  $  PbI  $  _{2.8}  $  Br  $  _{0.2}  $  (Shukla et al., 2017). The triple cation perovskite layer was spin coated on the top of mesoporous TiO  $  _{2}  $  layer at spin speed of 2000 rpms for 10 s followed by 6000 rpm for 30 s. The anitisolvent (chlorobenzene) was dripped on to the film during the second stage of spin-coating. The perovskite deposited substrates were annealed at 100 °C for 30 min to grow the crystals of perovskite. The NiCo  $  _{2}  $  O  $  _{4}  $  layer (3%, 5%, 10% and 15% w/v dispersed in 1-butanol) was deposited at the spin speed to 3000 rpm, 4000 rpm, and 5000 rpm for 30 s. The films were annealed at 70 °C for 20 min. The spiro-OMeTAD with and without dopants was taken as reference, and deposition process is the same as reported in the literature (Shukla et al., 2017). The hybrid HTL was prepared by depositing 5% (w/v) solution of NiCo  $  _{2}  $  O  $  _{4}  $  at the spin speed of 4000 rpm followed by deposition of Spiro-OMeTAD (50 mg/700 μL of chlorobenzene) at the spin speed of 6000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, the whole devices stack was completed by depositing the 100 nm Au through thermal evaporation. The active area of devices was 0.2 cm  $  ^{2}  $ , however, a metal mask with an active area of 0.09 cm  $  ^{2}  $  was used during measurements.

# 4.3. Characterizations

The synthesized nanoparticles and films were characterized using XRD Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer equipped with Cu K $ _{\alpha} $  radiation. The data was collected in the 2θ range of 10–70°. The microstructure and morphology analysis of films and nanoparticles was done by Jeol JSM-7600F field emission scanning electron microscopy. The J-V characteristics of devices were recorded using San-EI Electric, XEC-301S solar simulator. The xenon lamp with the power of 450 W was used as a source of light and Keithley 2612A as source meter. The solar simulator was calibrated using Si reference cell (Fraunhofer). The IPCE (PV measurements, Inc., Boulder, CO) was measured without bias light in the wavelength range of 300–800 nm. The EIS measurements were done using Autolab potentiostat (PGSTAT 302). The data was collected at different applied bias in the frequency range of 400 kHz to 1 Hz. The steady-state PL measurements were conducted using micro-PL setup based on fiber coupled microscope system, using a VIS-NIR microscope objective (10x, NA = 0.65). The samples were excited at 550 nm using pico second-pulse light emitting diode (Picoquant P-C-405B) with the spot size of 10 mm. The TrPL studies were done using Acton monochromator (SpectraPro 2300), fibre coupled to the microscope, and detected by Micro Photon Devices single-photon avalanche photodiode. The signal was then received by a time-correlated single photon counting card. The temporal resolution is ~5 picoseconds. The samples for PL was prepared by spin coating HTL on glass slide followed by spin coating of perovskite materials. The UV-vis absorption studies were done using Shimadzu UV-2501PC Spectrometer. The data were recorded in the wavelength range of 300–900 nm. The conductivity of films was measured by using Au interdigitated electrode with the length of 1000 μm and gap of 3 μm in width. The maximum power point tracking (MPPT) measurements were carried out using the Autolab machine ((PGSTAT302N, Software version- NOVA 1.11). The white LED light source was used to illuminate the sample. X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) characterization at the Co K-edge (7709 eV) and Ni-K-edge (8332.8 eV) of the same sample NiCo $ _{2} $ O $ _{4} $  was collected at beamline 1.1 W at Synchrotron Light Research Institute (SLRI), Thailand. The data was collected at ring energy of 0.7 GeV and a ring current of 200 mA. A Si (1 1 1) crystal monochromator was used and data was collected in fluorescence mode at ambient temperature. The X-ray energy was calibrated at the inflection point of the absorption edge of cobalt and nickel foil respectively. The XAS for cobalt and nickel reference samples were taken from the database of BL5.2 beamline of Synchrotron Thailand Central Lab. Data analysis was carried out with Athena and Artemis included in the Demeter package (Ravel and Newville, 2005) The powder sample was prepared into a pellet using a pelletizer of diameter 10 mm and mounted on the sample holder for XAS measurement. Usually, around 7 mg of the sample is mixed with 50 mg of boron nitride to make the pellet.
