# Bifunctional Chlorosilane Modification for Defect Passivation and Stability Enhancement of High-Efficiency Perovskite Solar Cells

Dongping Zhu, Yanru Guo, Cheng Mu, Zezhu Jin, Shuai Yuan, Yujun Qin,  $  ^{*}  $   Jian-Ping Zhang, and Xi-Cheng Ai  $  ^{*}  $

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Improving the photoelectric conversion efficiency and long-term stability are two substantial goals for the research work of perovskite photovoltaics, which can be realized through appropriate surface modification methods. In this work, we report a bifunctional modification strategy using (3-chloropropyl)trimethoxysilane with the aim to increase the efficiency and stability of the as-fabricated perovskite solar cells (PSCs) simultaneously. The mechanism of such bifunctional modification is studied with systematic spectroscopic and transient photoelectric characterizations. Specifically, the enhancement of efficiency results from the chlorine-induced passivation of iodine-vacancy trap states, as evidenced by the decrease of trap-state density through the interaction between chlorosilane and  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $ , which further leads to the prolonged photoluminescence lifetime of the intrinsic perovskite films and suppressed charge recombination of

photovoltaic devices. The corresponding champion device possesses a power conversion efficiency of 18.14%, 16% higher than that of the control device. In addition, the modified perovskite films exhibit superior moisture resistance owing to the introduced hydrophobic silane network, and the corresponding devices could preserve ~85% of the initial efficiency after 2 months' storage under ambient air conditions. The proposed bifunctional modification strategy and the mechanism studies provide new insights into both efficiency and stability engineering of the PSCs.

# 1. INTRODUCTION

In the context of global climate change and energy crisis, the research work on solar cells have attracted widespread attention from academia and industry. Since the advent of perovskite photovoltaics in 2009,  $  ^{1}  $  its power conversion efficiency (PCE) has exceeded 25%,  $  ^{2}  $  exhibiting strong competitiveness compared with the commercial photovoltaic technologies based on the inorganic materials such as Si, CuInGaSe, and GaAs.  $  ^{3,4}  $  The outstanding performance of perovskite material benefits from its excellent optical response ability,  $  ^{5,6}  $  ultra-long carrier diffusion length,  $  ^{7,8}  $  and high carrier mobility.  $  ^{9,10}  $  The convenient preparation process further contributes to its rapid development in the photovoltaic community. Generally, unlike the strict preparation conditions of inorganic photovoltaic materials, the polycrystalline perovskite films can be fabricated by facile solution spin-coating and annealing processes. Inevitably, defects in the crystal interior are produced due to the internal vacancies, interstitials, and impurities.  $  ^{11}  $  In addition, there also exit defects at the surface and grain boundaries of perovskites owing to the dangling bonds, free iodine ions, lead ions, and so on.  $  ^{12,13}  $  It is reported that the trap density in polycrystalline perovskite films is on the order of  $  10^{15}  $  to  $  10^{17}  $  cm  $  ^{-3}  $ , while the trap density in single-

Additionally, it is reported that the hydrophobic interfacial modifier could not only passivate the defects but also improve the device stability, which is of practical significance for the application of PSCs.  $  ^{33-37}  $

Besides the device performance improvement, the influence of the defect passivation and surface modification on the photoelectric conversion process has attracted increasing investigation. In principle, defect passivation could eliminate the trap states and release the trapped electrons—holes for effective carrier transfer.  $  ^{38}  $   The surface modification of a perovskite would also regulate the energy level, alter the interfacial contact, and affect charge carrier transport/recombination dynamics.  $  ^{39-41}  $   The exploration of the effects of perovskite modification on the properties of intrinsic perovskite films and the charge carrier dynamics in PSCs would be conductive to the deep understanding of the passivation mechanism and the proposing of new passivation strategy.

In this work, we report the introduction of a bifunctional chlorosilane, (3-chloropropyl)trimethoxysilane, to modify the active layer and examine the influence on the photoelectronic conversion dynamic process and the device performance. The halogen of chlorosilane could passivate iodine vacancy defects, while the hydrophobic silane chains could improve the stability of the perovskite film. The morphology and physical properties of the perovskite film and the charge carrier recombination/transfer dynamics in the complete device are systematically investigated. It is found that the chlorosilane modification significantly passivates perovskite defects, reduces charge recombination, and promotes charge transfer, thereby improving the photovoltaic performance of the device. The corresponding device achieves an enhanced PCE of 18.14 from  $ 15.64\% $  with long-term stability and good moisture resistance capabilities. In addition, halogen-free silane is also introduced for comparison, which shows no positive influence on the device performance because of the absence of a passivation effect.

# 2. MATERIALS AND METHODS

2.1. Materials. Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)-coated glass substrates (1.5 cm × 1.5 cm, 7 Ω sq  $  ^{-1}  $  ) were bought from Pilkington (China). SnO  $  _{2}  $  precursor [tin(IV) oxide, 15 wt % colloidal water dispersion], lead iodide (PbI  $  _{2}  $  ), (3-chloropropyl)trimethoxysilane, and n-butyltrimethoxysilane (the chemical structures are shown in Figure S1, Supporting Information) were purchased from Alfa Aesar. Methylammonium iodide (MAI) and 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-di-p-methoxyphenylamine)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD) were obtained from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology (China). N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), isopropanol (IPA), chlorobenzene (CB), and 4-tert-butylpyridine were acquired from J&K (China). Lithium bis(trifluoromethylsulphonyl)imide (Li-TFSI) and cobalt(III)FK209 were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. All reagents were used without any further purification.

2.2. Device Fabrication. FTO glasses were cleaned using aqueous detergent solution, deionized water, acetone, and ethanol by ultrasonic cleaning for 30 min, sequentially. Subsequently, the substrates were treated via oxygen plasma for 20 min. The electronic transport layer (ETL) was prepared by spin-coating colloidal   $  SnO_{2}  $   solution (1:5 by volume ratio with deionized water) at 4000 rpm for 20 s, annealed in air at 150 °C for 30 min, and treated by oxygen plasma for 5 min again. For the preparation of the active layer, a mixture of   $  PbI_{2}  $   (1.25 M) and MAI (1.2 M) dissolved in DMF and DMSO (7:3, v/v) was stirred for 2 h before being spin-coated on the ETL with a successive two-step procedure by 1000 rpm for 10 s and 5000 rpm for 20 s. CB was dropped onto the substrate at the 22nd s after the start of the coating progress. The   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   film was obtained by annealing at 100 °C for 0.5 h. For the silane modification layer, 200 μL of the solution with different concentrations (0.027, 0.036, and 0.054 M) of (3-chloropropyl)trimethoxysilane (abbreviated as Si–Cl) or n-butyltrimethoxysilane (abbreviated as Si–CH₃) in IPA was spun on the perovskite film at 6000 rpm for 30 s before further annealing at 100 °C for 5 min. The sample without silane treatment was fabricated as the control. Perovskite films treated by Si–Cl and Si–CH₃ solution were named as Si–Cl-perovskite and Si–CH₃-perovskite, respectively. The hole-transport layer (HTL) solution was prepared by dissolving Spiro-OMeTAD in CB (72.3 mg mL⁻¹). Then, 28.8 μL of 4-tert-butylpyridine, 17.5 μL of Li-TFSI in acetonitrile (520 mg mL⁻¹), and 28.8 μL of cobalt(III) FK209 in acetonitrile (300 mg mL⁻¹) were added to the above solution and stirred for 2 h. The solution was deposited by spin-coating at 5000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, an 80 nm gold counter electrode was deposited by thermal evaporation.

2.3. Characterization. The morphologies of the perovskite films were investigated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi SU8010) at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. The surface roughness of the perovskite films was characterized by atomic force microscopy (AFM, Dimension ICON, Bruker). X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted on a Shimadzu XRD-7000 diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation in the 2θ range from 10 to 50° at a scan rate of 2° min  $  ^{-1}  $  . The absorption spectra were measured by a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-3600). Steady-state, temperature-dependent, and time-resolved photoluminescence (SSPL, TDPL and TRPL) spectra were examined by using an Edinburgh FLS 980 spectrometer. SSPL was carried out at an excitation wavelength of 510 nm with irradiation from the side of spin-coated materials. TRPL was performed using a picosecond diode laser (EPL-475, 95 ps). For the TDPL spectra, the temperature range was set from 78 to 298 K with an excitation wavelength at 510 nm. Room-temperature emission spectra of the tested samples were compared before and after the temperature scan process from which no change was observed, indicating no permanent damage on the samples. Trap density was estimated using the space-charge limited current (SCLC) method on the Keithley 4200 system under the dark condition with the voltage range of -0.3 to 3.0 V. The water contact angle measurements were performed using a KRÜSS DSA30 analyzer. Raman spectroscopy was measured by a HORIBA XploRA PLUS spectrometer. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy were performed using Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi and Thermo Scientific NicolettiN10MX, respectively.

The current density–voltage   $  (J-V)  $   characteristics of PSCs were measured on the system equipped with a Keithley 2400 SourceMeter by exposing the target cells to a sunlight simulator calibrated to AM 1.5G solar illumination. The predetermined applied bias voltage region was set from open circuit to short circuit (reverse scan, RS) and from short circuit to open circuit (forward scan, FS). A metal aperture mask was covered to ensure an active area of   $  0.1\ cm^{2}  $   during the measurement. The incident photon-to-current conversion efficiency (IPCE) spectra were measured using an Enlitech QE-R, and the detected wavelength range was set from 300 to 800 nm. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted by a Zahner PP211 electrochemical workstation in the dark, and the AC amplitude value was set to 5 mV.

2.4. Charge Carrier Dynamics Measurement. Transient photovoltage (TPV) measurements were carried out by irradiating the target cells using a continuous-wave light-emitting diode (LED, 530 ± 5 nm) under open-circuit conditions to generate a steady-state photovoltage (  $  V_{ph}  $  ). A weak laser pulse (532 nm, 7 ns) was applied to neutral filters and superimposed on the steady-state light to generate a small perturbation with   $  \Delta V_{ph}/V_{ph}  $   less than 5%. Electric signals were recorded by a digital oscilloscope (LeCroy 64 Xs, input impedance of 1 MΩ). A series of desired   $  V_{ph}  $   values could be collected by adjusting the intensity of the LED. The transient photocurrent (TPC) results were recorded using a digital oscilloscope (LeCroy 64 Xs, input impedance of 50 Ω) after the TPV measurement.

SSPL and TRPL measurements were performed to get insight into the photophysical properties and carrier recombination performance of the perovskite films. When the perovskite film is deposited on the insulating substrate such as quartz, the PL intensity is mainly dependent on the band-to-band recombination of free charges in the film. It can be observed from Figure 2c that the perovskite films exhibit

strong luminescence peaks around 770 nm, which is in good accordance with the absorption band-edge from the UV–vis absorption spectra. In general, surface/interface defects are usually considered as deep trap states, and they act as nonradiative recombination centers.  $  ^{15}  $   Passivating these trap states would increase the PL intensity, while the PL peak would not exhibit a blue shift.  $  ^{30}  $   When Si–Cl is introduced onto the perovskite film, the fluorescence intensity is increased in contrast to that of the pristine perovskite film, which presents a suppressive nonradiative recombination process of the electron–hole pairs.  $  ^{11}  $   As for the Si–CH  $  _{3}  $  -perovskite film, the fluorescence slightly weakens than the control film. These results are corresponding to the perovskite film quality since the luminescent properties are closely related to the intrinsic film properties.

SCLC measurements were conducted to observe the trap density of the perovskite films, and the results are displayed in Figure 3. All curves clearly show three regions of distinct current behavior along with the bias voltage. In the low bias region, the current increases linearly as the voltage increases, indicating an ohmic response. Then the current exhibits a rapid non-linear increase, suggesting the emergence of the trap-filled limit (TFL) region. Subsequently, a child regime shows up as the current increases quadratically with the high bias voltage. The voltage between the ohmic contact and trap-filled regime is known as the TFL voltage (  $  V_{TFL}  $  ) determined by the trap state.  $  ^{53}  $   The trap density (  $  n_{t}  $  ) can be determined according to the equation  $  ^{54}  $

$$
n _ {\mathrm{t}} = \frac {2 V _ {\mathrm{TFL}} \varepsilon \varepsilon_ {0}}{e L ^ {2}}
$$

where   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   is the vacuum permittivity,   $  \varepsilon  $   is the dielectric constant of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  , e is the electron charge, and L is the thickness of the perovskite layer.   $  \varepsilon  $   could be estimated from the relation   $  \varepsilon = CL/(\varepsilon_{0}A)  $  , where A is the device area, and geometrical capacitance (C) is obtained by fitting the impedance spectra of FTO/perovskite/Au devices.  $  ^{55}  $   From the curves in Figure 3, the   $  V_{TFL}  $   values are acquired as 0.618, 0.420, and 0.680 V for the control, Si–Cl-perovskite, and Si–CH  $  _{3}  $  -perovskite films, respectively. The corresponding trap densities are calculated to be   $  1.021 \times 10^{16}  $  ,   $  6.534 \times 10^{15}  $  , and   $  1.139 \times 10^{16}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  .

# 4. CONCLUSIONS

In summary, bifunctional chlorosilane is introduced to modify the perovskite layer to passivate the defects with Cl groups and enhance the stability with the hydrophobic silane chains. The morphology characterizations demonstrate that the modification could smooth the perovskite film surface and enlarge the grain size. The XRD results indicate improved perovskite crystallinity properties, and the water contact angle measurements exhibit increasing hydrophobicity of the perovskite film. The SSPL and TRPL measurements imply that the trap states are effectively reduced upon the Si–Cl modification, which is confirmed by the trap density result from the SCLC examination and the interaction between Cl and Pb from the XPS results. Further analysis from the TDPL shows that the modified perovskite film possesses low exciton bonding energy, optical phonon energy, and polaron bonding energy, which benefit the separation of initial photo-induced excitons and the transfer of charge carriers. The device based on the Si–Cl-perovskite displays superior photovoltaic performance, and the champion device possesses the PCE of 18.14%, which is 16% higher than that of the control device, mainly owing to the FF improvement. The transient charge carrier dynamics measurements of TPV/TPC reveal that the charge recombination in the Si–Cl-PSC is greatly suppressed due to chlorine-induced passivation of iodine-vacancy trap states, while the charge transfer and collection are effectively promoted. Finally, stability tests demonstrate that the perovskite film and device earn dramatic moisture erosion resistance owing to the silane protection.
