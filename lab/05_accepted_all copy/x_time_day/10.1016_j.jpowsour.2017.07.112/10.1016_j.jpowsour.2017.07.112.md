# An isopropanol-assisted fabrication strategy of pinhole-free perovskite films in air for efficient and stable planar perovskite solar cells

# GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

# A B S T R A C T

As a promising photovoltaic device, perovskite solar cells have attracted numerous attention in recent years, where forming a compact and pinhole-free perovskite film in air is of great importance. Herein, we evaluate highly efficient and air stable planar perovskite solar cells in air (relative humidity over  $ 50\% $  ) with the modified two-step sequential deposition method by adjusting the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{I} $  (MAI) concentrations and regulating the crystallization process of the perovskite film. The optimum MAI concentration is  $ 60\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1} $  in isopropanol. With a planar structure of FTO/TiO2/MAPbI3/spiro-OMeTAD/Au, the efficient devices composed of compact and pinhole-free perovskite films are constructed in air, achieving a high efficiency of up to  $ 15.10\% $  and maintaining over  $ 80\% $  after 20 days storing without any encapsulation in air. With a facile fabrication process and high photovoltaic performance, this work represents a promising method for fabricating low-cost, highly efficient and stable photovoltaic device.

highest certified efficiency record of single-junction perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has reached up to 22.1% [3]. However, cost reduction and stability promotion in PSCs have yet to be resolved for the industrial production even though many previous efforts have been done [4,5].

Nowadays, preparing stable MAPbI $ _3 $  films in air remains an emnoumos challenge [6–8]. To promote the quality of perovskite film in PSCs, many advanced techniques including a dual-source vapour deposition, solvent-induced fast deposition-crystallization and the vacuum flash assisted procedure have been developed under inert atmosphere shielding [9–13]. Solvent engineering as an efficient technological method has proven to be effective in adjusting the crystallization and controlling the morphology of the perovskite layer [14,15]. Based on solution-process fabrication, a mixture solvent of γ-butyrolactone (GBL) and dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) has been successfully employed to form uniform and dense perovskite layer combining a toluene drip while spinning [16]. Thereafter, a mixture solvent of dimethylformamide (DMF) and DMSO are also used to prepare the perovskite film [17]. Recently, a solvent treatment method with isopropanol (IPA) by two-step sequential deposition process has been developed to modify the morphology of PbI $ _2 $  and convert PbI $ _2 $  to MAPbI $ _3 $  completely [18,19]. Based on IPA substitution, Tu and co-workers fabricated up-scaling and highly efficient PSCs through a novel three-step sequential coating method, which improved the PCE and enhanced reproducibility of photovoltaic performance [20]. Amongst these means, forming a compact and pinhole-free perovskite film in air is highly desired for a stable and efficient perovskite solar cell. However, owing to volatile DMF and uncontrollable nucleation process, the perovskite films are often rich in pinholes and incomplete coverage during the conventional spin-coating process in air, even under inert atmosphere shielding [21–24]. The existing pinholes produce recombination centers and accessible channels for oxygen to degrade MAPbI $ _3 $  films [25–27]. Therefore, a simple solution-processing strategy that can control crystallization process and eliminate pinholes accompanying with superior performance is highly desired for the construction of PSCs in air [28–30]. Efforts have been devoted to constructing devices in air through introducing carbon electrode to protect the perovskite films or replacing conventional lead (II) iodide with lead (II) thiocyanate to regulate the crystallization process in mesoporous PSCs [31,32]. Although over 10% PCEs have been achieved, the incomplete perovskite films with pinholes limit the further improvement. The compact and pinhole-free perovskite film in the planar PSCs is thus far quite difficult to obtain via a simple coating process in air due to the poor coverage [33–35].

Herein, we report a simple and effective strategy of preparing  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  films processed in air using isopropanol assisted two-step sequential deposition method. We demonstrate that isopropanol assisted the formation of compact and pinhole-free perovskite films in air and significantly improved device ambient stability. The best-performing planar cell with a  $ \mathrm{FTO / TiO_2 / MAPbI_3 / Spiro - OMe} $  TAD/Au architecture as shown in Fig. 1(a) has achieved PCEs of over  $ 15\% $  by adjusting the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{I} $  (MAI) concentrations in IPA and trimming film surface with the hot IPA solvent, where charge separation and collection are also shown in Fig. 1(b). More surprisingly, the stability of IPA treatment device maintaining over  $ 80\% $  PCE after 20-day storing without any encapsulation in air is better than the typical device.

# 2.2. Preparation of  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  precursor

The   $  TiO_{2}  $   precursor was prepared by the hydrolytic sol-gel method in moderate acidic solution as follows. 320   $  \mu  $  L, 480   $  \mu  $  L, 640   $  \mu  $  L and 800   $  \mu  $  L titanium (IV) isopropoxide (TTIP) was slowly added into 4 mL IPA with 10-min stirring respectively. Then the 4 mL 50 mM hydrochloric in IPA solution was slowly dropped into the as-obtain stirred precursor, controlling the dropping process about 10 min. Finally   $  TiO_{2}  $   precursor solutions were obtained with 30-min stirring and then filtered through a 0.45 mm PVDF syringe filter corresponding to La1, La2, La3, and La4 respectively.

# 2.3. Device fabrication and characterization

Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glasses were etched by zinc powder and 2M hydrochloric solution. The etched glasses were ultrasonically cleaned in deionized water, detergent solution, ethyl alcohol and acetone for 30 min respectively. After 30-min heating in the oven at 100 °C, the glasses were transferred to the ultraviolet-ozone (UV-  $  O_{3}  $  ) cleaner for 15 min. Then the   $  TiO_{2}  $

precursor was spin-coated onto the cleaned FTO substrates at 3000 rpm for 30 s, followed by drying at 100 °C for 10 min in air and then annealing at 500 °C for 45 min in muffle furnace. For the perovskite layer, sequential solution deposition method was employed. A solution of 450 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  PbI  $  _{2}  $  dissolved in DMF keeping at 70 °C was spin-coated onto the as-prepared FTO/TiO  $  _{2}  $  substrates at 3000 rpm for 30 s, and then dried at 80 °C for 30 min. After cooling to the room temperature, different concentrations of CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}  $  I in IPA (20, 40, 60 and 80 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was spin-coated onto the PbI  $  _{2}  $  film at 3000 rpm for 30 s, followed by baking in the oven at 100 °C for 20 min. After that, the hole transport layer solution of Spiro-OMeTAD, dissolved in 1 mL chlorobenzene containing hole transport material Spiro-OMeTAD 90 mg and the additives 17.5 μL Li-TFSI in acetonitrile (520 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) and 28.8 μL 4-tert-butylpyridine, was deposited onto the perovskite layer by spin-coating at 4000 rpm for 20 s. Finally, a 60 nm thick Au counter electrode was deposited on the top via thermal evaporation under high vacuum (8 × 10  $  ^{-4}  $  Pa). The active area was defined 0.12 cm  $  ^{2}  $  with the metal mask.

To characterize the crystal structure and phase of the materials, X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra was collected using a Empyrean Panalytical X'Pert Pro X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (  $  \lambda = 1.5418 \AA  $  ). A field-emission scanning electron microscope (FEI HELIOS NanoLab 600i) was used to analyze the morphology of the sample. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was performed using the tapping mode by Bruker Dimension FastScan Scanning Probe Microscope (SPM). The current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics of these planar devices were obtained employing an comprehensive electrochemical power workstation (VersaSTAT 3, Ametek, USA). The photovoltaic performance was recorded under AM1.5G illumination at a calibrated intensity of 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $   with the Newport 94021A class ABB solar simulator equipped with a 150 W xenon lamp. The illumination intensity was standardized before each test by a standard Si solar cell (1218, Newport, USA). The monochromatic incident photon-to-electron conversion efficiency (IPCE) was measured as functions of wavelength calibrated with the certified silicon solar cell using the Crowntech solar cell quantum efficiency measurement system (QTest Station 500AD, USA). The constant white-light radiated by a 300 W tungsten lamp was adjusted by a monochromator, a chopper and an operational amplifier. Finally the signal was recorded by a Lock-In Amplifier and a multimeter (Keithley Model 2000). The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was measured by the electrochemical workstation under dark condition with frequency range of 0.1–100 KHz and applied bias of -0.90 V.

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Characterization of  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  film

As an important factor for performance of planar perovskite solar cells, the morphology of perovskite layer is greatly affected by the surface of  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  film [36,37]. La1, La2, La3, and La4  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  sol-gel precursor is prepared and then span onto the UV- $ \mathrm{O}_3 $  treated FTO substrate to form the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  films along with sintering (see detailed procedure in Experimental section). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observation illustrates that surface of final  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  film is well controlled by TTIP concentrations as Fig. S1(a), (b), (c) and (d), as previously reported [38]. La1, La2 and La4 films is characterized with inhomogeneous particle size. In contrast, flat surface and homogeneous particles are observed for La3 films. The lower TTIP concentrations of La1 and La2 may promote the formation of larger grains of  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  due to fewer crystallization position, causing inhomogeneous particles. As for La4, however, excessive TTIP concentration likely leads to the inhomogeneous accumulation of  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $

grains. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) is employed to further characterize the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  films as Fig. S1(e), (f), (g) and (h). The surface roughness (RMS) of the La3 films with  $ 7.68\mathrm{nm} $  is smaller than La1, La2 and La4 films (11.4 nm, 9.93 nm and 8.51 nm, respectively). Therefore, the La3 film is selected as optimal electron transporting layer to fabricate the PSCs.

# 3.2. Characterization of perovskite film and PSCs

The two-step sequential solution deposition method, that   $  PbI_{2}  $   solution is first coated on the substrate to form a   $  PbI_{2}  $   film and then the as-prepared   $  PbI_{2}  $   film reacted with   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $   solution by soak or spin, is employed to fabricate perovskite films in air. 450 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   hot   $  PbI_{2}  $   solution dissolved in DMF is firstly span onto the La3-  $  TiO_{2}  $   film and then dried at 100 °C for 30 min [8]. By adjusting the concentration of the MAI in IPA (20, 40, 60 and 80 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ), different perovskite films are prepared in air. As increasing of MAI concentration from 20 to 80 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  , the perovskite gains change obviously and arrange closely as shown in Fig. 2(a), (b), (c) and (d). This situation is improved significantly in the films prepared with 60 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   MAI comparing with 20 and 40 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  . However, the obvious cracks appear on external surface with 80 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   MAI, it is indicated that unreacted MAI forms a cap and generates cracks in process of annealing. SEM images of the perovskite films with wider range are listed in Fig. 2(e), (f), (g) and (h) to further show the structural features. From the insert of optical photographs in Fig. 2(e), (f), (g) and (h), the films become dense and dark brown with the increasing MAI concentration.

AFM images of as-prepared perovskite films are listed in Fig. 3(a), (b), (c) and (d). A fraction of grains arranges closely after treating with  $ 40\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1} $  MAI, compared to the sample treated by  $ 20\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1} $  MAI. Besides, the fraction of coverage changes dramatically under 60 and  $ 80\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1} $  MAI treating. The higher fraction of coverage means the decrease of pinholes, which affected charge extraction behaviour and photocurrent. As shwon in Fig. S2, the surface roughness (RMS) of the films prepared with  $ 60\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1} $  MAI  $ (19.6\mathrm{nm}) $  is smaller than the films with 20  $ (27.1\mathrm{nm}) $ , 40  $ (23.6\mathrm{nm}) $ , 80  $ (28.9\mathrm{nm})\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1} $  MAI, it is indicated that flat surface can be obtained with  $ 60\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1} $  MAI in air. The low concentrations of MAI result in incomplete conversion of the  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  to the perovskite and then the crystalline grains of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  prepared with 20 and  $ 40\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1} $  MAI are loosely arranged and more space between crystalline grains are left, leading to the larger surface roughness of the films.

As for the perovskite films with 80 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  , the cracks appearing on external surface likely give rise to the larger surface roughness of the films, that are generated from the unreacted MAI in process of annealing. The 10   $  \mu  $  m × 10   $  \mu  $  m AFM images of perovskite also are presented in Fig. S3(a), (b), (c) and (d) to confirm the structural feature in the whole film, which is consistent with the above analysis. Even though the film quality has been improved greatly by adjusting the concentration of MAI, the pinholes emerge obviously in face of high MAI concentrations.

# 3.3. Characterization of perovskite film and PSCs with IPA treatment

To further improve the film quality, the perovskite film surface modification is conducted using hot IPA solution in process of continuous crystallization. The hot IPA solution is dripped after  $ 10\mathrm{s} $  MAI spin-coating at  $ 3000~\mathrm{rpm} $ . Compared with perovskite films without IPA modification as Fig. 5(a) and (b), the pinholes have been filled and compact films are successfully obtained as Fig. 5(d) and (e). AFM observation shows that film surface with IPA treatment has been modified and pinholes are filled with small crystal grains as Fig. 5(c) and (f). It is inferred that the dripping IPA solution

diluted the MAI concentration firstly generating small grain, and then such small crystal grains fill the pinholes and smooth the perovskite film. The secondary growth of perovskite grains is clearly observed from the AFM images of the   $  10 \mu m \times 10 \mu m  $   perovskite films with and without IPA treatment as Fig. S4(a) and (b). According to the crossing section image in Fig. S5, the thickness of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   film on   $  TiO_{2}  $   with IPA treatment is about 480 nm. It is well-known that the thickness of the perovskite layer has an effect on the photocurrents and fill factors [42,43]. Too thin perovskite layer below 100 nm cannot take full advantage of the larger light absorption capacity and longer carrier diffusion length. On the other hand, the thick perovskite films above 500 nm may lead to carrier recombination in extraction process [44–46]. In our work, the thickness of the device with IPA treatment is appropriate for both light-harvesting and carrier extraction. From the XRD patterns shown in Fig. 5(g), the tetragonal phase   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   is successfully synthesized and it is found that the peak of residual MAI and   $  PbI_{2}  $   dramatically changed after IPA treatment. According to the literature, the MAI can easily infiltrate into the bottom of   $  PbI_{2}  $   film with isopropanol pre-wetting resulting in completely converting into the perovskite efficiently [47]. Moreover, isopropanol can effectively extract the residual DMF in the   $  PbI_{2}  $   film, which damages the stability of the perovskite film [48]. In general, the dropping hot IPA during spin-coating process mainly serves two functions: (1) the dropping hot IPA can bring the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $   to the unreacted   $  PbI_{2}  $   resulting in completely converting into   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   and can effectively extract the residual DMF in the   $  PbI_{2}  $   film, which damages the stability of the perovskite film; (2) the dropping hot IPA transfers initial grain to the location full of pinholes and eliminates pinholes during the crystallization of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $  . It is inferred that the dripping IPA solution dissolves the surface perovskite firstly and then introduces the MAI to the unreacted   $  PbI_{2}  $   film. Finally, smooth and pinhole-free perovskite layers are prepared through IPA treatment for achieving highly efficient planar PSCs [19,20].

# 4. Conclusions

In summary, a kind of   $  TiO_{2}  $  -based planar PSCs has been successfully fabricated in air (relative humidity over 50%) through regulating the MAI concentrations and dropping the hot IPA solvent with two-step sequential deposition method. Consequently, the photovoltaic parameters of the devices with the IPA treatment are comprehensively improved with the best PCE over 15%,   $  J_{sc}  $   over

In addition, the stability of IPA treated devices without any encapsulation in ambient air maintains over 80% PCE after 20-day storing. Moreover, the MAI is successfully introduced into the PbI  $  _{2}  $   film to promote the generation of the dense perovskite film and passivate grain boundaries through the hot IPA solution. We believed that this IPA treatment strategy is a promising method to fabricate efficient and stable PSCs in air.
