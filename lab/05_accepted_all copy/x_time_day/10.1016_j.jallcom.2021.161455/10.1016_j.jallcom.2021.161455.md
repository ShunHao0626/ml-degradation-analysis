# 1. Introduction

With the gradual decrease of non-renewable resources, the research and utilization of renewable energy must speed up. Solar cells based on organic-inorganic hybrid perovskites are considered as one of the fast-growing energy supplies and have always been a research hotspot because they have demonstrated achieved power conversion efficiency (PCE) of up to 25.2% [1]. However, conventional perovskite solar cells (PSCs) usually employ noble electrode materials, such as Au, Ag [2–7]. These electrode materials are not only expensive but usually need a vacuum thermal evaporation process. As a commonly used electrode material, Ag can penetrate through the charge transport layer and react with the perovskite layer to generate silver halide [8]. This phenomenon also occurs when Au is employed as an electrode [9]. To a certain extent, the above factors will limit the development of perovskite solar cells. Therefore, various ways to overcome these problems have been adopted.

Herein, a low-melting point tin-bismuth (SnBi) alloy film combined with a silver layer was introduced to fabricate the inverted planar perovskite solar cells. SnBi alloy was prepared by high temperature melting method and was characterized by metallurgical microscope, XRD, SEM, DTA, XPS methods. The influence of silver thickness on the performance of perovskite solar cells was studied. The double-layer electrode can not only reduce the charge transfer resistance, increase the extraction efficiency of the charge, improve the photovoltaic performance of the device, but also improve the stability of the device in the environment.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1. Perovskite precursor solution

The  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  precursor solution prepared by dissolving  $ 207\mathrm{mg} $  of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{I} $  (MAI, Alfa Aesar) and  $ 599\mathrm{mg} $  of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  (Alfa Aesar,  $ 99.999\% $ ) in  $ 895~\mu \mathrm{L} $  of DMF (J&K Scientific) and  $ 105~\mu \mathrm{L} $  of DMSO (J&K Scientific); then, the mixed solution stirred at  $ 70^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 3\mathrm{h} $ .

# 2.2. SnBi alloy

In the present work, the alloy prepared by compositions (wt%) of Sn-Bi from 99.99% tin (Sn) powders and 99.99% bismuth (Bi) powders. These elements were melted in a tubular furnace for 2.5 h at a specific temperature for a homogenous mixture and melting of both the elements. The melting process can be divided into 2 steps. One step is melting for 0.5 h for 450 °C for -0.09 Pa, and the other step is melting for 2 h for 650 °C for standard atmosphere with   $  H_{2}  $   and Ar (Fig. S1).

# 2.3. Device fabrication

The patterned indium-doped tin oxide (ITO) was ultrasonically cleaned by detergent water, deionized water, acetone, and absolute ethanol for 15 min, separately. Then, the PEDOT:PSS layer was spin-coated on top of the ITO substrate at 3000 rpm for 30 s and then annealed at 120 °C for 25 min. After the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  was cooled down to room temperature, the perovskite precursor solution was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 30 s and annealed 100 °C for 30 min. Ethyl acetate (100 μL) was dropped at the center of the substrate with a 12 s delay time during the 4000 rpm spin-coating process. The PCBM:BCP layer (25 mg/mL in DCB) was deposited on the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  by spin coating at 1000 rpm for 60 s and annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. Finally, silver and alloy electrodes were deposited on the surface of the device by vacuum thermal evaporation through a shadow mask, which determined the cell area of 0.1125 cm  $  ^{2}  $ . The device structure and ten kinds of devices with different thickness electrodes are shown in Fig. 1.

# 2.4. Device characterization

The current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics of the devices were measured in an argon-filled glove box, with a programmed Keithley 2400 source meter under the illumination of a Newport Oriel 150-W solar simulator (AM 1.5G,  $ 100\mathrm{mWcm}^{-2} $ ). The spectra of monochromatic incident photon-to-current conversion efficiency (IPCE) for solar cell was performed by using a commercial Q-Test Station 2000 IPCE measurement system (Crowntech, USA). # 2.5. Tin-bismuth (SnBi) alloy characterization

Morphology of SnBi alloy plots and SnBi film were measured by the scanning electron microscope (SEM, AMETEK EDAX). The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded by an X-ray diffraction system (XRD, TTR-III) using Cu Kα radiation. Prior to the microstructure test, the alloys were polished and etched in Nital for 90 s and were observed under an optical microscope. Meanwhile, a sample of the solder was sent for differential thermal analysis (DTA) to evaluate the melting properties.

# 3. Results and discussion

As a low melting point alloy, SnBi alloy has excellent physical properties such as good electrical conductivity and more stable than pure metal inherent in most alloys. The SnBi alloy sample obtained by the high-temperature melting method is irregular in shape. To facilitate the test, we melt and press the alloy block into a thinner wafer, as showed in Fig. S2. Fig. 2a is the XRD pattern of SnBi alloy and shows the presence of only  $ \beta $ -Sn phase and Bi phase, with the formation of a little bit of additional compounds. This is consisted with the binary phase diagram of the SnBi system (Fig. S3). It also shows that no compound is formed between Sn and Bi. Alloy surface morphology is shown in Fig. 2b. For higher Bi content region, the surface morphology shows various shapes, which may be linked to the double-layer flake rhombic structure of Bi crystal. Fig. 3a is the DTA curve of the SnBi alloy sample. The melting point is approximately at 136.9 °C, which is consistent with previous report of about 138 °C [29]. The reason of melting point of the alloy prepared is lower than literature may be that there are some oxides existed in the alloy, which affects the physical properties of the alloy. Subsequently, we performed X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) tests to clarify the chemical state of the SnBi alloy, as showed in Fig. 3(b–d). It can be confirmed that in the high-resolution XPS spectra of Sn regions (Fig. 3c), the peaks at 497.2, 493.3, and 484.9 eV correspond to the Sn $  _{3d}  $ . At the same time, some satellite peaks are observed corresponding to the oxidation state of Sn, mainly SnO and SnO $  _{2}  $ . For the Bi regions (Fig. 3d), peaks at 162.3 and 157.0 eV are correspond to the orbitals of B $  _{4f}  $  with some oxidation states. In short, the results of XPS indicate that there are mainly zero-valent Sn and Bi in the alloy. However, since oxygen cannot be completely removed in the tube furnace during the alloy preparation process, Sn and Bi are slightly oxidized, and corresponding oxides are formed.

In order to know the energy level matching between the SnBi electrode and the perovskite active layer, we also measured the work function (WF) of the SnBi thin film by Kelvin probe. The energy level distribution diagram are showed in Fig. 4a. The WF of the SnBi layer is  $ 5.1\mathrm{eV} $ , which is very close to the WF of the Au electrode. We observed the cross-sectional view of device B (Fig. S7) using SEM. Due to the low-melting point of the SnBi alloy, during the evaporation process, the SnBi alloy layer easily collapses inward on the surface of the device, which may cause direct contact between the cathode and the perovskite layer. Fig. 4b is the SEM image of the cross-sectional view of device E. The connection between the layers is very tight. Owing to the presence of silver layer, the SnBi alloy will not collapse downwards.

The performance of perovskite solar cells mainly depends on the open-circuit voltage, short circuit current and fill factor. Therefore, it is necessary to find out the internal reasons for the changes in the photovoltaic parameters. In the Nyquist diagram, the high-frequency semicircle usually represents the charge transfer resistance at the interface [31]. Fig. 4c shows the Nyquist curves of six kinds of devices. It can be seen that the charge transfer resistance of different devices shows a big difference, but the change law is not obvious. Fig. 4d shows the Nyquist curves of device A, device B, and device E, where the charge transfer resistance of device E is the smallest, and the charge transfer resistance of device A is the largest. The difference in charge transfer resistance occurs because the structure of the double-layer electrode is flatter, which is conducive to the movement of carriers. At the same time, the small parallel impedance makes the short-circuit current of the device large, and the performance of the device can be improved. In addition, there are few trapping centers at the interface, which reduces the probability of carrier recombination. Therefore, the structure of this Ag/SnBi double-layer electrode enables the carriers to have a longer life and a lower recombination probability, thereby improving the performance of the device. Fig. S8 is the equivalent circuit diagram of the three devices. Since the resistance of the SnBi alloy itself is larger than that of Ag, the series resistance  $ (\mathrm{R_s}) $  of device A is the smallest. The  $ \mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{s}} $  and charge transfer resistance  $ (\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{ct}}) $  are relatively low for Device E with Ag/SnBi double-layer electrode. In order to intuitively understand the influence of different thicknesses of Ag layer on the photovoltaic performance of perovskite solar cells, we tested the J-V curve of the device. Fig. S9 is the comparison of the J-V curves of device A and device E. The open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current density of device E are greater than those of device A, but the fill factor of device E is slightly smaller, which may be attributed to the SnBi alloy has a relatively large resistance.

Fig. 5a, b are the positive and negative sweep curves of device A and device E, respectively. The positive and negative sweep curves of device E are more consistent than those of device A, indicating that the hysteresis of device E is relatively small. Fig. S10 is the statistical distribution of the efficiency of 90 devices of device E. Fig. 5c is the EQE curve and integrated current density curve of device A and E. It can be seen that the   $  J_{SC}  $   value obtained from the J-V curve is relatively close to the integrated   $  J_{SC}  $   density obtained by EQE spectral integration (difference less than 7%). We measured and compared the stability of device A and device E in the air for 7 days, as showed in Fig. 5d. The performance of device A degrades more faster, while device E can maintain about 88% of the initial efficiency. Fig. S11 are the optical images of device A and device E after being placed in air for 7 days. It is clear that device E is more stable. We also tested the maximum output power of device A and device E within 3 min, as showed in Fig. S12. Obviously, the stability of the maximum output power of the device E is better than that of device A.

# 4. Conclusions

In summary, the use of a low-melting alloy and silver double-layer electrode not only improves the photovoltaic performance of the device, but also improves the stability of the device in the environment. The optimized devices with  $ 20\mathrm{nm} $  Ag and  $ 80\mathrm{nm} $  SnBi combination achieved a PCE of  $ 13.52\% $ . Compared with devices  $ (11.30\%) $  without SnBi addition, the increased performance of such PSCs results from the improvement of charge extraction ability and reduction of charge transfer resistance between the perovskite and cathode. The results demonstrate that the low-melting point alloy as a part of the cathode can reduce the series resistance of the system, and at the same time can reduce the erosion of water and oxygen on the device and improve the stability of the device. Our findings show that low-melting point alloy can be used as cathodes for perovskite solar cells.
