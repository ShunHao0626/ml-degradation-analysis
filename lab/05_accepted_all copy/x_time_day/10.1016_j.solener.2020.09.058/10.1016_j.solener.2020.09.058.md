# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have captured substantial attention because of their impressive optoelectrical properties and swiftly improved efficiency (Jung et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2012). Through defect passivation, interface engineering and composition engineering, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs has elevated six-fold in the past decade (Jeon et al., 2015; Jiang et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2014). Although the PCE of PSCs has made tremendous progress, the stability is still the focus of continued attention in the scientific community (Graetzel, 2014). The main reason for the drop in efficiency is believed to be the decomposition of perovskite and charge transport layer induced by moisture, photochemistry under environmental conditions (Akbulatov et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2013). Especially high-cost organic hole-transporting materials (HTMs) are often used in PSCs, such as spiroOMeTAD (Yang et al., 2017). However, highly hygroscopic organic salts must be added to improve the conductivity of these HTMs, which greatly affects the long-term stability of PSCs (Nguyen et al., 2014). To solve this problem, some inorganic HTMs with excellent stability and high hole

Copper-based ternary semiconductors have been applied as hole-transporting materials (HTM) for perovskite solar cells (PSCs) in recent years. However, the unavoidable defects of inorganic nanoparticles affect the performance of PSCs. Here, we prepared   $  CuGaS_{2}  $   quantum dots (CGS QDs) and employed them as HTM to PSCs. The Cu content of CGS QDs was precisely controlled and its influence on the photoelectric properties of the solar cells was systematically investigated. An optimal power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 17.56% was obtained as the Cu content is 0.8:1 (Cu:Ga, molar ratio). Photoluminescence and electrochemical impedance analyses indicate that the surface defects of Cu-deficient CGS QDs were significantly reduced, thus resulting in effective suppression of carrier recombination between solar cell interfaces. Moreover, the stability of   $  CuGaS_{2}  $  -based PSCs is promoted obviously compared with that of the device utilizing Spiro-OMeTAD as HTM. This work demonstrated that reducing the Cu content of CGS QDs can effectively improve the PCE of   $  CuGaS_{2}  $  -based solar cells, and CGS QDs with Cu-deficiency can be applied as a potential HTM to PSCs.

An impressive PCE of  $ 17.56\% $  was obtained as the Cu content was reduced to 0.8:1 (Cu:Ga, molar ratio). In addition, compared with PSCs utilizing Spiro-OMeTAD as the HTM,  $ \mathrm{CuGaS}_2 $ -based devices exhibit better stability.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1. Materials and chemicals

Sulfur powder (S, 99.9%), diphenylphosphine (DPP, 98%), 1-octadecene (ODE, 90%), and oleylamine (OAm, 95%) were purchased from Aladdin. Gallium iodide (  $  GaI_{3}  $  , 99.999%) and   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloid solution were obtained from Alfa Aesar. Copper iodide (CuI, 95.995%) was acquired from Sigma-Aldrich. Lead iodine (  $  PbI_{2}  $  , 99.99%), formamidinium iodide (FAI), methylammonium bromide (MABr), and methylammonium chloride (MACl) were received from the Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corporation. All the solvent and chemicals were used without further purification.

# 2.2. Synthesis of CuGaS$_{2}$ QDs

$ \mathrm{CuGaS}_2 $  QDs were synthesized by the conventional hot-injection method with slight modifications (Chang et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2017). Briefly, the sulfur precursor solution was acquired by dissolving  $ 30\mathrm{mg} $  sulfur powder in  $ 1\mathrm{ml} $  DPP to form a transparent solution.

Subsequently, 0.3 mmol CuI, 0.3 mmol  $ \mathrm{Gal}_3 $ , 6 ml OAm and 6 ml ODE were placed in a three-neck flask. The Cu content was controlled by varying the amount of CuI while fixing the amount of  $ \mathrm{Gal}_3 $  and other components. The mixture solution system was degassed three times to remove moisture and other gases. Next, the mixture was heated to  $ 110^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  and maintained under the nitrogen protection for  $ 10\mathrm{min} $ . The color of mixture solution gradually changed from black to transparent light yellow, and then the reaction system was gradually heated to  $ 240^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . The pre-configured DDP-S precursor solution was quickly added to the reaction system and the solution changed from light yellow to orange. After the system was kept for  $ 60\mathrm{min} $ , the reaction system was cooled. The as-synthesized CGS QDs were precipitated three times by centrifugation with acetone. Ultimately, the acquired CGS QDs precipitate was re-dispersed into chlorobenzene for the following applications.

# 2.3. Device fabrication

ITO-coated glass etched by using 0.1 M HCL solution and Zn powder was used as a transparent conductive substrate. The substrates were ultrasonically cleaned with ethanol, deionized water, detergent and acetone for 15 min every time. Thereafter, the substrates were treated with ultraviolet ozone for 15 min to eliminate organic residues. The   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloid solution (8%) was spin-coated on the conductive substrate with a speed of 4000 rpm for 30 s and then annealed at 150 °C for 30 min. After   $  SnO_{2}  $   layer formation, the substrates were transferred to a glove box. The perovskite absorber layer was assembled using the previously reported method (Jiang et al., 2017a). Briefly, the   $  PbI_{2}  $   solution was acquired by dissolving 1.3 M   $  PbI_{2}  $   in DMF and DMSO (9.5:0.5, volume ratio) and stirring at 80 °C for 6 h. The   $  PbI_{2}  $   film was formed by spin-coating the prepared solution on the surface of the   $  SnO_{2}  $   layer at 1500 rpm for 30 s, and then drying at 70 °C for 1 min. Subsequently, the perovskite film was fabricated by spin coating a mixed solution (FAI: MABr:MACl = 60 mg:6 mg:6 mg dissolved in 1 ml of isopropanol) on the   $  PbI_{2}  $   layer at a speed of 1300 rpm for 30 s. Next, the substrates were annealed on a hot plate at 150 °C for 15 min. The CGS QDs with different Cu content dispersion solution were deposited on the perovskite layer by spin coating at 4000 rpm for 30 s, respectively. Subsequently, the substrates were transferred to a hot plate baking at 100 °C for 5 min. The Spiro-OMeTAD film was fabricated by spin coating the precursor solution at a rate of 4000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, Au counter electrode was deposited by thermal evaporation to form an entire device structure.

# 3. Results and discussion

In order to obtain CGS QDs with controlled composition, we adjusted the Cu content from 1.2:1 to 0.6:1 (Cu:Ga, molar ratio) during the synthesis process. To determine the final composition of CGS QDs, energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) measurement was carried out. The actual Cu content in CGS QDs is presented in Table S1 (Supporting Information). It can be clearly noticed that the measured Cu content is very close to the theoretical addition amount. Fig. 1a exhibits the UV–Vis absorption spectra of CGS QDs with varying Cu content. A small hump rather than obvious exciton peaks can be noticed in the absorption spectra. Meanwhile, as the Cu content decreases, the peak position of

CGS QDs exhibited a systematic blue shift. The optical band gap of CGS QDs can be deduced by the formula (Wei et al., 2019):

$$
(\alpha h v) ^ {2} = \left(h v - E _ {g}\right) ^ {1 / 2}
$$

where   $  \alpha  $   is absorbance coefficient, h is Plancak's constant, v is light frequency and   $  E_{g}  $   is optical band-gap energy. The band-gap of CGS QDs with Cu content of 1.2:1, 1:1, 0.8:1, and 0.6:1 (Cu:Ga, molar ratio) can be calculated to be 2.45 eV, 2.54 eV, 2.62 eV, and 2.73 eV from Tauc plots (see Fig. S1). This indicates that the bandgap of CGS QDs is widened as the Cu content decreases. A widely accepted view about the valence band of Cu-based QDs is determined by the hybridization of Cu3d and S3p orbitals. Hence, the decrease in the number of orbitals caused by Cu deficiency makes the maximum level of the valence band lower, which explains the effect of Cu content in Cu-based QDs on the optical band gap (Nam et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2017).

Fig. 1b presents the X-ray diffraction patterns of as-synthesized CGS QDs with varying Cu content. We can note that the three broad diffraction peaks assigned to (112), (204)/(220) and (116)/(312) planes of the CGS chalcopyrite structure (JCPDS 25-0279). The XRD patterns of CGS QDs with different Cu content are very similar, even if the Cu content decreases during the synthesis. This indicates that slight changes in composition do not affect the crystal structure of  $ \mathrm{GuGaS_2} $ .

To explore the influence of Cu content on the particle size and morphology of CGS QDs, TEM images of the CGS QDs with varying Cu content are presented in Fig. 2. It can be seen that all of the samples exhibit a spherical-like shape and concentrated nanoparticle size distribution. The mean crystal grain size is approximately  $ 5\mathrm{nm} $ . This

Fig. 4a shows the dependence of the PCE of the PSCs on the Cu content. With the decrease of the Cu content, the efficiency of the devices first improves, and then drops, reaching the optimum at the Cu content of 0.8 (Cu:Ga = 0.8:1, molar ratio). The exhaustive photovoltaic parameters of the PSCs with varying Cu content are summarized in Table 1. It can be found that the Cu content strongly affects the efficiency distribution range of  $ \mathrm{CuGaS}_2 $ -based PSCs. The average PCE of  $ \mathrm{CuGaS}_2 $ -based PSCs is from  $ 14.52\% $  to  $ 17.34\% $  as the Cu content decreases from 1.2:1 to 0.8:1 (Cu: Ga, molar ratio). However, with the further reduction of the Cu content

to 0.6:1 (Cu:Ga, molar ratio), the performance of PSCs deteriorated significantly. The J-V curves of the champion  $ \mathrm{CuGaS_2} $ -based PSCs with varying Cu content is displayed in Fig. 4b. The PCE of champion  $ \mathrm{CuGaS_2} $ -based PSCs with Cu content of 1.2:1, 1:1, 0.8:1 and 0.6:1 (Cu:Ga, molar ratio) are  $ 14.81\% $ ,  $ 16.85\% $ ,  $ 17.56\% $  and  $ 14.51\% $ , respectively. This indicates that Cu-deficiency is beneficial to the improvement of device performance. Fig. S2 (Supporting Information) shows the J-V curves (forward and reverse scan direction) of the optimal PSCs with different Cu content, which exhibits that the J-V curves of PSCs with  $ \mathrm{CuGaS_2} $  as the HTM has a slight hysteresis.

Fig. 4d exhibits the stabilized power outputs of   $  CuGaS_{2}  $  -based PSCs with different Cu content. It is can be seen that the PCE of the solar cells ascend rapidly to steady state and the optimal PCE of 17.56% was achieved at 0.85 V, as the Cu content is 0.8:1 (Cu:Ga, molar ratio).

To further analyze the carrier dynamics and recombination process at the perovskite/HTL interface, EIS measurements were performed (Dualeh et al., 2014; Pockett et al., 2015). Fig. 6a shows the Nyquist points of  $ \mathrm{CuGaS}_2 $ -based devices with different Cu content. The interface charge transfer and recombination process of PSCs is reflected by the arc at the mid-frequency region (Lv et al., 2015). The Nyquist plots can be fitted with a corresponding equivalent circuit exhibited in the inset of Fig. 6a. The simple circuit comprises the series resistance ( $ \mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{s}} $ ), charge recombination resistance ( $ \mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{rec}} $ ), and constant phase element (CPE) (Reddy et al., 2016). The fitted parameters of  $ \mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{s}}, \mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{rec}} $ , and CPE from Nyquist plots are listed in Table 3. It can be found that the  $ \mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{rec}} $  increases significantly with the Cu content decreasing from 1.2:1 to 0.6:1 (Cu:Ga, molar ratio). The results show that the PSCs based on copper-deficient  $ \mathrm{CuGaS}_2 $  QDs as HTMs exhibit relatively low charge recombination, which may be one of the reasons for the efficiency improvement of their PSCs. However, when the Cu content of  $ \mathrm{CuGaS}_2 $ -based solar cells was further reduced to 0.6:1 (Cu:Ga, molar ratio), the relatively wide band gap may play a key factor affecting carrier transport (see Fig. 1a and Fig. S1), resulting in degraded device performance (Wang et al., 2017).

# 4. Conclusion

In summary, we synthesized high-quality CGS QDs by precisely controlling the Cu content, and applied them to PSCs as a novel HTM. We systematically studied the effect of Cu content on the photovoltaic performance of  $ \mathrm{CuGaS_2} $ -based PSCs. It can be found that copper-deficient CGS QDs were good to improve the photovoltaic performance of solar cells. The improvement of device performance can be attributed to Cu-deficient CGS QDs with lower surface defects, which suppresses the carrier recombination process. The  $ \mathrm{CuGaS_2} $ -based PSCs (Cu:Ga = 0.8:1, molar ratio) achieved an impressive PCE of  $ 17.56\% $ . This work shows that high-quality CGS QDs are potential HTM for the preparation of efficient and stable PSCs.
