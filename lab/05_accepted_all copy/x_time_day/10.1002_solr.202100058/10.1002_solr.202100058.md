# Enhanced Charge Transport and Interface Passivation in Efficient Perovskite Solar Cells Using Sulfur-Doped Graphite Carbon Nitride-Modified   $  SnO_{2}  $  -Based Electron Transport Layers

Wei Cao, Jian Zhang,  $  ^{*}  $   Kaifeng Lin,  $  ^{*}  $   Lele Qiu, Junzhuo Li, Yayu Dong, Jiaqi Wang, Debin Xia, Ruiqing Fan, and Yulin Yang  $  ^{*}  $

High-quality electron transport layer (ETL) is beneficial to improve the charge extraction and transport, which determines the performance of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). However, the unbalanced charge extraction and interface problems commonly occur in the tin oxide  $ (\mathrm{SnO}_2) $  ETL. Herein, the sulfur-doped graphite carbon nitride (CNS) nanosheets are prepared and utilized for modifying the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  ETL to fabricate high-performance PSCs. The CNS-modified  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  ETL exhibits enhanced electron mobility and conductivity, and matched energy level with perovskite, which promotes the extraction and transport of charge carriers at the interface, and balances charge extraction with the hole transport layer. In addition, interfacial carrier recombination is significantly reduced through effective interface passivation of sulfur atoms in CNS with the undercoordinated lead ions in perovskite films. Meanwhile, the introduction of an interfacial control material CNS also contributes to improve the crystalline quality of perovskite films with increasing grain size and light absorption intensity. As a consequence, an outstanding improvement in power conversion efficiency (PCE) from  $ 18.98\% $  to  $ 20.33\% $  is achieved after introducing CNS into the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  ETL, as well as an enhancement in stability against humidity, retaining near  $ 90\% $  of the initial PCE after aging in the ambient atmosphere for 30 days.

# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) relying on the organic-inorganic metal halide hybrid perovskites as light-harvesting materials have achieved rapid development in recent years, due to their superior photoelectrical properties, such as high absorption coefficient, large carrier mobility, tunable bandgaps, and long charge carrier

crystallization of perovskite and determine its crystallization quality. Therefore, it is expected that more effective measures should be developed to optimize   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL quality and modify the interface with perovskite.

Additive engineering is regarded as an effective strategy to improve the electrical properties of   $  SnO_{2}  $   and the interface modification, which affects charge extraction and transport.  $  ^{[7]}  $   Metal cations and polymers including Li,  $  ^{[8]}  $   Nb,  $  ^{[9]}  $   triphenylphosphine oxide (TPPO),  $  ^{[10]}  $   polyethylene glycol (PEG)  $  ^{[11]}  $   have been demonstrated to be effective additives by acting on reducing the work function of   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL and tailoring the interface. Nevertheless, the induced defects within the inorganic lattice by the introduction of metal cations would impede the improvement of PSCs. In addition, carbon materials and their derivatives, such as, graphene,  $  ^{[12]}  $   carbon nanotubes,  $  ^{[13]}  $   and so on, also have been proved as functional materials to enhance the conductivity realizing high-efficient PSCs. Even though, the nonmatched interface problems between ETL and perovskite still remain in most of previous works. Therefore, improving the electrical properties of ETL

Graphite carbon nitride (CN), a 2D graphitic  $ \pi $ -conjugated stacking material with high pyridinic-N content, possesses superior photoelectric properties, and excellent thermal and chemical stability. Meanwhile, the properties of CN could be enhanced by simple modification, like elements doping and constructing heterostructure.[14] Recently, CN became a rising star in the field of PSCs and exhibited surprising achievements.[15] For example, Jiang et al. added the CN in perovskite films to improve crystal quality and conductivity with realizing high efficiency and stability of PSCs, due to the stable structure and outstanding conductivity of CN.[16] Nowadays, nonmetallic element-doped CN has attracted growing attentions for enhanced photoelectric properties and numerous active sites in many fields.[17] Nevertheless, the application of CN doped with nonmetallic elements in PSCs still remains largely unexplored. Furthermore, chlorine and sulfur atoms have been verified to passivate effectively interfacial trap states by interacting with undercoordinated lead ions in perovskite films, which exhibits great potential in eliminating the hysteresis in PSCs.[5,18]

Herein, the sulfur-doped graphite carbon nitride (CNS) nanosheets were prepared by a two-step calcination thermal-etching method. The as-prepared CNS shows typical irregular nanosheets stacking morphology and the sulfur atoms partly replace nitrogen atoms in the lattice. The obtained CNS was introduced into   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL-based PSCs to simultaneously modulate the electrical properties of   $  SnO_{2}  $   and passivate the trap states at the ETL/perovskite interface for efficient planar PSCs. Benefiting from the excellent conductivity of CNS, the electron mobility and conductivity of modified   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL (  $  SnO_{2}  $  -CNS) are significantly enhanced with the increase in CNS concentration. The energy-level alignment is optimized due to the enhanced matching between   $  SnO_{2}  $  -CNS ETL and perovskite. Furthermore, the sulfur atoms in CNS can interact with the undercoordinated lead ions in perovskite film, which significantly reduce the trap state density at the interface with perovskite. Meanwhile the quality of perovskite films is also improved with enlarged grain size and enhanced crystallinity, indicating the uniform morphology   $  SnO_{2}  $  -CNS ETL as the platform promotes the crystallization of perovskite. Thus, the device modified by CNS achieves a PCE up to 20.33% with significantly reduced hysteresis, which is superior to that of the control device (18.98%). More significantly, the CNS-modified device provides considerable improvement in stability, retaining 81% and 87% of the initial PCE after aging in   $  N_{2}  $   atmosphere at 85 °C for 10 days and ambient atmosphere (relative humidity 20–40%) for 30 days, respectively. The application of heteroatom-doped graphite carbon nitride in ETLs paves a way to further improve the performance of PSCs and facilitate optoelectronic applications.

# 2. Results and Discussion

The sulfur-doped graphite carbon nitride (named CNS) nanosheets were obtained using thiourea as precursor through a facile two-step calcination thermal-etching process, the fabrication schematic diagram and structural simulation are shown in Figure 1a. To clarify the structure of CNS, the Fourier transform

infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) and powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) were carried out. As shown in Figure 1b, the characteristic stretching vibration of s-triazine ring and the C–N heterocycle, around 803 and 1200–1600 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  , respectively, could be seen in the FT-IR spectrum, which indicates the main structure is maintained after the sulfur doping.  $  ^{[19]}  $   In addition, there are two distinct diffraction peaks appear in the PXRD pattern, including 13.06° for the (100) crystal plane and 27.44° for the (002) crystal plane, can be attributed to the interplanar packing of heptazine units and the π–π interlayer stacking, respectively (Figure 1c).  $  ^{[20]}  $   The X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) was used to further reveal the chemical compositions and electronic structure of as-prepared CNS. The N 1s peaks locate at 398.46 eV (C–N=C), 399.86 eV (N–(C)  $  _{3}  $  ), 404.32 eV (C–N–H) in Figure S1, Supporting Information, suggesting the main element of graphite carbon nitride.  $  ^{[21]}  $   The high-resolution XPS spectrum of C 1s (Figure 1d) can be split into three peaks at 284.79, 286.14, and 288.10 eV which belong to the sp  $  ^{2}  $   C–C bond, C–S bond, and N–C=N bond of carbon in N-containing aromatic rings, respectively.  $  ^{[22]}  $   Moreover, two peaks at 163.67 and 167.53 eV in the S 2p spectrum of CNS (Figure 1e) are assigned to C–S bonds and oxidized sulfur, respectively, which indicates the sulfur substitutes the lattice nitrogen and consistent with the results of C 1s spectrum.  $  ^{[23]}  $   Therefore, these results clearly demonstrate that CNS was successfully synthesized and the atomic composition of sulfur in CNS is about 1.67% based on the XPS results. Furthermore, the introduction of sulfur into CN not only obtains more active sites but exhibits superior optical properties according to the UV–vis absorption and photoluminescence (PL) spectra (Figure S2, Supporting Information). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were carried out to investigate the morphology of CNS, which shows a typical nanosheet structure with irregular sheets stacking. The distribution of sulfur element was further confirmed by the corresponding energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS) spectroscopy (Figure S3, Supporting Information). Meanwhile, the thickness of CNS nanosheets is less than 3 nm measured by the atomic force microscopy (AFM), which ensures that CNS can be effectively combined in the SnO  $  _{2}  $   ETL (Figure S4, Supporting Information).

The schematic illustration of CNS used in   $  SnO_{2}  $   is shown in Figure 2a. To confirm the existence of CNS and the interactions between CNS and   $  SnO_{2}  $ , the FT-IR, EDS, and XPS of   $  SnO_{2}-CNS  $   film were measured. In Figure 2b, the FT-IR spectra of   $  SnO_{2}-CNS  $   film exhibits a characteristic peak at   $  803\ cm^{-1}  $ , which is the s-triazine ring characteristic stretching vibration peak of CNS, whereas it is not found in   $  SnO_{2}  $   film. The EDS spectroscopy of the   $  SnO_{2}-CNS  $   film shows the uniform distribution and further confirm the existence of CNS in the   $  SnO_{2}  $   (Figure S5, Supporting Information). Comparing with the   $  SnO_{2}  $   film, the Sn 3d peaks from   $  SnO_{2}-CNS  $   film are shifted 0.2 eV toward high binding energy (Figure 2c), indicating the increased coordination number surrounding Sn atom which exhibits the interaction between S atoms and Sn atoms.  $  ^{[24]}  $   These results suggest the uniform   $  SnO_{2}-CNS  $   film can be prepared due to the interaction between as-synthesized CNS and   $  SnO_{2}  $   nanoparticles.

$$
V _ {\mathrm{TFL}} = \frac {e N _ {\mathrm{trap}} L ^ {2}}{2 \varepsilon \varepsilon_ {0}}
$$

(1)

where   $  V_{TFL}  $   is the trap-filling limit voltage, e is the elementary charge, L is the thickness of the perovskite film,   $  \varepsilon  $   and   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   are the relative dielectric constant, and vacuum permittivity, respectively.  $  ^{[27]}  $   The trap state density of the perovskite film deposited on   $  SnO_{2}-CNS  $   ETL is calculated to be   $  1.26 \times 10^{16} cm^{-3}  $  , which much lower than that on   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL (  $  1.47 \times 10^{16} cm^{-3}  $  ) and   $  SnO_{2}-CN  $   ETL (  $  1.40 \times 10^{16} cm^{-3}  $   in Figure S13, Supporting Information). The reduction of trap state density further

confirms the interfacial modification of CNS by the interaction of the undercoordinated lead ions and sulfur atoms in CNS.

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, we successfully prepared sulfur-doped graphite carbon nitride (CNS) nanosheets and optimized the concentration of CNS in  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  ETL for achieving high-performance PSCs with a

PCE of 20.33% and a small hysteresis. It has been proven that the introduction of CNS into   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL can effectively enhance electron mobility and conductivity, and optimize interfacial energy level, resulting in a reduced hysteresis. In addition, the   $  SnO_{2}  $  -CNS ETL with uniform morphology as the substrate brings about high crystalline perovskite film with large grain size and light absorption. Correspondingly, the interfacial trap states have been effectively passivated by sulfur in CNS interacting with the undercoordinated lead ions in the perovskite films, which further reduces the interfacial recombination. Furthermore, the stabilities of CNS-doped devices are improved with maintaining 81% and 87% of the initial PCE in   $  N_{2}  $   atmosphere at 85 °C for 10 days and an ambient atmosphere for 30 days, respectively, which can be attributed to the improved crystalline perovskite films and reduced interfacial trap states. This work provides an important insight in the design of novel additive and triggers unlimited reverie of element-doped graphite carbon nitride materials in PSCs, which would push on the industry of perovskite photovoltaics.

Synthesis of CNS nanosheets: Sulfur-doped graphite carbon nitride (CNS) nanosheets were synthesized by two-step calcination thermal-etching method using thiourea as precursor   $  \left(\mathrm{CH}_{4}\mathrm{N}_{2}\mathrm{S}\right)  $   via in situ doping of sulfur element into the graphitic carbon nitride network. In brief, 10 g thiourea was put in a tube furnace heating to 550°C with a heating rate 5 °C min  $  ^{-1}  $   and kept 2 h in nitrogen atmosphere. Then, the obtained solid powder was heated for another 1 h at 500°C with heating rate of 5 °C min  $  ^{-1}  $   to obtain nanosheets.  $  ^{[14b,30]}  $   The as-prepared solid powder was collected after carefully grinding and defined it as CNS.

Device Fabrication: FTO (14 Ω sq  $  ^{-1}  $  , NSG) was etched by 4 M HCl and zinc power, followed by ultrasonically washing with deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol, respectively. Before the SnO  $  _{2}  $  precursor solution deposited, the FTO was treated with UV ozone for 15 min. The 2.67 wt% SnO  $  _{2}  $  precursor solution was obtained by diluting 15 wt% SnO  $  _{2}  $  aqueous colloidal with different volumes of CNS dispersion and deionized water.  $  ^{[29a]}  $  The SnO  $  _{2}  $  (SnO  $  _{2}  $  —CNS) precursor solution was spin at 3000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 150 °C for 1 h. To prepare perovskite precursor solution, PbI  $  _{2}  $  (1.1 M), PbBr  $  _{2}  $  (0.2 M), FAI (1.0 M), and MABr (0.2 M) were dissolved in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) (4:1 volume ratio) mixed solution. Then, 10 vol% CsI was added. The perovskite films were prepared using a two-step spin-coated process of 1000 rpm for 10 s and 6000 rpm for 20 s. At the 5 s before the end of the procedure, 200 μL of chlorobenzene was dropped on the substrates followed by annealed at 100 °C for 1 h. The HTLs prepared at 4000 rpm for 20 s by dissolving 72.3 mg of Spiro-OMeTAD, 17.5 μL of Li-TFSI solution (520 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  in acetonitrile), 28.8 μL of 4-tert-butylpyridine, and 27 μL FK209(100 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  in acetonitrile) in 1 mL of chlorobenzene. Finally, 80–100 nm gold was deposited on perovskite film by thermal evaporation.

Characterization: FT-IR were obtained on a PerkinElmer Spectrum 100 FT-IR spectrometer. XRD patterns of powders and films were collected using a X'Pert PRO (PANalytical, Netherlands) instrument with a Cu Kα radiation source. XPS and UPS were carried out on an ESCALAB-250 spectrometer (Thermo, America) diffraction instrument (C 1s at 284.8 eV as the energy reference for XPS and He I [21.22 eV] emission line for UPS). The UV–vis absorption spectra and transmittance spectra were carried out on PerkinElmer 750S spectrophotometer. TEM images were acquired from Tecnai G2 F30 (FEI, America) transmission electron microscope. SEM and EDS mapping images were recorded using the Rili SU 8000HSD Series Hitachi new-generation cold field emission scanning electron microscopy. The electron mobilities of SnO₂ and SnO₂–CNS were measured using the SCLC model on an electron-only devices of FTO/PCBM/ETL/PCBM/Au. The mobility is calculated by the Mott–Gurney equation:  $  J = (9/8) \varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}} \varepsilon_{0} \mu_{\mathrm{e}} (\mathrm{V}^{2}/\mathrm{d}^{3})  $ , where  $  J  $  is the current density,  $  \varepsilon_{0}  $  is the permittivity of the vacuum,  $  \varepsilon_{r}  $  is the relative permittivity of SnO₂,  $  \mu_{\mathrm{e}}  $  is the electron mobility,  $  V  $  is applied effective voltage, and  $  d  $  is the thickness of ETLs (30 nm). $ ^{[9,26]} $  The conductivity  $  \sigma  $  of ETLs was measured on Keithley 2400 source measurement, calculating by fitting the  $  J-V  $  curves according to the equation:  $  \sigma = (l \times d)/(V \times A)  $ , where  $  d  $  is the thickness of the ETLs (30 nm). $ ^{[12a,31]} $  The AFM measurements were carried out on Bruker Dimension Fastscan. The steady-state PL and TRPL spectra were carried out on Edinburgh FLS 920 fluorescence spectrometer by the exciting light source of Edinburgh Xe 900 450 W xenon-arc lamp. The corresponding  $  J-V  $  curves and EIS were carried out on a Gamry interface 1000 E electrochemical workstation and a standard xenon-lamp-based solar simulator (Oriel, 300 W) at AM 1.5 G sunlight (1 sunlight intensity, 100 mW cm $  ^{-2}  $ ) with the condition of 100 mV s $  ^{-1}  $  scan rate in air and the frequency range from 0.1 to 10 $  ^{5}  $  Hz, respectively. The light intensity was calibrated by a standard silicon reference cell. Active area of solar cells is 0.06 cm $  ^{2}  $ . IPCE measurements were obtained from a Newport IPCE measurement system. Transient photocurrent and photovoltage decay measurements were carried out on the self-assembling system which is similar to that reported previously. $ ^{[29]} $
