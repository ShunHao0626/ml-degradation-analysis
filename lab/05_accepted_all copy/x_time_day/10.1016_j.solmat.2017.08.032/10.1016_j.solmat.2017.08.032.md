# 1. Introduction

In recent years, low temperature processed perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have demonstrated immense prospect for mass production in roll-to-roll process with flexible substrates [1-5]. In particular, PSCs, incorporating solution processed ZnO thin films as electron transport layer (ETL) provide a facile, low-cost device fabrication pathway [3,6,7]. However, the low temperature processed ZnO ETL exhibits energetic disorder induced deep trap states (as deep as  $ 0.2\mathrm{eV} $  below the conduction band [8]) due to the chemisorption of oxygen [9,10], which causes the electrons from perovskite LUMO (Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital) to hop between these trap states [9,11] before being finally collected by the cathode electrode. The electron hopping phenomena in the deep trap states of ZnO film can lead to trap-mediated recombination [9,12,13], which is a major process limitation of such low temperature processed ETL. # ABSTRACT

The presented work demonstrates the development of highly stable low temperature processed Cesium compound incorporated ZnO electron transport layer (ETL) for perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Cesium compounds such as CA (cesium acetate) and CC (cesium carbonate) modified ETLs are employed for fabricating highly efficient (PCE:  $ \sim 16.5\% $  ) mixed organic cation based  $ \mathrm{MA}_{0.6}\mathrm{FA}_{0.4}\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  PSCs via restricted volume solvent annealing (RVSA) method. Here, CA ETL demonstrates a  $ 50\mathrm{meV} $  upshift in Fermi level position with respect to CC ETL, contributing to higher n-type conductivity and lower electron injection barrier at the interface. Furthermore, CA ETL also exhibits profound influence on the perovskite microstructure leading to larger grain size and uniform distribution. Cesium acetate incorporated devices exhibit about  $ 82\% $  higher PCE compared to conventional CC devices. In addition to higher photovoltaic performance, CA devices exhibit mitigated photo-current hysteresis phenomena compared to CC devices, owing to suppressed electrode polarization phenomena. Besides, the stability of the CA devices are  $ 400\% $  higher than the conventional CC devices, retaining almost  $ 90\% $  of its initial PCE even after a month-long (30 days) systematic degradation study. The mechanism behind superior performance and stability is investigated and discussed comprehensively.

Despite being notable endeavours towards trap-state passivation of ZnO ETL for PSCs, the reported studies   $  [12–14]  $   demonstrate some process limitations requiring further research attentions into this direction. Firstly, small molecular buffer layer like PCBM, in conjunction with ZnO film   $  [12]  $   has been reported to either aggregate or diffuse during the perovskite annealing process   $  [16]  $  , which exposes the perovskite layer to the underlying ZnO film and thus effectively eliminates the benefit associated with the trap-state passivation. In this regard, it is intriguing to explore the ZnO surface modification with cesium based compound like cesium acetate (CA), which has been reported to exhibit superior conductive property compared to pristine ZnO film [18] that can compensate the lower transmittance for the doped ZnO film and contribute to enhanced device   $  J_{SC}  $  , as reported for organic solar cell devices [18]. So far, for perovskite solar cells, cesium carbonate (CC) ETL has been incorporated as a surface modifier along with high temperature (500 °C) processed   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETL [19,20]; however, the compatibility of CC based surface modification along with low temperature processed ZnO ETL is yet to be explored. Furthermore, to date, no perovskite study has been conducted incorporating CA doped ZnO as ETL, although CA doped ZnO ETL bids fair to be a promising replacement for pristine ZnO ETL due to its enhanced conductive property [18] and conceivable beneficial role in trap state passivation of pristine ZnO film in Burstein-Moss effect [21]. It is, thus, intriguing to investigate the effectiveness of ZnO surface modification with cesium compounds for perovskite solar cells and to delve into the charge transport property [22–24], inherent electrode polarization and concomitant photo-current hysteresis characteristics [25] and device aging phenomena [26] of such low temperature processed devices. Our current work is aimed to close these knowledge gaps.

In this work, we have reported surface modification of low temperature processed sol-gel ZnO ETL with cesium compounds (CA and CC) for fabricating highly efficient (highest PCE: 16.45%) mixed organic cation based   $  MA_{0.6}FA_{0.4}PbI_{3}  $   PSC in restricted volume solvent annealing (RVSA) method [27]. The photovoltaic performance, photocurrent hysteresis and device aging phenomena in CA and CC ETL based PSCs have been systematically investigated and compared. Enhanced device performance with CA devices has been explained with elaborate surface morphology, topography and hydrophobicity characterization, material workfunction and electron injection barrier investigation with ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) and X-Ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurement, along with charge transport and trap-assisted recombination analysis by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). The mitigated hysteresis phenomenon with CA devices has been elucidated in terms of inherent electrode polarization process. A systematic month-long degradation study has been conducted to comprehend the device stability of the fabricated PSCs. Augmented device stability with CA PSCs has been investigated with frequency-dependent capacitive response and Mott-Schottky characterization of the aged devices.

# 2. Experimental details

# 2.1. Device fabrication

ITO/glass substrates were cleaned sequentially with Hellmanex III, DI water, Acetone and Isopropanol with duration of 10 min for each. For CC ETL, at first, sol-gel processed ZnO film was deposited on top of ITO/glass substrate in a process reported in our earlier works [9,28]. In brief, 0.48 M sol-gel ZnO solution was prepared by dissolving zinc acetate dihydrate in 2-methoxyethanol with an additive ethanolamine by vigorous stirring for 24 h. ZnO precursor solution was spin coated on ITO/glass substrate at 4000 rpm for 60 s and then annealed on a hot-plate at 140 °C for half an hour. For CC ETL, 0.5 wt% CC in DI water was spincoated over the ZnO layer at 3000 rpm for 30 s and dried at 100 °C. The process was repeated once, as demonstrated in a previous perovskite literature with TiO₂ ETL [20]. For further referencing in our manuscript, by CC ETL, we will denote the ZnO/Cs carbonate bilayer ETL. For CA ETL, a 0.09 M cesium acetate (> 99.99% trace metal basis, sigma aldrich) solution with 2-methoxyethanol solvent was prepared by vigorous stirring for 4 h at 80 °C. Later, the aforementioned 0.48 M sol-gel ZnO solution was separately doped with the stock solution of cesium acetate to obtain five different solutions having the dopant concentration of 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 wt%. Unless otherwise stated, by CA ETL, we will refer to the 2% (optimized) cesium acetate doped ZnO ETL (the

details of the ETL optimization process have been explained in subsequent device performance section) in our work. All the cesium acetate solutions were spin-cast and annealed on ITO/glass substrate in a similar manner as sol-gel ZnO ETL. For both the CA and CC ETL devices, the mixed organic cation based perovskite (MA $ _{0.6} $ FA $ _{0.4} $ PbI $ _3 $ ) precursor solution was prepared using PbI $ _2 $  (1 M), MAI (0.6 M) and FAI (0.4 M) powder in anhydrous DMF (N, N-dimethylformamide) solvent and the solution was stirred at 70 °C for 24 h. The perovskite precursor solution was filtered with 0.45 μm PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene) filter and deposited on the ETL coated substatres with pipettes for spin-coating. A dry N $ _2 $  gas stream was blown over the central part of the film dynamically (during the spinning at 2500 rpm) after 9 s of the commencement of spin-casting in nucleation aided gas-assisted method [29]. After the spin-coating, the substrates were annealed at 100 °C for 10 min on hotplate in RVSA method [27]. In brief, a glass slide was placed on top of the substrates during the annealing session, which acted as a confining barrier for methyl amine gas to escape from perovskite surface and thus preventing thermal degradation of perovskite on alkaline ZnO film [30]. For HTL (hole transport layer), 73.3 mg/ml Spiro-OMeTAD in chlorobenzene was doped with 17.5 μL Li-TFSI (520 mg/ml in Acetronitrile) and 28.8 μL 4-TBP. The Spiro-OMeTAD layer was spin-casted on the perovskite film with 3000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, 100 nm Ag layer was deposited on the HTL coated substrate by thermal evaporation with an evaporation rate of 2 Å/s under a vacuum condition of 1 × 10 $  ^{-6}  $  mbar. The device area was fixed to be 4.5 mm $  ^{2}  $  with the use of a metal mask. So, the overall device structures were: ITO/CA or CC ETL/MA $ _{0.6} $ FA $ _{0.4} $ PbI $ _3 $  perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD/Ag (Fig. 5(A)).

# 2.2. Device characterization

The current–voltage characteristics of the devices were measured with a NREL calibrated Keithley 2400 Source Meter under 100 mW/cm  $  ^{2}  $  (AM 1.5G) simulated sunlight using a black, non-reflective aperture (4.5 mm  $  ^{2}  $  ) mask over the cells to avoid overestimation of the device current densities associated with the active device area. For optical characterization like transmittance, reflectance and absorbance measurement, a UV–VIS–NIR spectrometer (Perkin Elmer–Lambda 950) was used. X-ray diffraction (XRD) with CuKα radiation was performed by step-scanning with a step size of 0.02°. Surface topology view was captured by Carl Zeiss AURIGA Cross Beam SEM and the surface roughness was measured with Bruker Dimension ICON SPM AFM machine. Contact angle measurement was conducted using Ramé-hart contact angle goniometer (Model 200) and the contact angles were determined using DROPimage advanced software. XPS measurement was conducted in ESCALAB250Xi instrument (Thermo Scientific, UK) with background pressure of  $  2 \times 10^{-9}  $  mbar using a mono-chromated AlKα (hν = 1486.68 eV) anode (120 W, 13.8 kV, 8.7 mA). UPS measurement was carried out in ESCALAB250Xi instrument (Thermo Scientific, UK) using the He I photon line (hν = 21.22 eV) of a He discharge lamp under UHV condition (  $  4 \times 10^{-10}  $  mbar). The impedance analysis was conducted with an Autolab PGSTAT-30 equipped with a frequency analyzer module in the frequency range from 1 MHz to 1 Hz. AC oscillating amplitude was as low as 20 mV (RMS) to maintain the linearity of the response.

Deposited CA and CC ETL films have also been checked for material crystallinity and crystallite dimension using the spectral fitting of the corresponding XRD patterns. Fig. 1(D) shows the XRD patterns for CA and CC ETL films on ITO/glass substrates and Table S3 presents the normalized peak intensity, peak width or FWHM (full width at half maximum) and crystallite dimension of CA and CC films, as calculated with the Debye Scherrer equation [37] from XRD spectral fitting. From Fig. 1(D), the presence of (002) and (101) characteristic diffraction peaks for both CA and CC ETL films are consistent with the typical XRD patterns observed with cesium acetate doped [38] or cesium carbonate treated [31] ZnO films. Both CA and CC ETL films demonstrate a preferential orientation along (002) plane due to the minimal surface energy [38], which suggests that the  $ c $ -axes of the ETL grains become uniformly perpendicular to the substrate surface [31]. The preferential orientation of the CA and CC ETL films along  $ c $ -axis implicate towards efficient charge (electron) extraction property [9], as the dominant orientation along  $ a $ -axis has been reported to be less kinetically favourable in earlier literature [39]. As observed from Fig. 1(E) and Table S3, both the ETL films demonstrate similar material crystallinity, as indicated by nearly identical normalized peak intensity from the respective XRD pattern. However, the crystallite size for CC

ETL has been found relatively smaller as compared to CA ETL, specially pertaining to (101) characteristic peak (Fig. 1(E) and Table S3).

The optical bandgaps of CA (bandgap: 3.36 eV) and CC (bandgap: 3.34 eV) ETL films are slightly higher compared to pristine ZnO (bandgap: 3.32 eV) film, as observed from the Tauc plots presented in Fig. S1. Relatively large optical bandgap exhibited with cesium doped or modified ZnO films, compared to the pristine film is caused by the presence of excess donor electrons from cesium atoms located at the interstitial site of ZnO lattice matrix [31] in Burstein-Moss effect [21]. In Burstein-Moss effect, an extra amount of energy is required to excite additional valence electron (from cesium atom) to higher states in the conduction band [31], as a doubly occupied state is not possible according to Pauli's principle [40] and thus the excess cesium electrons increase the optical bandgaps of CA and CC films compared to pristine ZnO film.

We have also conducted optical characterization of the deposited ETL films with UV–VIS–NIR spectrometry. Fig. 1(F) shows the transmittance pattern of CA and CC ETL films on top of ITO/glass substrate. In a normal structured (n-i-p) perovskite device [41,42], the main optical role of ETL is to function as a highly transmitting thin film layer to ensure maximum light absorption in the photo-active perovskite film [9,13]. From Fig. 1(F), the AVT (average visible (370–740 nm) transmittance) [43,44] values of both CA and CC ETL films are over 86% (CA ETL: 86.89%, CC ETL: 86.55%), which demonstrate their excellent optical compatibility as ETL for a normal structured PSC.

# 3.3. Material characterization, surface morphology and topography analysis of perovskite film

On top of CA and CC ETL films,   $  MA_{0.6}FA_{0.4}PbI_{3}  $   perovskite films were fabricated in identical manner. Fig. 3(A) shows the XRD patterns of perovskite films on both ETL films. For both the perovskite films, the presence of (110), (202), (220), (310), (224) and (314) characteristic diffraction peaks ascertains the construction of tetragonal perovskite structure [45,46]. Fig. 3(B) presents the normalized peak intensity and crystallite dimension of the perovskite films from XRD spectral fitting. Corresponding numerical values have been tabulated in Table S5 for quantitative comparison. As observed from Fig. 3(B) and Table S5, the material crystallinity of the perovskite film on CA ETL is relatively high, specially pertaining to (110) perovskite characteristic peak. Moreover, the individual crystallite dimension for perovskite film atop CA ETL is relatively higher compared to the same (perovskite) on CC film, specially with respect to (202) and (220) characteristic peaks. The variation in individual crystallite dimension of identically fabricated   $  MA_{0.6}FA_{0.4}PbI_{3}  $   perovskite films on CA and CC ETL films is consistent with the observation from earlier perovskite studies [47,48] that the perovskite surface morphology can vary depending on the underneath layers, on which the perovskite structure forms [47,48]. To investigate the variation in perovskite surface morphology with CA and CC ETL layers underneath, we have done top view SEM imaging of the respective perovskite films. Fig. 4(A)–(B) and (C)–(D) present the SEM images of   $  MA_{0.6}FA_{0.4}PbI_{3}  $   perovskite films on top of CA and CC ETL films, respectively. As predicted from relatively higher constituent crystallite dimension from the XRD spectral fitting (Fig. 3(B)), the grain size of perovskite film atop CA ETL is relatively larger, as compared to the same (perovskite) on CC ETL film (Fig. 4). Larger perovskite grain on top of CA ETL films is correlated with the pinhole-free, compact surface morphology (Fig. 2(A)–(B)) and smooth surface topography (Fig. 2(E)–(F)) of the underlying CA film, which have been reported to contribute to high grain boundary mobility for homogeneous perovskite nuclei distribution, leading to enlargement of perovskite grain. Conversely, increased surface aberration (Fig. 2(G)–(H)) owing to the dendritic grain morphology (Fig. 2(C)–(D)) of the CC ETL film leads to perovskite nucleation in the pinhole-induced cavities [9] on the CC ETL films, which limits the grain boundary mobility of perovskite grain [49], resulting in smaller grain distribution of perovskite on top of CC ETL films. In this regard, we have also checked the wettability of CC and CA ETL films from contact angle measurement (Fig. 3(C) and (D)), since earlier literature [49] suggests that the perovskite grain size is also influenced by the wettability of the underlying layer, it grows on [49]. From Fig. 3(C) and (D), the average contact angles of water droplet on CC and CA ETL films are 33.20° and 49°, respectively, indicating that CC ETL film is more hydrophilic or has higher wettability as compared to CA ETL film. Owing to the higher wettability of CC ETL film, the surface tension dragging force (STDF) at CC ETL surface is

relatively higher [49] compared to that of CA ETL film, which causes dense, heterogeneous perovskite nuclei distribution [49] on CC ETL surface, leading to the formation of smaller perovskite grain atop CC ETL film.

We have also conducted surface topography analysis of the perovskite films on top of CA and CC ETL films. Fig. 4(E)–(F) and (G)–(H) show the two and three dimensional AFM images of perovskite films on top of CA and CC ETL films, respectively. The average and RMS (root mean square) surface roughness of the fabricated perovskite films have been presented in Table S6. As observed from Table S6, perovskite layer atop CC ETL film demonstrates about 10.5% higher RMS surface roughness (CC: 16.90 nm, CA: 15.30 nm) compared to the perovskite on CA ETL film. Higher perovskite film roughness over CC ETL is congruent with the rough surface topography and dendritic grain morphology of CC ETL films, which is an implication towards higher leakage current [9,50] in the perovskite film, when used as a photoactive layer in a complete PSC.

# 3.4. Device performance

Among them, 2% cesium acetate doped ZnO ETL has been found to render the optimum device performance (average PCE: 15.14%), as observed from the optimization data presented in Table S7. # 3.5. Charge transport characteristics

To delve into the charge transport properties of CA and CC devices, we have performed EIS analysis of the fabricated devices. EIS is a useful characterization tool to unravel detailed information regarding the electronic properties of PSCs that govern the charge extraction and carrier recombination phenomena   $  [22,23,25,61]  $  . Fig. 7(C) displays the Nyquist plots of the CC and CA devices at a bias of 800 mV under dark. The experimental data were fitted with an equivalent electronic circuit model (Fig. S3) previously reported with perovskite device   $  [7,9,62]  $  . The equivalent circuit model (Fig. S3) consists of a series resistance,   $  R_{SE}  $   along with two parallel R-C components. From the equivalent circuit, the symbols   $  R_{SE}  $  ,   $  R_{C}  $  ,   $  C_{C}  $  ,   $  R_{Rec}  $   and   $  C_{\mu}  $   refer to the resistance originating from the wire connection and metal contact, contact resistance at perovskite/ETL or perovskite/HTL interface, bulk capacitance, recombination resistance and chemical capacitance respectively. Table 2 presents the fitted values of various electronic parameters extracted from the Nyquist plots at Fig. 7(C). As seen from Table 2, the contact resistance,   $  R_{C}  $   in CA device is about 11% lower compared to CA devices (CC device: 6.30 Ω cm², CA device: 5.62 Ω cm²). Since, the HTL (SpiroOMeTAD)/perovskite (MA  $  _{0.6}  $  FA  $  _{0.4}  $  PbI  $  _{3}  $  ) interface remain unchanged for both the devices, any variation in the contact resistance between the two devices is originated from the ETL (CA or CC)/perovskite (MA  $  _{0.6}  $  FA  $  _{0.4}  $  PbI  $  _{3}  $  ) interface   $  [9,62]  $  . Thus, the lower   $  R_{C}  $   value in CA device confirms the superior charge transport property   $  [9,62]  $   from MA  $  _{0.6}  $  FA  $  _{0.4}  $  PbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite to the CA ETL, compared to CC ETL, which conforms to the higher n-type conductivity of CA ETL film and higher average FF value   $  [9,62]  $   in CA PSCs compared to CC PSCs. Relative to CC device, lower contact resistance (  $  R_{C}  $  ) of CA PSC from EIS analysis is also congruent with its lower series resistance (  $  R_{S}  $  ) value   $  [62]  $   from light J-V curve and lower leakage   $  [59]  $   and higher dark injection current   $  [60]  $   from dark J-V curve.

Conversely, apart from higher contact resistance, CC PSC also exhibits about 71.5% lower recombination resistance,   $  R_{Rec}  $   (CC device:   $  39.15 \Omega cm^{2}  $  , CA device:   $  67.10 \Omega cm^{2}  $  ) than CA device, Since, under dark condition, the charge carrier is injected from the external voltage supply only, the lower recombination resistance for CC PSCs is correlated with the increased charge trapping at the defect or dislocation states [21,62] present at the interfaces between CC ETL and   $  MA_{0.6}FA_{0.4}PbI_{3}  $   perovskite (as observed from pinhole-induced, dendritic surface morphology of CC ETL at Fig. 2(C)–(D)). Thus, lower recombination resistance in CC PSCs implicate that they exhibit higher carrier recombination phenomenon compared to CA PSCs [21,62]. With reference to CA devices, the lower recombination resistance value (  $  R_{Rec}  $  ) of CC devices from EIS analysis is also consistent with their lower value of shunt resistance (  $  R_{Sh}  $  ) from J-V curve [62].

As observed from Fig. 9(B) and (C), aged CC PSC demonstrates lower flat-band potential (CC device:  $ 0.56\mathrm{V} $ , CA device:  $ 0.95\mathrm{V} $ ) compared to aged CA PSCs. Both the aged devices show reduced flat-band potentials compared to their respective initial flat-band potential values (Fig. 7(A) and (B)) due to aging effect [24,61], however, comparatively lower flat-band potential in aged CC PSC insinuates towards more acute trap-assisted recombination phenomena [61] in them compared to aged CA device, which are consistent with the higher intermediate-frequency capacitive values [9,22,25] in aged CC PSC. Higher trap-mediated recombination phenomena in aged CC device thus result in lower normalized  $ \mathrm{V_{OC}} $  values [29,62] retained in them compared to aged CA PSCs, which also leads to lower device stability in CC devices.

# 4. Conclusion

In conclusion, we have reported surface modification of sol-gel ZnO ETL along with cesium compounds (CC and CA ETL) for fabricating highly efficient mixed organic cation based  $ \mathrm{MA}_{0.6}\mathrm{FA}_{0.4}\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  PSC in RVSA method, which has full temperature compatibility with flexible substrates. CA ETL demonstrates a  $ 50\mathrm{meV} $  upshift in Fermi level position with respect to CC ETL, that contributes to its (CA ETL) higher n-type conductivity and lower electron injection barrier for the photogenerated electrons from perovskite layer. Perovskite film grown on CA ETL has larger grain size due to the pinhole-free, uniform grain distribution and less hydrophilic nature of the underlying CA ETL, which

(large grain size) leads to the reduction of inter-particle contact resistance and device series resistance in CA PSC, compared to CC device. Suppressed leakage current and enhanced dark injection current from dark J-V measurement, lower interfacial contact resistance and higher recombination resistance from EIS characterization and higher flat-band potential from Mott-Schottky analysis also explicate the enhanced device performance with CA ETL. Overall, CA devices exhibit about  $ 82\% $  higher PCE compared to CC devices, while the champion CA device demonstrates a PCE of  $ 16.45\% $  in FB-SC scan direction. Apart from the higher photovoltaic performance, CA devices also show mitigated photo-current hysteresis phenomena compared to CC devices, which have been found to be correlated with suppressed electrode polarization phenomena in larger grain sized perovskite atop CA ETL. Additionally, CA devices demonstrate over four times higher device stability than CC devices during month-long, systematic degradation study, which is associated with the thermal-stress and moisture-assisted perovskite decomposition rate and mitigated trap-mediated recombination phenomena in aged CA devices. Thus, the reported low temperature processed CA ETL bids fair to be a promising electron selective contact for the mass production of highly efficient, stable and flexible substrate based perovskite solar cells compatible with roll-to-roll device processing and scalability.
