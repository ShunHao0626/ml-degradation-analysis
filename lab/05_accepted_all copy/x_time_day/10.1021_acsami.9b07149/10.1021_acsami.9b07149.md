# Amphiphilic Fullerenes Employed to Improve the Quality of Perovskite Film and the Stability of Perovskite Solar Cells

Qingxia  $ \mathrm{Fu}^{a,b} $ , Shuqin Xiao $ ^{b,c} $ , Xianglan Tang $ ^{a,b} $ , Yiwang Chen $ ^{a,b} $ , Ting Hu $ ^{*b,c} $

$  ^{b}  $  Institute of Polymers and Energy Chemistry, Nanchang University, 999 Xuefu Avenue, Nanchang 330031, China

$ ^{c} $ School of Materials Science and Engineering, Nanchang University, 999 Xuefu Avenue, Nanchang 330031, China

Corresponding author. Tel.: +86 791 83968703; fax: +86 7918369561. E-mail: huting@ncu.edu.cn (T. Hu)

ABSTRACT: Fullerene end-capped polyethylene glycol (C60-PEG) was introduced via antisolvent method to fabricate the perovskite films. C60-PEG could enlarge the perovskite crystals size and passivate the defects of perovskite films, facilitating the carrier transport and hindering the carrier recombination. In consequence, the superior optoelectronic properties were attained with an improved power conversion efficiency of 17.71% for the perovskite device with C60-PEG treatment. Meanwhile, amphiphilic C60-PEG enhanced the resistance of perovskite films to moisture. After 40 days, the C60-PEG based devices without encapsulation remained 93% and 86% of the original power conversion efficiency value under nitrogen and ambient condition (25 °C temperature, 60% humidity), respectively.

KEYWORDS: perovskite solar cells, amphiphilic fullerenes, passivation, stability

# INTRODUCTION

Perovskite solar cells (PVSCs) have received considerable concerns with rapid develop over the past ten years.  $  ^{1-7}  $   The PVSCs possess various outstanding optoelectronic merits, such as high absorption coefficients  $  ^{8}  $  , high carrier mobility  $  ^{9}  $   and long charge carrier diffusion lengths  $  ^{10}  $  . In addition, solution solution-processable PVSCs could be prepared at low temperature, which is favorable for the large area preparation.  $  ^{11}  $   So far, the current record-certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PVSCs has been upgraded to 24.2% due to these superior properties  $  ^{12}  $  .

In order to obtain great photovoltaic performance of devices, it is essential to fabricate the superior perovskite film.  $  ^{13}  $   Up to now, representative fabrication methods have been reported to prepare homogeneous perovskite layer.  $  ^{14-17}  $   Among them, the one-step method assisted by anti-solvent is the most commonly strategy for preparing the compact perovskite films.  $  ^{18}  $   Unsatisfactory, significant defects are inevitable at the perovskite grain boundaries.  $  ^{19-20}  $   The defects may have a significantly influence on the charge transport and the stability of PVSCs.  $  ^{21}  $   In addition, defects also occur at the perovskite film surfaces with the same effect as the grain boundaries. Consequently, it is vital to reduce the defects for improving the optoelectronic performance and the stability of PVSCs.  $  ^{22-24}  $   Recently, fullerene has been reported to improve the morphology of perovskite film and the photovoltaic performance successfully.  $  ^{25-26}  $   Among them, phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) has been employed to form a perovskite-fullerene graded heterojunction structure with high efficiency and good stability.  $  ^{27}  $   And then   $  \alpha  $  -bis-PCBM has been used to assist perovskite crystallization and passivate defects.  $  ^{28-29}  $   Moreover, polymer (poly(methyl methacrylate)) acted as a polymer-templated nucleation in the anti-solvent solution to boost heterogeneous nucleation and control crystal growth, resulting in less defects and larger crystal grains with a PCE over 21%.  $  ^{30}  $   Some other additives, acid-base additives,  $  ^{31-32}  $   alkylphosphonic acid   $  \omega  $  -ammonium chlorides,  $  ^{33}  $   poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG),  $  ^{34}  $   and poly(vinylpyrrolidone)  $  ^{35-36}  $   have been incorporated into the perovskite precursor solution to obtain a dense and uniform perovskite layer and increase the stability of PVSCs successfully. # EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

# Device Fabrication:

Indium-tin oxide (ITO)-coated glass substrates were cleaned in acetone, deionized water and isopropyl alcohol for 15 min and then dried with nitrogen stream, followed by air plasma for 5 min. NiO $  _{x}  $  film was prepared by spin coating with NiO $  _{x}  $  nanoparticles (20 mg mL $  ^{-1}  $ ) dispersions and baked at 120 °C for 30 min in air. The perovskite Cs $  _{0.1}  $ FA $  _{0.7}  $ MA $  _{0.2}  $ I $  _{3-x}  $ Br $  _{x}  $  precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 439 mg PbI $  _{2}  $  (99.9985%, Alfa Aesar), 37 mg PbBr $  _{2}  $  (Sigma-Aldrich), 120 mg Formamidinium iodide (TCI), 32 mg Methylamine iodide (TCI), 26 mg CsI (Alfa Aesar) in 800 μl mixture solvent (anhydrous N, N-Dimethylformamide (>99%, Sigma-Aldrich) and Dimethyl sulfoxide (4:1, v/v) (>99%, Sigma-Aldrich)). The perovskite solution was deposited at 4000 rpm for 30 s and then annealed at 100 °C for 10 min in the glovebox. In the spin-coating process, 200 μl pure chlorobenzene, PCBM (American Dye Source Inc) or C60-PEG chlorobenzene solution (99.8%, Sigma-Aldrich) was rapidly dropped for anti-solvent treatment. After that, PCBM in chlorobenzene solution and bathocuproine (BCP, 96.0%, Sigma-Aldrich) in ethanol solution (Alfa Aesar) were spin-coated on perovskite film at 2000 rpm for 30 s and 4500 rpm for 30 s, respectively. At the end, 100 nm Ag was deposited by thermal evaporation.

# Device Characterization

Current-voltage (J-V) characteristics were characterized with Keithley 2400 under 100 mWcm  $  ^{-2}  $  simulated AM 1.5 G irradiation (Abet Solar Simulator). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) were conducted on SU8020 at a voltage of 8 kV. X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were performed using a Rigaku D/Max-B X-ray diffractometer with Bragg-Brentano parafocusing geometry. The ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectra were recorded on a SHIMADZU, UV-2600 spectrophotometer. The photoluminescence (PL) spectra and time-resolved photoluminescence (Tr-PL) spectra were measured by an Edinburgh instruments FLS920 spectrometer. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were conducted on a Thermo-VG scientific ESCALAB 250

# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The molecular chemical structure of C60-PEG was illustrated in Figure 1a C60-PEG was synthesized by the Steglich esterification.  $  ^{39}  $   The corresponding synthesis scheme and FTIR spectra of C60-PEG were shown in Figure S1. The architecture of the planar PVSCs and the cross-sectional SEM micrograph were displayed in Figure 1c and Figure 1b, respectively. The C60-PEG molecule was dissolved in chlorobenzene to obtain the dripping solution, which was introduced into perovskite film via anti-solvent method (abbreviated to PVK/C60-PEG). The possible passivation mechanism of C60-PEG for the perovskite film was shown in Figure 1d. Besides, the PCBM was also distributed into the perovskite film with the same method (PVK/PCBM) for comparison. The perovskite device fabrication procedures were represented in the Experimental Section. It is expected that C60-PEG molecule could permeate into perovskite film and passivate the defects of the perovskite film.  $  ^{40}  $   As reported, the fullerene molecule has been proven to passivate the Pb-I defects.  $  ^{41,42}  $   The chemical interactions between the perovskite and C60-PEG were further proved by FTIR and XPS. As the result shown (Figure S2a), the peak originating from the stretching vibration of C=O in pure C60-PEG appeared at 1,735 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   and shifted to 1,710 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   for PVK/C60-PEG. The shift of the C=O vibration to lower wavenumber was probably traceable in the coordination with Pb  $  ^{2+}  $   in the perovskite, where the C=O may donate their lone unpaired electron to the empty orbitals of Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  .  $  ^{43}  $   As shown in Figure S2b, Pb peaks shifted toward higher binding energy for perovskite with C60-PEG treatment, which also proved that there was the chemical interaction between C60-PEG and perovskite. Furthermore, two small peaks (136.2 eV and 141.1 eV) in Pb 4f XPS spectra originated from metallic Pb were observed for pure perovskite.  $  ^{44}  $   The metallic Pb act as nonradiative recombination centers, leading to a worse electronic quality.  $  ^{45}  $   Interestingly, the metallic Pb peaks were much weaker for PVK/C60-PEG, which was ascribed to the interaction between C60-PEG and Pb in

The top-view and cross-sectional SEM images of pristine and treated perovskite films were presented in Figure 2. The PVK/C60-PEG film showed the larger grain size than that of the pristine perovskite film, with the average grain size increased from 240 nm to 320 nm, which was clearly clarified in the cross-sectional SEM images (Figure 2c, d). Furthermore, in the XRD measurements (Figure S3), the Scherrer equation was employed to the most intense peak (110)  $  ^{47}  $  to prove the effect of C60-PEG on the crystal quality of perovskite. As a result, the average crystallite sizes in PVK, PVK/PCBM and PVK/C60-PEG films were 37 nm, 51 nm and 67 nm, respectively. The increased crystallite size indicated that C60-PEG with long sidechain could act as network to accelerate the growth of perovskite to form uniform film with larger crystallite size. Besides, EDS mapping of Pb, I and O with homogeneous distributions in the perovskite/C60-PEG film further demonstrated the result in Figure S4. Additionally, black spots were watched clearly on the perovskite film in Figure 2b, which was supposed to be C60-PEG molecule according to the EDS result. As shown in Figure S5, a uniform and thin fullerene layer was on the perovskite crystals for PVK/PCBM, which was consistent with the report previously  $  ^{27}  $ . Overall, the C60-PEG molecule could well control the growth of perovskite to form uniform film with larger crystallite sizes.

UV-vis absorbance spectra of pristine and treated perovskite films were measured to study the optical properties. The absorption of PVK/PCBM and PVK/C60-PEG films were slightly higher than that of the pristine PVK film (Figure 3a), which was attributed to larger crystallite sizes and uniform films. In order to research the passivation effect of PCBM and C60-PEG on perovskite films, PL and Tr-PL measurements were carried out. Compared to pristine sample (Figure 3a), the PVK/C60-PEG sample quenched PL by 87%, and the PVK/PCBM sample quenched PL by 70%. The incorporation of a fullerene layer on perovskite layer could substantially quench the PL to ensure high electron-collection efficiency in devices  $  ^{27}  $  . # CONCLUSIONS

In summary, the photoelectric properties and stability of PVSCs were simultaneously enhanced by introducing the functional fullerene (C60-PEG) via anti-solvent method during the device fabrication. C60-PEG efficiently increased the perovskite crystals size and passivated defects of perovskite films with higher electron mobility and lower carrier recombination. Consequently, the PCE of devices treated by C60-PEG increased from 15.01% to 17.71%. At the same time, amphiphilic C60-PEG protected the perovskite films from water owing to the hygroscopicity of sidechains. After 40 days, only 7% and 14% degradation of the unencapsulated devices with C60-PEG treatment were observed under nitrogen and ambient condition (temperature   $  \approx25\ ^{\circ}C  $  , humidity   $  \approx60\%  $  ), respectively.
