# A facile surface passivation method for efficient inorganic   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite solar cells with efficiencies over 15%

Shan-Ci Chen  $  ^{1}  $  , Senya Zhang  $  ^{1,2}  $   and Qingdong Zheng  $  ^{1*}  $

ABSTRACT Recently, perovskite solar cells (PVSCs) based on   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   have attracted increasing attention owing to their good balance between efficiency and stability. Solution-processed   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskites usually contain various defects which need passivation for PVSCs with high-performance as well as good stability. Since the degradation of perovskite films usually begins at the grain boundaries, here we report a facile defect passivation strategy by spin-coating a KF solution on the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite surface. The deposited KF salt mostly locates at the grain boundaries of the perovskite surface, resulting in PVSCs with improved stability. Both steady-state and time-resolved photoluminescence results suggest that the defects of perovskite were significantly passivated by KF treatment. Consequently, the best-performance PVSC based on   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   with KF treatment shows an enhanced power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 15.01% with a larger open circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) of 1.26 V in comparison with the pristine   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $ -based counterpart which exhibits an inferior PCE of 14.14% with a   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.18 V.

Keywords: fluorides, energy conversion, perovskites, defect passivation, grain boundary

# INTRODUCTION

In the past five years, organic-inorganic halide perovskite solar cells (PVSCs) have been widely investigated and they have become one of the most promising technologies to convert sunlight to electricity with low-cost solution processing [1,2]. Although the best power conversion efficiency (PCE) of organic-inorganic PVSCs has now exceeded  $ 23\% $  [1], the volatility and thermal instability of organic cations (e.g.,  $ \left[\mathrm{(NH_2)_2CH}\right]^+ $ ,  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3^+) $  can lead to the stability problems of PVSCs which limit their further

commercialization [3]. To solve this issue, an emerging approach is to develop all-inorganic perovskites which can be prepared by replacing the organic cations with inorganic cations (such as   $  Cs^{+}  $  ). Such all-inorganic cesium lead halide perovskites   $  CsPbX_{3}  $   (X = Cl, Br, and I) do not readily decompose under heat and moisture conditions due to the absence of organic components. For example, the inorganic   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   perovskite is stable at temperatures up to 580 °C, while the organic   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   decomposes at a temperature of around 200°C [4]. Among all kinds of   $  CsPbX_{3}  $   perovskites,   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   and   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   were studied at the early stage of all-inorganic PVSC research [5,6]. The PVSCs based on   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   with PCEs up to 5.95% were firstly reported by Kulbak et al. [6] in 2015. However, its relatively large bandgap (  $  E_{g}  $  ) of ~2.30 eV limits its sunlight-harvesting ability. In contrast, with a more suitable   $  E_{g}  $   of ~1.73 eV [7],   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   perovskite has been used for the most efficient all-inorganic PVSC [8]. Unfortunately, black phase   $  \alpha-CsPbI_{3}  $   perovskite is unstable and will rapidly convert into a non-perovskite phase (  $  \delta-CsPbI_{3}  $  ) upon exposure to ambient atmosphere [7]. In 2016, both McGehee [9] and Snaith [10] groups reported that the optical absorption and the structural stability of   $  CsPbX_{3}  $   can be adjusted by regulating the halogen composition. Meanwhile, they found that the phase stability and phase transition temperature can be adjusted by partial incorporation of Br into the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   perovskite. Starting with a surprising PCE of 9.8% for the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $ -based PVSCs reported by Sutton et al. [10] in 2016, more and more PVSCs based on bromide-containing mixed-halogen perovskites (  $  CsPbI_{3-x}Br_{x}  $  ) have been reported [11–16]. Previously, our group reported a best PCE of 7.3% for all-inorganic PVSCs based on polyethylene glycol (PEG)-

To enhance the light-harvesting ability of PVSCs, we further developed a Zn-doped   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite with an   $  E_{g}  $   of 1.88 eV. The resulting best-performance PVSC showed an impressive PCE of 12.16% [18]. Unlike   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   and   $  CsPbI_{3}  $ ,   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   is able to achieve both good light-harvesting ability and good phase stability, suggesting that   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   is a promising active-layer material for PVSCs with its good balance between the efficiency and stability. In recent two years, many strategies have been reported for the fabrication of high-performance   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $ -based PVSCs [11,12,19–23].

High-quality thin films are essential to achieve high-performance PVSCs. However, many defects and traps are inevitably formed in most perovskite films obtained from solution-processing techniques. Prior studies have demonstrated that the defects at the crystal surface and grain boundaries (GBs) will generate trap states and have a detrimental effect on the performance of PVSCs   $  [24,25]  $  . More importantly, it was found that the degradation of perovskite films usually begins at the GBs   $  [26]  $  . To improve the PCE and stability of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   PVSCs, many defect passivation approaches have been proposed for the perovskite films   $  [27–34]  $  . Chemical doping is an effective way to control the crystallization speed and to passivate the defects of perovskite films. For example, by incorporating a small amount of Sr into   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $  , the surface of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film was enriched with Sr, which provides a passivating effect on the defects of the films. The PCE was increased from 7.7% for the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $  -based device to 11.3% for the   $  CsPb_{0.98}Sr_{0.02}I_{2}Br  $  -based device   $  [27]  $  . Recently, Bai et al.   $  [28]  $   demonstrated that interstitial   $  Mn^{2+}  $   ions doped in the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film could lead to suppressed nucleation and a decreased growth rate, thus resulting in films with better crystallinity and larger grains. Moreover, the excess   $  Mn^{2+}  $   cations would aggregate in the grain boundary and passivate the surface defects. An improved PCE of 13.47% was eventually achieved for the PVSCs based on the 2% Mn-doped perovskite film   $  [28]  $  . More recently, Europium (Eu) and Indium (In) cations were also used to stabilize the black phase   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite   $  [29,30]  $  . Besides, smaller alkali metal cations (such as   $  K^{+}  $  )   $  [31]  $   and some other anions (such as   $  SCN^{-}, Ac^{-}  $  )   $  [32–34]  $   were also incorporated into the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film to increase the grain size, passivate the grain boundary, and improve the performance of the corresponding PVSCs. In addition to the interior defects, perovskite films also have a lot of surface defects which are harmful to the performance and stability of PVSCs. Thus, surface passivation is of high importance in order

to achieve high-efficiency PVSCs. As mentioned previously, some doping ions (such as   $  Sr^{2+}  $   [27],   $  Mn^{2+}  $   [28], and   $  Zn^{2+}  $   [18]) can be enriched on the surface of the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film and passivate the traps at the surface and GBs. Unlike other types of perovskites, new passivation materials for surface modification of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskites are relatively less explored. As far as we know, there are only a few reports about solution-processed surface passivation of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite, in which bulky organic amines,   $  \mathrm{Pb(NO_{3})_{2}}  $  , and poly(3-hexyl-thiophene) (P3HT) were used to modify the perovskite surface [35–38]. For example, Yuan et al. [35] introduced a post-processing strategy with   $  \mathrm{Pb(NO_{3})_{2}}  $   solution to passivate the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   surface defects, resulting in reduced traps and decreased nonradiative recombination rate, thus leading to an improved PCE of 12.34%. Moreover, P3HT was shown to have a passivating effect on the surface defects, resulting in an improved PCE of 12.02% [36].

In this work, for the first time, potassium fluoride (KF) is used as a surface passivation agent for the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite with suppressed surface defects. The surface modification can be achieved by a simple solution process which will not destroy the original morphology or cause new phase formation in the perovskite films. We found that the KF salt was preferentially deposited at the GBs of the perovskite surface. Thus, the defects of the perovskite were significantly reduced and the recombination was significantly suppressed. As a result, PVSCs based on the KF-treated   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film achieved a PCE as high as 15.01% with a large open circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) of 1.26 V. In contrast, the control device based on the pristine   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film only showed an inferior PCE of 14.14% with a lower   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.18 V. Additionally, the devices with the KF-treatment showed improved stability and reduced hysteresis in comparison with the control devices. The solution passivation strategy demonstrated in this work is promising for PVSCs considering the low-cost of KF as well as the simple solution processes.

# Characterizations

Perovskite solar cells were measured in air (with relative humidity of  $ \sim 60\% $ ) after a simple encapsulation by epoxy kits (general purpose, Sigma Aldrich) in the glove-box. The PVSCs were illuminated through their indium tin oxide (ITO) sides, and the active areas were fixed at  $ 4\mathrm{mm}^2 $ . The current density versus voltage  $ (J - V) $  curves were recorded using a Keithley 2400 source measurement unit under AM  $ 1.5\mathrm{G} $  irradiation  $ (100\mathrm{mWcm}^{-2}) $  from an Oriel Sol3A simulator (Newport) with an NREL certified silicon reference cell. The  $ J - V $  curves were measured along the reverse scan direction from 1.3 to  $ -0.2\mathrm{V} $  or the forward scan direction from  $ -0.2 $  to  $ 1.3\mathrm{V} $ . The step voltage and scan rate were fixed at  $ 20\mathrm{mV} $  and  $ 150\mathrm{mVs}^{-1} $ . For device stability testing, all the PVSCs were stored and periodically tested in air under ambient conditions. The ambient conditions for the device storage in air were the darkness, temperature of  $ 25^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ , and relative humidity of  $ \sim 60\% $ . External quantum efficiency (EQE) measurements were conducted by using a Newport EQE measuring system. UV-vis absorption spectroscopy was performed using a Lambda35 spectrophotometer. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed on an ESCALAB 250Xi X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (Thermo Fisher) using an Al Kα source  $ (15\mathrm{kV}, 10\mathrm{mA}) $ . X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra were measured by a Rigaku MiniFlex 600 diffractometer equipped with a Cu Kα radiation. Top-view and cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were recorded on a Hitachi SU-8010 scanning electron microscope. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was conducted with a Bruker Nanoscale V station. The thickness of the film was measured by a Bruker Dektak XT surface profilometer. Photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved PL (TRPL) were measured with an Edinburgh Instruments FLS 980 spectrometer equipped with a  $ 450\mathrm{W} $  xenon lamp and a  $ 60\mathrm{W}\mu \mathrm{F} $  flash lamp as the excitation sources. The impedance spectroscopy was measured with a CHI 660D electrochemical workstation in the dark at an applied voltage of  $ 1.1\mathrm{V} $  with the frequency ranging between

1 MHz and 100 Hz.

# Fabrication of perovskite solar cells

ITO/glass substrate was cleaned in sequence with detergent, deionized water, acetone and isopropanol under sonication for 20 min each and then dried in an oven. The   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloid precursor diluted with   $  H_{2}O  $   (1:2) was spin-coated at 3000 rpm for 30 s onto the ITO substrates which were pretreated by   $  UV-O_{3}  $   for 15 min. Then the substrate was annealed at 150°C for 30 min. After cooling, the substrates were transferred into a glove box for the preparation of the perovskite layer. To form the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   precursor solution,   $  CsI  $ ,   $  PbBr_{2}  $   and   $  PbI_{2}  $   with an appropriate molar ratio were dissolved in DMSO with a concentration of 1.2 mol L  $  ^{-1}  $ . The precursor solution was then spin-coated onto the   $  SnO_{2}/ITO/glass  $   substrate. Next, the perovskite film was annealed at 42°C for 1 min and 160°C for 10 min. For the KF-treated film, KF/isopropanol solution (1.0 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $ ) was dropped on the perovskite film and then spin coated at 5000 rpm for 20 s in a   $  N_{2}  $ -filled glovebox. The resulting film was annealed at 80°C for 10 min to remove the residual solvent. Subsequently, a Spiro-OMeTAD solution was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 20 s as the hole transport layer. The Spiro-OMeTAD (72.3 mg) was mixed with 28.8 μL of 4-TBP, and 29 μL of FK 209 (300 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   in acetonitrile) in 1 mL of chlorobenzene to form the Spiro-OMeTAD solution. Finally,   $  MoO_{3}  $   (10 nm) and Ag (100 nm) were deposited by thermal evaporation.

# Fabrication and measurement of electron-only devices

Electron-only devices were fabricated with a device configuration of ITO glass/SnO $ _2 $ /perovskite/phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM)/Ag. The resulting devices were tested by current-voltage measurements in the dark. The trap densities of the films were calculated by the formula of  $ n_{\text{trap}} = 2\varepsilon \varepsilon_0 V_{\text{TFL}} / eL^2 $ , where  $ \varepsilon $  is the relative dielectric constant of perovskite,  $ \varepsilon_0 $  is the vacuum permittivity,  $ V_{\text{TFL}} $  is the onset voltage of the trap-filling limited (TFL) region,  $ e $  is the electronic charge, and  $ L $  is the thickness of the films.

The results show that the K and F signals are much stronger in the areas with more bright platelets than where the bright platelets are sparse. It can be speculated that the KF salt was preferentially deposited at the GBs and filled the valley between grains, thereby reducing the height difference between the grain surface and the grain boundary. This effect was further verified by the AFM results. As shown in Fig. 3b, d, the roughness of the perovskite layer was reduced from 22.3 to 13.4 nm after the KF-treatment. Water contact angles of two films were also measured (Fig. 3e, f). A larger contact angle of 67° was observed for the film treated with KF, in comparison with a contact angle of 47° for the control film without the KF treatment, which can be attributed to the hydrophobic nature of the fluorine atoms. It should be noted that the hydrophobic surface can protect the hydrophilic perovskite from the moisture which is harmful to the device stability.

To further assess the trap density of the PVSC films with and without KF-treatment, electron-only devices with a structure of glass/ITO/SnO $ _2 $ /CsPbI $ _2 $ Br/PCBM/Ag were fabricated. The trap densities were estimated by the space charge-limited-current (SCLC) method [39]. Typical dark current-voltage curves of electron-only devices with and without the KF-treatment are illustrated in Fig. 5a. It is clear that the current increases linearly at low bias voltages and then increases quickly when the bias voltage exceeds a certain value. This value at the kink point is known as the trap-filled limit voltage  $ V_{\text{TFL}} $ . It can be seen that the  $ V_{\text{TFL}} $  decreases from 0.42 V for the control device to 0.26 V for the KF-treated device. It means that the device with surface modification has a lower trap density ( $ 3.85 \times 10^{15} \text{ cm}^{-3} $ ) compared with the trap density of  $ 6.22 \times 10^{15} \text{ cm}^{-3} $  for the device without the KF modification. The decrease in trap density reconfirms the passivation effect of KF, which is in accord with the PL and TRPL results. Fig. 5b shows the Nyquist plots from the impedance spectroscopy measured in the dark with an applied voltage of 1.1 V. Generally, the low frequency region in the Nyquist plot is related to the recombination resistance ( $ R_{\text{rec}} $ ) [40–42]. The series resistance ( $ R_s $ ), transport resistances ( $ R_{\text{tr}} $ ) and  $ R_{\text{rec}} $  were extracted by fitting the Nyquist plots with simplified equivalent circuit model (Fig. S5). As can be observed in Fig. 5b, the low-frequency region clearly suggested that recombination resistance was improved notably for the KF-treated device, indicating that the charge recombination was effectively suppressed by the passivation effect of KF. The result is consistent with the change in the performance of

corresponding devices.

# CONCLUSIONS

In summary, we have demonstrated the use of KF as a surface modifier for all-inorganic  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  perovskite to enhance the performance and stability of the corresponding PVSCs. We have shown that the deposited KF gathered at the GBs of the perovskite surface can reduce the nonradiative recombination and trap density of the perovskite film. As a result, an improved PCE of  $ 15.01\% $  with a high  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  of  $ 1.26\mathrm{V} $  was achieved for the PVSCs based on the  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  with the simple solution post-treatment processing. In contrast, the control device without the treatment only showed a PCE of  $ 14.14\% $  with a  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  of  $ 1.18\mathrm{V} $ . Furthermore, improved storage stability and reduced hysteresis index were also achieved for the best-performance PVSC based on  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  with the KF treatment compared with the control device. This simple low-cost passivation strategy would be quite useful for high-performance and stable  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $ -based PVSCs, and it could be applied for other all-inorganic PVSCs as well.
