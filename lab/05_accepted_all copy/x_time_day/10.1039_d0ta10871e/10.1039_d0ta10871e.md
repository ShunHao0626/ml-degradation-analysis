# Quantifying the energy loss for a perovskite solar cell passivated with acetamidine halide†

Ligang Yuan, $ ^{a} $  Huiming Luo, $ ^{a} $  Jiarong Wang, $ ^{a} $  Zonghao Xu, $ ^{b} $  Jiong Li, $ ^{a} $  Qingsong Jiang $ ^{c} $  and Keyou Yan $ ^{*a} $

Defects at the grain boundary, surface and interface, acting as nonradiative recombination centers, result in considerable energy loss of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Herein, we passivated the defects of polycrystalline perovskite films with acetamidine halide (AAI or AABr) and identified their energy loss before and after passivation. We find that the trap state densities are  $ 9.20 \times 10^{15} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3} $  and  $ 9.62 \times 10^{15} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3} $  for AAI-treated and AABr-treated perovskite films, lower than  $ 1.09 \times 10^{16} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3} $  for the pristine film. As a result, PSCs with configuration of ITO/SnO $ _2 $ /perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD/Ag achieve a champion PCE of  $ 22.0\% $  with AAI treatment and  $ 21.5\% $  with AABr treatment, higher than  $ 20.4\% $  PCE for the pristine PSCs. In LED mode, the maximum electroluminescence external quantum efficiencies ( $ \mathrm{EQE}_{\mathrm{EL}} $ ) are  $ 4.06\% $  and  $ 3.45\% $  for AAI and AABr passivated PSCs, respectively, higher than  $ 0.94\% $  for the pristine device.  $ \mathrm{EQE}_{\mathrm{EL}} $  values are  $ 1.26\% $ ,  $ 1.20\% $  and  $ 0.40\% $  under an injection current of photocurrent for AAI, AABr and pristine PSCs suggesting that the nonradiative  $ V_{OC} $  loss is  $ 112 \mathrm{mV} $ ,  $ 114 \mathrm{mV} $  and  $ 142 \mathrm{mV} $ , respectively. In terms of charge transfer loss, fill factor reduction is estimated to be  $ 6.3\% $ ,  $ 7.0\% $  and  $ 7.4\% $  for AAI, AABr and pristine films, respectively. Passivation using acetamidine halide can significantly improve the energy loss and improve stability.

# Introduction

Organic-inorganic halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) exhibit a high power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 25.5% in a small area,  $  ^{1}  $  which exceeds that of thin film solar cells, such as CdTe and CIGS. The high performances are due to excellent properties of perovskite films such as ambipolar carrier transfer capabilities,  $  ^{2}  $  long charge-carrier diffusion lengths,  $  ^{2,3}  $  and high absorption coefficients (  $  10^{5}  $  cm  $  ^{-1}  $  ).  $  ^{4,5}  $  Therefore, PSCs have attracted significant attention in the past decade, and plenty of studies mainly focus on the enhancement of efficiency and stability by optimizing the device architecture, film deposition process, and material composition.  $  ^{6-10}  $  Since the perovskite layer is related to grain nucleation and growth through solution processes, there are lots of defects at the grain boundary and surface, which can potentially limit the performance.  $  ^{11}  $  Thus, various passivators such as polymers, small molecules, and

ionic liquids  $  ^{12-15}  $  have been developed to enhance efficiencies through defect passivation in the perovskite layer and the interface between the perovskite layer and electron/hole transport layer (ETL/HTL), leading to reduced photovoltage loss (  $  \Delta V_{OC}  $  ) within PSCs.  $  ^{16,17}  $  Therefore, minimizing the perovskite film and interfacial nonradiative losses is a crucial approach to enhance the performance of PSCs especially by inserting a layer of insulating layer (such as polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA))  $  ^{12}  $  or a semi-conductor polymer  $  ^{13,18-20}  $  at the perovskite/HTL interface to ameliorate nucleation and crystal growth. However, polymers are soluble in chlorobenzene (CB), and the subsequent coating of the HTL/ETL will remove the interfacial passivation layer, leading to incomplete passivation of defects in the perovskite film.

It has been demonstrated that slightly residual   $  PbI_{2}  $   on the surface can effectively passivate the perovskite film and enhance device performance,  $  ^{21}  $   which is mainly due to facile charge extraction.  $  ^{22}  $   However, the nonuniform distribution of   $  PbI_{2}  $   negates its beneficial effect on charge extraction but creates recombination sites. Thus, these sites need to be passivated. Several groups have reported that two-dimensional (2D) perovskites using alkyl ammonium salts can effectively improve the efficiency and stability of devices through the formation of a grade structure with three-dimensional (3D) perovskites.  $  ^{14,23}  $   Chen and co-workers  $  ^{24}  $   introduced phenethylammonium iodide (PEAI) for in situ growth of a 2D   $  PEA_{2}PbI_{4}  $   passivation layer on the surface of a perovskite film, leading to simultaneous

# Results and discussion

Acetamidinium (AA $  ^{+}  $ ) has stronger chemical bonding with the perovskite to stabilize its crystal structure.  $  ^{28,29}  $  As shown in the molecular structure in Fig. 1a, AA $  ^{+}  $  has a restricted C–N bond rotation due to the delocalized π-electron cloud over the N–C–N bond, which strengthens the resultant N–H⋯I bond at both terminals. Therefore, we introduce acetamidinium salts (AAI and AABr) as a surface passivation layer to passivate the surface excessive PbI $  _{2}  $  of the two-step fabricated perovskite film in planar conversional devices with a configuration of glass/ITO/SnO $  _{2}  $ /perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD/Ag. The corresponding detailed fabrication process is shown in Fig. 1b and the detailed description of the fabrication process is shown in the Experimental section in the ESI.†

First, the surface morphologies of pristine and AAI/AABr treated perovskite films were characterized with top-view scanning electron microscopy (SEM) measurements (Fig. 2a, c and S1†). The pristine perovskite film shows clearly excessive bright PbI₂ domains that are randomly distributed on the thin film. Residual PbI₂ with a uniform distribution was widely reported to be beneficial to improve performance, yet too much of PbI₂ and a nonuniform distribution were harmful to device performance.  $  ^{[21,22]}  $   AAI or AABr treatment can help overcome these imperfections on the surface, as shown in Fig. 2b and c. XRD measurements were performed to study the phase purity. As shown in Fig. 2e, there is a sharp peak with strong intensity around  $ 12.8^{\circ} $  identified as  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and slightly lower than the main characteristic diffraction peak of perovskite (110) at  $ 14.1^{\circ} $  in the pristine film, indicating excessive  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  on the perovskite surface. It is noted that the intensity of the diffraction peak of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  is appreciably decreased after AAI and AABr

treatment suggesting a lower   $  PbI_{2}  $   content in the perovskite layer, which is beneficial for improving the performance due to the probable interaction between   $  AA^{+}  $  and   $  PbI_{2}  $ . This result further demonstrated that AAI and AABr effectively passivated the perovskite film and enhanced phase purity. Interestingly, we carefully compared the perovskite diffraction peak of (110) and (220). As shown in Fig. S4 in the ESI,  $  ^{\dagger}  $   the diffraction peaks were slightly shifted to small angles. The peak shift could be ascribed to the ion exchange between FAMA-based perovskite and   $  AA^{+}  $  cations.  $  ^{[28,29]}  $   Thus, AAI/AABr not only can passivate residual   $  PbI_{2}  $   in the perovskite surface, but also can passivate the FAMA-based perovskite film by ion exchange. To directly study the interaction between the perovskite film and AAI/AABr passivators, we adopted X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to further analyse the chemical environment of the pristine and AAI/AABr treated perovskite films, in which the XPS peak shift is associated with electron bond polarization between the nearest-neighbour atoms. Fig. 2f and S5b  $  ^{\dagger}  $   show the high-resolution XPS spectra of I 3d and Pb 4f for the pristine film and AAI/AABr treated perovskite film, all calibrated with C 1s. The I 3d  $  _{3/2}  $   and 3d  $  _{5/2}  $   peaks located at 631.0 and 619.5 eV shift to lower binding energies of 630.9 and 619.4 eV and 4f  $  _{7/2}  $   peaks located at 143.3 and 138.4 eV shift to lower binding energies of 143.2 and 138.3 eV for the AAI/AABr treated perovskite film. All the above results strongly indicate that there is a strong interaction between   $  PbI_{2}  $   and   $  AA^{+}  $ . It is proposed that the excessive   $  PbI_{2}  $   on the surface of the pristine perovskite film can be effectively passivated by treating with AAI/AABr.

Then, we fabricated planar PSCs with a configuration of ITO/  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  /perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD/Ag and estimated the performance of the device with AAI and AABr treated perovskite films.

# Conclusions

We passivated polycrystalline perovskite films with AAI and AABr and identified the energy loss for the perovskite thin films. The perovskite film treated with AAI/AABr with a lower trap state density suppressed nonradiative recombination, which was confirmed by PL and Mott-Shockey measurements. In LED mode, the maximum   $  EQE_{EL}  $   values are 4.06% and 3.45% for AAI and AABr passivated PSCs, respectively, higher than 0.94% for the pristine device.   $  EQE_{EL}  $   values of 1.26%, 1.20% and 0.40% under an injection current of photocurrent for AAI, AABr and pristine films suggest the nonradiative   $  V_{OC}  $   loss of 112 mV, 114 mV and 142 mV, respectively. The FF losses are 6.3%, 7.0% and 7.4% for AAI, AABr and pristine devices, respectively. These results verified that AAI and AABr not only suppress non-radiative recombination by passivating the perovskite surface, but also reduced the charge transfer loss in the device. As a result, the PSCs achieve a champion PCE of 22.0% with AAI treatment and 21.5% with AABr treatment, which are higher than 20.4% for the pristine PSCs.
