# Guanidinium Induced Phase Separated Perovskite Layer for Efficient and Highly Stable Solar Cells†

Weihai Zhang,  $  ^{a}  $   Juan Xiong,  $  ^{b}  $   Jinhua Li  $  ^{c}  $   and Walid A. Daoud  $  ^{*a}  $

$ ^{a} $ School of Energy and Environment, City University of Hong Kong, Tat Chee Avenue, Hong Kong

$  ^{b}  $  Faculty of Physics & Electronic Sciences, Hubei University, Wuhan, 430062, China

$ ^{c} $ School of Materials Science and Engineering, Hubei University, Wuhan 430062, China

*Corresponding Author: E-mail: wdaoud@cityu.edu.hk

$  ^{\dagger}  $  Electronic supplementary information (ESI) available.

Guanidinium (GA) has been proposed as an effective organic cation in improving the stability of lead halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs). However, the reported efficiency of GA based PSCs is far behind traditional GA-free devices. Herein, a novel GA doped quadruple cation perovskite,   $  Cs_{0.05}(FA_{0.83}(MA_{1-x}GA_{x})_{0.17})_{0.95}Pb(I_{0.83}Br_{0.17})_{3}  $   (  $  CsFAMA_{1-x}GA_{x}  $  ), is presented. It is found that the introduction of GA induces a phase separation of 3D   $  CsFAMA_{1-x}GA_{x}  $  , 2D FAGAPbI  $  _{4}  $  , and 1D δ-FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  . By tuning the content of GA, a δ-FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  /CsFAMA  $  _{1-x}  $  GA  $  _{x}  $   (1D/3D) perovskite with superior optoelectronic properties is demonstrated. The novel 1D/3D perovskite shows an overall improvement in stability. The corresponding solar cell exhibits a PCE of 20.29% with negligible hysteresis, being the highest reported efficiency for GA based PSCs thus far. This study introduces a new perovskite model toward efficient and highly stable PSCs.

# Introduction

Organic-inorganic halide perovskites (OIHP) have attracted tremendous attention from both academic and industrial communities since the first development of the perovskite solar cells (PSCs) reported in 2012.  $  ^{1}  $   In the succeeding years, based on peculiar optoelectronic properties, such as strong light absorption,  $  ^{2}  $   tunable band gap,  $  ^{3}  $   small exciton binding energy,  $  ^{4}  $   and excellent charge carrier mobility and diffusion length,  $  ^{5}  $   PSCs have witnessed a steady development.  $  ^{6-8}  $   To date, a certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 23.7%, which is comparable to commercial crystalline silicon solar cells, has been achieved.  $  ^{9}  $   However, the poor stability of OIHP materials remains the key obstacle toward practical utilization.  $  ^{10-13}  $   To overcome this, all-inorganic lead halide perovskites (CsPbX  $  _{3}  $  , X=halide) have been proposed as an alternative to fabricate stable devices.  $  ^{14-16}  $   In addition, doping and encapsulation of hydrophobic groups have been investigated for preventing moisture ingress.  $  ^{17-19}  $   Unfortunately, there remain some challenges, such as, low efficiency and high temperature fabrication requirement for all-inorganic PSCs,  $  ^{20,21}  $   and light induced degradation, especially by UV irradiation, for traditional methylammonium based perovskite.  $  ^{22}  $   Therefore, further investigation on designing stable PSCs with high photovoltaic performance is urgently needed.

Recently, a growing number of studies has focused on two-dimensional (2D) Ruddlesden-popper phase perovskites by replacing the small hydrophilic cations, such as, methylammonium (MA), formamidinium (FA), and cesium (Cs) with larger organic cations.  $  ^{23,24}  $   Comparing with their three-dimensional (3D) counterparts, such as MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  , 2D perovskites tend to have better stability due to the stronger van der Waals interaction and hydrophobicity of large organic cations.  $  ^{25,26}  $   To date, cations, such as phenylethylammonium (PEA), butylammonium (BA), aminovaleric acid (AVA) and guanidinium (GA), have been reported to yield more stable PSCs.  $  ^{27-30}  $   In particular, GA-cation based perovskite has been proposed as an attractive absorber for devices with high efficiency as well as superior stability.  $  ^{31-34}  $   Analysis of the Goldschmidt tolerance factor (t) reveals that pure

$ \mathrm{GAPbI}_3 $  owns a  $ t $  value of 1.03, indicating it is slightly too large to be incorporated into the cavity formed by the corner-connected  $ \mathrm{PbI}_6 $  octahedra to form a 3D perovskite structure.[26,34] While, by using solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) method, Kubicki et al. indicated that GA can be incorporated into the lattice of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ , forming  $ \mathrm{GA_xMA_{1 - x}PbI_3} $  perovskite phase. This kind of perovskite shows potential to increase the charge carrier lifetime and open-circuit voltage.[32] Similarly, Jodlowski et al. reported that moderate concentration of GA can be incorporated into the crystal lattice of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  forming GA-cation based perovskite with enhanced thermal and environmental stability, delivering a stable power output above  $ 19\% $ .[33] Further, Nazarenko et al. synthesized a novel 2D FAGAPbI $ _4 $  perovskite by using glycerol bath method. They revealed that this kind of perovskite is thermally stable, photoconductive, and exhibits photoluminescence in red region.[35] These studies have provided some insights into GA-cation based perovskites and revealed that GA is an attractive candidate to fabricate novel PSCs with superior performance. However, the efficiency of GA-cation based PSCs remains much lower than traditional PSCs. Besides, the investigations on GA-cation based PSCs thus far have all concentrated on  $ \mathrm{GA_xMA_{1 - x}PbI_3} $  or  $ \mathrm{GA_xFA_{1 - x}PbI_3} $ . As a consequence, GA-cation based perovskites with other components, possessing attractive optoelectronic properties, are yet to be investigated.

Herein, we demonstrate a novel quadruple cation based perovskite, with a chemical formulation of   $  Cs_{0.05}(FA_{0.83}(MA_{1-x}GA_{x})_{0.17})_{0.95}Pb(I_{0.83}Br_{0.17})_{3}  $   (abbreviated as   $  CsFAMA_{1-x}GA_{x}  $  ), towards efficient and highly stable PSCs. The perovskites with different x value ranging from 0 to 1, were explored by gradually replacing MA with GA, and the corresponding phase, chemical composition, optical property, morphology, optoelectronic property and stability were systematically investigated. Our findings reveal that moderate content of GA can be incorporated into the crystal unit, forming a 3D perovskite with enhanced carrier life time. While, excess GA tends to induce the formation of 2D phase   $  FAGAPbI_{4}  $   and 1D yellow phase   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   (denoted as   $  \delta-FAPbI_{3}  $  ), which are proposed to have both advantageous and disadvantageous effects on device performance. On one hand, a few of these new phases can passivate the defects and deliver an improved device performance. While on the other, excess of these phases in the perovskite layer reduce the absorbance of the films, leading to deterioration in device performance. By finely tuning the content of GA, a champion device with an efficiency of 20.29% and an open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ) of 1.12 V was obtained, much larger than the GA-free reference device which has a PCE of 18.45% and a   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.08 V. Moreover, the stability tests reveal that GA-cation based PSCs provided an overall improvement in stability. The results revealed a phase separated quadruple cation based   $  CsFAMA_{1-x}GA_{x}  $   perovskite layer, which is important for efficient and stable PSCs.

# Results and discussion

As depicted in Fig. 1, GA is obtained by substituting the C-H hydrogen in FA with  $ -\mathrm{NH}_2 $  group. On its own, GA comprises one carbon and three  $ -\mathrm{NH}_2 $  groups, with an ionic radius of about  $ 278~\mathrm{pm} $ , larger than FA (253 pm) and MA (217 pm). Previous studies have demonstrated that GA can be directly incorporated into the lattice of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  or  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $ , forming pure 3D  $ \mathrm{GA}_x\mathrm{MA}_{1 - x}\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  or  $ \mathrm{GA}_x\mathrm{FA}_{1 - x}\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  phases.[32,33] In an attempt to extend the same strategy to construct more ideal GA doped perovskite, triple cation based perovskite  $ (\mathrm{Cs}_{0.05}(\mathrm{FA}_{0.83}\mathrm{MA}_{0.17})_{0.95}\mathrm{Pb(I}_{0.83}\mathrm{Br}_{0.17})_3 $ , CsFAMA) was adopted as the model system.[36,37] To obtain GA doped perovskites, part of MABr was replaced by GABr, and their ratio was carefully controlled as described in the Experimental Section. $ ^{\dagger} $  The analysis of the Goldschmidt tolerance factor ( $ t $ ) indicates that it is possible to form a series of 3D GA doped quadruple cation based perovskites (Table S1†).

The equation shown in Fig. 2 illustrates the perovskite formation. In this study, the perovskite films differ in the x value, for example, x=0 denoted as w/o GA, and x=1 as 1 GA. To gain insight into the structure of the perovskites, X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were conducted. Fig. 2a shows the XRD patterns of   $  CsFAMA_{1-x}GA_{x}  $   family of perovskites with different x value. It is noted that the diffraction intensity of the peak at around   $  14.1^{\circ}  $  , corresponding to (110) plane of the perovskite phase, gradually decreased with the increase of x value. At the same time, new diffraction peaks, which are attributed to 1D   $  \delta-FAPbI_{3}  $   (  $  11.5^{\circ}  $  ), 2D   $  FAGAPbI_{4}  $   (  $  12.1^{\circ}  $  ) and cubic   $  PbI_{2}  $   phase(  $  12.8^{\circ}  $  ), were observed.  $  ^{38,35,39}  $   This indicates that the introduction of GA suppressed the formation of tetragonal perovskite and introduce new phases of   $  \delta-FAPbI_{3}  $ , FAGAPbI  $  _{4}  $   and PbI  $  _{2}  $ . The formation of different phases is mainly due to the different precipitation kinetics of MA-based, FA-based and GA-based hybrids for their different ion radius and number of hydrogen bonds in the Pb-I framework.  $  ^{40}  $   While, a closer inspection of the (110) and (220) perovskite lattice plane shown in Fig. 2b reveals a peak shift to lower angles. According to Bragg's law:   $  2dsin\theta=n\lambda  $ , where a smaller   $  \theta  $   contributes to a larger interplanar distance (d), the lower angle shifted peaks reveal a gradual expansion of the crystal unit, indicating incorporation of the larger GA cation. Thus, a comprehensive comparison of XRD patterns shown in Fig. 2a and b can lead to the following three points. First, moderate content of GA (x<0.2) can be wholly incorporated into the crystal, forming pure 3D   $  CsFAMA_{1-x}GA_{x}  $   perovskite. Second, excess GA would introduce phase separation into   $  \delta-FAPbI_{3}/CsFAMA_{1-x}GA_{x}  $   (1D/3D) when x is 0.2-0.4, and   $  \delta-FAPbI_{3}/FAGAPbI_{4}/CsFAMA_{1-x}GA_{x}  $   (1D/2D/3D) when x is >0.4. Third, over excess GA (x>0.6) cannot incorporate into the crystal unit (Fig. 2b, unchanged XRD peaks), but contributes to the formation of 1D phase   $  \delta-FAPbI_{3}  $   and 2D phase FAGAPbI  $  _{4}  $ , leading to a significant decrease of the (110) diffraction of 3D   $  CsFAMA_{1-x}GA_{x}  $   perovskite.

Chemical compositions and environments of the films were studied by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). As shown in Fig. S1†, films with different x value (0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1) reveal clear peaks of N, Pb, I and Br. The absence of Cs peak is mainly attributed to the very low content of Cs cation. However, detection of Cs 3d core spectra shown in Fig. S1b† verified the existence of Cs. No observation of other peaks indicates pure composition of the films. Fig. 2c-f shows the corresponding N 1s, Pb 4f, I 3d and Br 3d core spectra, respectively. Noticeably, the photoemission spectra of N 1s (Fig. 2c) tend to be fitted into two peaks attributed to the NH₃⁺ group in MA (402.2 eV) and NH₂⁺ group in FA and GA (400.4 eV), where the corresponding peak intensity changes with the value of x. The atomic ratios of N between NH₃⁺ and NH₂⁺ groups in different films were estimated based on the XPS spectra, relevant results are summarized in Table S2.† The similar atomic ratio values derived from XPS spectra and perovskite precursor indicate a stoichiometric composition between MA, FA and GA. The core spectra of Pb 4f is presented in Fig. 2d, in which two main peaks at 138.5 eV and 143.4 eV were observed, consistent with the binding energies of lead halides (PbI₂, PbBr₂).⁴¹ Interestingly, two extra peaks at 136.7 eV and 141.6 eV were observed in the 0.8 GA and 1 GA films, as shown in the inset. The new peaks are assigned to metallic lead (Pb⁰), which is generally regarded as recombination centers of carriers in the film, leading to deterioration in device performance.⁴² Further, the I 3d and Br 3d XPS core spectra shown in Fig. 2e and f reveal that GA content has insignificant influence on the chemical environments of iodine (I) and bromine (Br).

To further investigate the effect of GA on morphology, field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM  $  ^{16}  $  ) was carried out. Fig. 4a-f shows SEM images of perovskite films (x=0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1). It is clear that all films have grown with full coverage of substrates. Compared with the reference w/o GA film (Fig. 4a), Fig. 4b of 0.2 GA film reveals a larger average grain size (Fig. 4g) due to expansion of the crystal unit for incorporating GA as discussed earlier. On the other hand, further increase of x value led to a relative poor morphology with unclear grain boundary and pinholes, as shown in Fig. 4c and d. More interestingly, some plate-like or nanorod-like crystals distributed around the grain boundaries were clearly observed in Fig. 4e and f for 0.8 GA and 1 GA films, respectively. As discussed earlier, excess GA (x>0.6) cannot incorporate into the crystal unit, but contributes to the formation of FAGAPbI  $  _{4}  $   and δ-FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  . In this case, the emerging plate-like or nanorod-like crystals are assigned to 2D FAGAPbI  $  _{4}  $   and 1D δ-FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  , respectively. The results indicate that moderate content of GA is conductive for uniform and pinhole free perovskite film with larger grains, whereas excess GA would lead to a poor morphology with apparent pinholes and new phases. To further study the distribution of 2D FAGAPbI  $  _{4}  $   and 1D δ-FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   in the CsFAMA  $  _{1-x}  $  GA  $  _{x}  $   perovskite film, grazing incident XRD (GIXRD) was performed with varying incident angles from 0.5° to 7°. It is known that at low incident angles, wave penetration is limited, thus, most of the diffraction signal is collected from the topmost layer. Fig. 4h,i shows the GIXRD results for 0.2 GA and 0.6 GA films, where the peak intensities were normalized to the (110) diffraction of CsFAMA  $  _{1-x}  $  GA  $  _{x}  $   perovskite. Noticeably, the peak intensity of 1D δ-FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   in both films decreased gradually with the increase of incident angle from 0.5° to 7°. Indicating that δ-FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   is present with slightly higher distribution on the surface. While, the almost unchanged diffraction peak for 2D FAGAPbI  $  _{4}  $   shown in Fig. 4h reveals a uniform distribution in the film.

In order to gain further insight into the effect of GA on device performance, time-resolved PL (TRPL), light intensity-dependent J-V, dark J-V characteristics, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were conducted. The TRPL curves shown in Fig. 6a can be fitted with a biexponential equation  $  ^{43}  $  :   $  Y = Y_{0} + A_{1} \exp(-t / \tau_{1}) + A_{2} \exp(-t / \tau_{2})  $  , in which a faster decay component (  $  \tau_{1}  $  ) refers to defect-induced nonradiative recombination while a slower decay component (  $  \tau_{2}  $  ) relates to radiative recombination.  $  ^{16,44}  $   The fitted parameters are summarized in Table S4.  $  ^{\dagger}  $   A longer average carrier lifetime (  $  \tau_{ave}  $  ) for 0.1 GA film (14.2 ns) than that w/o GA film (11.1 ns) was observed. This confirms the introduction of GA enhances carrier lifetime, which is in agreement with previous study.  $  ^{45}  $   Strikingly, 0.2 GA film shows a remarkable carrier lifetime of 22.7 ns, which is twice as much as that of w/o GA film. The unexpected prolongation of carrier lifetime can be explained by the suppressed nonradiative recombination due to the passivation effect of the formed 1D δ-FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  .  $  ^{38}  $   While, further increase of x value leads to a decrease of the average carrier lifetime due to the presence of metallic lead (Pb  $  ^{0}  $  ) (Fig. 2d) and large quantity of grain boundaries (Fig. 4d-f).  $  ^{46,47}  $   Fig. 6b shows J  $  _{sc}  $   versus light intensity based on w/o GA, 0.2 GA and 0.6 GA perovskite films. According to the power law equation  $  ^{48}  $  :   $  J_{sc} \propto I^{\alpha}  $  , where l is light intensity,   $  \alpha  $   is a factor related to bimolecular recombination, all devices revealed a linear correlation with   $  \alpha  $   value close to unity, indicating negligible bimolecular recombination.  $  ^{49}  $   While, the most ideal   $  \alpha  $   value (0.913) for 0.2 GA film suggests a reduced trap density.  $  ^{50}  $   Besides, the V  $  _{oc}  $   versus light intensity results shown in Fig. 6c reveal that the device based on 0.2 GA film exhibits the smallest slope at 1.23 KT/q. Previous studies concluded that a deviation between slope and KT/q indicates trap-assisted recombination, which is one of the main reasons for the loss of V  $  _{oc}  $  .  $  ^{47,51}  $   Therefore, the smallest slope suggests that the trap-assisted recombination process is effectively suppressed in 0.2 GA film. Further, the trap density of the perovskite films was studied using the space-charge-limited current (SCLC) technique. Fig. 6d shows the dark J-V curves of the electron-only devices with the structure of FTO/SnO  $  _{2}  $  /perovskite/PCBM/Ag. The trap density (N  $  _{t}  $  ) is determined by the trap-filled limit voltage (V  $  _{TFL}  $  ) based on the equation of   $  N_{t} = 2V_{TFL}\varepsilon_{r}\varepsilon_{0}/qL^{2}  $  , where   $  \varepsilon_{r}  $   is the relative dielectric constant for perovskite,   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   is the vacuum permittivity, q is the elemental charge and L is the thickness of the perovskite film.  $  ^{52,53}  $   It is found that while 0.2 GA film has a smaller V  $  _{TFL}  $   (0.129 V) compared with w/o GA film (0.177 V), 0.6 GA film has the largest V  $  _{TFL}  $   of 0.292 V, indicating a significantly reduced trap density in 0.2 GA film, which can be attributed to its morphology and the δ-FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   passivation effect, as discussed earlier.  $  ^{54}  $   Additionally, the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy results (Fig S5†) indicate that 0.2 GA film has the largest recombination resistance. These results suggest that the introduction of moderate GA can not only enhance carrier life time, but also reduce trap density, and suppress charge recombination, leading to enhanced device performance.

# Conclusions

In summary, a quadruple cation based  $ \mathrm{CsFAMA}_{1 - x}\mathrm{GA}_x $  perovskite was developed. The crystalline phase, chemical composition, morphology, optoelectronic property, and stability were systematically investigated. By finely tuning the content of GA, a  $ \delta\text{-FAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{CsFAMA}_{1 - x}\mathrm{GA}_x $  (1D/3D) perovskite with longer carrier life time, less trap density, and reduced charge recombination was obtained. Moreover, this novel 1D/3D perovskite demonstrated improvement of long-term, thermal and light-soaking stability. The corresponding device exhibited a PCE of  $ 20.29\% $ , which is the highest efficiency reported for GA doped perovskite.

# Experimental Section

# Materials

The  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  colloid was purchased from Alfa Aesar (tin (IV) oxide). Methylammonium iodide (MAI), Methylammonium bromide (MABr), and Cesium iodide (Csl) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. Formamidinium iodide (FAI) was purchased from Dyesol Ltd. (Queanbeyan, Australia). Lead iodide  $ (\mathrm{PbI}_2, 99\%) $ , Lead bromide  $ (\mathrm{PbBr}_2, 99.999\%) $ , Guanidinium bromide (GABr,  $ 99\% $ ), N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF,  $ 99.8\% $ ), Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO,  $ 99.9\% $ ), and Chlorobenzene  $ (99.8\%) $  were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

# Device Fabrication

Devices were fabricated on FTO substrates (15   $  \Omega  $   sq  $  ^{-1}  $  ) with dimensions of 20×20 mm. The substrates were thoroughly cleaned with a detergent solution, acetone, and isopropanol, and finally treated in plasma cleaner for 5 min. Then, a layer of SnO  $  _{2}  $   (purchased from Alfa Aesar) was deposited in ambient air as the electron transport layer at a speed of 4000 rpm for 30 s and then annealed at 140 °C for 20 min. After cooling to room temperature, the substrates were transferred to a nitrogen filled glove box.

To prepare the perovskite precursor solution, FAI (1 M),  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  (1.05 M), MABr (0.2(1-x) M), GABr (0.2x M) and  $ \mathrm{PbBr}_2 $  (0.2 M) were first dissolved in anhydrous DMF:DMSO 4:1 (v:v) for FA/MA/GA solution, where  $ x $  ranges from 0 to 1. Then appropriate amount of CsI (1.5 M in DMSO) was added into FA/MA/GA solution for the target quadruple cation based CsFAMA $ _{1-x} $ GA $ _x $  perovskite precursor. Before usage, all perovskite precursors were stirred at room temperature for 3 hours in glove box and filtered with a  $ 0.45\mu \mathrm{m} $  Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) filter.

Then, 60   $  \mu  $  L of the prepared perovskite precursor was dropped on the substrate and spin-coated at 600 rpm for 5 s and then at 4000 rpm for 40 s. During the second procedure, 150   $  \mu  $  L toluene was dropped on the spinning substrate 15 s before program ended. The resulting films were transferred to a hot plate at 120   $  {}^{\circ}  $  C and dried for 15 min. Subsequently, a traditional Spiro-OMeTAD layer, which consisted of 72.3 mg spiro-OMeTAD, 28.8   $  \mu  $  L 4-tertbutylpyridine, 17.5   $  \mu  $  L lithium-bis (trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (Li-TFSI) solution (520 mg Li-TFSI in 1 mL acetonitrile), and 1 mL chlorobenzene, was deposited on the perovskite films as the hole transport layer. Finally, an approximate 80 nm thick of Au electrode was fabricated using a shadow mask under high vacuum by thermal evaporation.

# J-V Characterization

Current density-voltage (J-V) curves of the devices were measured using a solar simulator (Newport Oriel Sol 3A Class AAA, 64023A) equipped with 450 W Xenon lamp (Oriel, USA) and a Keithley 2400 source meter. The light intensity was calibrated to AM 1.5G (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) by using a reference Si solar cell (Newport). The devices were masked with a metal aperture of 0.06 cm  $  ^{-2}  $   to define the active area. The J-V measurements were all conducted in ambient air with a scanning protocol of 1.2 to -0.05 V for the reverse scan (-0.05 to 1.2 V for the forward scan)

# Stability Measurements

For long-term stability, w/o GA and 0.2 GA films or corresponding unencapsulated devices were stored in a dry cabinet with  $ 25\% $  humidity at  $ 25^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  in the dark. The UV-vis absorption and XRD of the films were measured by every 7 days. The J-V curves of the devices were recorded by every day. Thermal stability was studied by exposing the devices to ambient environment of  $ 80^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  and  $ 40\% $  humidity and measuring the J-V curves by every day. For light-soaking stability test, unencapsulated devices were exposed to continuous 1 sun illumination generated by LED lamp without UV light. The J-V curves of the devices were recorded by every 5 or 10 min. Between each measurement, the cells were maintained at the maximum power point.

# Other characterizations

The XRD patterns (2θ scans) were obtained on Bruker Advanced D8 X-ray diffractometer using Cu Kα (  $  \lambda = 0.154  $  nm) radiation. The compositions and chemical environments of the films were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (PHI Model 5802). UV-vis diffuse reflection spectroscopy (UV-vis-DRS, Shimadzu UV-3600) was used to collect the absorbance spectra of the perovskite films. Steady state photoluminescence (PL) spectra were recorded on Shimadzu RF-5301pc with an excitation wavelength of 523 nm. A field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, JEOL 7100F) was used to study the surface and cross-section morphology. Grazing incident XRD (GIXRD) patterns were obtained by using Rigaku Smartlab X-ray diffractometer with an incidence angle ranges from 0.5° to 7°. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra were measured under a constant white light supplied by an array of white light-emitting diodes. The excitation beam from a 300 W xenon lamp was focused through a double-monochromator and chopped at 10 Hz. The monochromatic light intensity for the measurement was calibrated using a reference silicon photodiode. Time-resolved photoluminescence spectra were measured by a PL system (DW-PLE03). TGA analysis was conducted using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA/DSC 1 STARe System). The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy measurements were carried out by using electrochemical workstation (Zennium Zahner, Germany). A bias of the open-circuit voltage was applied during the measurement at frequencies ranging from 1 MHz to 1 Hz.
