# Abstract:

Although an efficient charge transport is essential to high-performance perovskite solar cells (PSCs), the serious charge trapping in perovskite films is still a barrier to improve the efficiency of PSCs. To overcome this issue, we efficiently suppress the charge trapping by using polar compound materials to reduce defects and improve the match of work functions in PSCs. 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate  $ \left[\mathrm{BMIM}\right]\mathrm{BF}_4 $  is used to form an interfacial dipole layer and triphenylphosphine oxide (TPPO) is employed to passivate defects. The interfacial dipole layer not only reduces the surface work function of electron transport layers (ETLs), but also substitutes organic/Cs cation vacancies. Oxygen atoms in TPPO molecules fill anion vacancies on perovskite crystal surfaces. As a result, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of the champion PSCs has been improved to  $ 21.1\% $  from  $ 18.7\% $ . The target PSCs retained  $ 98.3\% $  of its initial PCE after 214 days in dry air condition (relative humidity about  $ 22\% $

# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted much attention since the first report about PSCs with a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 3.8\% $ .[1-4] PSCs show great potential as a renewable energy resource because of their low fabrication cost and excellent photovoltaic performance.

The light harvester layers of PSCs consist of perovskite materials whose bandgap is generally at the range of 1.5-1.6 eV.[5-8] The Shockley-Queisser limit for the theoretical, maximum PCE (SQL-PCE) corresponding to the bandgap range of 1.5-1.6 eV is more than  $ 30\% $ .[8] Although the highest certified PCE is  $ 25.2\% $  up to now, it is still much lower than the SQL-PCE[2]. It is vital to understand the inner mechanism for the PCE loss of PSCs and reduce the distance between the highest reported PCE and SQL-PCE. A lot of works have been done to investigate the reason for the PCE loss.[9] The charge transport occurs after the generation of light-induced carriers, and the transport efficiency influences the photovoltaic performance of PSCs dramatically.[10-12]

The charge transport process takes place in each layer including perovskite layer, electron transport layer (ETL) and hole transport layer (HTL).[13-18] Hence, the conductivity of each layer has been studied widely to enhance the carrier transport.[13, 19, 20] However, the conductivity is not the only factor affecting the charge transport in PSCs. The defects in each layer especially perovskite layer also deteriorate the charge transport. The perovskite films are poly-crystallized and contain a lot of crystal boundaries. There are a lot of defects like under-coordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} $  on the boundary and these defects can

act as traps, inducing serious non-radiative recombination. Enlarging the grain size of perovskite crystals can reduce the crystal boundary and the defect density in perovskite films.[5, 21-24] The post-deposition passivation materials on perovskite films can also reduce the defects.[25-28] Yang et al.[25] used an organic semiconducting nonfullerene acceptor molecule IT-M as Lewis base to treat the perovskite film. The  $ \mathrm{C = O} $  bond can interact with under-coordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2 + }} $  and improve the PCE of PSCs from  $ 18.7\% $  to  $ 20.5\% $ . You et al.[29] developed an organic halide salt, phenethylammonium iodide, for post-treatment of perovskite films to suppress the surface defects of perovskite films, and they obtained the high-performance PSCs with a PCE of  $ 23.6\% $ .

The charge transport also occurs at the interface between different layers. The charge transport at interface is also called charge transfer. A high-efficiency charge transfer reduces the possibility of the charge accumulation and recombination near the interface. In PSCs, the efficiency of the charge transfer is often influenced by the electronic field induced by the work function difference between two adjacent layers. ETL with a lower work function is favored for high-efficiency electron transfer. A lot of researches on adjusting the work function have done. Increasing the concentration of free electron via doping ETL with other element is a common method to decrease the work function.[30-32] Bai et al.[30] doped  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  with Sb to increase the free electron concentration and decrease the work function of ETL. The Sb doped  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  not only increase the charge transfer efficiency at the interface, but also enhance the charge transport in the ETL, resulting in a better photovoltaic performance of PSCs. The interface modification is also an effective method to adjust the work function of ETL and passivate defects at the interface.[19, 33-36] Choi et al.[35] employed 3-(1-pyridinio)-1-propanesulfonate to modify the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  ETL surface and shift the work function of ETL. The modification improved the PCE from  $ 19.6\% $  to  $ 21.4\% $ .

In this work, we introduce an interfacial dipole layer (IDL) and passivation layer in PSCs to enhance the transport of the photo-generated charge carriers and improve photovoltaic performance. The 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate ([BMIM]BF $ _4 $ ) induces an IDL formed at the interface between SnO $ _2 $  and perovskite films due to the Coulomb interaction between tetrafluoroborate anion and unsaturated Sn $ ^{4+} $ . The IDL results in a reduced surface work function of ETL and promoted charge transfer. The triphenylphosphine oxide (TPPO) contains P=O bonds which have lone electron pairs and low electrostatic potentials, leading to an interaction between TPPO and cation. The interaction between TPPO and cation reduces anion vacancy induced defects on the perovskite crystal grain surface. Combining IDL with defect passivation, both fill factor (FF) and open-circuit voltage (V $ _{\text{OC}} $ ) of PSCs have been improved. The highest PCE of SnO $ _2 $  based planar PSCs has been improved from 18.7 % to 21.1 %. The PCE of the target PSCs showed almost no change after 124 days in dry air condition, but the PCE of the reference PSCs dropped to the 52.5 % of the initial PCE.

The formation process of the interface dipole on  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  films is illustrated in Figure 1(d). The IDL can shift the work function of the ETL and promote the charge transport at the interface between ETLs and perovskite films.[34, 35] As shown in Figure 1(b),  $ \mathrm{P = O} $  bonds in TPPO molecules contain lone electron pairs which can be coordinated with  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2 + }} $ . The reduced under-coordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2 + }} $  cation can reduce the charge trap state density in perovskite films.

The TPPO dissolved in chlorobenzene was spin-coated onto perovskite films without annealing. The photovoltaic performance of devices is shown in Figure 4(a), and the corresponding parameters extracted from these J-V curves are listed in Table 2. The statistical data for PSCs with different TPPO content treatment is shown in Figure 4(b) (Supporting Information). The optimal concentration of TPPO solution is  $ 0.5\mathrm{mg / mL} $ . In comparison of reference device, the  $ \mathrm{V_{OC}} $  of PSCs with TPPO treatment (PSCs-TPPO) has been increased distinctly. The FF of PSCs-TPPO also has been enhanced slightly. Hence, the PSCs-TPPO yield a higher PCE.

The morphologies of perovskite with/without TPPO treatment are shown in Figure S7. No distinct variation can be observed on the surfaces of both perovskite films. Furthermore, the crystallinity of both perovskite films has been characterized by XRD measurements. As shown in Figure S8, the main peaks at  $ 14.37^{\circ} $  exhibit similar shape and the peak width at half height is same in XRD patterns of both perovskite films. From above, it can be concluded that the TPPO post-treatment don't affect the morphology and crystallinity of perovskite films. The improved photovoltaic performance of PSCs-TPPO wasn't attributed to the morphology change in perovskite films.

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, the   $  [BMIM]BF_{4}  $   and TPPO has been deposited onto   $  SnO_{2}  $   films and perovskite films, respective, resulting in an efficient charge transport in PSCs. The   $  [BMIM]BF_{4}  $   decreases the work function of   $  SnO_{2}  $   films and promotes the electron transfer at the interface. The   $  [BMIM]BF_{4}  $   also stabilizes the perovskite films and suppress the generation of   $  PbI_{2}  $   at the anneal step. The P=O bonds in TPPO molecules interact with under-coordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and reduce the trap-state density in perovskite films, resulting in a reduced non-radiative recombination and longer carrier lifetime. As a result, the photovoltaic performance of target PSCs has been improved dramatically, yielding a champion PCE of 21.1 % under reverse voltage scan, which is very closed to the highest reported PCE of PSCs based on   $  Cs_{0.05}(MA_{0.17}FA_{0.83})_{0.95}\mathrm{Pb(I_{0.83}Br_{0.17})_{3}}  $  . [53] Vacancy substitutions also induced the improved performance stability of PSCs. Further efficiency improvements are expected through engineering the ingredient (e.g., bromine reduction) and crystallinity of perovskite films.[29]

# Solution preparation

$ \mathrm{[BMIM]BF_4} $  solution: The high-concentration  $ [\mathrm{BMIM}] \mathrm{BF}_4 $  solution was prepared by adding  $ 20~\mu \mathrm{L} $   $ \mathrm{[BMIM]BF_4} $  into the  $ 980~\mu \mathrm{L} $  methyl alcohol firstly. Then,  $ [\mathrm{BMIM}] \mathrm{BF}_4 $  solutions with different concentrations were obtained via diluting the high-concentration  $ [\mathrm{BMIM}] \mathrm{BF}_4 $  solution with methyl alcohol. Perovskite solution: The perovskite solution was prepared as following.  $ 507~\mathrm{mg~PbI}_2 $ ,  $ 73.4~\mathrm{mg~PbBr}_2 $ ,  $ 172~\mathrm{mg~FAI} $  and  $ 22.4~\mathrm{mg~MABr} $  was dissolved into  $ 1~\mathrm{mL} $  solvent mixture (V(DMSO):V(DMF)=3:7) to prepare the solution 1. Then  $ 52~\mu \mathrm{L} $  CsI solution ( $ 390~\mathrm{mg} $  in  $ 1~\mathrm{mL} $  DMSO) was added into the solution 1 and then the final solution was stirred for 2 hours.

HTL solution: The HTL solution was prepared by dissolving 72.3 mg spiro-OMeTAD, 28.8 ml 4-tert-butylpyridine, 17.5 ml of a stock solution of 520 mg/mL lithium bis(trifluoromethylsulphonyl)imide in acetonitrile and 29 ml of a solution of 300mg/ml FK209 in acetonitrile in 1 ml chlorobenzene.

# Device fabrication

The ITO glasses were cleaned sequentially in acetone, absolute ethyl alcohol, and deionized water

ultrasonic bath for 15 minutes, respectively. After ITO glasses were cleaned by the UV-Ozone (120 W) treat for 20 minutes, a SnO₂ film was deposited by spin-coating diluted SnO₂ nanoparticles colloidal solution with a concentration of 2 % (original SnO₂ solution: Alfa Aesar (tin(IV) oxide, 15% in H₂O colloidal dispersion)) according to Reference [41]. At 9 s before the ending of spin-coating program, 150 μL chlorobenzene was dropped onto the spinning substrate. Then, the perovskite films were heated at 100 °C for 60 min. For perovskite-TPPO/perovskite-TBPO samples, an 80 μL TPPO/TBPO solution was dropped onto the perovskite surface and spin-coated at a speed of 5000 rpm. The HTL was prepared by spin-coating the 60 μL HTL solution at 5000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, 100 nm of Au top electrode was thermally evaporated onto the HTL with a speed of 0.2 nm/s.
