# Defect Passivation by a D–A–D Type Hole-Transporting Interfacial Layer for Efficient and Stable Perovskite Solar Cells

Yu-Duan Wang,  $  ^{§}  $   Yang Wang,  $  ^{*,§}  $   Jiang-Yang Shao,  $  ^{*}  $   Yangjie Lan, Zhong-Rui Lan, Yu-Wu Zhong,  $  ^{*}  $   and Yanlin Song

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: In this work, we present a series of donor–acceptor–donor type of small molecules (BDAD, ODAD, and DDAD) based on the triphenylamine (TPA) and dithienopyrrolobenzothiadiazole (DTPBT) units, which are distinguished by alkyl chains of different lengths grafted on the DTPBT unit, as a hole-transporting interfacial layer for perovskite solar cells (PSCs). The incorporation of the DTPBT units is beneficial for not only carrier transportation but also potential defects passivation via Pb–N/S interactions. A champion power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 22.76% has been achieved on PSCs based on BDAD with the n-butyl side chains. Those devices without encapsulation retain over 96% of their initial PCE for more than 120 days. The improved stability is attributed to the restraint of the generation of δ-phase perovskite as supported by the grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering analyses.

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have shown exciting performances due to their high light absorption coefficient, broad light absorption range, fast charge carrier mobility, and long diffusion length. Lead halide perovskites have an attractive feature to tune their band gap from 1.48 to 2.30 eV by changing cations and/or halides. Formamidinium lead iodide (FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ) have been the preferred material for the preparation of efficient PSCs because its band gap (1.48 eV) is closer to the Shockley-Queisser limit.  $  ^{1-3}  $   The PSCs fabricated with   $  \alpha  $  -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   have achieved a certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 25.2%.  $  ^{4}  $   However,   $  \alpha  $  -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   is chemically unstable, easily undergoing the transformation from the black   $  \alpha  $  -phase to the yellow photoinactive   $  \delta  $  -phase at room temperature. Therefore, various strategies have been employed to stabilize the FA-based perovskite.  $  ^{5-7}  $

In addition, due to the ionic nature of perovskite materials and the solution-based fabrication processes, various defects such as vacancies, interstitials, and antisites are present at the surface of the perovskite film.  $  ^{8}  $   The uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   defects act as nonradiative recombination centers to seriously affect the device stability and performance. It has also been reported that   $  Pb^{0}  $   is another detrimental defect, which is usually generated during the processing steps.  $  ^{9}  $   To overcome these issues, numerous strategies such as compositional engineering, interface passivation, and additive engineering have been developed.  $  ^{10}  $   Among these, passivation techniques appear to be efficient approaches for the fabrication of highly efficient and

In this work, three D-A-D hole-transporting interfacial materials (BDAD, ODAD, and DDAD) featuring triphenylamine (TPA) as the donor along with DTPBT as the acceptor are presented, in which the first capitalized letter B, O, or D stands for the different alkyl chains ( $ n $ -butyl,  $ n $ -octyl, or  $ n $ -dodecyl, respectively) on the DTPBT core (Figures 1a and S1). The D-A-D structure endows these compounds with suitable energy levels. The extended planar structure with multiple fused heteroatom rings of the DTPBT unit is believed beneficial for the hole transportation and defect passivation. Moreover, the alkyl chains of different lengths attached to the DTPBT are expected to affect the structural planarity and device performance. Gratifingly, the PCE of the device with BDAD as the hole-transporting interlayer at the perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD interface was found to be remarkably enhanced from  $ 19.73\% $  (pristine device) to  $ 22.76\% $ . Grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) measurements support that BDAD defers the generation of  $ \delta $ -phase perovskite. The BDAD-based device shows excellent stability, with  $ 96\% $  of the initial PCE being retained after 120 days.

The DTPBT monomer was synthesized according to the reported literature,  $  ^{21}  $  followed by the attachment of alkyl chains of different lengths. The brominated DTPBT containing different alkyl chains was coupled with triphenylamine boric acid through the Suzuki reaction to afford the final products BDAD, ODAD, and DDAD (Figure S1). The identity and purity of these materials were confirmed by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and mass spectrometry (MS). The molecular geometry structures were constructed through density functional theory (DFT) calculations (Figure S2). DTPBT and triphenylamine units maintain the two smallest dihedral angles in BDAD, which are 30.02° and 23.05°, respectively (Figure 1b). However, the two dihedral angles

increase in ODAD (30.34° and 26.03°) and DDAD (31.84° and 26.35°). This suggests that BDAD has a better planarity than the other two molecules, which is believed beneficial for intermolecular   $  \pi-\pi  $   stacking and charge transfer.  $  ^{20}  $   In other words, the excessively long alkyl chains in ODAD and DDAD may lead to entangled molecular stacking and thus weaken intermolecular   $  \pi-\pi  $   stacking and charge transport.

Figure 1c shows the UV–vis absorption spectra of BDAD, ODAD, DDAD, and Spiro-OMeTAD. Compounds BDAD, ODAD, and DDAD have a similar shape of absorption spectra, which are however distinctly broader and red-shifted with respect to that of Spiro-OMeTAD. The reason for the broad absorptions of these three new compounds is their extended D–A–D structures, giving rise to more effective   $  \pi-\pi^{*}  $   and intramolecular charge transfer transitions.  $  ^{22,23}  $   The optical bandgap (  $  E_{opt}  $  ) of BDAD, ODAD, and DDAD was estimated from the absorption spectra as 2.20, 2.21, and 2.22 eV, respectively. The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy levels of BDAD, ODAD, and DDAD were determined by cyclic voltammogram (CV). As shown in Figure 1d, all of the first anodic waves of BDAD, ODAD, and DDAD are located at +0.55 V, which is slightly positive with respect to that of Spiro-OMeTAD (+0.54 V). Hence, the HOMO energy levels of BDAD, ODAD, and DDAD were estimated to be -5.21 eV,  $  ^{24}  $   which is distinctly higher than the valence band edge of the perovskite used (-6.04 eV). The valence band edge of the perovskite was calculated by the ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (Figure S3). According to the   $  E_{opt}  $   obtained from the absorption spectra, the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels of BDAD, ODAD, and DDAD were estimated to be -3.00, -2.99, and -2.99 eV, respectively. The significantly higher LUMO level relative to the conduction band of perovskite (-4.46 eV) can efficiently block the electron transfer from perovskite to the Au counter electrode. A series of devices were fabricated with the configuration of an indium tin-oxide (ITO) substrate/PEDOT:PSS/molecules/Au to characterize the hole mobility   $  \left(\mu_{\mathrm{h}}\right)  $   of different molecules based on the space charge limited current (SCLC) method. As shown in Figure S4, the hole mobilities of the pristine BDAD, ODAD, and DDAD films were measured to be   $  7.24 \times 10^{-4}  $  ,   $  4.22 \times 10^{-4}  $  , and   $  7.70 \times 10^{-5}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $   V  $  ^{-1}  $   s  $  ^{-1}  $  , respectively. It is clear that BDAD with shorter alkyl chains exhibits a higher mobility, which may be caused by the more efficient intermolecular   $  \pi-\pi  $   stacking and charge transfer of BDAD. Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved PL (TRPL) measurements were carried out to prove that BDAD, ODAD, and DDAD were capable of hole transport on perovskite interfaces. As shown in Figure 2a, the perovskite films with these reagents exhibit significant emission quenching compared to the bare perovskite film, suggesting that they can achieve efficient hole extraction.  $  ^{[17,26,27]}  $   During the device procedure, the BDAD molecules have a very short-time contact with the chlorobenzene solvent used for the later spin

coating of Spiro-OMeTAD, which is believed insufficient to redissolve the BDAD molecules. This point is also supported by steady-state photoluminescence measurements (Figure S5). The TPRL results (Figure 2b) are fitted through the biexponential equation   $  \left(\tau_{\mathrm{ave}}=A_{1}\exp(-t/\tau_{1})+A_{2}\exp(-t/\tau_{2})\right)  $  , in which   $  \tau_{1}  $   and   $  \tau_{2}  $   represent the fast and slow decay times (Table S1). The bare perovskite (PVK) shows a long-living decay   $  \left(\tau_{1}=192.18\;\mathrm{ns},\tau_{2}=2000.00\;\mathrm{ns},\tau_{\mathrm{ave}}=1961.85\;\mathrm{ns}\right)  $  ; however, the PVK/BDAD film is characterized by a much faster decay   $  \left(\tau_{1}=3.38\;\mathrm{ns},\tau_{2}=63.85\;\mathrm{ns},\tau_{\mathrm{ave}}=57.25\;\mathrm{ns}\right)  $  . The other two films have a   $  \tau_{ave}  $   of around 61 ns. The better hole extraction and faster hole transfer will accelerate the process of charge transmission,  $  ^{27}  $   which is conducive to improving the open-circuit voltage   $  \left(V_{\mathrm{oc}}\right)  $   and fill factor (FF) of PSCs.  $  ^{14,28,29}  $

We further investigated the effect of different concentrations of BDAD used in the device fabrication on the device photovoltaic parameters (Figure S7 and Table S2). All of the devices modified with different concentrations (1–10 mg/mL) of BDAD exhibit a higher FF (76.43–79.49%), but the   $  J_{sc}  $   and   $  V_{oc}  $   first increased with the use of 1–2 mg/mL of BDAD and then decreased with a higher concentration of BDAD. As a result, the PSC with 2 mg/mL of BDAD exhibits the best PCE of 22.76%, pointing to 15.3% enhancement with respect to that of the pristine device (19.73%; Figure 3a). Efficiency statistics based on 20 devices confirm the good reproducibility of the results (Figure 3b). In addition, the device of ITO/SnO  $  _{2}  $  /(FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  )  $  _{1-x}  $  (MAPbBr  $  _{3}  $  )  $  _{x}  $  /BDAD/Au, with a single layer of BDAD as the HTM, was also fabricated and tested (Figure S8 and Table S3). The PSCs using doped and undoped BDAD (30 mg/mL) offered efficiencies of 18.05% and 12.09%, respectively. The hysteresis effect is evaluated by the hysteresis index (HI) as calculated by the equation HI = (  $  \eta_{R} - \eta_{F}  $  )/  $  \eta_{R}  $  , in which   $  \eta_{R}  $   and   $  \eta_{F}  $   represent the PCE obtained in the reverse and forward scan, respectively (Figure S9).  $  ^{14}  $   The BDAD-based PSC has a negligible hysteresis with a low HI of 0.025. The reduced hysteresis is attributed to the improved interfacial charge transfer. Compared to the pristine device, the PSC with BDAD exhibits a higher external quantum efficiency (EQE) over the whole light-absorption range (Figure 3c). The integrated photocurrent density of the devices based on BDAD is 23.71 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  , which is slightly inferior yet comparable to the   $  J_{sc}  $   obtained in the above J-V measurements.

In order to gain insight into the surface passivation effect of the BDAD interlayer, the defect changes on device surfaces were examined. Figure 3d shows the variation of  $  V_{\mathrm{oc}}  $  as a function of the light intensity. The reduced ideality factor  $  (n_{\mathrm{id}})  $  from 1.85 for the pristine device to 1.50 for the device with BDAD indicates that the treatment with BDAD leads to the

suppression of the defect-assisted recombination.  $  ^{30}  $   As shown in Figure 3e, the relationship between the current and light intensity is fitted by the power-law equation   $  (J_{\mathrm{sc}} \propto P^{\alpha})  $  , where the   $  \alpha  $   value is used to describe the degree of bimolecular recombination. The PSC with or without BDAD exhibits an   $  \alpha  $   value of 0.99 or 0.96, respectively, indicating that all free carriers are swept out and collected at the electrodes with ignorable recombination and accumulation.  $  ^{31}  $   Furthermore, the SCLC model is employed to quantitatively evaluate the surface defect density of the perovskite film with or without BDAD. The trap-filled limit voltage   $  (V_{\mathrm{TFL}})  $   for the perovskite with or without BDAD is 0.36 or 0.61 V, respectively (Figure 3f). According to the equation   $  N_{d} = 2\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{r}V_{TFL}/eL^{2}  $   (  $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   is the vacuum permittivity,   $  \varepsilon_{r}  $   is the relative dielectric constant,   $  V_{TFL}  $   is the defect-filled limited voltage, e is the electron charge, and L is the film thickness), the defect density of the perovskite film with or without BDAD is determined to   $  3.81 \times 10^{15}  $   and   $  6.46 \times 10^{15}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  , respectively, suggestive of the beneficial effect of BDAD in reducing surface defects.  $  ^{32,33}  $   The reduction of defects has a positive effect on enhancing   $  V_{oc}  $   and suggests that BDAD plays a crucial role in passivating surface defects.

In order to further understand the role of BDAD in the passivation process of surface defects, UV–vis and X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectral analyses were performed to characterize the basic properties of the perovskite films. Figure 4a shows that the addition of BDAD does not change the absorption boundary of perovskite and indicates that the band gap of perovskite is maintained. XRD spectra in Figure 4b confirm that the existence of BDAD does not affect the main crystal structure of perovskite. The peak strength of   $  PbI_{2}  $   at the position of   $  2\theta = 12.85^{\circ}  $   is relatively weakened in the perovskite films modified by BDAD. Previous reports have shown that a moderate amount of   $  PbI_{2}  $   in perovskites prepared by the two-step method does contribute to defect passivation.  $  ^{30,34}  $   X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was measured to characterize perovskite, BDAD, and PVK/BDAD films to further support that BDAD passivates the surface defects. The pristine perovskite without the modification of BDAD has two strong peaks at 143.3 and 138.5 eV, which are respectively attributed to the signals of   $  4f_{5/2}  $   and   $  4f_{7/2}  $   of   $  Pb^{2+}  $   (Figure 4c). In addition, two weak peaks at 141.3 and 136.7 eV are observed, which are caused by the   $  Pb^{0}  $   defects.  $  ^{14,33,35}  $   After modification with BDAD, the   $  4f_{5/2}  $   and   $  4f_{7/2}  $   signals shift to 143.2 and 138.3 eV with a lower binding energy, and the   $  Pb^{0}  $   peak completely disappeared. The N 1s peak of BDAD is located at 399.9 eV. After combining with perovskite, the N 1s peak shifts to 400.3 eV with a higher binding energy (Figure S10a). BDAD exhibits two additional signals at 165.4 and 164.3 eV, which belong to

The aging process of the perovskite films was examined by XRD to gain insight into the role of BDAD in enhancing the film stability. As shown in Figure S12, after 500 h of exposure in the ambient environment (20–25 °C, RH 30–50%), the pure perovskite film has taken some degree of degredation as indicated by the distinctly enhanced PbI  $  _{2}  $  diffractions.  $  ^{37}  $  In addition, a new weak diffraction peak at 2θ of 11.73° was observed, which is classified as the δ-phase FAPbI  $  _{3}  $ .  $  ^{38}  $  Previous studies have confirmed that the presence of the δ phase significantly reduces the efficiency and stability of devices.  $  ^{39-42}  $  In contrast, after the same period of exposure (500 h) under the same conditions, the perovskite film modified with BDAD did not exhibit any signal of the generation of the δ phase. This result is further confirmed by the GIWAXS measurements. After 500 h of exposure, the pure perovskite film exhibits three obvious diffraction rings at 0.82 (marked with white dashed lines), 0.90, and 0.98 Å  $  ^{-1}  $ , while the perovskite coated with BDAD only exhibits two rings at 0.91 and 1.00 Å  $  ^{-1}  $  (Figure 5).

The emergence of the diffraction at   $  0.82 \, Å^{-1}  $   of the pure perovskite film provides a solid evidence of the generation of the   $  \delta  $  -phase perovskite. As demonstrated by the one-dimensional (1D) extracted profiles (Figure S13), the GIWAXS diffraction ring at   $  0.82 \, Å^{-1}  $   corresponds to a   $  2\theta  $   value of   $  11.62^{\circ}  $  , which matches well with the XRD peak of the   $  \delta  $  -phase perovskite.  $  ^{41,43}  $   These results demonstrate that BDAD can inhibit the generation of the   $  \delta  $   phase and stabilize the crystal structure of perovskite, which is crucial to improve the stability of the devices.  $  ^{44}  $   Considering that pure PVK film has a higher defect density compared to that modified with BDAD (Figure 3f). Therefore, it can be inferred that the reduction of surface defects is one of the reasons for suppressing the phase transition.

Figure 6a shows the change of PCE over time at the maximum power point of different devices. The pristine device shows an obvious decline of the PCE from 19.82% to 13.89% after 600 s of test (average PCE: 16.48%). In contrast, the device with BDAD keeps a stable efficiency during the same period of measurement, with an average efficiency of 21.78% being achieved. Figure 6b shows the long-term stability of the device performance. After storing for 120 days in the clean room (25 °C, RH 20%), the PSC device with BDAD still keeps over 96% of its initial PCE. In contrast, the control PSC shows a significant decrease of PCE, with only 44% of the initial PCE being retained after 50 days. This suggests that the device stability is significantly enhanced by the incorporation of BDAD. This effect is partially caused by the enhanced hydrophobicity of perovskite in combination with BDAD. As shown in Figure 6c, the water contact angle of the bare perovskite increases distinctly from 66.8° to 83.6° when it is modified with BDAD, which can hinder the water penetration into the perovskite interface and thus suppress the phase transition.  $  ^{45,46}  $

In summary, in order to improve the efficiency and stability of PSCs, an organic small molecule BDAD is developed as an interfacial Lewis base to passivate the perovskite defects and slow down the generation of the  $ \delta $  phase. The PSC based on BDAD achieves the best PCE of  $ 22.76\% $  and is able to maintain  $ 96\% $  of the initial efficiency after 120 days. The extended D-A-D conjugated structure and the suitable energy level of BDAD that matches well with that of perovskite are believed critical for the improved interfacial hole extraction and transfer.
