# In-Situ Growth of Perovskite Stacking Layer for High-Efficiency Carbon-Based Hole Conductor Free Perovskite Solar Cells

Jianhua Liu,  $  ^{a}  $   Qisen Zhou,  $  ^{a}  $   Nan Kyi Kyi Thein,  $  ^{b,c}  $   Lei Tian,  $  ^{b}  $   Donglin Jia,  $  ^{a}  $   Erik M. J. Johansson,  $  ^{b}  $   and Xiaoliang Zhang  $  ^{a*}  $

The interfacial properties between the perovskite layer and carbon electrode are critical for the photovoltaic performance of the carbon electrode-based perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Herein, a methylammonium lead mixed halide (MAPbI $ _{x} $ Br $ _{3-x} $ ) perovskite layer in-situ grows on the top of methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI $ _{3} $ ) perovskite layer forming MAPbI $ _{3} $ /MAPbI $ _{x} $ Br $ _{3-x} $  perovskite stacking structure (PSS) to improve the interfacial properties at the perovskite/carbon electrode interface. The charge carrier dynamics in both perovskite and PSC device induced by the MAPbI $ _{x} $ Br $ _{3-x} $  perovskite stacking layer are studied using extensive characterizations. The charge interfacial recombination at the perovskite/carbon electrode interface is significantly diminished using the PSS within PSC, resulting in largely improved charge extraction and therefore high photovoltaic performance. The PSS-based PSC shows a power conversion efficiency up to 16.2% (increased by 43% compared with that of the conventional MAPbI $ _{3} $ -based PSC), which is among the highest efficiency of the carbon electrode-based hole conductor free PSCs. Meanwhile, the PSS-based PSC exhibits good stability under both continuous illumination and storage under dark condition. This work may provide a new avenue to fine control the interfacial properties of the carbon electrode-based PSC for further improving photovoltaic performance.

# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite materials have attracted tremendous attention in the application of solar cells, photodetectors, light emitting devices and lasers due to their remarkable optoelectronic properties including high light absorption coefficient, long charge carrier diffusion length, and an ambipolar charge transporting property.  $  ^{1-6}  $   A significant achievement in terms of improving photovoltaic performance and stability of the perovskite solar cells (PSCs) was obtained in the past few years through fine controlling the perovskite composition,  $  ^{7,8}  $   charge (hole and electron) transport materials,  $  ^{9,10}  $   interfacial properties,  $  ^{11,12}  $   device architectures and with better understanding the device physics.  $  ^{13-15}  $   The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of the PSC was rapidly improved from an initial value of 3.8% to a recent value of over 23%.  $  ^{16,17}  $

Noble metals, such as Au and Ag, are generally used as the counter electrode in the PSCs. The metal-based electrode is deposited by means of thermal evaporation approach under the high temperature and high vacuum conditions, which may remain challenges toward scalable fabrication of the PSCs and therefore hinder their commercialization.  $  ^{18-20}  $   Meanwhile, the hole transport

material (HTM) is necessary for the metal electrode-based PSCs, which may suffer from traveling of the metal nanoparticles from such electrode that the metal nanoparticles could penetrate the HTM layer or even perovskite layer, significantly affecting the device stability.  $  ^{[21,22]}  $   The commercialized carbon-based electrode is believed as one of the most promising alternative candidates for the metal electrode owning to their earth abundant, low-cost, easy-preparation, low-temperature processability and similar work function with the metal electrode.  $  ^{[23-30]}  $   Meanwhile, the HTM-free is allowed in the carbon electrode-based PSC, further lowering the PSC cost. However, the photovoltaic performance of the carbon electrode-based PSC is determined by the interfacial properties at the perovskite/carbon electrode interface due to undesirable charge interfacial recombination.  $  ^{[31,32]}  $

To suppress such interfacial recombination, Aitola et al. used hybrid HTM-counter electrode based on a thin single-walled carbon nanotube film and a drop-cast 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-di-p-methoxyphenylamine)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD) as a HTM to improve charge collection and therefore obtained 15.5% PCE.  $  ^{33}  $   Yang et al. used copper phthalocyanine (CuPc) as an HTM to improve energy alignment at the perovskite/carbon interface, benefiting the charge extraction and a high PCE of 16.1% was obtained.  $  ^{34}  $   However, the PCE of the carbon electrode-based HTM-free PSCs is far behind the PCE of the carbon electrode-based PSCs with an organic or inorganic HTM due to the charge interfacial recombination.

The  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $  perovskite layer was prepared using the composition engineered approach that a methylammonium bromide (MABr) precursor is spin-coated on the top of pristine  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite layer with excess  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and thereafter performed sintering treatment. # 2. Results

# 2.1. PSS fabrication.

Fig. 1a schematically presents the fabrication processes of the MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -PSS, and the detailed information is demonstrated in the Experimental Section. The pristine MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite layer with excess PbI $ _2 $  (molar ratio of PbI $ _2 $ :MAI=1.05:1) was firstly prepared on the top of FTO/c-TiO $ _2 $ /m-TiO $ _2 $  substrate using one-step deposition method with anti-solvent treatment, and thereafter the sample was annealed on a hot-plate (100 °C) for 15 min. During the MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite formation, excess PbI $ _2 $  mostly presented on the top surface of the perovskite layer and between the grain boundaries within the perovskite layer. $ ^{35,36} $  The MABr isopropanol (IPA) solution was spin-coated on the top surface of pristine MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite layer with excess PbI $ _2 $  and then annealed at 100 °C for 15 min. The MABr reacted with the excess PbI $ _2 $  distributed on the top surface of MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite layer or even left in the grain boundaries forming a thin MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $  perovskite stacking layer, resulting in the MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -PSS.

The inset in Fig. 1a displays the assumed PSS. The morphology of the top surface of MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite and PSS was characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite layer exhibits dense property covering a large area and the grain size is  $ \sim $ 200 nm (Fig. 1b). But a few pinholes were observed on the sample surface, which affects the charge collection and therefore photovoltaic performance of the PSCs. $ ^{37} $  However, after in-situ grew MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $  perovskite stacking layer on the top surface of the MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite layer forming PSS, the perovskite layer is pinhole free and a more homogeneous and smoother morphology was obtained (Fig. 1c). The grain boundaries within the MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite layer are fully covered by the perovskite stacking layer. These results suggest that the excess PbI $ _2 $  in the pristine MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite layer could be diminished using MABr and therefore converts to MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $  perovskite forming PSS, which was further characterized in the following part.

# 2.2. PSS characterization.

The  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $  perovskite stacking layer deposited on the top surface of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite layer may affect the physical properties of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbl}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x}\mathrm{-PSS} $ , such as energy levels and optoelectronic properties, which plays an important role for the photovoltaic performance of the PSCs. To confirm such hypothesis, detailed characterizations were carried out.

Fig. 2a shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite layer with excess PbI $ _2 $  and MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -PSS. For comparison, the conventional MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite layer was also prepared with the precursor molar ratio of PbI $ _2 $ :MAI=1:1. The PbI $ _2 $  peak at  $ \sim $ 12.7° in the XRD pattern clearly reveals that PbI $ _2 $  did not fully convert to the perovskite, which is in agreement with the literature. $ ^{35,38-40} $  As expected, compared with the conventional MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite (PbI $ _2 $ :MAI=1:1) the intensity of the PbI $ _2 $  peak at  $ \sim $ 12.7° becomes stronger after using excess PbI $ _2 $  in the perovskite precursor (PbI $ _2 $ :MAI=1.05:1), marked as MAPbI $ _3 $ +PbI $ _2 $  in the Fig. 2a, which indicates that more PbI $ _2 $  is left in the perovskite layer. However, the PbI $ _2 $  peaks thoroughly disappear in the XRD pattern after treated the perovskite film (MAPbI $ _3 $ +PbI $ _2 $ ) with MABr, and meanwhile the negligible change in intensity and position of the diffraction peaks of MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite phase is found in the XRD pattern. Due to that the MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $  stacking layer is also a perovskite structure we did not observe any significant changes in the XRD patterns after deposited MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $  stacking layer on MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite layer. The IPA may affect the amount of PbI $ _2 $  left in the perovskite layer. Therefore, the IPA alone was applied to treat the perovskite film (MAPbI $ _3 $ +PbI $ _2 $ ) for comparison, and the result shows that the perovskite layer has no obvious difference in the XRD pattern before and after the IPA treatment (Fig. S1, ESI). This important evidence suggests that the excess PbI $ _2 $  in the perovskite layer reacts with MABr forming a thin MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $  perovskite stacking layer covering on the former MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite layer, forming the PSS.

Fig. 2b presents the light absorption spectra of the pristine MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite (PbI $ _2 $ :MAI=1:1) and the MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -PSS, and a partial enlargement of the spectra at the wavelength of 725-800 nm is shown in Fig. 2c. Both perovskite samples exhibit strong light absorption in the visible wavelength region. However, a slight blue shift in the light absorption spectra is observed for the MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -PSS sample (Fig. 2c), which reveals that the MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $  perovskite stacking layer may affect the overall optoelectronic properties of the PSS sample. Generally, the bandgap energy of the perovskite material is determined by the material composition. The bandgap energy of the MAPbI $ _3 $  and MAPbBr $ _3 $  perovskite is  $ \sim $ 1.6 and  $ \sim $ 2.3 eV, respectively, and the bandgap energy of the intermediate composition MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $  perovskite gradually becomes higher with an increasing amount of Br element in the MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $  perovskite material. $ ^{41-43} $  The Tauc plot figure of the MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite and MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -PSS is presented in Fig. S2 (ESI). The bandgap energy with an increased value of  $ \sim $ 0.02 eV was calculated for the MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -PSS comparing with that of the pristine MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite sample. The detailed mechanism of such band edge shift is not exactly known currently.

To gain more information concerning the effect of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $  perovskite stacking layer on the composition and energy levels of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $ -PSS, X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) measurement was performed. Fig. 2d, e, and f display the core-level XPS spectra of Pb 4f, I 3d and Br 4d in the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite and  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $ -PSS, respectively. The overview of XPS spectra is shown in Fig. S3 (ESI). The binding energy of Pb  $ 4\mathrm{f}_{7/2} $  and I  $ 3\mathrm{d}_{5/2} $  peak shift from 138.38 and  $ 619.30\mathrm{eV} $  to 138.07 and  $ 618.99\mathrm{eV} $  for the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite and  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $ -PSS, respectively. Therefore, the core level of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $ -PSS processes an upward shift of  $ \sim 0.31\mathrm{eV} $  comparing to that of the conventional  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite, which reveals the occurrence of

interfacial charge redistribution at the interface of MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -PSS. $ ^{44} $  Comparing with the MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite sample, the peak of Br 3d was obviously observed in the PSS, confirming the existence of Br element in the MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -PSS (Fig. 2f). Due to that the XPS measurement is surface sensitive, the thickness of the MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $  perovskite stacking layer in the MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -PSS was determined using the time of flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS), as shown in Fig. S4 (ESI). The results show that the Br element is mainly distributing at the top part of the MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -PSS with a depth of  $ \sim $ 75 nm. It is worth to note that the Br element is not uniformly distributing in the MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $  stacking layer, which may result from the halide exchange or ion migration.

As described above, the energy levels of the perovskite material are dominated by the material composition and thus the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3-x} $  perovskite stacking layer may also affect the energy levels of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $  PSS. Therefore, the valence band (VB) edge of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite and  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $  PSS was tested (Fig. 2g). The valence band maximum (VBM) of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite is below the Fermi level of  $ \sim 1.40\mathrm{eV} $ , and thus the conduction band minimum (CBM) is placed above the Fermi level of  $ \sim 0.21\mathrm{eV} $  considering the band gap energy value of  $ \sim 1.61\mathrm{eV} $  for the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite. For the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $  PSS, the VBM and CBM up shifts to 1.17 and  $ 0.46\mathrm{eV} $ , below and above the Fermi level, respectively, considering a band gap value of  $ \sim 1.63\mathrm{eV} $  for the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $  PSS (Fig. S2, ESI). Therefore, the VBM and CBM of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $  PSS have an upshift of  $ \sim 0.23 $  and  $ \sim 0.25\mathrm{eV} $ , respectively, comparing to that of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite.

To study the application of such PSS in the photovoltaic device, the PSC device with a device architecture of FTO/c-TiO $ _2 $ /m-TiO $ _2 $ /MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -PSS/carbon electrode was constructed, as shown in Fig. 3a. The corresponding energy levels of each material in the device are summarized in Fig. 3b. The TiO $ _2 $  (c-TiO $ _2 $  and m-TiO $ _2 $ ) works as an efficient electron transport material and the MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $  perovskite stacking layer acts as an electron blocking layer in the device. The commercial carbon paste was applied for the counter electrode preparation, which was deposited on the top of MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $  perovskite stacking layer using the doctor blading technique under the ambient conditions, followed annealing at 100 °C for 20 min. Due to the favorable energy level alignment of each layer within the device and the ambipolar nature of perovskite, the photoinduced electron and hole in the perovskite layer might be efficiently extracted by the corresponding electrodes, which will be detailed discussed in the following part. Fig. 3c displays the cross-sectional SEM image of the MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -based PSC device.

The device photovoltaic performance was measured under AM1.5G 100 mW/cm² illumination provided with a solar simulator and different voltage scanning directions were applied to record the photocurrent density-voltage (J-V) curve. Fig. 3d shows the J-V curves of the conventional MAPbI₃-based PSC and the corresponding photovoltaic parameters, such as open-circuit voltage (Voc), short-circuit current density (Jsc), fill factor (FF) and PCE, are summarized in the figure. Both backward (from Voc to Jsc) and forward (from Jsc to Voc) voltage scanning directions were used for the J-V measurement to study the hysteresis effect. A small hysteresis is observed in the J-V curves of the MAPbI₃-based PSC. An impressive PCE of 11.3% was obtained under the backward voltage scanning direction, whereas a slightly lower PCE of 10.7% was achieved under the forward voltage scanning direction.

In contrast, the photovoltaic performance of the MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -based PSC was significantly improved and neglectable hysteresis is observed. A PCE as high as 16.2% and 16.1% was achieved for the backward and forward voltage scanning direction, respectively, as shown in Fig. 3e. The improved PCE of the MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -based PSC mainly attributes to the higher  $ J_{sc} $  and FF, which is largely affected by the interfacial properties in the device and that will be discussed in the following part. The statistical PCE for the MAPbI $ _3 $ -based and MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -based PSCs was calculated from 16 devices (Fig. 3f). The statistics of photovoltaic parameters are summarized according to the literature.  $ ^{45-47} $  The result shows that both PSCs have high reproducibility and the MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -based PSC gives relative higher PCE value. The statistic results of the  $ J_{sc} $ , FF, and  $ V_{oc} $  of the MAPbI $ _3 $ -based PSC and MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -based PSC is shown in Fig. S5 (ESI). The MAPbI $ _3 $ -based PSC with different organic HTMs, such as Spiro-OMeTAD and poly(3-hexylthiophene-2,5-diyl) (P3HT), was also

fabricated for comparison (Fig. S6 ESI). After used organic HTM in the PSC, the device photovoltaic performance is largely improved, but the device efficiency is still lower than that of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $ -based PSC.

Fig. 3g presents the incident photo-to-current efficiency (IPCE) spectra of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -based and  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $ -based PSCs and the corresponding integrated photocurrent is shown in Fig. S7 (ESI). The  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $ -based PSC has a slightly higher IPCE value than that of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -based PSC in the wavelength region of  $ 350 - 750~\mathrm{nm} $ , which may result in a higher  $ J_{sc} $  value in the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $ -based PSC comparing to that of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -based PSC. In addition, a small blue-shift in the IPCE spectra was observed for the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $ -based PSC that is in agreement with the light absorption spectrum of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $ -PSS (Fig. 2c).

Device stability is one of the crucial factors to evaluate the overall performance of a solar cell device. Therefore, the stability of the MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -based PSC was measured under both continuous illumination and storage under dark condition. The steady-state efficiency of the MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -based PSC at the maximum power point (MPP) was tracked under continuous AM1.5G 100 mW/cm $ ^2 $  illumination provided by a solar simulator (Fig. 3h). The photovoltage and photocurrent under the MPP tracking are presented in Fig. S8 (ESI). None of any significant degradation in the efficiency is observed during the photostability test, whereas the device efficiency is slightly increasing with the illumination. During the photostability test, the illumination was blocked by a mechanical shutter letting the device under the dark condition for several minutes, and after that time the shutter was removed again letting the device under the illumination again. The device exhibits a fast light response and quickly returns to the original efficiency value of before blocking the illumination. The unencapsulated PCSs were stored under the dark condition and the device efficiency was measured again after an interval of days. Fig. 3i shows the normalized PCE of both MAPbI $ _3 $ -based and MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $  based PSCs as a function of the storage time. The unencapsulated PCSs were stored under the dark condition (23 °C and ~30% humanity) and the device efficiency was measured again after an interval of days. It is encouraging that the MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -based PSC maintains ~85% of its initial PCE value, whereas the MAPbI $ _3 $ -based PSC only remains ~50% of its original PCE value after storage for 55 days. These important results indicate that the MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $  perovskite stacking layer in the PSC could significantly improve the stability of the carbon electrode-based PSC. Therefore, we conclude here that the PSC with the PSS in the device exhibits superior photovoltaic performance compared with that of the conventional MAPbI $ _3 $ -based PSCs. Meanwhile, the perovskite stacking structured PSC shows good stability under both continuous illumination and storage under the dark condition.

# 2.4. Charger carrier dynamics.

As described, the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $  perovskite stacking layer may affect the charger carrier dynamics in the perovskite, which will provide more insights to comprehensively understand the transportation and recombination of the photoinduced charge carriers. Therefore, the steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra and time-resolved PL decay were measured to obtain more information in terms of the charge transport mechanism at the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbl}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $  perovskite interface. The perovskite layer was prepared on the microscope slide for the PL spectra measurement, and an incident excitation light with a wavelength of  $ 550~\mathrm{nm} $  was applied to excite the perovskite sample. The PL intensity of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbl}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $ -PSS sample is significantly decreased compared to that of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite sample (Fig. 4a), which may due to the photoinduced charge was efficiently quenched by the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $  perovskite stacking layer in the PSS. The PL peak with a blue shift of  $ \sim 19~\mathrm{nm} $  is observed for the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbl}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $ -PSS sample compared with that of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite sample (Fig. S9, ESI), ascribing to the higher bandgap energy of the PSS (Fig. S2). The results of the PL spectra are in good accordance with the results of the light absorption of the perovskite layer and the IPCE spectra of the PSC device.

Even though the detailed mechanism of such band edge shift is not exactly known currently, there may be two causes leading to

the band edge shift: (1) After prepared the  $ \mathrm{MAPbBr}_x\mathrm{l}_{3 - x} $  on the top of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  layer forming the double-layered structure, the Br element may diffuse into the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite layer forming an uniform  $ \mathrm{MAPbBr}_x\mathrm{l}_{3 - x} $  perovskite instead of the double-layered structure, which results in the band edge shift compared with that of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite. However such hypothesis is in contradiction with the element analysis with TOF-SIMS that the Br element is mainly distributing on the top of the perovskite layer with a thickness of  $ \sim 75~\mathrm{nm} $  (Fig. S4). (2) The coupling of such two layers may be too strong, and the band-edge state may be mixed leading to a single PL emission. The PL mainly comes from the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite layer in the double-layered perovskite. Otherwise, the two PL peak or a broad PL peak should be observed. We think that the photoinduced electron in the  $ \mathrm{MAPbBr}_x\mathrm{l}_{3 - x} $  layer may efficiently inject into  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite layer and the photoinduced hole in the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite layer injects into  $ \mathrm{MAPbBr}_x\mathrm{l}_{3 - x} $  perovskite layer. Therefore the  $ \mathrm{MAPbBr}_x\mathrm{l}_{3 - x} $  somehow works as an electron blocking layer (or hole transfer layer) in the solar cell.

When the solar cell device is under the illumination at the open-circuit condition, a photovoltage is formed across the full device. However, as the illumination is removed, the photoinduced charge carrier will be recombined, resulting in a   $  V_{oc}  $   decay (or TPV). Therefore, the   $  V_{oc}  $   decay could offer more information regarding the charge extraction that a slower   $  V_{oc}  $   decay generally results from a slow charge recombination rate in the solar cell device.  $  ^{50}  $   Fig. 4d shows the normalized TPV curves of the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  -based and MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  /MAPbI  $  _{x}  $  Br  $  _{3-x}  $  -based PSC and the charge recombination time (or called electron lifetime) can be extracted from the TPV.  $  ^{50}  $   The electron lifetime as a function of Voc is shown in Fig. S10 (EIS). The result reveals that a longer electron lifetime was calculated for the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  /MAPbI  $  _{x}  $  Br  $  _{3-x}  $  -based PSC, suggesting more charge carriers could be extracted from the perovskite layer. Therefore, these results confirm that the MAPbI  $  _{x}  $  Br  $  _{3-x}  $   perovskite stacking layer covered on the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite layer forming PSS could largely improve the charge extraction in the solar cell device, leading to diminished charge interfacial recombination at the perovskite/carbon electrode interface.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy measurements (EIS) were also conducted to investigate the internal charge transfer behavior in the MAPbI $ _3 $ -based and MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -based PSCs (Fig. S11, ESI). The Nyquist plots of the PSCs show a semicircle at high frequency, which is related to the perovskite/carbon interface. $ ^{24} $  Compared with the MAPbI $ _3 $ -based PSC, the MAPbI $ _3 $ /MAPbI $ _x $ Br $ _{3-x} $ -based PSC shows a lower charge transfer resistance (R $ _{ct} $ ), implying a faster carrier transporting and a better contact at the perovskite/carbon interface, which is consistent with the results obtained from TPC and TPV.

# 3. Discussion

For a solar cell device, the photovoltaic performance is directly determined by the charge extraction, which is dominated by the materials' optoelectronic properties, energy band structure and device architecture.  $  ^{51-53}  $   Based on the above results we further propose the mechanism of the improved photovoltaic performance in the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  /MAPbI  $  _{x}  $  Br  $  _{3-x}  $  -based PSC induced by the additional MAPbI  $  _{x}  $  Br  $  _{3-x}  $   perovskite stacking layer in the PSC. Considering the results from the light absorption and XPS analysis, the energy levels of each material are schematically presented in Fig. 5a. As discussed in the XPS section, the core levels of Pb 4f  $  _{7/2}  $   and I 3d  $  _{5/2}  $   shows upward shift of ~0.31 eV after deposited MAPbI  $  _{x}  $  Br  $  _{3-x}  $   perovskite stacking layer on the top of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite layer forming the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  /MAPbI  $  _{x}  $  Br  $  _{3-x}  $  -PSS. Meanwhile, by combining the VB spectra (Fig. 2g) and Tauc plot (Fig. S2, ESI), the VBM of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite and MAPbI  $  _{x}  $  Br  $  _{3-x}  $   perovskite is ~1.40 and ~1.17 eV below the Fermi level, and the CBM is ~0.21 and ~0.46 eV above the Fermi level, respectively.  $  ^{44,54}  $   The upshift of the core level (~0.31 eV) and VBM (~0.23 eV) shows the same band bending trend at the interface of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  /MAPbI  $  _{x}  $  Br  $  _{3-x}  $   perovskite.

In-situ growth an additional perovskite layer forming a bilayer perovskite structure was proven as an efficient way to improve the photovoltaic performance of the gold electrode-based PSC.  $  ^{56-58}  $   Comparing with the reported carbon electrode-based PSC,  $  ^{59-63}  $   none of any hole conductors was used in this work and the MAPbI  $  _{x}  $  Br  $  _{3-x}  $   perovskite stacking layer somehow works as an electron blocking layer (or HTM) in the PSC. These encouraging results indicate that the interfacial properties within the carbon electrode-based PSC are significantly desired to be optimized for fine controlling the device operation. However, it is worth to note that despite the improved interfacial properties were obtained in the carbon electrode-based PSC through in-situ growth of an additional perovskite stacking layer, the charge interfacial recombination at the perovskite/carbon-electrode interface may also somewhat remain. Therefore, further studies may be needed to further understand the effect of Br ion migration and further control the interface properties in the PSC toward further improving the device photovoltaic performance and stability.

# 4. Conclusion

In conclusion, the perovskite stacking structure was successfully developed to improve the performance of the carbon electrode-based hole conductor free PSCs. By incorporating an additional  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $  perovskite stacking layer at the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite/carbon electrode interface, a favorable energy level alignment was formed within the PSC device. The perovskite stacking structured PSC is among in the highest efficiency of the carbon electrode-based hole conductor free PSC results from significantly diminished charge interfacial recombination at the perovskite/carbon electrode interface. Meanwhile, the perovskite stacking structured PSC shows good stability under both continuous illumination and storage under dark condition. This work may provide a new avenue to fine control the interfacial properties in the PSC toward higher photovoltaic performance and stability.

# 5. Experimental Section

Materials: Titanium (IV) isopropoxide (97%), isopropanol (anhydrous, 99.5%), N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, anhydrous, 99.8%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, anhydrous, 99.8%), chlorobenzene (anhydrous, 99.8%) were provided by Sigma-Aldrich. Methylammonium iodide (MAI) and methylammonium bromide (MABr) were purchased from Dyesol. Lead iodide (PbI  $  _{2}  $  ) was obtained from TCI. Other solvents were obtained from Aladdin. The commercial carbon paste was bought from Shanghai Materwin New Materials Co., Ltd. All chemicals were used without any further purification.

Solar Cell Fabrication: The patterned FTO glass substrate was

cleaned sequentially with water, acetone, and ethanol using an ultrasonic water bath. Subsequently, a compact TiO  $  _{2}  $   layer was deposited on the FTO substrate by spray pyrolysis of a precursor solution containing 0.2 M titanium isopropoxide and 2 M acetylacetone in anhydrous isopropanol under 500 °C. The diluted commercial TiO  $  _{2}  $   paste (Dyesol paste, 30NRD) with ethanol was spin-coated on the compact TiO  $  _{2}  $   layer at 4000 rpm and then sintered under 500 °C for 30 min. After cooling down to the room temperature, the substrate coated with the mesoporous TiO  $  _{2}  $   layer was immersed in a TiCl  $  _{4}  $   aqueous solution (40 mM) at 70 °C for 30 min, following sintered again at 500°C.

The preparation of the perovskite precursor and perovskite layer was carried out in the glovebox. Briefly, the perovskite precursor solution with excess  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  was prepared by mixed  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  (1.05 M), MAI (1 M) in a solvent of DMF:DMSO=4:1 (volume ratio). The precursor solution was then spin-coated onto the top of  $ \mathrm{TiO}_2 $ -coated FTO substrate by continuous two-step procedure at 1000 and 4000 rpm for 10 and 30 s, respectively. During the second spin-coating step,  $ 150~\mu \mathrm{l} $  anhydrous chlorobenzene was quickly injected on the rotating substrate 15 s before the end of the process. Then the sample was annealed on a hot plate at  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 15\mathrm{min} $ . For the preparation of the additional  $ \mathrm{MAPbI_xBr_{3 - x}} $  perovskite stacking layer,  $ 100~\mu \mathrm{l} $  of MABr isopropanol solution (5 mg/ml) were spin-coated onto the prepared  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite film with excess  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  at 4000 rpm for 30 s and annealed on a hot plate at  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 15\mathrm{min} $ . For HTM based devices,  $ 70~\mathrm{mM} $  spiro-OMeTAD in chlorobenzene solution and  $ 15\mathrm{mg / ml} $  P3HT in chlorobenzene solution was deposited on the MAPbI3 perovskite film by spin coating g at 3000rpm for 30s, respectively. The spiro-OMeTAD solution was doped at a molar ratio of 0.5, 0.03 and 3.3 with bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)-imide lithium salt (Li-TFSI), tris(2-(1H-pyrazole-1yl)-4-tert-butylpyridine) cobalt(III) tris-(bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)-imide) (FK209) and 4-tert-butylpyridine (TBP), respectively. Finally, the carbon paste was doctor-bladed on the top of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI_xBr_{3 - x}} $  perovskite layer and followed drying at  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 20\mathrm{min} $  to remove the solvent in the carbon paste to complete the PSC device.

Material Characterization: SEM images were measured using a scanning electron microscopy (JEOL-7500). EDS was measured using an energy dispersive spectrometer (OXFORD X-Max 50). X-ray diffraction pattern was recorded using a Rigaku D/max2500 in an angle range of 10-45° with a Cu Kα radiation. The light absorption spectrum was measured using a UV-VIS-NIR spectrophotometer (UV-3600). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (Thermo Scientific Escalab 250Xi US) was applied to test chemical states and the valence band of the perovskite layer, which was prepared on the FTO glass substrate. Time of Flight Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry (Germany ION TOF 5) was used to detect the distribution of Br element with depth. The steady-state PL spectrum was tested using the Fluorolog 3 Spectrophotometer (HORIBA JOBIN YVON) with an excitation wavelength of 550 nm and a cut-off filter (575 nm) was applied to block the scattered excitation light. TCSPC measurement was performed by means of a picosecond diode laser (EPL 470) of 470.4 nm as excitation light. A cut-off filter (OG 570 nm) was applied to block the scattered excitation light during the measurement. TAC (Ortec 566, 50 ns) was read by DAQ-1 MCA computer card using 4096 channels and collected with Horiba Jobin Yvon DataStation 2.3.
