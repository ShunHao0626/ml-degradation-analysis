# GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

# ABSTRACT

The poor long-term stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) tremendously hampers their future commercialization though their superior photovoltaic efficiencies. To enhance the device stability, a new poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):sulfonated acetone-formaldehyde (PEDOT:SAF) with higher PEDOT content (2:1) is developed considering the excellent dispersing capacity of SAF. PEDOT:SAF exhibits extremely lower acidity with pH value of 6 and higher conductivity of 3.12 S/cm comparing with the former reported sample with lower PEDOT content. Moreover, PEDOT:SAF film shows superior ultraviolet (UV) absorptivity originated from the fluorescence effect of SAF and unexceptionable film waterproofness on account of the high PEDOT content. As a result, the PSC incorporating PEDOT:SAF as the hole extraction layer (HEL) achieves higher power conversion efficiency (PCE) and highly enhanced device stability than the traditional PEDOT:PSS-based device. After 28 days of storage time, our device retains 83.2% from its original PCE, while almost half-degradation is experienced in the PEDOT:PSS controlled device. In addition, SAF is renewable with more simple and inexpensive preparation than that of PSS. Undoubtedly, this new PEDOT:SAF provides us a scaffold for designing stable PSC, and this platform is also shared in other photovoltaic technologies.

# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been regarded as one of the promising next-generation photovoltaic devices due to their advantages of high photovoltaic efficiency, simple preparation and low cost. Research on the materials and devices of PSCs was tempestuously emerging over the past few years   $  [1-5]  $  . In 2009, organometal halide perovskites   $  \left(\mathrm{CH}_{3}\mathrm{NH}_{3}\mathrm{PbBr}_{3} \text{ and } \mathrm{CH}_{3}\mathrm{NH}_{3}\mathrm{PbI}_{3}\right)  $   was firstly introduced as the light absorber to fabricate PSC with a power conversion efficiencies (PCE) of 3.81% by T. Miyasaka's group   $  [1]  $  . Subsequently, the PCE record of PSCs has been constantly refreshed and reached to over 22% in four years to date   $  [6-12]  $  . However, the stability of PSC devices still faces huge challenges in order to realize its future outdoor applications. Several problems are concerned about the devices stability, including the effects of ambient moisture and oxygen, ultraviolet irradiation, thermal degradation and interplay between the layers   $  [13-15]  $  .

The device stability of solar cells, especially under ambient humidity and ultraviolet radiation, is one of the main issues recently addressed by the photovoltaic scientific community. It is reported that water can diffuse into the device via the hygroscopic PEDOT:PSS buffer layer and metal electrodes, then lower the device lifetime [16]. Although the solar cell is to convert light into electricity, some devices are sensitive to UV irradiation due to the photochemical/physical degradation of the active layer [17]. Massive efforts have been endeavoured to enhance the device stability of solar cells, especially on PSCs, through reducing the humidity and radiation effects. Recently, M. Grätzel's group obtained long-term stable PSCs by coating a top-layer of photocurable fluoropolymers to form a strongly hydrophobic barrier toward environmental moisture and absorb ultraviolet then re-emit light in the visible range [18]. Similarly, F. Bella et al. reported a new design paradigm for stable photoelectrochromic devices under real outdoor operating conditions by incorporating a photocurable fluoropolymeric system as external protective coating film with easy-cleaning and UV-shielding functionalities [19]. Y. Yang's group reported the highly improved PSC stability against water and oxygen degradation with a new solution-processed metal oxide transport layers [20]. Z. Wei et al. designed a new carbon-based composite (C+ epoxy/Ag-paint) cathode that endowed PSCs with excellent waterproof performance and high PCE [21]. Meanwhile, many other ameliorative techniques have been developed to enhance the device stability focusing on different aspects [22–24]. Nevertheless, relatively less attention has been paid to improve device stability by ameliorating poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):

poly(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS) as it is a conventionally used anode modified material [25,26].

PEDOT:PSS was widely studied and used as anode modifier in organic electronic (OE) devices due to its superior film-forming property, light permeability and suitable work function   $  [27]  $  . Spin-coating a PEDOT:PSS layer onto indium tin oxide (ITO) surface can effectively improve the device performance because it can reduce the hole transport energy barrier and planarize the ITO surface   $  [28–30]  $  . However, there are some unfavourable properties of PEDOT:PSS which limit the further improvement on the efficiency and stability of PSCs: (1) the high acidity of PEDOT:PSS (  $  pH \approx 2  $  ) corrodes adjacent layers including ITO anode and perovskite layer, and hence accelerates device degradation; (2) the structural/electrical inhomogeneity caused by the insulated PSS; (3) the high hydrophilic nature facilitates the degradation of devices toward environmental moisture   $  [13]  $  . Many methods of improvement have been reported focusing on different photovoltaic devices, such as self-doped conjugated polyelectrolyte or additive treatment of PEDOT:PSS   $  [31–34]  $  . Considering that the drawbacks of PEDOT:PSS mentioned above are almost all produced by PSS, our group focuses on seeking breakthrough from the novel dopant design of PEDOT   $  [35–39]  $  . We proposed and applied semiconductive dopants, such as lignosulfonate, as alternative of PSS to reduce acidity and enhance conductivity   $  [35–37]  $  . More importantly, in our most recently published work, we provided and summarized a map for the dopant design. Using methylnaphthalene sulfonate formaldehyde (MNSF) resin to dope PEDOT as the HEL of highly efficient PSC, we confirmed that the branched MNSF chemical structure improved the film structural/electrical homogeneity   $  [39]  $  . However, the stability of these devices using novel PEDOT as the anode modifier still need systematical studies.

Herein, based on the previous research, a new PEDOT-based HEL using sulfonated acetone-formaldehyde (SAF) as the dopant of PEDOT with specific lower SAF doping ratio was designed to enhance the PSCs stability [38]. SAF is one of the second generation high-performance water-reducing agent of cement in the construction industry [40,41]. Moreover, due to the raw materials of SAF can be obtained from biomass fermentation, it is more sustainable, inexpensive and with simple preparation process than that of PSS (Scheme 1). Motivated by its excellent dispersing performance with tunable sulfonic acid group and linear structure with small molecular space size, SAF was attempted to disperse PEDOT by solution-process in aqueous [38]. Through regulating the PEDOT/SAF mass ratio from 1:2 to 2:1, we successfully obtained a highly conductive PEDOT:SAF with extremely low acidity.

Moreover, the high doping ration of PEDOT can reduce the hydrophilia of HEL and further alleviate the effect from ambient water for the PSC device. Interestingly, SAF is a reported unconventional luminogen with strong green fluorescence and aggregation-enhanced emission (AEE) property   $  [42,43]  $  . It was reported that reducing the effect of ultraviolet irradiation can effectively enhance the device stability of PSCs   $  [18,19]  $  . Thus, the powerful UV-absorptivity and re-emitted visible light of SAF within the HEL will contribute to relieve the UV-degradation towards perovskite layer and further stabilize the device. Consequently, The PSC device using the new PEDOT:SAF as the HEL achieved an excellent device performance with higher PCE of 14.05% comparing with the PEDOT:PSS-based device. # 2.2. Acidity and conductivity of PEDOT:SAF

As we known, the high acidity of PEDOT:PSS is a typical drawback for the performance of OE devices due to its corrosion effect on ITO anode and active layer. The acidity of PEDOT:PSS and PEDOT:SAF dispersion were studied and vividly displayed on pH paper as shown in Fig. S4a. The testing concentration of PEDOT:SAF was 1.2 wt% which achieved the highest device efficiency. After comparing with standard color card, it is obviously that the pH values of PEDOT:PSS (red color) and PEDOT:SAF (jade-green color) are around 2 and 6, respectively. The acidic property must be caused by the sulfonic acid groups within the composites, which is relevant to the doping ratio and sulfonation degree of the dopants. In term of the doping ration, PEDOT:SAF involves low doping ration of SAF (EDOT:SAF = 2:1), while the doping ration of PSS for PEDOT:PSS (PVPAI 4083) is 1:6 (Table 1). From element analysis results in Fig. S4b, the S content of SAF (8.24%) is just half of PSS (17.39% deduced from its regular structure). Moreover, although the SAF possesses much lower S content, PEDOT:SAF involves higher S content of 15.99% than that of PEDOT:PSS (13.20%). There are two different sulfur atoms within thiophene ring and sulfonate from PEDOT and dopants like PSS or SAF, respectively. It means that PEDOT:SAF possesses much higher content of PEDOT against dopant, matching with the lower doping ratio of SAF. Therefore, from these two sides, the low acidity of PEDOT:SAF owes to its both low doping ratio and sulfonation degree of SAF. The extremely low acidity of PEDOT:SAF can overcome the device degradation caused by highly acidic PEDOT:PSS when using them as the HELs of PSCs.

Besides the acidity, the high PEDOT content will greatly enhance the conductivity of PEDOT:SAF film as the dopants like PSS and SAF both are non-conducting polymer. The conductivity of PEDOT:PSS and PEDOT:SAF films with specific film thickness were tested in a four point probes measurement system. By this method, the low conductivity of PEDOT:PSS could not be detected with the film thickness around  $ 1.0\mu \mathrm{m} $ , so it was tested under a thicker film (Table S1). Meanwhile, the huge distinction of the sheet resistance between these two films also reflected their different conductive ability. # 2.4. Waterproofness of PEDOT:SAF

Besides the UV radiation, ambient moisture also is a key influential factor for the poor device stability of PSCs. Thus, we strived to investigate the hygroscopicity of the PEDOT:SAF film. Water erosion experiment and a combination of measurements including morphology image, XPS (S2p) analysis, particle size analysis and static contact angle were conducted and the results were shown in Fig. 2.

For revealing the different water erosion effects to the HELs, waterproof experiment was conducted as displayed in Fig. 2a. PEDOT:PSS and PEDOT:SAF films was prepared by dropping their dispersions onto the ITO treated substrate and drying under room temperature. By investigating the change of their films after being put into the different petri dishes containing ultrapure water, the effect of water erosion can be vividly observed from the photos. When putting the PEDOT:PSS film into water, the film edges were rapidly destroyed and the whole film presented loose state with slight spread after  $ 2\mathrm{min} $ . This loose film was easily spread out by slightly shake the petri dish and a bare ITO glass was left. However, there was almost no change happened on PEDOT:SAF film under the same condition, even after long-term observation about one week. This distinct phenomenon obviously revealed the drastic erosion effect of water towards PEDOT:PSS film and the better waterproof ability of PEDOT:SAF film. Moreover, acidoresistance of PEDO:SAF film was also tested by adding hydrochloric acid into the soaking water for adjusting the pH value to 1. After a more week of exposure time in strong acidic condition, the PEDOT:SAF film still showed almost no change. Moreover, through stripping the film

# 3. Conclusion

In this work, in order to improve the device performance and stability of PSCs from the perspective of HEL, a new water-soluble PEDOT:SAF with higher PEDOT content (EDOT:SAF = 2:1) was prepared. PEDOT:SAF showed extremely lower acidity with pH value at around 6 and much higher conductivity of 3.12 S/cm, more than 200 times to conventional PEDOT:PSS-4083. PEDOT:SAF-based PSC achieved an impressive PCE of 14.05% which is better than PEDOT:PSS controlled PSC (13.31%). More significantly, the PEDOT:SAF-based PSC showed highly enhanced device stability with 83.2% retention from its initial PCE after 28 days of storage

time, while PEDOT:PSS-based PSC showed almost half decline on PCE. The superior properties of PEDOT:SAF especially the excellent UV-absorptivity and waterproofness were regard as the decisive factors for the enhanced device stability. Moreover, the pervoskite property on HELs also showed that PEDOT:SAF layer was beneficial to the formation of pervoskite crystals and in favour of charge transfer. In addition, the inexpensive raw materials of formaldehyde and acetone, simple preparation of SAF make PEDOT:SAF be a promising HEM for OE devices. More importantly, our results provide researchers a prospective scaffold, as well as novel design concept to enhance the potentially applicable device stability of photovoltaic technologies via PEDOT modifications in future.

Materials: PEDOT:PSS (Baytron PVPAI 4083) used for comparison was supplied by Heraeus Holding GmbH [6,6].-phenyl-C61-butyric acidmethylester (PCBM) and bathocuproine (BCP) were bought from Nano-C. Preparation of SAF and PEDOT:SAF: SAF was synthesized by polymerization and sulfonation of formaldehyde, acetone and sodium sulphite (molar ratio 2:1:0.6) in alkaline condition. 8.2 g of   $  Na_{2}SO_{3}  $   solid was dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water at 45 °C and adjusting its pH to 10 by NaOH (20%). Then, 16 mL of formaldehyde (37%) and 8.0 mL of acetone were added rapidly. The

polymerization was stopped after 5 h of reflux at 85 °C and the mixture became brownness. The residual reactants were removed by vacuum rotary evaporation, and its pH value was adjusted to 2–3 by addition of HCl (20%). The solid content of the obtained raw product which will be directly used to prepare PEDOT:SAF is 14.5%. PEDOT:SAF aqueous dispersion was prepared using the unpurified SAF liquor as follows. 5.0 g of SAF liquor and 1.0 g of EDOT were added into 50 mL deionized water (mass ratio of EDOT:SAF was 2:1). After stirring slowly for 10 min, the pH of the solution was adjusted to 2 by HCl (20%). Then, 1.9 g of APS used as oxidant was dissolved in water (5 mL) and added in drops. The reaction was kept under high stirring speed (700 rps) and room temperature after the EDOT monomers being completely reacted (about 48 h). For the following characterization, the products were purified by using a dialysis membrane (MWCO 1000 Da) for 5–7 days. The mass fraction of PEDOT:SAF was concentrated to more than 1.2 wt% by rotary evaporation quantified by UV absorption at the wavelength of 800 nm.

Characterization of SAF and PEDOT:SAF: FTIR spectra were performed using the KBr pellets in the 4000-400 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  region by Auto system XL/I-series/Spectrum 2000 spectrometry (Thermo Nicolet Co., Madison, WI, USA). Element contents of C, H and S were rationed by Vario EL cube (Elementar, Germany) with about 5.0 mg packed in aluminized paper. UV–vis absorption and transmittance spectra were measured by Shimadzu UV-3600 spectrophotometer (Japan) and Shimadzu UV-2600 spectrophotometer (Japan), respectively. Photoluminescence (PL) spectra were detected on F-4500 fluorescence spectrometer (Hitachi, Japan) with photomultiplier tube voltage of 400 V. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) experiments were performed on a Zeta PALS instrument (Brookhaver, America). Cyclic voltammetry (CV) test was conducted with a film on glassy carbon electrode against Ag/AgCl (3 M KCl solution) reference electrode at scanning rate 100 mV/s. The conductivity and sheet resistance of PEDOT:PSS and PEDOT:SAF films were measured with a KDY-1four point probes resistivity/resistance measurement system. The films were prepared by dropping the sample on glass and air drying at room temperature. The films thicknesses were tested by a step profiler (Dektak150, Veeco, USA). UPS and XPS measurements were conducted on a Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi with a He(I) UV source (21.22 eV) in ultrahigh vacuum. For testing UPS and XPS, the samples were spin-coated onto ITO glass and kept in an oven at 110 °C for 15 min before the measurement, and all of the experimental processes were conducted in ultrahigh vacuum environment. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) images (height and phase images) were observed by a Park XE-100 in tapping mode. Surface wettability was measured using a static contact angle instrument (Powereach JC2000 C1, Shanghai, China).

Fabrication and Characterization of PSCs: The configurations of PSCs were ITO/HELs/CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ PbI $ _{3-x} $ Cl $ _x $ /PC $ _{61} $ BM/BCP/Ag. ITO-coated glass substrates were cleaned via a series of ultrasonication in detergent, acetone, DI water, isopropyl alcohol and followed by UV-ozone plasma treatment. PEDOT:SAF layer was spin-coated onto the pre-patterned ITO glass substrate and annealed using a hot plate at 140 °C for 15 min to remove residual solvents. PEDOT:PSS (Baytron PVPAI 4083) based device was also fabricated as comparison with the same method. The substrates with PEDOT:SAF or PEDOT:PSS were then transferred into a glove box filled with highly pure N $ _2 $ . Then MAPbI $ _{3-x} $ Cl $ _x $  precursor solution (1.26 M PbI $ _2 $ , 0.14 M PbCl $ _2 $  and 1.35 M MAI in cosolvent of DMSO:GBL at vol ratio of 3:7) was spin-coated to form a perovskite layer of about 280 nm on the modified ITO substrate. After annealing at 100 °C for 20 min, the PCBM layer (~55 nm) was deposited by spin coating onto the surface of perovskite layer. Then, 0.5 mg/mL BCP solution was spin coated onto PCBM layer. The devices were completed by thermal

deposition a layer of 100 nm Ag as cathode in a vacuum of   $  <1 \times 10^{-6}  $   Torr. The devices area was   $  0.07 \, cm^2  $   defined by shadow mask. The photovoltaic performance of the PSCs was tested in air with a computer-programmed Keithley 2400 source/meter and a Newport's Oriel class solar simulator, which simulated the AM1.5 sunlight with energy density of   $  100 \, mW/cm^2  $   and was certified to the JIS C 8912 standard. IPCEs of PSCs were measured with a 300 W Xenon Lamp (Oriel 6258) and a Cornerstone 260 Oriel 74125 monochromator. The photovoltaic stability of PSCs was investigated by storing the unencapsulated devices in glovebox with humidity below 0.1 ppm and normal room light for 28 days. For the perovskite layer, UV–visible absorption spectra of were measured on a Perkin-Elmer Lambda 950 spectrophotometer. Photoluminescence spectra were collected on an Edinburgh Instruments FLS920 spectrofluorometer, the excitation wavelength was 630 nm. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained on a JSM-7800F SEM. Thin film X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were conducted on a Bruker D8 Advance XRD instrument.
