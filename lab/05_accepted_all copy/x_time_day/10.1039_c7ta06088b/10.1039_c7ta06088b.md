# $ \mathrm{Cu}_{2 - x}\mathrm{GeS}_3 $  : A New Hole Transporting Material for Stable and Efficient Perovskite Solar Cell

Xin Jin,  $  ^{a}  $   Xunyong Lei,  $  ^{b}  $   Chunyan Wu,  $  ^{a}  $   Guoshun Jiang,  $  ^{a}  $   Weifeng Liu,  $  ^{a}  $   Hualing Zeng,  $  ^{*b}  $   Tao Chen,  $  ^{*a}  $   Changfei Zhu  $  ^{*a}  $

$ \mathrm{Cu}_{2-x}\mathrm{GeS}_3 $  is a kind of inorganic semiconducting compound with earth abundant elemental storage, excellent chemical stability and environmental friendly characteristics. It has been used as light absorption material in traditional p-n junction thin film solar cell. This research presents that  $ \mathrm{Cu}_{2-x}\mathrm{GeS}_3 $  can also serve as highly efficient hole transporting material for perovskite solar cells with enhanced stability. The results show that perovskite solar cell based on  $ \mathrm{Cu}_{2-x}\mathrm{GeS}_3 $  film can retain  $ 95\% $  power conversion efficiency when exposing to  $ 50\% $  relative humidity for 30 days in air under room lamp light irradiation, while the device using spiro-OMeTAD as hole transporting material degrades considerably in 2 days and nearly completely degrades in 10 days. Mechanistic investigation shows that the inherent stability and hydrophobic nature of  $ \mathrm{Cu}_{2-x}\mathrm{GeS}_3 $  film contribute to the enhanced moisture stability. This investigation provides a material choice for high-efficiency perovskite solar cells with enhanced moisture stability.

# Introduction

Since the initial utilization of organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite in solar cell in 2009,  $  ^{1}  $  the power conversion efficiency (PCE) has surpassed 20%.  $  ^{2-4}  $  For a practical solar cell, it should simultaneously meet the requirements of high efficiency, low cost and long-term stability. A major concern thus far regarding perovskite solar cell (PSC) is the durability.  $  ^{5-7}  $  It has been realized that organic-inorganic hybrid perovskites suffer from rapid degradation upon exposure to moisture, leading to very short device lifetime.  $  ^{8,9}  $  In general, there are two approaches to improve the moisture stability. One is to engineer the crystal structures of perovskite to improve intrinsic stability.  $  ^{10}  $  For example, by substituting  $  CH_{3}NH_{3}^{+}  $  (MA) in MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  for formamidinium (FA),  $  Cs^{+}  $  or  $  Rb^{+}  $  ion could considerably enhance the moisture stability.  $  ^{11-13}  $  The other strategy is to use stable electron and hole transporting materials (ETMs, HTMs) to protect perovskite absorbing materials from moisture invasion. The ETMs are generally inorganic metal oxides such as  $  TiO_{2}  $  and  $  SnO_{2}  $ ,  $  ^{14,15}  $  possessing high efficiency and good stability. In comparison, the organic p-type 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-di-p-methoxyphenylamine)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (spiro-OMeTAD) is widely adopted as HTM in

most state-of-the-art perovskite solar cells. Nevertheless, spiro-OMeTAD possesses low hole mobility (  $  10^{-4}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $   V  $  ^{-1}  $   S  $  ^{-1}  $  ) and low conductivity (  $  10^{-5}  $   S cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) in its pristine form.  $  ^{16}  $   Doping spiro-OMeTAD with lithium-bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide (Li-TFSI) and 4-tert-butylpyridine (TBP) is the standard method to achieve appreciable mobility and conductivity.  $  ^{17}  $   Due to the highly hygroscopic nature of the lithium-based dopant, however, this treatment is accompanied by attracting water molecules into the device,  $  ^{18, 19}  $   thus speeding up the decomposition and degradation of perovskite. In addition, the spiro-OMeTAD is very expensive, posing a challenge in cost effective mass production.  $  ^{20}  $   To address this problem, inorganic materials including Cu-based compound and metal oxides have been utilized as alternative HTMs in PSCs. For example, CuI and CuSCN have been applied as HTMs in perovskite solar cell and delivered PCE of 6.0% and 18%,  $  ^{20, 21}  $

$ \mathrm{Cu}_{2 - x}\mathrm{GeS}_3 $  (CGS), as a p-type semiconductor with band gap of about  $ 1.55\mathrm{eV} $ , has been utilized as light-absorption material in the traditional p-n junction thin film solar cells.[27,28] Because of the p-type conductivity, it would also be possible for application as HTM in perovskite solar cell. In this article, we present that CGS nanoparticles (NPs) film can serve as a new kind of cost-effective HTM for perovskite solar cell. The employment of CGS yields a more favourable energy level alignment at the interface between perovskite and HTM without using additional interfacial layer. What's more, CGS nanoparticle film forms a compact layer with relatively high hydrophobicity, which impedes water molecules permeation into perovskite absorbing layer and in turn enhances the moisture stability of the devices. At a relative humidity (RH) of  $ 50\% $  in air, the device can maintain  $ 95\% $  of its initial PCE after storing for 30 days, while the device based spiro-OMeTAD HTM shows considerable degradation in 2 days.

# Results and discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of CGS Nanoparticles. Considering that organic-inorganic perovskite cannot sustain high temperature annealing, here we resort to synthesize of CGS nanoparticle firstly (Figure 1a). Afterwards, spin-coating the nanoparticle ink on top of perovskite absorber layer with

additional low annealing temperature (< 150 °C) leads to the formation of a thin CGS film (Figure 1b). In order to synthesize colloidal CGS nanoparticles, organometallic compound diaquabis(oxoacetato-O,O")germanium   $  \left[\mathrm{Ge}(\mathrm{C}_{2}\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{3})_{2}(\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O})_{2}\right]  $  , denoted as Ge-gly] was prepared as the Ge precursor according to the reported method, where germanium dioxide and glycolic acid was used as starting materials.  $  ^{29}  $   Subsequently, copper (II) chloride dihydrate   $  \left(\mathrm{CuCl}_{2}\cdot2\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O}\right)  $   and Ge-gly were placed in a three-neck flask contained oleylamine (OLA) at room temperature. The mixture was repeatedly purged by argon for three times with vigorous stirring. The obtained light-yellow solution was further heated to 210 °C and then the sulfur precursor prepared by dissolving sulfur powder in OLA was quickly injected into the flask. After 20 min, the flask was taken away from oil bath and allowed to cool down to room temperature (more details are provided in experimental section). The as-synthesized product was washed twice via repeating precipitation with isopropyl alcohol and re-dispersed in toluene to remove the excess surfactant. One of the key steps in the deposition of sulphide thin films from nanoparticle is the formation of an available ink. In addition, three aspects should be considered when choosing an appropriate solvent for dispersing the nanoparticles. (1) The solvent should have a functional group that can stabilize the nanocrystals effectively. (2) The solvent should be easily removed after coating. (3) The solvent should has good ability to wet substrate. We have tried to use many solvents (for example, toluene, 1-hexanethiol and chlorobenzene) to disperse CGS NPs, eventually the 1-hexanethiol yielded the highest device efficiencies in our study. Actually, 1-hexanethiol has moderate boiling temperature (150-154 °C), which enables detaching it from the film at a low temperature, especially with the help of vacuum evacuation. So the synthesized nanoparticle was finally dispersed in 1-hexanethiol to form a homogenous ink for the following application.

Figure 2a shows the powder XRD pattern of the as-synthesized product. Three broad diffraction peaks present at 29.14, 48.65 and 57.61° are assigned to (111), (220) and (311) lattice planes of cubic CGS, respectively.  $  ^{28}  $   In the Raman spectrum (Figure 2b), the major peaks centred at 324, 363 and 386 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   are also confirmed to stem from the vibrational modes of CGS.  $  ^{27}  $   Therefore, it can be concluded that the as-synthesized nanoparticles are pure CGS without noticeable secondary phases. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed to reveal the microstructure of CGS NPs. As shown in Figure 2c, the low-magnification TEM image reveals that CGS nanoparticles are in the monodisperse form with average diameter of about 7 nm. The high-resolution TEM (HRTEM, Figure 2d) image shows well-formed crystallinity with d-spacing of 0.31 nm, consistent with the (111) plane of the cubic CGS. Meanwhile, the corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) pattern showed in Figure 2e matches well with the structure of CGS (JCPDS 65-5562), as indicated by the diffraction spots corresponding to the (111) and (220) planes. The optical band gap of the CGS thin film was determined to be 1.53 eV from the plot of (  $  \alpha h\nu  $  )  $  ^{2}  $   versus h  $  \nu  $   (Figure 2f), which is corresponding to the reported value.  $  ^{27,30}  $

Charge Transfer Kinetics. To evaluate hole extraction ability of CGS from  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  film, we conducted steady-state photoluminescence (PL) measurement upon excitation of 514 nm (Figure 3a). In this regard, samples composed of glass/MAPbI $ _3 $ , glass/MAPbI $ _3 $ /CGS and glass/MAPbI $ _3 $ /spiro-OMeTAD were prepared. The PL emission of bare MAPbI $ _3 $  in absence of quenching layer appears at  $ 766~\mathrm{nm} $  with strong intensity (Figure 3a, black line). When CGS and spiro-OMeTAD are applied, the PL intensities are significantly suppressed (Figure 3a, red and blue line), which originate from the carrier extraction across the interface. The PL quenching ratio for CGS is slightly larger than that of spiro-OMeTAD, indicating more efficient charge transfer between  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ /CGS.

Further, time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectroscopy was used to investigate the charge transfer kinetics from  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  to spiro-OMeTAD and CGS HTLs. Figure 3b displays the TRPL spectra with fitting curves from bare perovskite and perovskite/HTM bilayers. The TRPL was recorded via time-correlated single photon counting by integrating the whole PL peak emission at  $ 766~\mathrm{nm} $ . The decay of PL intensity could be described with a biexponential equation (1):

$$
F (t) = A _ {1} \exp (\frac {- t}{\tau_ {1}}) + A _ {2} \exp (\frac {- t}{\tau_ {2}}) + B
$$

lifetime  $ (\tau_{\mathrm{ave}}) $  is estimated from the fitting parameters according to equation (2):

$$
\tau_ {a v e} = \frac {\sum A _ {i} \tau_ {i} ^ {2}}{\sum A _ {i} \tau_ {i}}

The fitted   $  A_{i}  $  ,   $  \tau_{i}  $   and   $  \tau_{ave}  $   values are listed in Table S1 for comparison. For the perovskite film deposited on glass,   $  \tau_{ave}  $   is 18.02 ns, which is comparable to the reported values.  $  ^{31,32}  $   The long decay time indicates long exciton diffusion length and low density of defects in the bulk of perovskite. By contrast, the introduction of either spiro-OMeTAD or CGS quenching layers on the surface of perovskite pronouncedly accelerate the PL decay. The   $  \tau_{ave}  $   for spiro-OMeTAD HTM decreased to 6.79 ns, while it reduced to only 4.86 ns when CGS was used as HTM. This feature indicates that hole transfer in CGS/perovskite is faster than that in spiro-OMeTAD/perovskite, which is consistent with the observation from the above steady-state PL quenching measurement, reaffirming the potential application of CGS in perovskite solar cells as a competitive HTM.

# Conclusions

In summary, for the first time we show that  $ \mathrm{Cu}_{2 - x}\mathrm{GeS}_3 $  can be an alternative hole transporting material in perovskite solar cells. Compared with the conventional spiro-OMeTAD, the  $ \mathrm{Cu}_{2 - x}\mathrm{GeS}_3 $  nanoparticle film can generate  $ 5\% $  improvement in power conversion efficiency. The improved performance stemmed from the well-matched energy level and better charge transport at the interface of perovskite/hole transporting layer. More importantly,  $ \mathrm{Cu}_{2 - x}\mathrm{GeS}_3 $  nanoparticle film is able to considerably improve the moisture stability of the perovskite solar cells, due to the inherent stability of inorganic compound and the hydrophobic effect of the nanoparticle film. Since  $ \mathrm{Cu}_{2 - x}\mathrm{GeS}_3 $  is a semiconducting material with earth-abundant and environmental friendly characteristics, the employment of  $ \mathrm{Cu}_{2 - x}\mathrm{GeS}_3 $  as hole transporting material is expected to reduce the cost and increase the long-term stability of perovskite solar cells.

# Experimental

Preparation of CGS nanoparticles: Diaquabis(oxoacetato-O,O")germanium   $  [\mathrm{Ge}(\mathrm{C}_{2}\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{3})_{2}(\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O})_{2}]  $   was prepared by adding germanium dioxide (0.52 g, 5 mmol) to an aqueous solution (100 ml) of glycolic acid (1.52 g, 20 mmol) and refluxing the acidic mixture at 115 °C for 1 h. Then the mixture was concentrated by rotary evaporator. The obtained colorless crystals was collected and then transferred to a vacuum drying oven at 100 °C for 2 days (For convenience, the product is denoted as Ge-gly). A typical synthetic procedure of CGS NCs was briefly described below. First, 1.5 mmol copper(II) chloride dihydrate and 0.75 mmol Ge-gly and 10 ml oleylamine were mixed at room temperature and cycled between vacuum and nitrogen for three times in a 100 ml three neck flask connected to a Schlenk line. Afterward, the mixture was kept at 120 °C under vacuum for 40 min with stirring and then raised to 210 °C (solution A). 4 ml oleylamine and 2.25 mmol S powder were mixed in a separate round flask at 150 °C under vacuum. When the S powder was completely dissolved, the mixture was then cooled to room temperature (solution B). Finally, solution B was injected into solution A under vigorous stirring and the reaction was maintained for 20 min. The mixture is cooled to ~100 °C, followed by adding 5 ml toluene and 40 ml isopropanol. The nanocrystals were collected by centrifugation

at 5000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant, which contained unreacted precursor and byproducts, was discarded. The precipitate was washed again by 7.5 ml toluene and 45 ml isopropanol to remove the excess oleylamine from CGS NPs.

Perovskite precursor synthesis: Methylammonium iodide was synthesized by reacting 17.4 mL methylamine (40 wt% in water) and 16.6 mL hydroiodic acid (57 wt% in water) in 250 mL round bottom flask at 0 °C for 2 h with stirring. After reaction, the white solid of MAI was recovered by rotary evaporation and then washed with diethyl ether and recrystallized twice with ethanol. The   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $   powder was collected and dried at 50 °C in a vacuum oven for 24 h. To prepare the perovskite precursor solution,   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $   and   $  PbI_{2}  $   (Aldrich) were dissolved in mixed solvent of anhydrous N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) (7:3 in volume) in 1 M concentration. The solution was stirred overnight at 60 °C and filtered with 0.22 μm PVDF filters before spin-coating.

Device fabrication: Prior to the deposition of   $  TiO_{2}  $   compact layer, the FTO substrate (15   $  \Omega  $     $  sq^{-1}  $  ) was etched with zinc powder and 2 M HCl to obtain the desired pattern. The etched FTO substrate was sequentially cleaned by ultrasonication in acetone, isopropanol and de-ionized water for 40 min each and then treated in a   $  UV/O_{3}  $   cleaner for 20 min. A dense   $  TiO_{2}  $   blocking layer of ca. 40 nm thickness was spin-coated onto FTO with a mixture solution of 140   $  \mu  $  L of titanium isopropoxide, 2 mL of ethanol, and 26   $  \mu  $  L of HCl (2 M) at 2000 rpm for 40 s, followed by annealing at 500 °C for 1 h in a muffle furnace. To deposit perovskite films, the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   solution (18   $  \mu  $  L) was first dropped onto a   $  TiO_{2}  $   coated FTO substrate (1.5 cm × 2 cm). The substrate was then spun at 4000 rpm for 30 s. During this process, anhydrous chlorobenzene (100   $  \mu  $  L) was quickly dropped onto the center of the substrate twelve seconds ahead of the end of rotation. Subsequently, the obtained films were then dried at 60 °C for 30 s and further annealed at 100 °C for 10 min to form crystallized   $  MAPbI_{3}  $  . To complete the fabrication of devices, two hole transporting materials (spiro-OMeTAD and CGS) were spin-coated onto the perovskite films, separately. For the deposition of the Spiro-OMeTAD HTL, 73.2 mg Spiro-OMeTAD was first dissolved in 1 mL chlorobenzene, then 28.8   $  \mu  $  L 4-tert-butyl pyridine and 18.8   $  \mu  $  L Li-TFSI solution (520 mg Li-TFSI in 1 mL acetonitrile) were added. The as-prepared Spiro-OMeTAD solution was spin-coated onto the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   films at 3000 rpm for 30 s. For the deposition of CGS HTL, the synthesized CGS NPs was dispersed in 1-hexanethiol to form a stable ink with a concentration of 200 mg ml  $  ^{-1}  $  . The CGS NPs ink was then spin-coated onto the perovskite films at various speed for 30 s. In experiment, we adopt a facile method to control the thickness of CGS by changing the spin speed. When the spin speed increased from 1000 rpm to 7000 rpm with a step of 1000 rpm, the thickness of CGS decreased from 390 nm to 60 nm, respectively. After that, the films were transferred into a heated vacuum chamber placed in glove box to remove the solvent. Finally, device fabrication was completed by thermally evaporating 80 nm of gold layer with a mask (well-defined area size of 0.12 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ).
