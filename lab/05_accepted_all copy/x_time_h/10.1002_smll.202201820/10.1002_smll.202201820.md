# Crosslinkable and Chelatable Organic Ligand Enables Interfaces and Grains Collaborative Passivation for Efficient and Stable Perovskite Solar Cells

Zongwen Ma, Runnan Yu, Zhiyang Xu, Guangzheng Wu, Huaizhi Gao, Ruyue Wang, Yongshuai Gong, Jing Yang, and Zhan'ao Tan*

The organic-inorganic halide perovskite solar cell (PerSC) is the state-of-the-art emerging photovoltaic technology. However, the environmental water/moisture and temperature-induced intrinsic degradation and phase transition of perovskite greatly retard the commercialization process. Herein, a dual-functional organic ligand, 4,7-bis((4-vinylbenzyl)oxy)-1,10-phenanthroline (namely, C1), with crosslinkable styrene side-chains and chelatable phenanthroline backbone, synthesized via a cost-effective Williamson reaction, is introduced for collaborative electrode interface and perovskite grain boundaries (GBs) engineering. C1 can chemically chelate with  $ \mathrm{Sn}^{4+} $  in the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  electron transport layer and  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  in the perovskite layer via coordination bonds, suppressing nonradiative recombination caused by traps/defects existing at the interface and GBs. Meanwhile, C1 enables in situ crosslinking via thermal-initiated polymerization to form a hydrophobic and stable polymer network, freezing perovskite morphology, and resisting moisture degradation. Consequently, through collaborative interface-grain engineering, the resulting PerSCs demonstrate high power conversion efficiency of  $ 24.31\% $  with excellent water/moisture and thermal stability. The findings provide new insights of collaborative interface-grain engineering via a crosslinkable and chelatable organic ligand for achieving efficient and stable PerSCs.

# 1. Introduction

Organic–inorganic halide perovskite solar cells (PerSCs) with excellent photovoltaic performances, low-cost solution processing, and attractive optoelectronic properties have emerged

as a potential green energy-generating technology, proving to be a game-changer in photovoltaics.  $  ^{[1-3]}  $   Significant efforts, including material design, thin-film growth control, and interface engineering, have been devoted to promoting device performance.  $  ^{[4-6]}  $   Within only a few years, the power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of single-junction PerSCs have been enhanced to 20% threshold.  $  ^{[7-10]}  $   Despite the leaps and bounds in efficiency, the stability of PerSCs, especially moisture/water and thermal stability, is still a severe concern, since a long-term environmental atmosphere potentially makes degradation or phase transition of the perovskite.  $  ^{[11-13]}  $   Accordingly, device instability issues must be overcome along with the improvement of the PCE of PerSCs.

# 2. Results and Discussion

A crosslinkable organic ligand (4,7-bis((4-vinylbenzyl)oxy)-1,10-phenanthroline, namely, C1) with the structures of phenanthroline and styrene groups was designed and synthesized via a cost-effective and straightforward one-step synthesis method (Figure S1 and Experimental Section, Supporting Information). Phenanthroline is a significant class of bidentate containing a macrocyclic aromatic conjugation system, which can form stable complexes with many ions, such as   $  Sn^{4+}  $   and   $  Pb^{2+}  $  . [41,42] Moreover, styrene groups enable in situ crosslinking to establish an insoluble and stable network structure. With an ambition to incorporate the dual-function of crosslinking and coordination, we chose phenanthroline as the conjugated backbone along with styrene as the functional group to construct a multifunctional molecule for suppressing uncoordinated ions defects in   $  SnO_{2}  $   and perovskite as well as enhancing long-term stability of PerSCs.

To verify the crosslinking property of C1, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (ATR-FTIR), and UV-visible absorption spectra (UV-vis) were studied. The DSC measurements revealed the thermal properties of C1 under the nitrogen atmosphere (Figure S2, Supporting Information). It can be seen that there is a broad exothermic peak from 100 to  $ 170^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  in the first heating, which is attributed to the thermal-initiated polymerization of the styrene groups. No thermal behavior can be observed during the second heating, confirming that a fast and complete thermal crosslinking reaction of C1 occurred during the first heating cycle. In addition, the almost unchanged UV-vis spectra for C-C1 measured before and after washing with chloroform (CF) demonstrate its solvent resistance property. Figure 1b and Figure S3 in the Supporting Information show the absorption spectra changes of C1 before and after CF washing for the samples treated at different temperatures (120, 140, 160, and  $ 180^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ ) for  $ 30\mathrm{min} $  to conduct crosslinking. With the increase of the treatment temperature, the UV intensity of C-C1 after CF washing is gradually closer to that before rinsing, verifying its increased crosslinking degree at higher temperature. Until  $ 180^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ , the two UV absorption curves of C-C1 before and after CF washing coincide, indicating the complete crosslinking and the excellent solvent-resistant performance of the C-C1 sample treatment at  $ 180^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 30\mathrm{min} $ . However, the treatment temperature of  $ 180^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for the crosslinking of C1 is too high to match the treatment temperature of the perovskite

absorber. To solve this problem, we employed a facile thiol-ene thermal crosslinking reaction to lower the crosslinking temperature of C1. $ ^{[32]} $  As illustrated in Figure 1c and Figure S4 in the Supporting Information, the unchanged UV-vis spectra before and after CF washing of the C1 with PETMP film (C-C1-P) treated at 120  $ ^{\circ} $ C for 10 min indicate the complete crosslinking and the outstanding solvent-resistance of C-C1-P after the treatment at a lower temperature of 120  $ ^{\circ} $ C. Afterward, ATR-FTIR measurement was performed for C1 with PETMP before and after crosslinking reaction (Figure 1d). After crosslinking, the characteristic peaks of vinyl at 903, 1675, and 3083 cm $ ^{-1} $  decrease significantly, indicating that most vinyl has reacted after the thermal treatment. Meanwhile, a characteristic peak (1738 cm $ ^{-1} $ ) of the ester group appears for the sample of crosslinked C1 with PETMP. Moreover, there is no significant characteristic peak of sulfhydryl at the position of about 2500 cm $ ^{-1} $ , proving the occurrence of facile and mild thiol-ene thermal crosslinking reaction between C1 and PETMP.

Subsequently, we investigated the strong interaction between C1 and the periodic   $  SnO_{2}  $   or the   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   by performing VASP calculations. First, the initial and optimized geometry structures of C1 and   $  SnO_{2}  $   were constructed (Figure 2a). The final optimized bonding length between   $  Sn^{4+}  $   in   $  SnO_{2}  $   and N in C1 is 2.47 Å, and the complexation energy is -3.53 eV. Negative complexation energy indicates that the adsorption process is exothermic and spontaneous at room temperature, resulting in the adsorption of the C1 molecule on the upper surface of   $  SnO_{2}  $ . Thus, the C1 molecule can inhibit the surface defects and oxygen vacancies of   $  SnO_{2}  $ , improving the device stability of PerSCs. Similarly, Figure 2b shows the final optimized structure between C1 and   $  FAPbI_{3}  $ , where the bonding length between   $  Pb^{2+}  $   in   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   and N in C1 is 3.06 Å with the complexation energy of -2.18 eV. The results indicate that C1 can react with uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ion, restraining the traps/defects existing at either surface or GBs of perovskite. Then, we carried out density functional theory calculations to study the molecule configuration and electronic cloud distribution. Figure 2c shows that the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of the C1 molecule mainly locates on intramolecular styrene groups. On the other hand, the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) primarily distributes on the skeleton of phenanthroline.

Then water contact angle measurements were conducted to confirm the coordination ability of C1 with   $  Sn^{4+}  $   (Figure 2d). C1 in dioxane solution was spin-coated onto the surface of   $  SnO_{2}  $   layer and the resulting film was rinsed by CF five times to guarantee the removal of all the unbound and physically absorbed molecules. The water contact angles of the pure   $  SnO_{2}  $   film before and after CF washing are   $  5.5^{\circ}  $   and   $  3.1^{\circ}  $  , respectively, indicating the highly hydrophilic surface of   $  SnO_{2}  $   and unchanged wetting property of the film surface after the CF washing. In contrast, the water contact angle of   $  SnO_{2}/C1  $   film is   $  57.1^{\circ}  $  , while the water contact angle on it with several times washing maintains   $  41.7^{\circ}  $  . The retained hydrophobicity of the film can be contributed to the compact attachment of C1 molecules on the   $  SnO_{2}  $   surface via coordination bonds.

According to the outstanding property of crosslinking and coordination effect of C1, we employed it as an interlayer spin-coated on   $  SnO_{2}  $  . First, ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) and UV-vis measurements revealed their energy levels and optical bandgaps (Figures S6 and S7, Supporting Information). Besides, the energy band alignment picture of different ETL and corresponding results are illustrated in Figure 3a and Table S1 in the Supporting Information. The calculated Fermi levels of pristine   $  SnO_{2}  $   and the   $  SnO_{2}  $   after being modified by C-C1 (  $  SnO_{2}/C-C1  $  ) are -4.34 and -4.29 eV, respectively. Furthermore, the LUMO of   $  SnO_{2}/C-C1  $   (-4.21 eV) is lower than the CB of perovskite, promoting the electron transfer of ETL. In contrast, the LUMO of pristine   $  SnO_{2}  $   (-3.65 eV) and   $  SnO_{2}/C-C1-P  $   (-3.88 eV) is higher than the CB of perovskite in some obstruction to the transmission of electrons.  $  ^{[44]}  $

the conductivity of   $  SnO_{2}  $   before and after being modified by C-C1. Compared to the pristine   $  SnO_{2}  $   film (  $  1.51 \times 10^{-3}  $   S m  $  ^{-1}  $  ), the conductivity of   $  SnO_{2}/C-C1  $   is enhanced to   $  2.06 \times 10^{-3}  $   S m  $  ^{-1}  $  , which is likely ascribed to the reduced surface traps/defects of   $  SnO_{2}  $  . The improvement in conductivity is beneficial for facilitating the electron transport process at the ETL/perovskite interface, effectively reducing the charge accumulation in the PerSC devices.  $  ^{[45]}  $   Besides, atomic force microscopy (AFM) images of the ITO/  $  SnO_{2}  $   and ITO/  $  SnO_{2}/C-C1  $   thin film are shown in Figure S8 in the Supporting Information. The root-mean-square roughness of ITO/  $  SnO_{2}/C-C1  $   film (0.8 nm) is smaller compared to the roughness of ITO/  $  SnO_{2}  $   (1.71 nm), which exhibits a key figure-of-merit for the PerSC devices.  $  ^{[46]}  $

Then the quality of the perovskite films was evaluated by the steady-state photoluminescence (PL) measurement (Figure 3d). Compared with the sample of  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2/ $  perovskite, the more vigorous PL intensity of  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2/ $  C-C1/perovskite confirms that the quality of the corresponding perovskite thin films is markedly enhanced. Besides, the time-resolved PL (TRPL) measurements fitted with a bi-exponential decay function were carried out (Figure 3e and Table S2, Supporting Information). The fast decay component ( $ \tau_1 $ ) is attributed to the quenching of carriers at the ETL/perovskite interface (namely, charge extraction from perovskite to ETL), while the slow decay component ( $ \tau_2 $ ) originates from the radiative recombination of charge carriers associated with the traps/defects in perovskite films.[49] Compared with the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2/ $  perovskite film ( $ \tau_1 = 4.04 $  ns,  $ \tau_2 = 127.00 $  ns), the  $ \tau_1 $  of the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2/ $  C-C1/perovskite (2.53 ns) is shorter, demonstrating a more effective electron extraction from perovskite to ETL. Meanwhile, the longer  $ \tau_2 $  is acquired for the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2/ $  C-C1/perovskite (193.50 ns), which proves the enhanced quality and reduced traps/defects density in the perovskite. On the other hand, due to the introduction of C-C1 on  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $ , the conductivity and the charge extraction efficiency of ETL are enhanced (Figure 3b), and the traps/defects of perovskite are decreased a lot (Figure 3c). To further prove the effect of the interlayer C-C1 on interfacial charge transfer, the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy technique was employed.[50] As shown in Figure S9 in the Supporting Information, the Nyquist plots of the PerSC devices based on  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  and  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2/ $  C-C1 were measured at a bias of  $ 1.0\mathrm{V} $  under AM 1.5G illumination. From the fitting results, the values of series resistance ( $ R_{\mathrm{s}} $ ) are reduced from  $ 67.2\Omega $  of  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  to  $ 61.0\Omega $  of  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2/ $  C-C1, which is attributed to the enhanced quality of  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  and perovskite films after C-C1 modification.[51] Furthermore, the values of transport resistance ( $ R_{\mathrm{ct}} $ ) are decreased from  $ 168.4\Omega $  of  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  to  $ 137.0\Omega $  of  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2/ $  C-C1, which is ascribed to the enhanced interfacial charge extraction as is consistent with the results of PL and TRPL. In conclusion, employing C-C1 not only enhances the charge extraction from perovskite to ETL, but also reduces the traps/defects in perovskite. Furthermore, the introduction of C-C1-P can reduce the traps/defects of perovskite to suppress nonradiative recombination centers, demonstrated by the significantly enhanced PL intensity compared with the intensity of pure perovskite. Moreover, the PL peaks of perovskite (C-C1-P) shift toward the shorter wavelength direction compared to the pure perovskite, implying that the defects in the perovskite highly decreased for the introduction of C-C1-P. As for perovskite (C-C1-P), the

average TRPL lifetime is 2250.56 ns, while the lifetime of the pure perovskite is only 296.76 ns. The enhanced lifetime of perovskite films exhibits reduced nonradiative recombination sites in the perovskite (C-C1-P) films, which can be attributed to the reduced traps/defects in the perovskite films.  $  ^{[52]}  $

The surface morphology of perovskite films was studied by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) measurements. It can be seen that the perovskite grains grown on   $  SnO_{2}/C-C1  $   (the sample of C-C1) are more uniform and compact than that grown on the pure   $  SnO_{2}  $   (control), obtaining a continuous and completely covered pinhole-free film (Figure S10, Supporting Information). Moreover, the average grain size of the sample of C-C1 is relatively larger than the control, which may be ascribed to the different wettability at the two interfaces. Furthermore, the SEM figures confirm that the further introduction of C-C1-P into the perovskite layer (the sample of C-C1/C-C1-P) exhibits no significant difference compared to the sample of C-C1. Furthermore, the   $  SnO_{2}/C-C1  $   surface (Figure S11, Supporting Information) shows stronger hydrophobicity than pure   $  SnO_{2}  $   (Figure 2d), indicating that the   $  SnO_{2}/C-C1  $   surface is more difficult to be infiltrated by polar solvents. Moreover, the introduction of C-C1-P into the perovskite layer did not significantly change its roughness. It is indicated that the reduction of the roughness of perovskite is owing to the change of ETL surface properties rather than the introduction of C-C1-P into perovskite. Furthermore, the X-ray diffraction (XRD) of the different perovskites demonstrates that the three kinds of perovskites exhibit similar crystallinity (Figure S13, Supporting Information). As shown in Figure S14 in the Supporting Information, UV-vis absorption spectra of the different perovskites show almost the same absorption band edge, proving the added small amount of C1 molecule causes a negligible impact on the bulk crystal structure of pristine perovskite.

Then the dependence of   $  V_{OC}  $   on light intensities was further investigated (Figure 4e). Bimolecular recombination dominates in perovskite when the slope is close to 1 kT/q, while trap-assisted Shockley–Read–Hall (SRH) recombination is the primary way of recombination for the slope close to 2 kT/q.  $  ^{[56]}  $   Figure 4e shows that the slope of the device of C-C1 (1.69 kT/q) is lower than the control device (1.99 kT/q), indicating reduced trap-assisted SRH recombination as a result of the decreasing defects on the lower surface of perovskite via introducing C-C1 as an interlayer. Furthermore, the device of C-C1/C-C1-P exhibits the smallest slope among the three conditions, suggesting the least trap-assisted recombination. Moreover, those results agree with the effects of trap-state density shown in Figure 3c. On the other hand, the dependence of   $  J_{SC}  $   on light intensities is displayed in Figure 4f. The slopes of the three devices are very close to 1, verifying less bimolecular recombination for the three devices.  $  ^{[47]}  $   As a result, introducing C-C1-P into the perovskite layer can further reduce the defects on the surface and GBs of the perovskite, implying a successful DFCC strategy.

Besides the strategy of lower interface and the grain passivation, the formation of a passivation layer on the top of perovskite film is another efficient strategy to passivate the traps/ defects of perovskite, thus extremely enhancing the efficiency of PerSCs. To incorporate interfaces and grains collaborative optimization, an organic halide salt phenethylammonium iodide (PEAI) was used as the passivation layer on the top of the perovskite and the   $  FA_{1-x}MA_{x}PbI_{3}  $   was selected as the perovskite layer, further improving the PCE of the PerSCs. Beyond expectation, Figure 4g exhibits an excellent PCE of 24.31% for the triple-passivated PerSCs (C-C1/C-C1-P/PEAI), along with the   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.18 V,   $  J_{SC}  $   of 24.99 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and FF of 82.54%, which is higher than the PCE of 22.34% for the PEAI passivated device. The integrated current value of the C-C1/C-C1-P/PEAI-based PerSC is 24.30 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , while the value of PEAI-based PerSCs is just 23.81 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   (Figure 4h). There is a small mismatch between the integrated current values and the   $  J_{SC}  $   from the J-V curves. It is extremely demonstrated that the three-passivated

strategy plays an important role in improving the efficiency of PerSCs.

To investigate the effect of the introduction of C1 on the device stability, we investigated the water contact angles of control and C-C1/C-C1-P perovskite films to verify water/moisture stability (Figure S21, Supporting Information). The water contact angles of perovskite film were enhanced from 67.2° (control) to 80.9° (C-C1/C-C1-P), implying more excellent resistance to water/moisture for the sample of C-C1/C-C1-P. Intuitively, we put the control and C-C1/C-C1-P perovskite films into the water for about 2 s to study their water/moisture tolerance (Medias S1–S3, Supporting Information). Afterward, the control perovskite film quickly turns the black perovskite phase to the yellow phase after immersing into water. Interestingly, the C-C1/C-C1-P perovskite film still maintains the black perovskite phase, demonstrating its more robust water/moisture tolerance. Similarly, we tested the water immersion experiment of control and C-C1/C-C1-P PerSC devices to confirm their water/moisture stability (Media S4, Supporting Information). The final results also show that the stability of the C-C1/C-C1-P device is much better than the control device. Moreover, XRD measurements for the two kinds of perovskite films before and after immersing in water were carried out. Figure 5a,b exhibits that the control perovskite film completely transforms its initial perovskite phase into either PbI₂ or nonperovskite δ-FAPbI₃ phase. Besides, the C-C1/C-C1-P film reveals that the initial perovskite phase shows only part of decomposition or phase change after being immersed into water, which further confirms the improved water stability.

Further, we compared the moisture and thermal stabilities of the unencapsulated devices for the two samples of control and C-C1/C-C1-P. As shown in Figure 5c, the unencapsulated devices were exposed to two different humidity ranges under ambient environments. Under the humidity of about  $ 40 \pm 5\% $ , the C-C1/C-C1-P device retained  $ 94.4\% $  of its initial PCE after  $ 384\mathrm{h} $ , and the PCE still maintained  $ 92.2\% $  of the initial performance after the exposure in the high humidity atmosphere ( $ 70 \pm 5\% $ ) for another  $ 120\mathrm{h} $ . Under the same condition, the control device just maintained  $ 73.4\% $  and  $ 48.9\% $  of its initial PCE under the two different humidity ranges, exhibiting that its stability is far lower than that of the C-C1/C-C1-P device. The photos of the devices exposed to specific moisture for different periods are shown in Figure 5c. After aged for  $ 504\mathrm{h} $ , perovskite decomposition has appeared in the control device, while there is no significant change in the C-C1/C-C1-P device, demonstrating that the introduction of C1 significantly improves moisture stability. As shown in Figure 5d, we further tracked the thermal stability of the unencapsulated control and C-C1/C-C1-P devices ( $ 80^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ ,  $ \mathbf{N}_2 $  glove box). After aging for  $ 100\mathrm{h} $ ,  $ 90.0\% $  of its original PCE was maintained for the C-C1/C-C1-P device, while the control device quickly decayed to  $ 12.5\% $  of its initial performance under the same condition. Namely, the introduction of C-C1/C-C1-P effectively enhances the moisture and thermal stabilities of PerSCs, which is advantageous for the large-scale manufacture of PerSCs.
