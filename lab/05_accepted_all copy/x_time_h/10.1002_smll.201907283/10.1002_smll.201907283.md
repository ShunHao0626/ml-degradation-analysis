# Extrinsic Ion Distribution Induced Field Effect in CsPbIBr  $  _{2}  $  Perovskite Solar Cells

Yulong Wang, Kai Wang,  $  ^{*}  $   Waqas Siddique Subhani, Congqiang Zhang, Xiao Jiang, Shimin Wang, Huaxi Bao, Lu Liu, Li Wan,  $  ^{*}  $   and Shengzhong (Frank) Liu  $  ^{*}  $

Excellent power conversion efficiency (PCE) and stability are the primary forces that propel the all-inorganic cesium-based halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) toward commercialization. However, the intrinsic high density of trap state and internal nonradiative recombination of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film are the barriers that limit its development. In the present study, a facile additive strategy is introduced to fabricate highly efficient   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   PSCs by incorporating sulfamic acid sodium salt (SAS) into the perovskite layer. The additive can control the crystallization behaviors and optimize morphology, as well as effectively passivate defects in the bulk perovskite film, thereby resulting in a high-quality perovskite. In addition, SAS in perovskite has possibly introduced an additional internal electric field effect that favors electron transport and injection due to inhomogeneous ion distribution. A champion PCE of 10.57% (steady-output efficiency is 9.99%) is achieved under 1 Sun illumination, which surpasses that of the contrast sample by 16.84%. The modified perovskite film also exhibits improved moisture stability. The unencapsulated device maintains over 80% initial PCE after aging for 198 h in air. The results provide a suitable additive for inorganic perovskite and introduce a new conjecture to explain the function of additives in PSCs more rationally.

# 2. Results and Discussion

In general, additives can be classified into two parts as follows: volatile additives only improve the crystallization process; and additives that are ever-present in perovskite that improve the crystallization process and passivate the defects, such as undercoordinated surface ions. In this experiment, SAS was incorporated into a perovskite precursor, and the film was annealed at 225 °C after spin coating. Therefore, thermo-gravimetric analysis was primarily conducted to investigate whether SAS decomposed after the annealing process. The small weight loss at 45 °C is attributed to the water volatilization (Figure S1, Supporting Information). After a long flat platform, SAS started the decomposition at around 269 °C, indicating that SAS was still present after the annealing process. Encouragingly, with an optimal molar ratio of 0.7%, we obtained a champion PCE as high as 10.57%. Generally, the notorious hysteresis behavior is frequently observed in the photovoltaic parameters, which is usually ascribed to the ion migration and deeper trap effects in the interface and bulk perovskite film.  $  ^{[47,48]}  $   Phase segregation is a common phenomenon in Br-rich perovskite due to ion migration, which aggravates hysteresis. Thus, quantitatively evaluating the hysteresis effect is necessary by calculating the hysteresis index according to Equation (4)  $  ^{[39]}  $

$$
\mathrm{HI} = \frac {\mathrm{PCE} _ {\mathrm{RS}} - \mathrm{PCE} _ {\mathrm{FS}}}{\mathrm{PCE} _ {\mathrm{RS}}}
$$

(4)

where   $  PCE_{RS}  $   and   $  PCE_{FS}  $   represent the PCE measures in the case of reverse and forward scans, respectively. As shown in Figure 4c and Figure S9 (Supporting Information), the HI of PSCs with and without SAS are 10.44% and 20.29%, respectively. The minor hysteresis in SAS-incorporated sample is in accordance with its large grain size along with fewer boundaries and lower trap-state density. Certainly, this non-negligible hysteresis may give rise to an overestimation of PCE. Thus, researchers focus on the steady output power to obtain an exact device performance. The PSCs incorporated with SAS show an excellent steady output PCE of 9.99% and a constant current density of 11.10 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   after illuminating for more than 500 s (Figure 4d). This result revealed that the   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   PSCs have an excellent photovoltaic performance by adopting SAS, which is beneficial for the commercialization of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   PSCs. We proposed that an extra electrical field induced by SAS that can possibly facilitate electron transport is necessary. To cross-check this viewpoint, inverted PSCs with a p-i-n structure of FTO/NiO  $  _{x}  $  /CsPbIBr  $  _{2}  $  /PCBM/bathocuproine/Ag were fabricated, and the corresponding photovoltaic parameters were summarized as shown in Figure S10 in the Supporting Information (detailed formation in Table S3 in the Supporting Information). Compared with the control samples, PSCs incorporated

with SAS exhibit a lower PCE, which is abnormal because SAS can enhance the crystallinity and improve the morphology of perovskite layers. This phenomenon was ascribed to the screening effect of the extra electrical field in the case of inverted devices, which provided partial evidence for our statement.

Electrochemical characterizations are used to explore the increase of photovoltaic parameters and reveal the charge dynamic process. Figure 5a shows the Mott–Schottky curves of PSCs with and without SAS. The CsPbIBr₂ incorporated with SAS showed a larger built-in potential of 2.02 V than that of the counterpart (1.83 V), indicating that the devices containing SAS have enhanced driving force for dissociating photogenerated carriers and suppressing recombination due to the extended depletion region.[16,19] In addition, the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy was performed to analyze the charge-transfer process. As shown in Figure 5b, the Nyquist plot obtained in a dark condition was composed of two irregular semicircles. At present, the long-term stability issue of PSCs is a big hurdle that prevents the commercialization of PSCs. In the current work, we systematically investigated the moisture stability of inorganic perovskite, because moisture enables the phase transition effortlessly due to the unsatisfied Goldschmidt tolerance factor. Thus, the hydrophobicity of perovskite is primarily studied via the water contact angle measurement. The SAS-incorporated perovskite film and control sample show the angles of 25.3° and 23.9°, respectively (Figure S12a,b, Supporting Information). The slight increase of the contact angle predicts that SAS may enhance the hydrophobicity of perovskite in parallel with improving stability. In addition, the UV–vis absorption spectra were determined to investigate the stability of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite films that were aged in ambient atmosphere with 40% relative humidity (RH) at room temperature for 500 h. The absorption variation of the modified   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film was intensely small, as reflected by the still red film and constant bandgap after aging (Figure S12c,d, Supporting Information). However, a remarkable absorption decreased and color change was observed in the control sample. Meanwhile, the bandgap increases from 2.10 to 2.21 eV. We detected the stability of the unencapsulated PSCs in ambient air with a relative humidity of 35–37% at room temperature. As shown in Figure 6, the reference sample only retained 30% of its original PCE after 96 h. For the SAS-modified devices, the efficiency maintained more than 70% of its initial value even after 216 h. The enhanced stability should be ascribed to the increased crystallinity, enlarged crystal grain, and passivated defects in SAS-contained perovskite. Notably, the device stability was inferior to the bare perovskite films because the hygroscopic additives, such as LI-TFSI in hole transport layers, accelerate the perovskite degradation.

# 3. Conclusion

We successfully incorporated SAS into all-inorganic halide perovskite and demonstrated  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  PSCs with a stable and excellent efficiency. SAS can modulate the crystallization behavior and lead to a favorable morphology with large grain size accompanied by fewer boundaries. Then, the nonradiative recombination inside the perovskite embodiment and at interface was suppressed powerfully, and the charge extraction also improved. In addition, an extra electrical field was possibly formed by the uneven ion distributions that favor the charge dynamics. As such, a champion PCE of  $ 10.57\% $  is obtained, and the steady-output PCE is  $ 9.99\% $ . Furthermore, the

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials and Methods: Cesium iodide (Csl, 99.999%, Sigma Aldrich), PbBr₂ (99.99%, Sigma Aldrich), dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO, 99.9%, Sigma Aldrich), SAS (98%, Adamas), spiro-OMeTAD (Borun, China), 4-tert-butylpyridine (TBP, 99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), lithium bis-(trifluoromethylsulphonyl) imide (Li-TFSI, Sigma-Aldrich), and chlorobenzene (99%, Sigma-Aldrich) were used in the experiment.

Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) grass substrate was cleaned sequentially via deionized water, ethanol, and isopropanol under ultrasonication for 15 min.   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs were prepared by chemical bath deposition method.  $  ^{[19]}  $   The precursor solution of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   was prepared by mixing CsI (1 M) and   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (1 M) in high-purity DMSO solvent. SAS (Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in pure DMSO solvent. The SAS (0.1 M) solution was appended in   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   precursor solution with various concentrations of 0%, 0.5%, 0.7%, 0.9% m m  $  ^{-1}  $  . Then, the solution was spin-coated on   $  TiO_{2}  $   at 1500 rpm for 90 s, followed by annealing at 225 °C for 10 min. The HTL was prepared by dissolving 72.3 mg spiro-OMeTAD and 28.8 μL 4-tert-butylpyridine, and 17.5 μL of a stock solution of 520 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   lithium bis-(trifluoromethylsulfonyl) imide in acetonitrile in 1 mL chlorobenzene. The mixture was spin-coated on the   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   film at 3500 rpm for 30 s to form an HTL layer. Finally, a 100 nm thick gold electrode was deposited by thermal evaporation under a pressure of   $  8.0 \times 10^{-5}  $   Pa. The active area was 0.09 cm  $  ^{2}  $  . All process of fabricated   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   PSCs in a glove box with a   $  N_{2}  $   atmosphere (  $  O_{2} < 0.01\%  $  ).

Characterization: The crystallization of PSCs was recorded with a Bruker D2 PHASER equipped with Cu KαI irradiation (  $  \lambda = 1.5406  $   Å). FTIR spectroscopy was obtained by Infrared spectrometer (Nicolet iS50, Thermo). The UV–vis absorption spectra were provided by UV–vis spectrophotometer (U-4150, Hitachi). The XPS measurement was conducted with an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi), which is used the C 1s peak at 284.5 eV for standard. The microscope morphology of PSCs film was performed by SEM (Hitachi SU8020), at the same time, EDS-mapping images also obtained, where the electron beam was set to 3 kV. ToF-SIMS was performed with the pulsed primary ions from an O₂ (2 keV) gun for the sputtering and a Bi²⁺ pulsed primary ion beam for the analysis (30 keV). 3D microscope images were measured by AFM (Bruker, Dimension Icon). The photocurrent density–voltage (J–V) characteristics of CsPbIBr₂ PSCs were measured under AM 1.5 G illumination at 100 mW cm⁻² (Newport Thermal Oriel 91 192) using a Keithley 2450 source meter. The EQE measurements were taken with QTest Station 2000ADI system (Crowntech, Inc., USA), combined with a 300 W xenon lamp under air atmosphere. PL spectrum was recorded using an FLS920 spectrometer (Edinburgh Instruments Ltd.) in air at room temperature. The SCLC measurements were gotten at the Keithley 2450 source meter. The Mott–Schottky analyses were performed using an impedance spectrum analyzer (ZENNIUM, Zahner, Germany). Under dark condition, the EIS measurements were conducted on this instrument, where the measured frequencies ranged from 100 mHz to 2 MHz and the amplitude was 20 mV.
