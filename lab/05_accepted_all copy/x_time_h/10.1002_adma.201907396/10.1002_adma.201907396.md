# Intermolecular   $  \pi-\pi  $   Conjugation Self-Assembly to Stabilize Surface Passivation of Highly Efficient Perovskite Solar Cells

Hongshi Li, Jiangjian Shi, Jun Deng, Zijing Chen, Yiming Li, Wenyan Zhao, Jionghua Wu, Huijue Wu, Yanhong Luo, Dongmei Li,* and Qingbo Meng*

Surface passivation is an effective approach to eliminate defects and thus to achieve efficient perovskite solar cells, while the stability of the passivation effect is a new concern for device stability engineering. Herein, tribenzylphosphine oxide (TBPO) is introduced to stably passivate the perovskite surface. A high efficiency exceeding 22%, with steady-state efficiency of 21.6%, is achieved, which is among the highest performances for   $  TiO_{2}  $   planar cells, and the hysteresis is significantly suppressed. Further density functional theory (DFT) calculation reveals that the surface molecule superstructure induced by TBPO intermolecular   $  \pi-\pi  $   conjugation, such as the periodic interconnected structure, results in a high stability of TBPO–perovskite coordination and passivation. The passivated cell exhibits significantly improved stability, with sustaining 92% of initial efficiency after 250 h maximum-power-point tracking. Therefore, the construction of a stabilized surface passivation in this work represents great progress in the stability engineering of perovskite solar cells.

Comparatively, defects of the perovskite absorber and the interface are more intrinsic factor influencing the device stability.  $  ^{[6]}  $   Defects and their distribution are instable when the PSC works under light illumination or bias voltages.  $  ^{[7]}  $   Moreover, the defects can capture photoinduced charges to form local electric field, drive the ion redistribution and induce phase segregation, thus degrading the cell structure and photoelectric conversion performance. A variety of defect passivation strategies have been developed to improve the cell stability as well as the efficiency.  $  ^{[8]}  $   For example, defect density could be reduced by introducing Lewis base (such as thiourea) through the interaction between   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and coordinated atom with lone-pair electrons at the surface and the grain boundary.  $  ^{[9]}  $   A buffer layer, such as fullerene derivative, has been exploited to suppress interface defects by releasing interface stress.  $  ^{[10]}  $   Surface passivation by using organic ammonium salts such as phenethylammonium iodide (PEAI), can effectively eliminate surface halogen deficiency to promote the cell efficiency to exceeding 23%.  $  ^{[2a]}  $   However, the stability issue of these coordination interactions or passivation effects is still seldom mentioned, which needs to be concerned because some passivation could be easily damaged under the erosion of the external environment. How to sustain the passivation effect for a long time is a new research perspective for the device stability engineering.

In this study, we focus on improving the stability of surface passivation for the perovskite cell by using small aromatic phosphine oxide molecules. It is revealed that this improvement is realized through strong  $ -\mathrm{P = O - Pb} $  coordination and intermolecular  $ \pi -\pi $  conjugation induced superstructure on the perovskite surface. Specifically, this synergistic effect from intermolecular side chains not only benefits for achieving sufficient surface passivation, but also helps stabilize the surface molecule arrangement. PCE of exceeding  $ 22\% $  with significantly suppressed hysteresis has been achieved, which is among the highest performances of the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ -based planar cells. The stability of passivated cell is also greatly improved, with sustaining  $ 92\% $  of initial efficiency after  $ 250\mathrm{h} $  maximum-power-point tracking (MPPT). This work provides a first attempt to promote the stability of passivation effect by constructing intermolecular self-assembly strategy.

The perovskite photoluminescence (PL) performance was explored to study the influence of passivation. Compared to the neat perovskite film, PL intensity of the perovskite/TPPO is enhanced by two times while over five times of PL enhancement is obtained for the perovskite/TBPO, as presented in Figure 1d. Asymmetric profile can be observed from these PL spectra. This phenomenon usually appears in hybrid perovskite materials while the origin is still under controversial.  $  ^{[14]}  $   Accompanying with the PL intensity improvement, blue-shifts in the PL peak are apparently observed, whereas the absorption edge is basically unchanged, indicating that the perovskite band-tail state has been suppressed. This PL enhancement mainly arises from the reduction in the nonradiative recombination velocity, as reflected by an obvious increase in the PL decay lifetime (  $  \tau_{0}  $  ).   $  \tau_{0}  $   increases from 1505 to 2678 ns for the TBPO modified perovskite, however, to 1935 ns for the perovskite/TPPO (Figure 1e). Moreover, further research reveals that this passivation effect still exists even after the TBPO/perovskite (TPPO/perovskite) film was washed with chlorobenzene (Figure S2, Supporting Information). That is, surface passivation with strong coordination bond can be guaranteed after the hole transporting layer deposition process.

To further understand the passivation effect of TPPO and TBPO, carrier diffusion length   $  (L_{\mathrm{D}})  $   is derived from the PL decay lifetimes of the perovskite with extraction layers (i.e., [6,6]-phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) for electron (e) and spiro-OMeTAD for hole (h)) (Figure 1f; Figure S3 and Table S1, Supporting Information).  $  ^{[15]}  $   With the passivation, the electron diffusion length   $  (L_{\mathrm{D}}^{\mathrm{e}})  $   is enhanced from 2913 to 3550 and 4289 nm for the perovskite modified with TPPO and TBPO, respectively. Similarly, the hole diffusion length   $  (L_{\mathrm{D}}^{\mathrm{h}})  $   is increased by 20% and 50% for TPPO and TBPO passivation, respectively. These improvements in the charge transport and recombination properties would benefit for achieving higher charge extraction efficiency. The increase in the   $  L_{D}  $   benefits not only from the reduced charge recombination velocity but also from an enhanced carrier mobility (Figure S4, Supporting Information), which could arise from an improvement in the perovskite crystallization, as presented by the top-view scanning electron microscope (SEM) images (Figures S5 and S6, Supporting Information) and the following defect analysis.

The performance of planar solar cell was explored with a configuration of   $  SnO_{2}:F  $   (FTO)/compact   $  TiO_{2}/[6,6]  $  -phenyl-C61-butyric acid (PCBA)/perovskite (TPPO or TBPO)/spiro-OMeTAD/Au. This device structure can be clearly seen from a representative cross-sectional SEM image (Figure S8, Supporting Information). To maximize the passivation effect of the TPPO and TBPO, the concentration of these two molecules in the antisolvent was systematically regulated, and the statistic efficiency of these cells is presented in Figure 2a. Here, the reverse scan data is adopted. The unpassivated cell exhibits an averaged PCE of 20.4%. For the TBPO passivated cell, PCE improvements can be realized in a wide concentration range from   $  0.2 \times 10^{-3}  $   to   $  5 \times 10^{-3}  $   M. The averaged efficiency reaches 21.7% under moderate concentrations ranging from   $  0.5 \times 10^{-3}  $   to   $  1 \times 10^{-3}  $   M. For the TPPO cell, however, the averaged efficiency reaches 21.2% with   $  0.2 \times 10^{-3}  $   M TPPO but continuously decreases when the concentration increases. Clearly, the TBPO enables a wider

operation window for high-PCE cell fabrication, which can afford more controllable and repeatable fabrication process.

As summarized in Table 1, the improved PCE for passivated cells mainly benefits from the enhancement in both the open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) and fill factor (FF). Figure 2b gives a statistic histogram of the   $  V_{OC}  $   gathered from several batches of cells. The averaged   $  V_{OC}  $   has been improved by 25 and 33 mV under the TPPO and TBPO passivation, respectively. This is a direct result of the suppressed nonradiative charge recombination as exhibited by PL results. Current–voltage (I-V) characteristic curves of the champion cells without and with passivation are shown in Figure 2c. To TBPO passivated cell, 22.1% PCE has been achieved with short-circuit current density (  $  J_{SC}  $  ) of 23.9 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ,   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1140 mV, and FF of 0.81. This is one of the highest PCEs that have been reported based on planar TiO  $  _{2}  $   structure. Current output at the maximum power point (at 950 mV) demonstrates a steady-state efficiency of 21.6%, agreeing well with the I-V result (backward-scanning efficiency: 22.1%, and forward-scanning efficiency: 20.8%). Comparatively, champion PCEs of 20.8% and 21.4% were obtained for the control group and TPPO passivated cell, respectively. The steady-state efficiency of the control cell is 20.4%, as shown in Figure S9 in the Supporting Information. Moreover, the I-V hysteresis widely existed within TiO  $  _{2}  $  -based planar cells, has been significantly suppressed in this work by using TBPO passivation (Figure 2d), implying that ion migration and interface defects have been effectively suppressed. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectrum of the TBPO passivated cell also exhibits a slight improvement over the control group (21.8 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) (Figure S10, Supporting Information). The EQE integration gives a   $  J_{SC}  $   of 22.3 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , which is   $  \approx7\%  $   smaller than the I-V result. These results clearly demonstrate the passivation effect realized by the -P=O group. More interestingly, TBPO exhibits a superior passivation effect than the TPPO, although they possess similar -P=O groups.

In terms of the above results, much stronger passivation effect of TBPO toward perovskite than that of TPPO is obvious, although the two molecules have similar structures except for using phenyl (TPPO) and benzyl (TBPO) groups, respectively. It is suggested that the side group also plays critical roles in influencing the perovskite–molecule interactions. To shed deep insight into the TBPO (TPPO)–perovskite interaction, density

functional theory (DFT) calculation has been applied here. In our calculation, Pb–I plane vertical to the [001] direction was used as the exposed surface because the perovskite lattice is usually terminated with excess Pb atoms due to the volatility of the organic halides. To accelerate the calculation, lattice parameters derived from X-ray diffraction measurement were directly used and organic cations (FA $  ^{+}  $ , MA $  ^{+}  $ ) of the perovskite were substituted by Cs $  ^{+}  $  to reduce the total number of atoms in the system. The surface atomic structure was first optimized within a periodical 3 × 3 × 2 cubic supercell (19.17 × 19.17 × 12.78 Å $  ^{3}  $ ) with a 35 Å vacuum layer along c-axis (see details in the Supporting Information). Afterward, single TPPO and TBPO molecules were put on the perovskite surface and the entire structure was fully optimized for total energy minimization. The difference charge density distribution of the optimal surface structures (perovskite–TPPO and perovskite–TBPO) is presented in Figure 4a,b. It is apparent that the O atom of both molecules is coordinately anchored to the exposed Pb, with similar bond lengths of 2.35 Å (TPPO) and 2.37 Å (TBPO), respectively. The formation of Pb–O bonding is consistent with FTIR results. These Pb–O bond lengths are much smaller than the sum of Pb $  ^{2+}  $  (1.2 Å) and O $  ^{2-}  $  (1.4 Å) ion radii, implying that the coordination between the O and Pb is realized through strong polar covalent bond. This is further supported by the delocalized difference charge density distribution between the P, O, and Pb atoms. These results demonstrate that the perovskite–TBPO coordination system with intermolecular   $  \pi-\pi  $   conjugation has distinctive advantages for realizing sufficient surface passivation and coordination stability. Moreover, periodic TBPO dimer (Figure 4f) or even close-packed superstructure (Figure 4g) may appear on the perovskite surface. These interconnected structures are supposed to possess two advantages, including 1) significantly reducing the TBPO freedom of rotation, vibration, and motion at high temperatures and thus helping to sustain highly ordered surface structure and passivation stability; 2) forming a relatively dense molecule passivation layer to isolate detrimental ions within the hole transporting layer, humidity, and oxygen. These positive factors finally help afford more efficient TBPO passivation with wider concentration window, as represented by the PL, PCE, and defect performances.

To verify these theoretical findings, we carried out surface energy characterizations to explore the surface properties of these passivated perovskites. The total surface energy was estimated by using Owens–Wendt–Rabel–Kaelble (OWRK) method, in which a variety of liquids with different surface tension was used and each of their liquid/perovskite contact angle was measured.  $  ^{[19]}  $   As presented in Figure 4h, a linear relationship is found between the liquid disperse   $  (\sigma_{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{d}})/\mathrm{polar}  $   (  $  \sigma_{L}^{p}  $  ) tension and the contact angle   $  (\theta)  $   related function. Apparently, the TBPO passivated perovskite exhibits a much smaller slope. Quantifying these linear relationships (see details in Table S2 in the Supporting Information) reveals that the total surface energy of the perovskite reduces from 52 to 44 mN m  $  ^{-1}  $   (TPPO) and 36 mN m  $  ^{-1}  $   (TBPO), respectively, after the passivation. Specially, the polar tension significantly reduces from 35 to 25 mN m  $  ^{-1}  $   (TPPO) and 15 mN m  $  ^{-1}  $   (TBPO), respectively. This reduction means that the perovskite surface dipole polarity has been effectively decreased by the introduction of phenyl terminals. Besides the difference in the molecule dipole polarity, we think the lower surface energy obtained by the TBPO passivation is a clear signature of a more sufficient -P=O-Pb coordination, which is an experimental support to the DFT results. Apparently, this low surface energy would help to decrease the perovskite surface activity, such as the adsorption of water and other detrimental molecules, and thus can stabilize the perovskite material.

The advantages of the intermolecular   $  \pi-\pi  $   conjugation and self-assembly superstructure in improving the stability of passivation effect were further studied by using an in situ contact angle measurement. An evolution tracking of the liquid/solid contact angle with time is an effective approach to reflect the molecule rearrangement of the solid surface.  $  ^{[20]}  $   For a stabilized surface passivation, the molecular distribution, orientation, and

corresponding contact angle are supposed to keep unchanged when the surface molecules are contacting with external liquid, such as the glycerol used here, and vice versa.

The results of time-dependent contact angle are shown in Figure 5a,b. In the starting stage, both the TBPO and TPPO passivated surfaces show much higher contact angle of over  $ 60^{\circ} $ . Similar to the above surface energy result, this increase in contact angle demonstrates that the perovskite surface is indeed passivated by these molecules, at least in the initial stage. However, the surface properties of both the neat perovskite and the TPPO cannot sustain after continuously contacting with the glycerol. As shown in Figure 5a, after  $ 30~\mathrm{s} $ , their contact angles obviously decrease to  $ \approx 30^{\circ} $ . This result means that the TPPO-perovskite interaction is not stable enough to resist the erosion of glycerol, that is, the passivation effect is not stable. Comparatively, the TBPO-perovskite surface can still sustain a high contact angle of over  $ 55^{\circ} $  even after  $ 90~\mathrm{s} $  erosion, confirming that the interconnected TBPO superstructure enabled by the intermolecular  $ \pi-\pi $  conjugation can realize a significantly improved stability of the passivation effect.

Perovskite phase and cell efficiency stability were further investigated under aging conditions. The perovskite films with or without the TBPO passivation as well as the cells without any encapsulation were first aged in a double 60 condition (i.e.,  $ 60^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  and  $ 60\% $  relative humidity) (Figure 5c,d). Without the passivation, the perovskite began to degrade into  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  after only  $ 10\mathrm{h} $  aging, which is over two times as fast as that of the TBPO passivated perovskite. Consequently, after  $ 40\mathrm{h} $  aging, the passivated cell retained  $ 82\% $  of its initial efficiency while the efficiency of the nonpassivated cell had dropped to  $ 70\% $ . This result clearly demonstrates the stability advantages of the TBPO passivation and implies that the surface passivation has not been significantly destroyed by the external extreme conditions.

The stability performance was further studied using MPPT mode under nitrogen atmosphere and LED illumination (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ), as shown in Figure 5e. The control cell exhibited an obvious burn-in behavior in the early 20 h, maybe due to the irreversible interface structure degradation under high voltage and illumination. In contrast, no obvious initial burn-in was observed for the TBPO cell, indicating that the interface structure has been greatly protected and the passivation effect is stabilized by the intermolecular   $  \pi-\pi  $   conjugation. Eliminating the burn-in would significantly enhance the initial efficiency of the commercialized solar cell and would help to realize a much longer service lifetime. After 250 h MPPT, 92% of initial efficiency had still been sustained by using the TBPO passivation. MPPT performance of the TBPO cell is also much superior to the TPPO cell although they exhibited similar initial efficiencies. This further confirms that the conjugated TBPO coordination possesses a stronger ability to sustain the passivation effect owing to the formation of surface superstructure. When stored in dark and ambient condition, the TBPO passivated cell can still retain over 90% of its initial efficiency after 3500 h, significantly superior to the control cell (Figure 5f). Thus, it can be concluded that our construction of a stabilized surface passivation has made a great progress in promoting the stability performance of the PSC.
