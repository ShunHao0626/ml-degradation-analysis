# Passivation of defects in inverted perovskite solar cells by imidazolium-based ionic liquid

Chuanyao Luo, $ ^{ab} $  Guannan Li, $ ^{ab} $  Lijia Chen, $ ^{c} $  Jun Dong, $ ^{bd} $  Miao Yu, $ ^{ab} $  Cunyun Xu, $ ^{b} $  Yanqing Yao, $ ^{b} $  Meng Wang, $ ^{ab} $  Qunliang Song $ ^{*ab} $  and Sam Zhang $ ^{*ab} $

During perovskite film preparation, defects in the film are almost impossible to avoid because of the migration of the halide ions, which is detrimental to achieve high-quality film. In general, the introduction of additive is an effective strategy to control the film morphology and to reduce the defect density. Here, a representative and simplest ionic liquid, 1-methyl-3-propylimidazolium bromide (MPIB), is selected as an additive due to its high conductivity and lone-pair electron in its cation group. Remarkably, the adding of MPIB additive into the perovskite film improves the power conversion efficiency (PCE) from  $ 15.9\% $  of pristine device to  $ 18.2\% $ . With the help of characterization analysis of scanning electron microscopy, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, Fourier transform infrared spectrometer, etc., two contributions of MPIB additive are addressed: (1) the major one is the passivation of the uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} $  to reduce the defects in the perovskite film due to the lone-pair electron in its cation group, and (2) the secondary one is beneficial to promote crystal growth to improve the film quality. Hence, this work provides an easy approach to achieve high-performance perovskite solar cell via passivation of the uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} $  in the perovskite film due to the lone-pair electron in the cation group.

# 1. Introduction

The organometallic perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been extensively investigated in the past few years due to their potential to promote high-efficiency,  $  ^{1, 2}  $  low-cost,  $  ^{3}  $  solution-processible  $  ^{4, 5}  $  devices thanks to the inherent properties of perovskite materials, including high mobility  $  ^{6}  $ , turnable bandgaps  $  ^{7}  $  and long diffusion length  $  ^{8}  $ . During the nucleation and crystal growth of perovskite film, vacancies and charged defects are always created because of the migration of the halide ions. Undoubtedly, these vacancies or defects are detrimental to the device performance due to the undesirable charge recombination and then performance deterioration.  $  ^{9-11}  $

To reduce the vacancies or defects in polycrystalline perovskite film, it is an effective strategy by introducing additives in the active layer to control the morphology and/or passivate the uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $  .  $  ^{12, 13}  $   Lewis base, who contains the lone-pair electrons in its electron-rich group, is one of the widely used additives in the

perovskite film to restrain unsaturated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   or passivate the uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and then to reduce the recombination of charge carriers and facilitate carrier transfer.  $  ^{14, 15}  $   However, the passivation agents based on small molecular Lewis base may not sustain their interaction with the halide perovskite under high temperature due to their low thermal stability.  $  ^{16}  $   To circumvent this, polymer additives  $  ^{17}  $   with multiple Lewis base functional groups have been designed and synthesized to provide a durable passivation of defects in halide perovskite film. However, the low conductivity is another drawback of small molecules and polymers, which may impede the carrier transport in perovskite film.

Within this context, ionic liquids (ILs) are considered as alternative candidates to serve as additives for high-efficiency PSCs because of their low vapor pressure and high ionic conductivity.  $  ^{18-20}  $   ILs have been widely employed as additives in PSCs via incorporating with   $  TiO_{2}  $  -modified layer,  $  ^{21}  $     $  C_{60}  $  -modified layer,  $  ^{22}  $   interface layer,  $  ^{23}  $   and perovskite precursor.  $  ^{24-27}  $   In general, the role of ILs as additive aims to promote the processes of crystal nucleation and growth to achieve better morphologies and high-quality perovskite film. Aside from this, the IL additives could provide an opportunity to passivate the defects when added into the perovskite precursor.  $  ^{28-30}  $   Bai et al.  $  ^{31}  $   have incorporated 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate (BMIMBF  $  _{4}  $  ) into the perovskite precursor to achieve long-term operational stability due to the passivation between the PbI  $  _{2}  $   of perovskite and the anion ([BF  $  _{4}  $  ]  $  ^{+}  $  ) of the additive. In addition, Wang  $  ^{32}  $   and co-workers designed and synthesized a new hydrophobic IL, 3-(3-aminopropyl)-1-methylimidazolium hexafluorophosphate (APMimPF  $  _{6}  $  ), and then incorporated this IL into perovskite precursor to improve the device stability due to the interaction between the IL anion ([PF  $  _{6}  $  ]  $  ^{-}  $  ) and perovskite precursor. To date, many research works have concentrated on the interaction between the ILs anions and perovskite. A question naturally rises about the possible utilization of cations of ILs to achieve high-performance PSCs.

1-Methyl-3-propylimidazolium bromide (MPIB, as shown in Figure 1a) is one of the representative and simplest ILs with considerable thermodynamic property,  $  ^{33}  $  which has never been used as an additive in perovskite precursors. In our study, simple structure is a better choice as an additive. 1) Short-chain branching provides the opportunities to cooperate with the perovskite. It is not easy to form steric hindrance. 2) The simplified structure is necessary that promotes the facile synthesis process and low-cost additives engineering. On the other hand, MPIB is a Lewis base with a structure of the nitrogen atom on the cation group imidazole, which can provide a lone-pair electron in the electron-deficient system. Thus, the MPIB could serve as an additive to passivate the uncoordinated  $  Pb^{2+}  $  in perovskite film.

Herein, we demonstrate how MPIB additive can be successfully exploited for controlling the film morphology and reducing the defect by passivation the uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} $  in perovskite film through the interaction between the cations of MPIB and  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} $ . The p-i-n architecture was employed in this work, as shown in Figure 1b. A dense and homogeneous film was achieved when  $ 0.5\mathrm{mg/mL} $  MPIB additive was added into the perovskite precursor due to its high wettability. In addition, the introduction of MPIB additive into the perovskite precursor enhances the PCE from initial  $ 15.9\% $  to  $ 18.2\% $ . This enhancement of electrical performance is mainly attributed to the reduced defects due to the passivation of the uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} $  through the interaction between the cations of MPIB and the uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} $  in the perovskite film, which was supported by the XPS and FTIR measurements. # 2. Experimental section

# 2.1.Materials

Methylammonium iodide (CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ I), lead chloride (PbCl $ _2 $ , >99.99 %), lead iodide (PbI $ _2 $ , >99.99 %), PEDOT: PSS (Heraeus-Clevios PVP AI 4083), [6,6]-phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PC $ _{61} $ BM, >99%) and bathocuproine (BCP, >99%) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. (China). 1MPIB (98%) was bought from TCI (Shanghai) Development Co., Ltd. All solvents, including N,N-dimethylformide (DMF), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), ethyl alcohol, isopropanol (IPA) and chlorobenzene (CB), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All chemicals were used without further purification.

# 2.2. Device Fabrication

To prepare the perovskite precursor,  $ 0.14\mathrm{M} $   $ \mathrm{PbCl}_2 $ ,  $ 1.26\mathrm{M} $   $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and  $ 1.4\mathrm{M} $   $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3 $  were simultaneously dissolved in  $ 1\mathrm{mL} $  co-solvent of DMSO and DMF (volume ratio = 1:9). The mixture solution was prepared in a glove box and stirred overnight at room temperature. To prepare the perovskite precursor with different concentrations of MPIB additive, the different weights of MPIB additive were directly added into  $ 1\mathrm{mL} $  perovskite precursor solutions (Figure 1c).  $ \mathrm{PC}_{61}\mathrm{BM} $   $ (20\mathrm{mg}) $  and BCP  $ (0.5\mathrm{mg}) $  were dissolved in  $ 1\mathrm{mL} $  of CB and  $ 1\mathrm{mL} $  of ethyl alcohol, respectively, and then stirred overnight at room temperature before using. PEDOT:PSS was diluted with deionized water (volume ratio = 1:9) and stirred 3 hours at room temperature.

The indium tin oxide (ITO) glass substrates were sequentially cleaned by detergent of Decon 90, deionized water and ethanol in an ultrasonic machine (KQ3200DV) for 15 min, respectively, followed by drying with   $  N_{2}  $   flow. Once cleaned, the PEDOT:PSS solution was spin coated on the ITO substrate at 6000 rpm for 40 s, followed by annealing at 130 °C for 25 min in air. After that, the PEDOT:PSS film was cooled down to room temperature and transferred to glove box for the next step. Perovskite films were deposited by one-step method (Figure 1c), that is, the perovskite precursor or MPIB-perovskite solution was spin casted onto the PEDOT:PSS layer at 400 rpm for 3 s and 4000 rpm for 25 s. When the precursor was rotated for 12 s, the anti-solvent of CB (170 μL) was dropped onto the film. The wet perovskite film was put on hotplate for annealing at 50 °C for 120 s and 85 °C for 25 min in the glove box, respectively. Then, the   $  PC_{61}BM  $   solution (20 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was spin casted on perovskite layer at speed of 5000 rpm for 30 s followed by exposing to ambient environment (Room temperature, humidity=25%) for 30 min to oxidize the   $  PC_{61}BM  $  .  $  ^{34}  $   Finally, the BCP solution was spin-coated on the pre-formed   $  PC_{61}BM  $   film at 2000 rpm for 30 s in glove box followed by depositing 100 nm Ag electrode in vacuum chamber (5×10  $  ^{-5}  $   pa) by using a shadow mask.

To prepare the sample for Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer measurement,   $  PbI_{2}  $   and MPIB were mixed in a DMF solution and stirred overnight in the glove box at room temperature. Then the sample powder was obtained by putting the above solutions or the perovskite precursor solution on hotplate at 100 °C until complete evaporation of the solvent.

# 2.3. Characterization

Film morphology and crystal structure of perovskite films were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JSM-6700F) and X-ray diffraction system (Shimadzu XRD 7000), respectively. Contact angel measurement was performed with Contact Angle Meter (POWEREACH, Shanghai Zhongchen Co., Ltd.). FTIR spectra were recorded using Thermo Scientific Nicolet 6700 FTIR spectrometer. XPS was performed on Thermo Fisher ESCALAB 250Xi using a monochromated Al K α source and a pass energy of 50 eV at a base pressure of   $  1 \times 10^{-8}  $   mbar. The current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics were measured in a reverse direction (from 1.2 to -0.2 V) with a voltage step of 10 mV and 0 ms delay time by using a Keithley 2400 source measurement unit, coupled with a standard xenon-lamp-based solar simulator (Newport 94043A) to provide an AM 1.5G irradiation (100 mWcm  $  ^{-2}  $  ). Before measurement, the intensity of simulated solar light was calibrated by a monocrystalline silicon reference solar cell. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) of the device was recorded by calculating the photocurrent and the light intensity with a lock-in technique.

A small average grain size (  $  \sim  $  200 nm) with some pinholes is observed in the pristine perovskite film, while the MPIB-perovskite (0.5) film exhibits a uniform and dense surface without any pinholes. In addition, the grain size gradually decreases with further increasing the concentration of MPIB additive in perovskite film (see the top-view SEM images in Figure S1). The uniform and pinhole-free morphology in the MPIB-perovskite (0.5) film is possible attributed to the low solid-liquid interfacial energy induced by the MPIB additive.  $  ^{35}  $   Hence, a comparison of the contact angle between the pristine perovskite and MPIB-perovskite precursor solution has been carried out on the PEDOT:PSS film. As shown in Figure 2b, the contact angles of pristine perovskite precursor and MPIB-perovskite (0.5) precursor are about 19° and 11°, respectively. The higher wettability of MPIB-perovskite (0.5) precursor indicates this precursor can be better spread during spin-coating process.  $  ^{36}  $   As a consequence, a homogeneous perovskite film is easy to be achieved in the MPIB-perovskite case, which seems to suppress the disordered crystal growth and provide well-distributed nucleation sites.  $  ^{35,37}  $

The photovoltaic performances of the pristine perovskite device and MPIB-perovskite device have been investigated by employing a device structure of ITO/PEDOT:PSS/perovskite or MPIB-perovskite(x)/PC  $  _{61}  $  BM/BCP/Ag. The current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics of the pristine perovskite device and MPIB-perovskite (0.5) device under the illumination of AM 1.5G are presented in Figure 3a. The pristine perovskite device exhibits a PCE of 15.9% with a short-circuit current (  $  J_{sc}  $  ) of 19.20 mA·cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , an open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ) of 1.028 V, and a fill factor (FF) of 0.81. On the other hand, the champion device of the MPIB-perovskite (0.5) case achieves a PCE of 18.2% with a   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.114 V, a   $  J_{SC}  $   of 19.83 mA·cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and a FF of 0.82. In other terms, an obvious enhancement of photovoltaic performance has been observed in the MPIB-perovskite (0.5) device. This improvement is consistent with the result obtained from the external quantum efficiency (EQE) and integrated   $  J_{SC}  $  . There is no obvious hysteresis in both devices based on p-i-n configuration, which can be ascribed to PCBM due to its important role in suppressing of hysteresis.  $  ^{38,39}  $   The even less hysteresis of MPIB modified device compared with the pristine device can be attributed to the passivation effect of MPIB.

To understand the influence of MPIB additive on the crystallinity of the perovskite film, X-ray diffraction (XRD) has been firstly carried out by comparing the pristine perovskite and MPIB-perovskite (0.5) film. As shown in Figure 4a and Figure S3, the pristine perovskite film shows a small peak at  $ 12^{\circ} $ , which is mainly attributed to the residual  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ . In general, the residual  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  origins from the incomplete growth of the perovskite crystal because of the fast but unbalanced reaction between  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and MAI during annealing process. In contrast, no obvious peak at the same position is observed in the MPIB-perovskite (0.5) film, which indicates the MPIB additive may incorporate with perovskite crystal and then remove the excess  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ . Another key issue is to understand the influence of MPIB additive on the absorption of the perovskite. Therefore, the UV-vis absorption measurement was performed for the above two films. As reported in Figure 4b, no obvious discrepancies have been observed in the absorption spectra between the pristine perovskite film and MPIB-perovskite (0.5) film. Such behavior indicates 0.5 mg/mL MPIB additive in perovskite film would not affect the optical absorption.

As stated before, a possible explanation of the improvement of electrical performance in MPIB-perovskite device can be ascribed to the reduced defects in this film due to the passivation of the uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $  . Aiming at confirming the above mechanism, we have performed a series of cross-checks. First, the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) has been employed to check the binding energy of the Pb atom at the surface of perovskite film. As shown in Figure 5a and Figure S3, the binding energy of Pb 4f shifts 0.2 eV to lower energy in the MPIB-perovskite when compared to that of pristine perovskite case. A similar behavior has been observed by Wang et al.  $  ^{27}  $   and Li et al.,  $  ^{43}  $   who attributes this shift to binding energy change of the Pb atom due to the chemical interaction between 1-alkyl-4-amino-1,2,4-triazolium and uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $  . Thus, this indicates that surface binding energy of Pb has been changed due to the adding of the MPIB additive in our case.

To further confirm the passivation of uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} $  is due to the MPIB additive, we have performed the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer aiming to understand the changing of N atom in MPIB additive. Three kinds of powder samples, including  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ , MPIB and  $ \mathrm{MPIB - PbI}_2 $ , have been prepared (see Experimental section) and tested. As displayed in Figure 5b and Figure S4, the stretching vibration of C=N at  $ 1629~\mathrm{cm^{-1}} $  is observed in MPIB sample. However,

two absorption band at 1618 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  and 1650 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  are recorded in the MPIB-PbI  $  _{2}  $  sample. Kanatzidis  $  ^{44}  $  and co-workers have already shown that passivation of Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  responds in a similar manner to thiazole additive, which can be ascribed to the chemical interaction of Pb-N. Therefore, in our case, both the red shift and the blue shift in the MPIB-PbI  $  _{2}  $  sample are mainly associated to the splitting of the C=N band into two C-N bonds in MPIB structure. In addition, it is found that the skeleton vibration peak of the aromatic ring (1572 cm  $  ^{-1}  $ ) is also split into two peaks at 1568 and 1560 cm  $  ^{-1}  $ , which indicates that the electron cloud density of the imidazole ring has been changed. Considering these results, it can be unambiguously affirmed that the passivation of Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  is due to the formation of Pb-N bond in our case.

A schematic vision of the passivation process that taking place between the lone-pair electron and uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   in the MPIB-perovskite sample is illustrated in Figure 5c. In general, the passivation of uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   leads to reduction of defects in the perovskite film. Within this context, the photoluminescence (PL) test has been carried out on the pristine perovskite film and MPIB-perovskite (0.5) film. As shown in Figure 5d, a strong PL intensity is observed in the MPIB-perovskite (0.5) film when compared to that of pristine perovskite film, which confirms the defect reduction due to the passivation of uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $  . In addition, in Fig.5e, we compared the time-resolved photoluminescence spectra of the two samples. For the pristine perovskite film,   $  \tau_{ave}  $   was determined to be 38.8 ns. Upon MPIB addition,   $  \tau_{ave}  $   was increased to 91.7 ns for the optimized film, indicating less defects and charge recombination in the MPIB-modified perovskites. Thus, we conclude that the Voc improvement may derive from the low non-radiative recombination of perovskite film  $  ^{39}  $  .
