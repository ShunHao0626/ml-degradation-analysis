# Performance Promotion through Dual-Interface Engineering of CuSCN Layers in Planar Perovskite Solar Cells

Yuzeng Xu, Ying Tian, Minna Hou, Yan Wu, Yi Ding,  $  ^{*}  $   Ying Zhao, Xiaodan Zhang, and Guofu Hou

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Although perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with organic hole-transporting layers (HTLs) have demonstrated the certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) over 25%. Unavoidable performance degradation in the atmosphere is still one of the key hurdles facing the practical application. In this work, low-temperature solution-processed CuSCN was attempted as the inorganic HTL, which has been expected to provide an appreciable enhancement in device stability. In addition, concerning the potential-induced degradation around the CuSCN/Au interface, a thin PTB7 layer was inserted as a spacer, yielding a dramatically promoted device stability. Furthermore, in order to avoid the destruction to perovskites by the solvent of CuSCN, PTAA was attempted as a protection sheath of perovskites. Consequently, with optimized dual-interface engineering, the best performing cell delivers a PCE of 18.41% (average 17.63%) and maintains approximately 98.8% of the initial value more than 1000 h without encapsulation, manifesting the superior stability. This approach paves a way for achieving PSCs with both high efficiency and long-term durability.

# INTRODUCTION

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) are a new paradigm in photovoltaic research because of their jumping efficiency surpassed over 25% in the last decade.  $  ^{1-6}  $   Benefiting from the low-cost, high efficiency, and facile fabrication process, PSCs are becoming a promising candidate in industry as the next-generation photovoltaic technology.  $  ^{7-9}  $   It is well known that most of the PSCs with high performance (PCE > 20%) succeeded by now are adopting organic materials, such as 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis[N,N-di(4-methoxyphenyl)amino]-9,9'-spiro-bifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD), as the hole-transporting layer (HTL), which offers a compact coverage of the active perovskite layer beneath and efficient carrier extraction as well as transportation.  $  ^{10-12}  $   However, these organic materials are commonly featured as expensive and susceptible in an ambient environment, casting a cloud in the large-scale commercialization of PSCs.  $  ^{13,14}  $   Hence, it is imperative to explore substitutions capable of developing PSCs with excellent long-term stability in a low-cost and simple fabrication process, meanwhile without sacrificing the performance. Thereby, dual-interface engineering is proposed to solve such problems in this work. We fabricated PSCs by adopting low-temperature solution-processed CuSCN as the inorganic HTL. In addition, PTB7 was introduced as a spacer to substantially improve device durability by prohibiting the potential-induced reaction between CuSCN and the Au electrode; meanwhile, it can also partially function as a passivation layer and annihilate the surface states, yielding a performance enhancement. Moreover, it has been found that the solvent of CuSCN is detrimental to perovskite films beneath; hence, a PTAA thin layer was fabricated on perovskites as a protection sheath. The best performing cell after dual-interface engineering delivers a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 18.41%, and resulted devices exhibit excellent stability, which still maintains approximately 98.8% of the initial efficiency after a more than 1000 h storage in a dry environment. Therefore, our strategy demonstrates an effective approach toward PSCs with both high efficiency and long-term stability.

# MATERIALS AND METHODS

Preparation of the CuSCN Film. CuSCN is dissolved in diethyl sulfide (DES, 30 mg/mL). Precursor is spin-cast using a dynamic spin method at 5000 rpm for 30 s and then baked on a hot plate in glovebox at 100 °C for 5 min.

Solar Cell Fabrication. The glass/ITO substrate is sequentially rinsed in detergent, distilled water, and isopropanol. The sheet resistance of ITO is  $ \sim 10\Omega / $  square, and the thicknesses of glass and ITO are 0.5 and  $ 140~\mathrm{nm} $ , respectively. The substrate is treated with UV ozone for  $ 20\mathrm{min} $  before electron transporting layer deposition. The  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  nanoparticle solution (Alfa Aesar) is diluted first  $ (\mathrm{NH}_3\cdot \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}/ $   $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 = 3:1) $  and then spin-cast on the substrate in ambient air. The film is annealed at  $ 150^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 30\mathrm{min} $ , followed by an UV ozone treatment for  $ 20\mathrm{min} $  before perovskite film deposition.

The perovskite layer is deposited by a two-step fabrication method;  $ 1.3\mathrm{M} $   $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  solution in dimethylformamide (DMF) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) mixture (DMF/DMSO = 9:1) is first spin-cast on a  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  layer at  $ 1500~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 30~s $  and

then annealed at 70 °C for 1 min; the mixture solution of FAI/MABr/MACl (60:6:6 mg in 1 mL of isopropanol) is spin-cast on the PbI  $  _{2}  $  layer at 1300 rpm for 30 s; finally, the film is transferred from the glovebox and annealed in ambient air (30–40% humidity) at 150 °C for 15 min, which delivers a high performance because of the formation of the PbI  $  _{2}  $  phase being critical for passivation in the perovskite layer. The sample is transferred back to the glovebox, and the PTAA solution (1 mg/mL in CB) is spin-cast above if necessary at 2000 rpm for 30 s. The CuSCN HTL is then fabricated with the method mentioned above. The PTB7 solution (1 mg/mL in CB) is spin-cast at 2000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, a 100 nm thick Au electrode is thermally evaporated at a pressure of 3 × 10  $  ^{-6}  $  Torr.

Characterizations. The device photo J-V curve is measured under 1 sun illumination using a Keithley 2400 digital source meter. Unless otherwise specified, bias is scanned from 1.2 to -0.2 V first (reverse scan) and returns back (forward scan) with a step of 70 mV and a scan speed of 30 mVs  $  ^{-1}  $  . The reverse curve is mainly adopted to evaluate the device performance. The light source is calibrated by Asahi Spectra Co., Ltd. Incident light power is checked before every measurement by using a standard silicon photodiode. The device size is 0.24 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ; to reduce the influence of the scattered light during the J-V measurement, we use a metal mask of 0.0887 cm  $  ^{2}  $  . The external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectrum is measured on an EQE system (CEP-2000MLQ, Bunkoukeiki Co., Ltd.) in the DC mode without any voltage bias. Stability test is recorded by regularly measuring the device photo J-V more than 40 days. Sample morphology is characterized by using a scanning electron microscope (JSM-6700F, JEOL, or Apreo S LoVac, FEI). The atomic force microscopy (AFM) image of the film surface is measured using an atomic force microscope (Dimension 3100 V, Veeco). The film crystal structure is examined on a Rigaku ATX-XRD diffractometer in an out-of-plane grazing incident diffraction mode. Cu Kα radiation (  $  \lambda = 0.15406  $   nm) is employed as the radiation source, and the incident angle is fixed to 1.2° and the out-of-plane exit angle is scanned from 5 to 60°. Film transmittance, reflectance, and absorbance are measured using a UV–vis–NIR spectrophotometer (Cary 5000, VARIAN). Photoluminescence (PL) spectra are recorded using a Fluorolog–HORIBA fluorometer. Time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurement is performed at room temperature with a laser wavelength of 470 nm.

# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Devices with the structure of Glass/ITO/SnO $ _2 $ /(FAPbI $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.97} $ (MAPbBr $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.03} $ /CuSCN/Au have been fabricated, and the detailed fabrication processes are described in the Solar Cell Fabrication section. Figure S1 illustrates the typical cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image and the possible band alignment of the device. Active layers with a thickness of around 550 nm are developed with perpendicularly arrayed large grains, which provide the efficient transportation of photogenerated carriers by eliminating grain boundaries along the carrier transportation direction. $ ^{29-31} $  The active layer is properly sandwiched with SnO $ _2 $  and CuSCN layers, and from the perspective of energy, photogenerated electrons and holes in perovskite films can be extracted fluently, respectively, without crossing any obvious energy barriers. The thickness of CuSCN was first optimized by systematically adjusting the precursor concentration, as shown in Figure S2. The device with a PCE of  $ \sim $ 16.13% (average 15.95%) has been achieved with a CuSCN concentration of 30 mg/mL, as shown in Table S1. The corresponding thickness of CuSCN in the device has been tailored to  $ \sim $ 50 nm, as confirmed from Figure S1, which guarantees a homogeneous coverage of the perovskite film beneath while maintaining a small series resistance ( $ R_s $ ) of  $ \sim $ 3.5  $ \Omega\cdot $ cm $ ^2 $  in the corresponding solar cell discussed below.

Figure 1 shows the typical  $ J - V $  curves and the stability assessment data of the corresponding device. However, the resultant pristine device has not shown expected long-term durability, and device PCE deteriorates to almost half ( $ \sim 8.6\% $ ) of the initial value after a  $ 1000\mathrm{h} $  shelf storage in a dry nitrogen atmosphere, as shown in Figure 1b. Concomitantly, as illustrated in Figure S3,  $ R_{\mathrm{s}} $  witnesses a linear ascent with storage time, while short circuit current ( $ J_{\mathrm{sc}} $ ) keeps almost stable, which consequently results in the decrease of open-circuit voltage ( $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $ ) because of the enhanced voltage drop divided on  $ R_{\mathrm{s}} $ . In addition, increased  $ R_{\mathrm{s}} $  can also accelerate the carrier recombination rather than transportation, ultimately leading to the descent of fill factor (FF) and shunt resistance ( $ R_{\mathrm{sh}} $ ), although the decrease of the latter one is also probably related to the change of interfaces at the initial stage of device operation. Therefore, it is believed that the increase of  $ R_{\mathrm{s}} $  plays a prominent role for the deterioration of device performance. It has been proposed that reactive -SCN radicals in CuSCN are prone to react with the Au electrode resulting in an undesired

potential barrier for carriers, especially when the device is under an electrical bias.  $  ^{18,32,33}  $   This has also been confirmed in this work, as shown in Figure 2, which illustrates the elemental mapping of the CuSCN surface by peeling off the Au electrode evaporated above. Meanwhile, the atomic ratio of each element can be derived quantitatively through the corresponding spectra of energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), as shown in Figure S4. Au with an atomic percent as high as 0.43 at. % has been detected, implying the remarkable Au diffusion and reaction into CuSCN. Therefore, potential-induced degradation between CuSCN and the Au electrode might be affordable for the increase of   $  R_{s}  $   and device deterioration.

Accordingly, a strategy prohibiting the undesirable reaction between CuSCN and the gold electrode has been proposed. A thin PTB7 layer (  $  \sim5  $   nm) has been introduced in between as a spacer to avoid the direct contact of CuSCN and gold. PTB7 has been selected considering the suitable HOMO energy level of   $  \sim-5.3  $   eV, matching well with the energy level of CuSCN and Au, as illustrated in Figure S1, and hence guarantees efficient carrier transportation.  $  ^{34}  $   The Au diffusion and reaction process has been substantially prohibited, as shown in Figure 2. The Au atomic ratio has been suppressed effectively to 0.02 at.% by employing the PTB7 spacer. Consequently, device stability has been prominently improved, as shown in Figures 1b and S3. Series resistance and other photovoltaic parameters keep almost stable during a 1000 h storage in the dry atmosphere; device PCE just shows a trifling decline from 16.32 to 16.11%, demonstrating the effectiveness of our strategy. Moreover, compared with the pristine device, a slight enhancement in initial PCE from   $  \sim16.13  $   to   $  \sim16.32\%  $   has been realized, as shown in Figure 1a, which is probably because PTB7 can also partially passivate the defects on the CuSCN/Au interface.

On the other hand, employing solution-processed CuSCN in PSCs brings a common intractable problem, that is, most of the highly polar solvents applicable for CuSCN could damage the susceptible perovskite layer beneath, which has also been encountered in this work.  $  ^{28,35,36}  $   Figure 3 illustrates the SEM images of the perovskite surface, and the pristine perovskite exhibits a typical compact morphology assembled with large grains in the size from several hundred nanometers to micrometers, as shown in Figure 3a. Diethyl sulfide (DES), which is employed as the solvent of CuSCN in this work, was then spin-cast on the perovskite surface. Figure 4a shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra of perovskite films; the peaks centered at   $  \sim12  $   and   $  \sim14^{\circ}  $   are assigned to   $  PbI_{2}  $   (001) and perovskite (110), respectively. A prominent   $  PbI_{2}  $   peak can be detected in all samples, and it has been proposed that moderate   $  PbI_{2}  $   phase in films is practically beneficial for device performance by effectively passivating the defects in perovskites; however, excessive   $  PbI_{2}  $   will conversely degrade the device performance.  $  ^{37-39}  $   The analyzed results of   $  PbI_{2}  $   (001) and perovskite (110) diffraction peaks are illustrated in Table S2, coinciding with the SEM results; an enhanced   $  PbI_{2}  $   peak after DES treatment manifests the partial decomposition of the perovskite layer. Moreover, the FWHM of the perovskite (110) peak broadened slightly, denoting the considerable deterioration of perovskite crystallinity in terms of the Scherrer equation commonly employed for crystal size estimation.  $  ^{40,41}  $   Therefore, it is conclusive that the DES solvent involved in CuSCN film fabrication is detrimental to the perovskite film beneath. Accordingly, adopting an effective method to protect perovskite films during CuSCN film fabrication is essential toward efficient photovoltaics.

Hence, a thin PTAA layer (  $  \sim  $  5 nm) as a protection sheath has been prefabricated on the perovskite surface before CuSCN deposition to avoid the solvent damage.
