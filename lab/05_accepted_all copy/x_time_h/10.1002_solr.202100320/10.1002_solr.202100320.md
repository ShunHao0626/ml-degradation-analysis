# Multifunctional Reductive Molecular Modulator toward Efficient and Stable Perovskite Solar Cells

Mengjia Li, Deyu Gao, Boxue Zhang, Sai Xu, Xinmeng Zhuang, Chen Wang, Liqun Yang, Xiaohui Ma, Shijian Zheng, Hongwei Song,* Jiangzhao Chen,* and Cong Chen*

The defects (e.g.,   $  I_{2}  $  , anion, and cation vacancies) in the perovskite films are detrimental to the power conversion efficiency (PCE) and stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Herein, a multifunctional additive molecule 4-fluorophenylhydrazine hydrochloride (denoted as 4F-PHCl) is reported, which can improve perovskite crystallization, passivate film defects, and enhance film moisture stability, finally leading to the simultaneous increase in PCE and stability. It is revealed that the hydrazine functional group can effectively reduce the   $  I_{2}  $   defects back to I'. In addition, the hydrazinium contained cations and chloride anions can passivate the cationic defects (like anion vacancies) and anionic defects (like cation vacancies), respectively. The hydrophobic fluorinated benzene ring should be mainly responsible for the enhanced moisture stability of films and devices. As a result, the 4F-PHCl-modified device achieves a promising efficiency of 21.80% along with a high open-circuit voltage of 1172 mV and exhibits excellent long-term ambient stability. A guide for developing multifunctional additive molecules for the simultaneous enhancement of efficiency and stability is provided.

In this work, we develop a multifunctional additive molecule, 4-fluorophenylhydrazine hydrochloride (denoted as 4F-PHCl), which can improve perovskite crystallization, passivate film defects, and enhance film moisture stability, finally leading to the simultaneous increase in PCE and stability. Second, the incorporation of fluorine-substituted benzene ring was expected to be able to improve the moisture stability of perovskite film and device. We first investigated the chemical interaction between 4F-PHCl and perovskites by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurement (Figure 1c–e and Figure S1, Supporting Information). As shown in Figure 1c, the peak of F 1s was detected in the 4F-PHCl modified perovskite film, indicating the existence of 4F-PHCl in the final perovskite films. With the introduction of 4F-PHCl, we can confirm the chemical interaction between 4F-PHCl and the perovskite film by a shift to the increased binding energy in the peaks of Pb $  ^{2+}  $  (Figure 1e). Due to the ionic properties of perovskite, uncoordinated Pb $  ^{2+}  $  is a widespread defect in perovskite films. The introduction of 4F-PHCl (4F-PH $  ^{+}  $  cation and Cl $  ^{-}  $  anion) can interact with perovskite, in which Cl $  ^{-}  $  can effectively interact with uncoordinated Pb $  ^{2+}  $ . At the same time, 4F-PH $  ^{+}  $  can effectively fill the vacancy defects of organic cations (FA $  ^{+}  $ , MA $  ^{+}  $ ). The aforementioned results reveal that amino-containing 4F-PHCl can bind to the uncoordinated Pb $  ^{2+}  $  and fill the organic vacancy at the crystal boundary. $ ^{[28,29]} $  We also found that “C-N” characteristic peak from C 1s and N 1s belonging to FA $  ^{+}  $  moves to the reduced binding energy (Figure 1d and Figure S1, Supporting Information), which can be attributed to the electron-rich environment of FA $  ^{+}  $  that is related to the electron-donor effect of phenyl-containing 4F-PHCl on the matrix perovskite. $ ^{[30]} $  Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of 4F-PHCl and PbI $  _{2}  $  were Then characterized to verify the interaction and function mechanism of 4F-PHCl in perovskite (Figure 1f,g). It can be seen that 4F-PHCl showed a characteristic absorption at 3452 cm $  ^{-1}  $ , which is assigned to N–H in Figure 1g. The peak of 4F-PHCl mentioned earlier was shifted to 3440 cm $  ^{-1}  $  in the 4F-PHCl-modified films, which can be related to the coordination bonds formation by 4F-PHCl with PbI $  _{2}  $ .

To clarify the chemical interaction between 4F-PHCl and perovskite and determine the reduction of   $  I_{2}  $   to   $  I^{-}  $ , we subsequently conducted an in-depth analysis through the ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption spectra by characterizing the characteristic absorption peaks of   $  I_{2}  $   or   $  I_{3}^{-}  $   (365 nm) and   $  I^{-}  $   in the fresh and aged FAI solution with and without 4F-PHCl additive.  $  ^{[31,32]}  $   Consistent with previous reports, we can see that the color of the FAI precursor solution turned yellow after aged in the air for 7 days, revealing that the   $  I^{-}  $   in FAI precursor solution will inevitably be oxidized to   $  I_{2}  $   during the aging process,  $  ^{[33]}  $   as is shown in Figure S2a,b Supporting Information. Specifically, the absorption peak at 365 nm (Figure S2c, Supporting Information) in the absorption spectrum of the aged solution can be ascribed to   $  I_{2}  $   or   $  I_{3}^{-}  $ . After adding 4F-PHCl into the aged FAI solution, the peak at 365 nm in the absorption spectrum of the solution reduced drastically, confirming that the hydrazine group of 4F-PHCl can effectively reduce the   $  I_{2}  $   or   $  I_{3}^{-}  $  defects back to   $  I^{-}  $ . Consequently, we can infer that the hydrazine group of 4F-PHCl can effectively reduce the   $  I_{2}  $   defects back to   $  I^{-}  $ .

Then we fabricated the PSCs with the device structure of FTO/  $  SnO_{2}/Cs_{0.05}(FA_{0.83}MA_{0.17})_{0.95}Pb(I_{0.83}Br_{0.17})_{3}/Spiro-OMeTAD/Ag  $  . The effects of 4F-PHCl modification concentration on device performance were compared. The perovskite films were prepared by the one-step antisolvent method, as described in detail in the Experimental Section. Figure 2a shows a statistical distribution diagram of PCEs of the devices modified by different concentrations of 4F-PHCl, and the corresponding photovoltaic parameters are exhibited in Table S1, Supporting Information. At the same time, the J-V curves of the champion devices modified by different concentrations of 4F-PHCl are presented in Figure S3, Supporting Information. The control device exhibited an average short-circuit current density (  $  J_{SC}  $  ) of   $  22.77 \pm 0.39  $   mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , an average   $  V_{OC}  $   of   $  1050 \pm 30  $   mV, an average fill factor (FF) of   $  75.64 \pm 3.25\%  $  , and an average PCE of   $  18.08 \pm 1.34\%  $  , respectively. In contrast, the device modified by 4F-PHCl of   $  1.0 mg mL^{-1}  $   gave an average   $  J_{SC}  $   of   $  23.02 \pm 0.36  $   mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , an average   $  V_{OC}  $   of   $  1151 \pm 30  $   mV, an average FF of   $  77.65 \pm 2.93\%  $  , and an average PCE of   $  20.60 \pm 1.20\%  $  , respectively. Thereinafter, the perovskite film or device without 4F-PHCl was denoted as the control, whereas the perovskite film or device modified by the optimal concentration of 4F-PHCl was denoted as the 4F-PHCl modified. The J-V curves of the best-performing control and 4F-PHCl modified devices are shown in Figure 2b. The corresponding photovoltaic parameters are shown in Table 1. As can be seen, the 4F-PHCl modified PSC exhibited a PCE of 21.80% in reverse scan (RS) and a PCE of 21.03% in forward scan (FS). By contrast, the control device presented a PCE of 19.32% in RS and a PCE of 18.02% in FS. Evidently, the hysteresis was slightly reduced after 4F-PHCl modification. Figure 2c shows the representative external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectrum of the best-performing control and 4F-PHCl-modified devices. The EQE intensity was almost not affected by the introduction of 4F-PHCl. The   $  J_{SC}  $   obtained from the J-V curve matched well with the integrated value from the EQE curves. The stable output current density and PCE of the control and 4F-PHCl-modified devices are shown in Figure 2d. A current density of   $  21.7 mA cm^{-2}  $   and correspondingly a PCE of 18.21% were obtained for the control device measured at 0.815 V, whereas a current density of   $  22.6 mA cm^{-2}  $   and correspondingly a PCE of 21.07% were obtained for the 4F-PHCl-modified device measured at 0.920 V after 150 s.

Figure 3a,b and S4, Supporting Information, show the top-morphology scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the perovskite films modified with different concentrations of 4F-PHCl. Adding 4F-PHCl resulted in smoother morphology with larger grains and thus smaller GB density, which was one of the reasons for suppressed nonradiative recombination and thus improved  $  V_{\mathrm{OC}}  $  and FF. The cross-sectional SEM images for control and 4F-PHCl-modified devices are shown in Figure 3c,d. It was seen that the cross-section of the control perovskite film consisted of several small grains while a large grain is across the whole cross-section of the 4F-PHCl-modified perovskite film. These top-morphology and cross-sectional SEM images indicated that the grain size of perovskite films was increased, and crystallization was improved after incorporating 4F-PHCl. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns revealed that (Figure 4a and S5, Supporting Information) all the films showed the same diffraction peaks at 14.2°, 20.3°, 24.7°, 28.2°, 31.6°, 35.3°, 40.4°, and 43.7°, which can be assigned to the crystallographic planes of (100), (110), (111), (200), (210), (211), (220), and (221), respectively.  $  ^{[34]}  $   Compared with the control film, the intensity of main diffraction peaks was remarkably increased in the 4F-PHCl-modified film, which was indicative of

Moreover, 4F-PHCl-modified perovskite films exhibit the linewidth with full width at half maxima (FWHM) value of 0.0953 at (100) plane (Figure S6, Supporting Information), which is less than that of 0.1061 for the control perovskite film. The narrowed linewidth confirmed the enhanced crystallinity.  $  ^{[35,36]}  $   Furthermore, the intensity ratio of (100) and (200) peaks enhanced from 2.09 to 2.19 after 4F-PHCl modification, indicating the improved film crystallinity and crystal orientation (Table S2, Supporting Information).

To gain insight into the trap state density, we investigated the Urbach energy   $  (E_{\mathrm{u}})  $   of the perovskite films by absorption spectra of the perovskite films (Figure S7, Supporting Information). In the case of control films, the   $  E_{u}  $   was calculated to be 50 meV while it decreased to 34 meV after 4F-PHCl modification (Figure 4b), which was most likely attributed to the lower band edge disorder and trap state density. Figure 4c,d shows steady-state photoluminescence (SSPL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectra of the control and 4F-PHCl modified perovskite films. The photoluminescence intensity was much increased after 4F-PHCl modification, which was in accordance with much-enhanced carrier lifetimes from 74.15 ns of the control film to 176.02 ns of the 4F-PHCl-modified film. Improved crystallization and effective defect passivation should be mainly responsible for the improved carrier lifetimes.

# 3. Conclusions

In summary, we demonstrated a multifunctional additive molecule 4F-PHCl to improve perovskite crystallization, passivate film defects, and enhance film moisture stability, translating into a simultaneous increase in PCE and stability. It is demonstrated that the hydrazine functional group can effectively reduce the   $  I_{2}  $   defects back into   $  I^{-}  $  . In addition, the hydrazinium contained cations and chloride anions can passivate the cationic defects and anionic defects, respectively. The hydrophobic fluorinated benzene ring contributes to enhanced moisture stability of perovskite films and devices. As a result, the 4F-PHCl-modified device could exhibit a much higher PCE of 21.80% as compared with that of 19.32% for the control device. Furthermore, the unencapsulated device with 4F-PHCl displays excellent long-term ambient stability. This work provides a guide for developing multifunctional additive molecules for achieving simultaneous enhancement in efficiency and stability.

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials: All the reagents and chemicals were purchased from commercial sources and used without further purification. The   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloid precursor was obtained from Alfa Aesar (tin (IV) oxide).   $  PbI_{2}  $   was purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. Methylammonium bromide (MABr) was purchased from Greatcell solar materials. Cesium iodide (CsI) (99.9%), lead bromide (  $  PbBr_{2}  $  ) (99.9%), chlorobenzene (CB) (99.5%), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) (>99.9%), and dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) (>99.8%) were purchased from Macklin. Formamidinium iodide (FAI) and spiro-OMeTAD were bought from Advanced Election Technology Co., Ltd. 4-Tert-butylpyridine (t-BP), acetonitrile (ACN) (>99.9%), and lithiumbis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (Li-TFSI) were received from Aladdin. 4-Fluorophenylhydrazine hydrochloride (CAS:823-85-8, Chemical structure:   $  C_{6}H_{7}FN_{2}\cdot HCl  $  ) was obtained from Alfa Aesar.

Device Fabrication: First, FTO-coated glass substrates were etched by a Laser etcher before cleaning. Later, etched substrates were cleaned with glass cleaner, detergent water, deionized water, and absolute ethyl alcohol sequentially. Each step took about 20 min by ultrasonication. The substrates were dried with dry   $  N_{2}  $   and then cleaned by UV-  $  O_{3}  $   for 15 min. Compact   $  SnO_{2}  $   electron transporting layer was deposited on FTO/glass by the spin-casting method. After the deposition, the FTO/  $  SnO_{2}  $   substrates were annealed at 150°C in the air for 30 min. Then the FTO/cp-  $  SnO_{2}  $   substrates were modified with UV-ozone for 15 min. The perovskite films were deposited through an antisolvent-assisted one-step solvent engineering method. Perovskite precursor solution contains CsI (0.06 M), FAI (1.0 M),   $  PbI_{2}  $   (1.1 M), MABr (0.2 M), and   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (0.2 M) in anhydrous DMF:DMSO(4:1, volume ratio). Then the perovskite precursor was spin coated first at 600 rpm for 6 s and then at 4000 rpm for 30 s. During the second step, 500 μL anhydrous diethyl ether was dropped onto the spinning substrates at the first 15 s. The substrates were then annealed at 110°C for 60 min. The whole process was operated in a nitrogen-filled glovebox. As for 4F-PHCl-modified perovskite film, a certain amount of 4F-PHCl powder was first dissolved in DMSO solution and then added to the perovskite precursor solution in proportion. The hole transporting layer was prepared by spin coating the spiro-OMeTAD solution first at 600 rpm for 4 s and then at 3000 rpm for 30 s. The spiro-OMeTAD solution consists of 73.2 mg spiro-OMeTAD, 28.8 μL 4-tert-butylpyridine

(t-BP), 17.5 μL lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (Li-TFSI) solution (predissolved in acetonitrile at a concentration of 520 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ), and 1 mL chlorobenzene. Finally, an 80 nm Au or 80 nm Ag layer was deposited via thermal evaporation in a vacuum chamber (5 × 10  $  ^{-4}  $  Pa). To test the long-term stability of the device, we replaced the Ag electrode with the gold electrode.

Device Characterization: The current density–voltage (J-V) characterizations were measured by a Keithley 2400 source measurement unit under simulated AM 1.5 irradiation (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) with a standard xenon-lamp-based solar simulator (7ISO503A, SOFN INSTRUMENTS). Around 50 devices were fabricated and measured independently under each experimental condition to warrant the reproducibility of the result and to obtain the statistical data of the photovoltaic parameters. The J-V curves were measured by forward (from -0.2 to 1.2 V forward bias) or reverse (from 1.2 to -0.2 V) scans. The SEM images were obtained via the field-emission scanning electron microscope (JEOL 7610F) using secondary electron. The scanning voltage is 5.5 kV. Agilent 5500 scanning probe microscopy was utilized for AFM topography and KPFM in tapping mode, whereas CS-AFM was in contact mode with 1 V applied bias. XRD patterns were attained on a Rigaku SmartLab (9 kW) X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (0.154 nm). The measuring power is 4 kW, the scanning angle is 10°–60°, and the scanning speed is 8° per minute.

The UV–vis spectroscopy spectra were recorded on a Shimadzu UV-1900 spectrophotometer. The SSPL and TRPL were acquired using the FLS980 Series of Fluorescence Spectrometers with an excitation wavelength of 480 nm. XPS measurements were carried out by ESCALAB 250Xi. EQE was measured by QE-IRIS of Enlitech. EIS measurements were carried out in the dark using an electrochemical workstation (AMETEK VersaSTAT 3F) with a frequency range from 1 Hz to 1 MHz under 0 V in the dark. Mott–Schottky measurement was carried out using the same instrument as used in EIS but at a fixed frequency of 1000 Hz. The applied bias voltage range is 0–1.5 V.
