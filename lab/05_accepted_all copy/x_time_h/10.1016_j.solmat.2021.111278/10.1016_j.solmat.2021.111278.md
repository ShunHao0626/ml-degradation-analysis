# 1. Introduction

Although the state-of-the-art efficiency of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) is over 25% [1]. Their commercialization is still severely impeded so far mainly due to inferior stability issues. Understanding and overcoming the degradation problems, especially long-term operational instability, are urgently demanded by the community towards commercialization [2–4].

Degradation occurs at all different parts of a device stack. The related factors are generally classified into extrinsic and intrinsic. The former is environmental induced, including moisture [5,6], oxygen [7,8], heat [9–12] and ultraviolet light [13], etc. The latter refers to light illumination [13], ion migration [14], bias [15,16] and electric field [17] etc. Various strategies [18] have been adopted to promote the stability of different parts in stack, so far with more efforts on extrinsic factors [4]. Among them, most works focus on perovskite material/layer, e.g. substitution of A site cation [19,20] and X site anion [21], and elimination of defects at grain boundaries or film surface [18], to restrain

Operational stability is becoming one of the most crucial parameters for commercialization of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). However the stability issue of PSCs is currently far from being resolved due to complicated and still unclear degradations. In this work, we systematically demonstrate the degradation of metal cathode PSCs under operation conditions. Discussions about influence of intrinsic factors i.e. light illumination, voltage and current, on device degradation are conducted. It is concluded that metal cathode stripping/plating behavior (electrochemical metallization effect) due to current together with perovskite degradation could dominate the device degradation. It is deduced that electrochemical cell in the PSC system could be formed by metal/counter electrodes and perovskite electrolyte. Both cells accelerate degradation of metal electrode and perovskite in working conditions, hence device degradation. These insights into the degradation and mechanisms can help further understand the working principle and solve the instability problem of perovskite-based optoelectronic devices.

Fewer works treat with electrodes and device structure   $  [23]  $  . To rule out the possibility that ETL interlayers participate in the degradation reactions that disintegrates the copper cathode, control devices (ITO/MAPbI $ _3 $ /Cu) without any interlayers were tested under the same conditions. Electric connections for each individual device on substrate were planned exactly the same as applied for interlayer-based devices (Fig. 4a). Similar but accelerated degradation was observed (Fig. 4b and c). Copper cathode at C area was seriously degraded in 2 days. Pad-shaped coppers were demonstrated again by SEM image and elemental mapping. However the 'carbon' accumulation was not observed in this case (Fig. 4f), confirming the accumulated 'carbons' in Fig. 3h were from HTL, implying the role of HTL together with ITO as cathode in electrochemical metallization reaction, which is discussed in the following section. Based on these observations, we believe that the ETLs were not involved in the cathode degradation reactions. But the reactions between perovskite and copper should account for that.

Based on the results, we propose electrochemical metallization effect to explain the degradation and mechanism for metal cathode PSC devices. It should mainly account for the fast device degradation/failure in most cases for this type of PSCs, rather than perovskite decomposition alone. Fig. 4g illustrates the schematic diagram of cell in open circuit under illumination. The separated electrons and holes are collected at Cu and ITO electrode till balanced, to form photogenerated voltage  $ (\mathrm{V_{OC}}) $ . Similar electric filed forms at anode interface and helps to form the final voltage. Once resistor load is introduced (Fig. 4h), electrons on Cu cathode will flow through the load to supply power. Meanwhile, due to ionic conductivity of perovskite [30,52], the active layer can serve as solid state electrolyte. Together with Cu and counter electrode ITO, they could form an electrochemical cell (Fig. 4i). With strong reducibility,  $ \mathrm{Cu}^0 $  can be dissolved and changed to  $ \mathrm{Cu}^+ $  by releasing electron through redox reaction with perovskite. Following perovskite decomposition [10,40], it is hypothesized that iodine or iodide products ( $ \mathrm{I}_2 $ , HI, MAI or  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ ) form first. Then  $ \mathrm{Cu}^0 $  reacts with iodine/iodide (most probably HI [53]) to form  $ \mathrm{Cu}^+ $  and gets dissolved. The proposed chemical reactions are shown in supporting information. Once electrodes are connected via resistor under illumination, electrons will flow through external load. The dissolved  $ \mathrm{Cu}^+ $  ions will move towards counter electrode under electric field and get reduced to  $ \mathrm{Cu}^0 $  by accepting electron back from external circuit, causing plating of Cu on anode and device degradation. We consider the slow decay process (Fig. 1c) is attributed to degradation of both Cu electrode and perovskite, but the drastic drop/failure could be mainly attributed to breakdown of cathode. Although results and explanations here are based on copper cathode, the conclusions can be applied to other metal electrodes, like Al, Ag, Au, etc. But the effect would be dependent on reduction potential of metals. This is confirmed by aging test of devices under further accelerated conditions, i.e. short circuit and illumination (Figure S5). Similar efficiency drop and cathode degradation were recorded for Cu and Ag device (Figure S6a, b). But it was not observed for Au device in the same time scale (Figure S6c), probably due to its high reduction potential ( $ \sim 1.5\mathrm{V} $ , higher than  $ \mathrm{V_{OC}} $  and  $ \mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{max}} $ ), compared to Cu (0.52 V) and Ag (0.8 V). This is consistent with and could also partly explain for the above mentioned fact that all those reported highly stable PSCs are based on Au electrode.

It is deduced that electrochemical cell and photovoltaic cell could simultaneously exist in a PSC system. Both of them accelerate degradation of metal electrode and perovskite in working conditions. It should be noted that, the current direction of metal cathode PSC in operation happens to be coincide with that of the electrochemical cell in discharge process, which promotes the metallization effect. This could explain for the much better stability of metal anode PSCs currently achieved in the community compared to metal cathode PSCs, especially under working conditions.

To further certify the proposed mechanism, aging test of devices under fixed bias was carried out without light illumination. Similar degradation was recorded as expected (Figure S7). It was found the degradation rate is related to bias voltage. The results confirm again the direct influence of current on metal cathode degradation and meanwhile point out that the light illumination is not necessarily needed for that. Moreover, it is deduced that 'metal-anode' perovskite-based LED devices would suffer from similar problem in operation, as evidenced by short lifetimes (minutes to hours) in literature [54]. # 3. Conclusions

In summary, we demonstrated degradation and mechanisms of metal cathode PSCs under MPP operation. Discussions with emphasis on influence of intrinsic factors of light, voltage and current on degradation were mainly conducted. Firstly, relative fast degradation of device was demonstrated in MPP operation. Under triple-stress of intrinsic factors, metal cathode degradation due to stripping/plating behavior together with perovskite degradation causes 50% efficiency loss in   $  \sim  $  210 h. Breakdown of metal cathode should account for the fast drop/failure at   $  \sim  $  250 h. Without current flow, the light and voltage present limited degradation to device in the same time scale. We propose electrochemical metallization effect for the degradation and mechanism of device under operation. This effect could dominate the degradation/failure of different type of perovskite-based optoelectronic devices in operation. It is suggested that designing proper electrode (like non-metal electrode) or ideal barrier layer to avoid the electrochemical metallization effect is crucial to achieve stable PSCs. These insights into the degradation and mechanisms can help further understand the working principle of perovskite-based optoelectronic devices and solve their instability problems.

# 4. Experimental section

# 4.1.Materials

Dimethylformamide (DMF) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), isopropanol (IPA) and chlorobenzene (CB) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Lead iodine  $ (\mathrm{PbI}_2) $ , methylammonium iodide (MAI), (6,6)-phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PC61BM), and bathocuproine (BCP) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology in China.

# 4.2. Device fabrication

Patterned ITO substrates (2 cm × 2 cm) were ultrasonically cleaned with detergent, deionized water, acetone and isopropanol (IPA) in sequential. Before using, ITO was treated with ultraviolet ozone for 10

min. Then the HTL was spin-coated on substrate with a P3CT-N solution (1 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  in methanol) at 4000 r.p.m. for 30 s, and annealed in ambient air at 100 °C for 10 min. The perovskite layer is deposited by a typical anti-solvent method. Perovskite precursor solution is prepared by mixing 1.45 M PbI  $  _{2}  $  and 1.45 M MAI in anhydrous DMF:DMSO (9:1) solvent, then spin-coated on ITO/P3CT-N at 4000 r.p.m. for 20 s. During spincoating, 270 μL chlorobenzene is dropped on the center of the substrate 9 s prior to the end of the program. The substrate is then annealed at 60 °C for 2 min and 80 °C for 5 min to form perovskite layer. After cooling down to room temperature, PC61BM (10 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  in chlorobenzene) was spin-coated on perovskite layer at 2000 r.p.m. for 30 s to form ETL. Finally, 20 nm C60, 8 nm BCP and 100 nm metal electrode were deposited in sequential through a shadow mask by thermally evaporation. The active area defined by the overlap of Cu and ITO is 0.09 cm  $  ^{2}  $ .

# 5. Characterizations

Current density-voltage (J-V) curves were measured using a Keithley model 2400 digital source meter. A Newport Sol3A solar simulator with AM 1.5G spectrum and light intensity of  $ 100\mathrm{Mwcm}^{-2} $  was used for illumination. The intensity of which was determined by a calibrated crystalline Si-cell.

For stability test, white LED (spectra region: 430–800 nm, Suzhou D&R instruments Co., Ltd. PVLT-6001M-16A) was continuously applied to device for illumination. Its intensity was adjusted in a way that the generated photocurrent matched the one under a solar simulator. Semiconductor refrigeration technique was applied to control the device temperature at   $  30\ ^{\circ}C  $   in aging process to avoid heat influence. The moisture and   $  O_{2}  $   concentrations were kept below 0.1 p.p.m for ageing in   $  N_{2}  $   atmosphere. The aging test for devices in ambient air was carried out at room temperature, with relative humidity (RH) of   $  \sim65\%  $  . J-V scan was conducted in situ (without moving the devices) every 30 min to trace the performance and MPP point. Variable resistor as load was introduced to connect with device immediately after each J-V scan, to make the device always work at MPP.

For device disassembling, top metal cathode was removed by scotch tape. Perovskite materials and interlayers were dissolved and cleaned completely through repeated rinse with their corresponding solvents DMF, DMSO, chlorobenzene, isopropanol and methanol.

XRD patterns for perovskite at A, B and C area were conducted on Bruker D8 Discover two-dimensional X-ray diffractometer with   $  \Phi  $   2 mm spot, with Cu-Kα radiation, at 40 kV and 40 mA, scan range 10–90°, scan step 20°, and scan rate 100 s/step. By which the 1D-diffraction patterns were obtained via integrating. X-ray powder diffraction analysis was performed for the as-prepared perovskite film and aged perovskite in ambient air on a Bruker D8 Advance equipment.

GDOES profiles of device stack were obtained on GDA750HP (Spectruma Analytik GmbH), with GDS anode diameter  $ 2.5\mathrm{mm} $ , Carrier gas Argon, analysis conditions  $ 700\mathrm{V} $ ,  $ 2.5\mathrm{hPa} $ .

SEM measurements were conducted on Verios G4 UC (Thermo Scientific, USA). The elemental analysis and element mapping were obtained by OXFORD X-Max50 EDS equipped on the Verios G4 UC. Optical photos were obtained with optical microscope Olympus BX41M-LED ( $ \times $  250). Photograph of devices were obtained by digital camera.

The ITO cathode was deposited using a 3 inch ITO target at room temperature by magnetron sputtering system. Before deposition, the sputtering chamber was evacuated down to  $ 5 \times 10^{-4} $  Pa. The Ar gas flow rate was controlled at 30 sccm with the pressure maintained at 0.7 Pa during sputtering. To mitigate ion bombardment damage to solar cells, a buffer ITO layer with thickness of  $ 10\mathrm{nm} $  was fabricated using low RF sputtering power of  $ 50\mathrm{W} $ . Then top ITO layer with  $ 90\mathrm{nm} $  was deposited using fixed power of  $ 100\mathrm{W} $ .
