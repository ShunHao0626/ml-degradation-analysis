# Paradoxical Approach with a Hydrophilic Passivation Layer for Moisture-Stable, 23% Efficient Perovskite Solar Cells

Chunqing Ma and Nam-Gyu Park*

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Since the first report on the  $ \sim 10\% $  efficient solid-state perovskite solar cell (PSC) in 2012, perovskite photovoltaics surged swiftly. Although there has been a quantum leap in the power conversion efficiency (PCE), stability has still been an issue despite considerable progress in material and device stability. In this report, we propose a paradoxical approach for improving the stability of perovskite against moisture, which is a surface treatment of perovskite film with hydrophilic 2-aminoethanol hydroiodide (2AEI) or 4-amino-1-butanol hydroiodide (4ABI). The post-treated hydrophilic materials protect effectively the underneath perovskite layer from moisture because water molecules are absorbed by the hydrophilic layer. Over  $ 90\% $  of the initial PCE is retained after  $ 1000\mathrm{h} $  stored under relative humidity of  $ \sim 20\% $ , while a faster degradation is monitored from the untreated perovskite layer. The

surface anchored hydrophilic materials are found to improve PCE simultaneously, leading to a value of 23.25%, due to lowered trap-state distribution by passivating surface defects.

Since the seminal works by Miyasaka et al. and Park et al., organic-inorganic perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have shown great potential for the future photovoltaic field due to their high power conversion efficiency (PCE) and simple solution processability.  $  ^{1-6}  $   However, these perovskites are unstable under stimuli of moisture, high temperature, and electric field due to their ionic nature (i.e., weak bonds between organic cations and inorganic components).  $  ^{7-11}  $   This intrinsic instability imposes a challenge for the long-term application of PSCs, which may hinder their commercialization.

Efforts to improve the stability of PSCs have focused on hydrophobic passivation agents, which can protect the perovskite by repulsing water molecules.  $  ^{12-14}  $   These hydrophobic passivation agents are typically organic materials featuring the combination of functional groups and nonpolar hydrophobic parts, which can be attached to the perovskite by the functional group and improve water resistance by the hydrophobic tail.  $  ^{15-19}  $   For example,   $  C_{60}  $  , thiophene, pyridine, theophylline, alkylalkoxysilane, polymers, and their derivatives have been reported to improve the stability of PSCs.  $  ^{20-30}  $   Since hydrophobicity is improved by extending aliphatic carbon chain length, the passivation with such hydrophobic materials in between a perovskite layer and a charge transporting layer (hole or electron transporting layer) may

Post-treatment with Hydrophilic Materials on the Perovskite Film Surface. Hydrophilicity of 4ABI and 2AEI was first investigated. We stored 4ABI and 2AEI in a sealed bottle with a relative humidity (RH) of  $ \sim 80\% $  and weighed the chemicals regularly to monitor their ability to absorb water molecules. Formamidinium iodide (FAI) and the typically used hydrophobic phenylethylammonium iodide (PEAI) were also tested for comparison. As shown in Figure S1, the weight ratio of 4ABI and 2AEI increases rapidly compared to FAI and hydrophobic PEAI, indicating that 4ABI and 2AEI are more hydrophilic. This is due to the hydroxyl group that can absorb water molecules via hydrogen bonding.

The hydrophilic 4ABI and 2AEI were then spin-coated on the surface of the annealed perovskite films (see details in the Methods section and Figure 1a). Perovskite films post-treated with 4ABI and 2AEI are hereafter referred to as Pero/4ABI and Pero/2AEI, respectively. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra measured with the Pero/4ABI film and the Pero/2AEI film in Figure 1b show that the characteristic C–O stretching vibration of both Pero/4ABI and Pero/2AEI is observed at a frequency of   $  \sim1086~cm^{-1}  $   that is shifted to higher frequency compared to that of pure 4ABI (  $  \sim1070~cm^{-1}  $  ), which is attributed to a distortion of CO band and/or an underlying interaction between hydroxyl groups and perovskite layer.  $  ^{31,32}  $   The full FTIR spectra are shown in Figure S2. To further investigate the surface interactions, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were conducted. All peaks were calibrated using the C 1s peak at 284.6 eV to correct the shift of binding energies, as shown in Figure S3.  $  ^{33}  $   As shown in Figure 1c, the Pb 4f spectrum of the perovskite film without treatment (control) exhibits doublet peaks of   $  4f_{7/2}  $   and   $  4f_{5/2}  $   located at 138.05 and 142.90 eV, respectively.  $  ^{34,35}  $   The Pb 4f

XPS peaks of Pero/4ABI and Pero/2AEI samples shift toward higher binding energies (  $  4f_{7/2}  $  : 138.18 eV (4ABI) and 138.13 eV (2AEI),   $  4f_{5/2}  $  : 143.01 eV for both 4ABI and 2AEI) compared to those of the control sample, which is likely to result from the bonding between Pb ions and hydroxyl group on 4ABI or 2AEI. Moreover, the I 3d doublet peaks (Figure S4a) of the Pero/4ABI and Pero/2AEI samples also shift to slightly higher binding energies, probably due to ionic (  $  NH_{3}^{+}-I^{-}  $  ) and/or hydrogen bonds (hydrogen acts as an electron acceptor in hydrogen bonding). The shift in N 1s peaks (Figure S4b) of the Pero/4ABI and Pero/2AEI samples compared to the control sample is also attributed to the interaction between 4ABI or 2AEI and the perovskite.  $  ^{36}  $

The effects of the surface treatment with hydrophilic materials on grain size and surface morphology of the perovskite film were studied by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The grain size distribution (0.5   $  \mu  $  m-1.5   $  \mu  $  m) is not altered by the surface treatment (insets in Figure 1d–f and Figure S5), while surface morphology is changed from a flat surface to a contour-line-like surface (Figure 1d–f), indicating the modification of the perovskite surfaces by 4ABI and 2AEI. We attribute such modification to the enhanced crystallinity of perovskite by passivating the surface defects as confirmed by XRD measurements (Figure S6).  $  ^{37}  $   Compared to the control sample, no new peaks are observed in the low 2  $  \theta  $   region from the XRD data of Pero/4ABI and Pero/2AEI samples, indicating that a 2D structure is not likely to be formed on the perovskite surface. The absorption spectra of perovskite films before and after post-treatment with 4ABI and 2AEI are nearly identical (Figure S7), suggesting that the intrinsic absorption coefficient of bulk perovskite is not influenced.

Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) in Figure 3a shows an enhanced PL intensity after the surface treatment with 4ABI and 2AEI, which is indicative of an increase in radiative recombination or a reduced nonradiative recombination. Time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) data in Figure 3b are fit with a biexponential decay equation, where a relatively fast decay component (  $  \tau_{1}  $   around 1 ns) is assigned to carrier trapping induced by trap states on the surface while the much slower component (  $  \tau_{2}  $  ) is due to radiative recombination of free carriers in the bulk.  $  ^{39}  $   With the surface passivation of 4ABI and 2AEI, the fast decay component (  $  \tau_{1}  $  ) is increased from 0.84 ns (control) to 1.35 ns (4ABI) and 1.21 ns (2AEI), while   $  \tau_{2}  $   is significantly elongated from 245 ns (control) to 611 ns (4ABI) and 471 ns (2AEI), which supports that the surface treatment with hydrophilic materials reduces surface trap density and enhances charge carrier lifetime (see detailed fitting results in Table S1).

Capacitance at low frequency is increased by 3 orders of magnitude for the device with untreated perovskite (control) film as measured by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, which is, however, significantly reduced by surface treatment with 4ABI and 2AEI (Figure 3c). Stabilities of the Hydrophilic Materials Passivated PSCs. Long-term stability of PSCs was studied by storing the devices under ambient conditions with a RH of   $  \sim20\%  $  . PCE of the control device is degraded to around 60% of the initial value after 700 h, whereas both the 4ABI- and 2AEI-treated devices hardly drop PCE with time because 90% of the initial PCE is maintained after 1000 h (Figure 4a). The operational stability of the devices was also compared at the maximum power point (MPP) in Figure S13; the PCE of the control device degraded to 90% after 150 min, whereas those of the Pero/4ABI and Pero/2AEI devices remained at up to 95%. Furthermore, we investigated the water resistance of the control, Pero/4ABI, and Pero/2AEI films by exposure to air with a RH of   $  \sim80\%  $   for 1 day. Figure 4b shows photos and XRD results of the 1 day-aged perovskite films. The control sample quickly bleached because of its transformation to yellow phase as confirmed by the XRD pattern. On the other hand, the black color is sustained in the Pero/4ABI and Pero/2AEI films, which is indicative of strong waterproof characteristics of perovskite due to the hydrophilic passivation layer interacting with moisture. The surface-treated perovskite film is found to be thermally more stable than the untreated one as confirmed from thermal stability tests by treating the samples at 85 °C under a RH of   $  \sim20\%  $   (Figure 4c).  $  ^{51}  $   The control perovskite film is almost decomposed to PbI  $  _{2}  $  , while no obvious change in color is observed for the Pero/4ABI and Pero/2AEI samples. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted on FAI, 2AEI, 4ABI, and PEAI (Figure S14). The FAI and PEAI show a decomposition temperature of around 350 °C, while 2AEI and 4ABI show a little higher decomposition temperature of around 370 and 360 °C, respectively. The high decomposition temperature is beneficial for thermal stability of PSCs operating at a relatively low temperature less than   $  \sim85  $   °C.  $  ^{52}  $

The mechanism of the enhanced stability by hydrophilic material treatment has been considered to be different from that by hydrophobic compounds. To scrutinize the influence of the hydrophilic 4ABI and 2AEI on the surface properties of perovskite, a lateral Au/perovskite/Au configuration was prepared (see details in the Methods section).  $  ^{53}  $   In brief, two gold electrodes with   $  \sim30\ \mu m  $   distance were deposited on top of the perovskite surface, and the image of the device is shown in Figure S15. We measured the dark current of the lateral device under 2 V bias in vacuum (without water molecules) and in air with a RH of   $  \sim35\%  $   (with water molecules) sequentially to test the current response to water molecules.

Figure S16 clearly demonstrates the current change with the presence and absence of water molecules, from which the ratio of the current in air to the current in vacuum,   $  I(\text{Air})/I(\text{Vac})  $  , is estimated. Figure 4d shows that   $  I(\text{Air})/I(\text{Vac})  $   is relatively low for the control sample, indicating that the untreated perovskite surface is less sensitive to the water molecules. However,   $  I(\text{Air})/I(\text{Vac})  $   is estimated to be 2.4 and 10.5 for Pero/4ABI and Pero/2AEI, respectively, which means that the current measured in air is 2.4 and 10.5 times higher than that measured in vacuum. The increase of current in air with a RH of 35% is then attributed to the water molecules absorbed by the abundant hydroxyl groups of 4ABI and 2AEI deposited on the perovskite surface, which indicates that the perovskite surfaces modified with 4ABI and 2AEI are more sensitive to water molecules.  $  ^{53,54}  $   To further confirm the water absorption on the perovskite surface, an XPS study was performed. As shown in Figure S17, the O 1s spectrum of the control sample shows one peak at 532.7 eV, which is attributed to the water or oxygen adsorbed on the perovskite surface.  $  ^{55}  $   However, the Pero/4ABI and Pero/2AEI samples show a shoulder peak located at higher energy of 534.6 eV. We attribute this peak to the water molecules absorbed by the 4ABI and 2AEI, which can form strong hydrogen bonds between water and hydroxyl groups of the 4ABI and 2AEI.

Another issue that limits the operational stability of the perovskite devices is the mobile ions driven by the electric field. We suppose that ion migration is inhibited by the hydrophilic layer on the surface. We measured temperature-dependent electrical conductivity with the lateral Au/perovskite/Au devices to calculate the activation energy   $  (E_{\mathrm{a}})  $   of ion migration (Figure S18).  $  ^{56}  $   The activation energy can be extracted from the Nernst–Einstein relation:   $  \sigma(T)T = \sigma_{0} e^{-E_{\mathrm{a}}/kT}  $  , where   $  \sigma_{0}  $   is a constant, k the Boltzmann constant, and T the temperature.  $  ^{57}  $   The applied electric field was fixed at 2 V, and constant slopes were obtained for all three of the devices. As shown in Figure 4e, an   $  E_{a}  $   value of 83 meV is estimated for the control film, while the Pero/4ABI and the Pero/2AEI films show much higher   $  E_{a}  $   values of 295 and 265 meV, respectively. We thus conclude that ion migration is efficiently suppressed by the post-treatment with hydrophilic materials, which is related to the reduced surface defects and the reduced J-V hysteresis as well.

We demonstrated a paradoxical strategy for stable and efficient PSCs by using hydrophilic materials (i.e., 4ABI and 2AEI). We observed bifunctional activity of the hydrophilic layers deposited on the perovskite film, that is, a function for protecting the perovskite layer from moisture and a surface passivation to reduce surface traps by interaction with the under-coordinated lead and iodide centers. Surface trap density was substantially decreased and ion migration was also effectively suppressed, which eventually led to a PCE of 23.25%. Paradoxically, the hydrogen bonding characteristics of the hydroxyl group in the hydrophilic materials were proved to protect the underneath perovskite layer from moisture by moisture absorption, as evidently confirmed by the current gain in the humid air atmosphere, which resulted in a significant improvement stability of PSCs. This work is believed to provide important insights into stable and high efficiency PSCs.

# EXPERIMENTAL METHODS

Materials. Formamidinium iodide (FAI), formamidinium chloride (FACl), 2AEI, and 4ABI were prepared by reacting 0.04 M formamidinium acetate (2-hydroxyethylamine and 4-hydroxybutylamine for 2AEI and 4ABI) with 0.05 M HI (57 wt % in water, Sigma-Aldrich) (0.05 M HCl for FACl) in an ice bath for 2 h. Then, the solution was evaporated at 70 °C for 1 h until a solid powder was formed. The solid powder was washed with diethyl ether (99.0%, Samchun) and recrystallized using anhydrous ethanol. The resulting powder was dried under vacuum for 2 days. FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  powders were synthesized according to methods reported elsewhere.  $  ^{58,59}  $  Spiro-MeO-TAD was purchased from Merck. Colloidal tin oxide solution (SnO  $  _{2}  $ , 15% in H  $  _{2}  $  O) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. Other chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Device Fabrication. Patterned FTO glass substrates were cleaned by ultrasonication with acetone and ethanol sequentially. The electron transport layer   $  SnO_{2}  $   with a concentration of 4 mg/mL was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 20 s, and the resulting sample was annealed at 180 °C for 30 min. The perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving the pre-synthesized   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   powder in a mixed solvent (DMF/NMP = 8:1), where 30 mol % FACl and 5% CsCl were included. Then, 20 μL of the precursor solution was spun onto the annealed   $  SnO_{2}  $   film at 4000 rpm for 20 s, where 1 mL of diethyl ether was dropped on the rotating substrate 10 s after spinning. Subsequently, the sample was annealed at 150 °C for 20 min. The 5 mM 2AEI and 4ABI solutions were prepared by dissolving 2AEI and 4ABI in isopropanol. The annealed perovskite films were post-treated with 100 μL of 2AEI or 4ABI solution by spin-coating at 3000 rpm for 20 s with a second annealing. The spiro-MeOTAD layer was formed on the perovskite layer by spin-coating 20 μL of the stock solution comprising 60 mg of spiro-MeOTAD in 0.7 mL of chlorobenzene (CB), including 25.5 μL of tBP (tert-butylpyridine) and 15.5 μL of the Li-TFSI solution (520 mg Li-TFSI in 1 mL of acetonitrile (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.8%)) at 3000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, a Ag electrode was deposited on top of the spiro-MeOTAD layer using a thermal evaporator. The active area of the devices was 0.125 cm².

The lateral devices with a metal-perovskite-metal configuration were prepared by deposition of Au electrodes with distances of   $  \sim30\ \mu m  $   on the perovskite film by a thermal evaporator, where the perovskite film before and after post-treatment with 2AEI or 4ABI was prepared by the same process applied for the perovskite solar cell devices.

The electron-only devices for the SCLC studies were fabricated with a configuration of FTO/SnO $ _2 $ /Perovskite/PCBM/Au. The FTO/SnO $ _2 $ /Perovskite structure was prepared by the same process applied for the perovskite solar cells. The PCBM solution was prepared by dissolving the PCBM in CB with a concentration of 25 mg/mL. Then, the PCBM was spin-coated on the annealed perovskite layer at 3000 rpm for 20 s. The Au electrodes were deposited by thermal evaporator. Structure and Material Characterizations. X-ray diffraction (XRD) results of the samples were obtained by a Rigaku SmartLab diffractometer, where Cu Kα radiation was used ( $ \lambda = 1.5406 $  Å). The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was measured (SPECS XR-MF) by using a monochromatized Al source ( $ h\nu = 1486.6 $  eV). Peak deconvolution was performed based on a Shirley-type background using a XPSPEAK software. The Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra of

perovskite powder were collected in the transmittance mode on the PerkinElmer IR spectrometer in the 400–4000 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  region. The morphology of the samples was characterized by a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (JSM-7600F, JEOL). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was measured on a Seiko Exstar 6000 (TG/DTA6100) at temperatures ranging from room temperature to 600 °C at a 20 °C/min rate. The sample weight was ~10 mg for the TGA measurement.

Optical Characterization. Optical absorption spectra were measured by a UV–vis spectrometer (Lambda 45, PerkinElmer). Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) was measured using a fluorescence spectrometer (QuantaurusTau C11367-12, Hamamatsu). Time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) was performed on the perovskite films by a time-correlated single-photon counting (TCSPC) system by FluoTim 200 (PicoQuant). The perovskite films were excited by a 464 nm laser source, and then the emitted PL from the samples was collected by a photon multiplier tube (PMT) detector (PMA 182, PicoQuant-GmbH).

Ion Migration Studies. The activation energy for ion migration was tested using metal-surface-metal devices by a Keithley 2400 source meter at different temperatures. The electric field was 2 V. The device was set in a Lakeshore Probe Station to obtain the desired temperature.

Device Characterizations.
