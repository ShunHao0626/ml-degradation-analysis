# Synchronous defect passivation of all-inorganic perovskite solar cells enabled by fullerene interlayer

# ABSTRACT

All-inorganic   $  CsPbI_{3-x}Br_{x}  $   perovskite solar cells (PSCs) are advantageous in terms of high thermal stability, while its efficiency lags behind those of organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite counterparts. Defect passivations have been extensively applied for enhancing efficiency of all-inorganic PSCs, which are mainly based on univocal defect passivation of perovskite layer. Herein, we incorporated a bis-dimethylamino-functionalized fullerene derivative (abbreviated as PCBDMAM) as an interlayer between ZnO electron transport layer (ETL) and all-inorganic   $  CsPbI_{2.25}Br_{0.75}  $   perovskite layer, accomplishing synchronous defect passivations of both layers and consequently dramatic enhancements of efficiency and thermal stability of PSC devices. Upon spin-coating PCBDMAM onto ZnO ETL, the surface defects of ZnO especially oxygen vacancies can be effectively passivated due to the formation of Zn-N ionic bonds. In addition, PCBDMAM incorporation affords effective passivation of   $  Pb_{1}  $   and   $  I_{Pb}  $   antisite defects within the atop perovskite layer as well via coordination bonding with   $  Pb^{2+}  $ . As a result, the regular-structure planar   $  CsPbI_{2.25}Br_{0.75}  $   PSC device delivers a champion power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 17.04%, which surpasses that of the control device (15.44%). Moreover, the PCBDMAM-incorporated PSC device maintains ~80% of its initial PCE after 600 h heating at 85 °C hot plate in   $  N_{2}  $   atmosphere, whereas PCE of the control device degrades rapidly to ~62% after 460 h heating under identical conditions. Hence, PCBDMAM incorporation benefited dramatic improvement of the thermal stability of PSC device.

# 1 Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been attracting worldwide interests with the certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) rising to 25.7% [1]. High-efficiency PSC devices are mostly based on organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite light-absorbing materials, which are however intrinsically volatile and thermally unstable due to the existence of organic cations, resulting in poor thermal stability of PSC devices and hampering large-scale commercialization of organic-inorganic hybrid PSCs [2, 3]. Hence, developing all-inorganic   $  CsPbI_{3-x}Br_{x}  $   (x = 0–3) PSCs with higher thermal stability than organic-inorganic hybrid counterparts is needed [4, 5]. Among the reported all-inorganic   $  CsPbI_{3-x}Br_{x}  $   PSCs, iodine-rich   $  CsPbI_{3-x}Br_{x}  $   (0 < x ≤ 1) perovskites bear a reasonable balance between bandgap and phase stability because partial substitution of I⁻ anion with smaller Br⁻ is the most effective way to stabilize the perovskite phase [6–8]. The highest PCE of   $  CsPbI_{3-x}Br_{x}  $   (0 < x ≤ 1) PSCs without any organic salt

additive reaches 18.43% [7], which obviously lags behind those of organic-inorganic hybrid PSCs. To enhance PCE of   $  CsPbI_{3-x}Br_{x}  $   (  $  0 < x \leq 1  $  ) PSCs, interface engineering has been extensively applied and demonstrated to be effective in promoting interfacial charge transport via improving film morphology, lowering the energy level mismatch and passivating the traps in perovskite. In particular, by using different types of interlayers including small molecules [9, 10], polymers [11, 12], inorganic compounds [13, 14], 2D perovskite layers [15, 16] as well as fullerene and its derivatives [17–20], defect passivations of all-inorganic   $  CsPbI_{3-x}Br_{x}  $   (  $  0 < x \leq 1  $  ) PSCs have been accomplished. Among the reported interlayer passivators, few fullerene derivatives such as [6,6]-phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) featuring strong electron-accepting abilities have been implemented to modify ZnO [20] and   $  SnO_{2}  $   [17, 18] electron transport layer (ETL), leading to not only passivated trap states of perovskite surface but also enhanced electron transport. # 2 Experimental

# 2.1 Materials

Zinc acetate dehydrate (99.5%), ethanolamine, and 2-methoxyethanol (99.8%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich, N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, > 99.8%), dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO, > 99.8%), chlorobenzene (99.9%), ethanolamine (> 99.5%), 2-methxyethanol (> 99.0%) and lead bromide (PbBr₂, 99.999%) were purchased from Alfa Aesar. Cesium iodide (CsI, 99.99%) and tris(2-(1H-pyrazol-1-yl)-4-tert-butylpyridine) cobalt(iii)-tris(bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide) (FK209) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. (PLT). Lead iodide (PbI₂, 99.99%) were purchased from TCI. Ethanol, isopropanol, acetone, and acetonitrile were all purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. 2,2',7,7'-Tetrakis[N,N-di(4-methoxyphenyl)amino]-9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD) was purchased from 1 M company Ltd. Lithium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl) imide (Li-TFSI 99%) was purchased from Aladdin. 4-Tertbutylpyridine (t-BP 97%) was purchased from Adamas Reagent. Synthesis process of PCBDMAM was reported in our previous work [21].

# 2.2 Device fabrication

The FTO-coated glass substrate with a sheet resistance of   $  10\ \Omega\cdot sq^{-1}  $   was ultrasonicated in detergent, deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol for 15 min before being dried at   $  60\ ^{\circ}C  $  . The ZnO precursor solution was prepared according to literature [22]. The fresh films were put into   $  N_{2}  $   glove box to annealed at   $  150\ ^{\circ}C  $   for 10 min and then at   $  300\ ^{\circ}C  $   in air. For PCBDMAM modification, the PCBDMAM solution in chlorobenzene was spun coated on ZnO substrate at 4000 rpm for 30 s and then annealed at   $  70\ ^{\circ}C  $   for 5 min. The   $  CsPbI_{2.25}Br_{0.75}  $   precursor solution (comprised of 1.1 M CsI,   $  0.6875M\ PbI_{2}  $  ,   $  0.4125\ M\ PbBr_{2}  $   in   $  700\ \mu L  $   DMF and   $  300\ \mu L  $   DMSO) was spin coated on the substrate at 2900 rpm for 40 s. Then the prepared precursor films were thermally annealed by a two-step process at   $  55\ ^{\circ}C  $   for 60 s and   $  255\ ^{\circ}C  $   for 10 min.

For the deposition of hole transporting layer, Spiro-OMeTAD (73.2 mg) was first dissolved in 1 mL of chlorobenzene, and then 29   $  \mu  $  L of t-BP and 18.8   $  \mu  $  L of Li-TFSI solution (520 mg Li-TFSI in

1 mL of acetonitrile) as well as 29 μL of a tris(2-(1H-pyrazol-1-yl)-4-tert-butylpyridine)cobalt(iii)-tris(bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl) imide) (FK209) stock solution (300 mg·mL  $  ^{-1}  $  in acetonitrile) were added. The Spiro-OMeTAD solution was spin-coated onto the perovskite layer at 3000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, 65 nm gold (Au) was thermally evaporated under vacuum (  $  \approx 10^{-6}  $  Torr) respectively. A shadow mask was employed to determine the effective device area of 0.09 cm  $  ^{2}  $ . All device fabrication procedures were carried out in a N  $  _{2}  $ -purged glovebox (< 0.1 ppm of O  $  _{2}  $  and H  $  _{2}  $ O).

# 2.3 Measurements and characterization

The current density voltage (J-V) characterizations were measured by a Keithley 2400 source measurement unit under simulated AM 1.5 irradiation (100 mW·cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) with a standard xenon-lamp-based solar simulator (XEC-300M2), which was calibrated with a monocrystalline silicon reference cell (Oriel P/N 91 150 V, with KG-5 visible color filter) calibrated by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory. The measurements were performed with a mask (well-defined area of size 0.09 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ) to ensure accurate measurement under ambient conditions. Around 20 devices were fabricated and measured independently under each experimental condition to warrant the reproducibility of the result and to obtain the statistical data of the photovoltaic parameters. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) measurements were performed on an ORIEL Intelligent Quantum Efficiency 200TM Measurement system established with a tunable light source.

The scanning electron microscope (SEM) measurements were obtained via field-emission scanning electron microscope (FEI Apero). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) images were acquired on a XE-7 scanning probe microscope in noncontact mode (Park systems, Korea). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were attained on a Rigaku SmartLab X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (0.154 nm). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopic (XPS) measurements were performed on a Thermo ESCALAB 250 instrument with a monochromatized Al Kα X-ray source under vacuum. The UV–Vis spectroscopy spectra were recorded on a UV–Vis–NIR 3600 spectrometer (Shimadzu, Japan). The steady-state PL spectra were acquired employing an Edinburgh Instruments FLS920 fluorescence spectrometer with an excitation wavelength of 460 nm. The time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectra were measured via the time-correlated single-photon counting method with a Picoquant Gmbh Solea Supercontinuum Laser. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed in the dark using an electrochemical workstation (Autolab 320, Metrohm, Switzerland) with a frequency range from 1 Hz to 1 MHz under 1.0 V. Alternating current (20 mV) perturbation was applied with a frequency from 1 MHz to 1 Hz. The obtained impedance spectra were fitted with Z-View software (v2.8b, Scribner Associates). The deep-level transient spectroscopy (DLTS) measurements were carried out with a Phystech FT-1230 HERA DLTS system at various temperatures with reverse bias. Temperature scans were made between 120 and 430 K, at a heating rate of 2 K·min  $  ^{-1}  $  . The capacitances of samples were measured by using a Boonton 7200 Phystech capacitance meter with a 1 MHz capacitance meter. The reverse bias, pulse height, pulse width, and period width set as -0.6 V, 0.6 V, 10 ms, and 20 ms, respectively. The measurements were conducted two times to ensure reproducibility. The pulse frequency for the voltage was 100 MHz.

# 3 Results and discussion

Bis-dimethylamino-functionalized fullerene derivative PCBDMAM was first synthesized by our group via a facile esterification reaction of [6,6]-phenyl-C61-butyric acid (PCBA) with bis(4-(2-(dimethylamino) ethoxy) phenyl) methanol (DMAM-OH), and incorporated as an auxiliary fullerene interlayer atop of PCBM to form a PCBM/PCBDMAM double fullerene cathode buffer layer (CBL) of inverted (p-i-n) planar  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  PSCs as reported previously [21]. The nitrogen atom in PCBDMAM can bond with Ag cathode so that an interfacial dipole layer formed between PCBM and Ag cathode, resulting in decrease of the work function of the Ag cathode [21]. Stimulated by this work, we managed to incorporate PCBDMAM between ZnO ETL and  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_{2.25}\mathrm{Br}_{0.75} $  perovskite layer in the present work. The decomposition temperature of PCBDMAM is determined to be beyond  $ 392.3^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  [21], such a high thermal stability ensures its applicability in ZnO-based  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_{2.25}\mathrm{Br}_{0.75} $  PSC devices.

PCBDMAM was dissolved in chlorobenzene with variable concentrations (1, 2, and 5 mg·mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) and then spin-coated onto thermally annealed ZnO layer. We first employed AFM to investigate the influence of PCBDMAM modification on the morphology ZnO film. Figures 1(a)–1(d) compares the AFM images of the PCBDMAM-modified and bare ZnO films. The bare ZnO film exhibits a rough surface composed of stripes, and the root-mean-square (RMS) roughness reaches 9.26 nm. Upon modification with low-concentration PCBDMAM (1 and 2 mg·mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ), the surface looks smoother with the RMS roughness decreases to 6.25 and 2.61 nm, and the valleys between the stripes become smaller and finally disappear. The smoother surface is beneficial for improving the interfacial contact between the ZnO ETL and perovskite layer [23]. However, when the concentration of PCBDMAM increases to 5 mg·mL  $  ^{-1}  $  , the RMS roughness increases to 4.13 nm due likely to aggregation of PCBDMAM molecules. In addition to the influence on the ZnO film morphology, PCBDMAM modification is also found to affect the wettability of ZnO film. Upon PCBDMAM modification, the DMF contact angle of the ZnO film increases dramatically from 2.4° to 12.8° (1 mg·mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ), 13.8° (2 mg·mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) and 35.2° (5 mg·mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ), indicating that PCBDMAM modification improves the hydrophobicity of ZnO film because PCBDMAM is hydrophobic

To confirm the presence of PCBDMAM on the surface of ZnO and the influence of PCBDMAM on the defects of ZnO films, we carried out XPS characterizations. As seen from the N 1s spectra, the nitrogen signal peak at 399.8 eV observed in PCBDMAM appears obviously in ZnO/PCBDMAM film (Fig. 1(e)), indicating the presence of PCBDMAM on ZnO film. This peak shifts negatively to 399.2 eV, suggesting electron transfer from ZnO to PCBDMAM [24]. Besides, after 2 mg·mL  $  ^{-1}  $  PCBDMAM modification, both Zn 2p  $  _{1/2}  $  and Zn 2p  $  _{3/2}  $  signals detected at 1044.3 and 1021.2 eV shift positively by 0.3 eV (Fig. 1(f)). This suggests electron donation of ZnO, agreeing with the N 1s result. These results suggest electron transfer from ZnO to PCBDMAM, very likely via the formation of Zn–N ionic bonds. As a result, the surface defects of ZnO such as oxygen vacancies are expected to be passivated [24–26], as discussed further below.

To gain deeper insight into the specific defects of ZnO which are passivated by PCBDMAM modification, we quantitatively measured the deep traps of ZnO by using the DLTS. We fabricated Schottky-knot device with a structure of FTO/Au/ZnO/PCBDMAM/Au [27]. Figure 1(g) compares the DLTS spectra of the device with and without PCBDMAM modification, from which the activation energy  $ (E_{\mathrm{C}} - E_{\mathrm{T}}) $  and concentration of trap  $ (N_{\mathrm{T}}) $  can be obtained from Arrhenius plots and the fitting results (see Fig. S2 and Table S1 in the ESM). Upon PCBDMAM modification, the latter two peaks become undetectable, and only  $ \mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{Z1}}(\mathrm{ZnO}) $  is observed with much lower  $ \mathrm{N_T} $  value. As an interlayer between ETL and perovskite in n-i-p PSCs, it is necessary to examine the influence of PCBDMAM incorporation on the optical transmittance of ZnO film. Optical transmission spectra of the bare and PCBDMAM modified ZnO films are compared in Fig. S3 in the ESM, indicating that low-concentration (1 and 2 mg·mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) PCBDMAM incorporation leads to slight decrease of the optical transmittance of ZnO film in the short-wavelength range of 350–500 and 850–1100 nm due to the optical absorption of PCBDMAM, whereas obvious optical transmittance drop is observed when the PCBDMAM concentration increases to 5 mg·mL  $  ^{-1}  $  . This phenomenon is similar to the cases of other fullerene passivators reported previously [29]. Fortunately power density of sunlight is not very high in this range, so slight optical transmittance loss induced by low-concentration PCBDMAM incorporation is expected to bring negligible influence on PSC device efficiency as discussed below.

Since perovskite film is deposited on top of ZnO layer, it is necessary to investigate the influence of PCBDMAM incorporation on the morphology of the   $  CsPbI_{2.25}Br_{0.75}  $   perovskite film atop, which is characterized by SEM. The   $  CsPbI_{2.25}Br_{0.75}  $   perovskite film deposited on bare ZnO substrate looks compact without discernible pinholes (Fig. 2(a)), and the average crystalline grain size is about 387.1 nm (see Fig. S4 in the ESM for grain size distribution) with vague grain boundaries. After low-concentration (1, 2 mg·mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) PCBDMAM incorporation, the grain boundaries appear clearer (Figs. 2(b) and 2(c)), and the average perovskite grain size increases to 458.3 nm (1 mg·mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) and 549.7 nm (2 mg·mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ). When PCBDMAM concentration increases to 5 mg·mL  $  ^{-1}  $  , although the average perovskite grain size keeps on increasing (650.2 nm), obvious pinholes are observed (Fig. 2(d)). This is resulted from the increased hydrophobicity of ZnO/PCBDMAM substrate as discussed above. On the other hand, according to a comparison of the cross-sectional SEM images of the perovskite films deposited on PCBDMAM-modified and bare ZnO substrates, the largest crystal grains with perfectly vertical orientations are obtained when PCBDMAM concentration is 2 mg·mL  $  ^{-1}  $   (see Fig. S5 in the ESM).

Since PCBDMAM is incorporated as an interlayer between ZnO and   $  CsPbI_{2.25}Br_{0.75}  $   perovskite, it is also necessary to probe whether PCBDMAM can interact with the atop   $  CsPbI_{2.25}Br_{0.75}  $   perovskite as well. We then carried out XPS characterization to probe the change of the binding energy of   $  Pb^{2+}  $   within   $  CsPbI_{2.25}Br_{0.75}  $   after PCBDMAM incorporation. According to a comparison of the Pb 4f XPS spectra of the pristine   $  CsPbI_{2.25}Br_{0.75}  $   and   $  CsPbI_{2.25}Br_{0.75}/PCBDMAM  $   films (Fig. 2(f)), both Pb   $  4f_{5/2}  $   and Pb   $  4f_{7/2}  $   signals at 143.3 and 138.5 eV detected for the pristine   $  CsPbI_{2.25}Br_{0.75}  $   shift negatively to 142.9 and 138.1 eV respectively after PCBDMAM incorporation. In order to uncover the factors accounting for the increases of  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  and FF, we carried out a series of characterizations to elucidate the influence of PCBDMAM incorporation on the charge carrier dynamics of the device. The electron extraction and electron-hole recombination dynamics at the ZnO/perovskite interface is first investigated by steady-state and TRPL spectroscopies. Figure 3(e) compares the steady-state PL spectra of the CsPbI $ _{2.25} $ Br $ _{0.75} $  perovskite films deposited on of the bare and PCBDMAM modified ZnO substrates measured under an excitation wavelength of 460 nm, revealing that the characteristic PL peak at 667 nm of CsPbI $ _{2.25} $ Br $ _{0.75} $  perovskite deposited on the bare ZnO is quenched after PCBDMAM modification, and the highest quenching ratio is observed for 2 mg·mL $ ^{-1} $  PCBDMAM. In addition, such a PL peak exhibits a blue-shift from 667 to 664 nm (2 mg·mL $ ^{-1} $  PCBDMAM). These results solidify passivation of the trap states of CsPbI $ _{2.25} $ Br $ _{0.75} $  [23]. Furthermore, as obtained from TRPL spectra shown in Fig. 3(f), the carrier lifetimes ( $ \tau $ ) of the CsPbI $ _{2.25} $ Br $ _{0.75} $  perovskite films deposited on 1, 2, and 5 mg·mL $ ^{-1} $  PCBDMAM modified ZnO are 17.69, 13.96, and 21.81 ns, respectively, which are all smaller than that on the bare ZnO (48.48 ns) (see Table S5 in the ESM). The decrease of  $ \tau $  value indicates that PCBDMAM incorporation promotes effectively electron extraction from perovskite to ZnO ETL, contributing to the increases of  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  and FF [37]. We then investigate the dependence of  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  on light intensities ( $ I $ ) so as to understand further the charge recombination kinetics within the PSC device (Fig. 3(g)), which can be fitted according to the equation:  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} = nkT\ln(I)/e + \text{constant} $ , where  $ n $  is the ideal factor related to the dominant recombination mechanism in the device,  $ k $  is the Boltzmann constant,  $ T $  is the absolute temperature, and  $ e $  refers to the elementary charge [38]. In particular, when an ideal factor ( $ n $ ) is larger than 1, the additional trap-assisted Shockley-Read-Hall (SRH) recombination occurs, and decrease in the slope ( $ nkT/e $ ) suggests suppression of the monomolecular SRH recombination [38]. Upon PCBDMAM modification, the  $ n $  value decreases obviously from 1.34 (control) to 1.19 (2 mg·mL $ ^{-1} $  PCBDMAM). This confirms suppression of trap-assisted carrier recombination resulted from trap states passivation as discussed above.

The effect of PCBDMAM incorporation on the interfacial charge transport behavior of the device is probed by EIS. The Nyquist plots of device with and without PCBDMAM measured in dark under a reverse potential of 1.0 V are compared in Fig. 3(h). According to a comparison of the corresponding fitted parameters based on an equivalent circuit model shown in the inset of Fig. 3h (see Table S6 in the ESM) [39], the   $  R_{s}  $   (series resistance) value decreases slightly from 2.37 to 1.95   $  \Omega\cdot cm^{2}  $   after 2 mg·mL  $  ^{-1}  $   PCBDMAM incorporation, whereas the   $  R_{ct}  $   (charge transfer

resistance) value decreases dramatically from 179.5 to  $ 78.0\Omega \cdot \mathrm{cm}^2 $ . These suggest that PCBDMAM incorporation facilitates interfacial charge transport, contributing directly to the increase of FF [39, 40].

To disclose the reason responsible for the increase of   $  V_{oc}  $  , it is also necessary to check whether PCBDMAM incorporation improves the energy level alignment between ZnO and   $  CsPbI_{2.25}Br_{0.75}  $   perovskite. We employed ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) to determine the valence band maximum (VBM), conduction band minimum (CBM) and work function (  $  W_{F}  $  ) of ZnO and   $  CsPbI_{2.25}Br_{0.75}  $   perovskite (see Figs. S12 and S13 and Table S7 in the ESM) [34, 41]. The LUMO energy level of PCBDMAM calculated from the onset reduction potential (  $  E_{red}^{onset}  $  ) of PCBDMAM was -3.85 eV (see Fig. S14(a) in the ESM). On the basis of the optical band gap measured by UV–vis absorption onset of (1.73 eV), the HOMO energy level of PCBDMAM was calculated to be -5.58 eV (see Fig. S14(b) in the ESM). Since LUMO of PCBDMAM lies between the CBM levels of ZnO and   $  CsPbI_{2.25}Br_{0.75}  $   perovskite, a cascade energy level arrangement forms and benefits electron extraction from   $  CsPbI_{2.25}Br_{0.75}  $   perovskite to ZnO ETL, leading to increased   $  V_{oc}  $   [34, 42, 43].

Thermal stability is another crucial parameter decisive for the commercial applications of PSC devices [4, 5]. ZnO is seldom used in organic-inorganic hybrid PSCs due to the basic nature of the ZnO surface inducing proton-transfer reactions at the  $ \mathrm{ZnO / CH_3NH_3PbI_3} $  interface when heated, resulting in poor thermal stability of the devices [44]. For all-inorganic  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_{3 - x}\mathrm{Br}_x $   $ (0 < x\leq 1) $  PSCs, although such a proton-transfer reaction would not happen, previous reports on ZnO-based  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_{3 - x}\mathrm{Br}_x $   $ (0 < x\leq 1) $  PSCs indicate that these PSC devices still have inferior thermal stability, which is likely due to quick  $ \delta $ -phase transformation of  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  when it is in contact with unstable ZnO surface during heating [45, 46]. We probe the influence of PCBDMAM incorporation on the thermal stability of  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_{2.25}\mathrm{Br}_{0.75} $  PSC devices under continuous heating at  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  hot plate in  $ \mathbf{N}_2 $  atmosphere. 2D cloud maps versus diffraction angle  $ (2\theta) $  of XRD patterns and the heating time for  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_{2.25}\mathrm{Br}_{0.75} $  perovskite films deposited on different substrates are illustrated in Figs. 4(a) and 4(b). # 4 Conclusions

In summary, a bis-dimethylamino-functionalized fullerene derivative PCBDMAM is incorporated as an interlayer between ZnO ETL and all-inorganic   $  CsPbI_{2.25}Br_{0.75}  $   perovskite layer, resulting in synchronous defect passivations of both layers and consequently dramatic enhancements of efficiency and thermal stability of PSC devices. After PCBDMAM modification, PCBDMAM interacts with ZnO via formation of Zn–N ionic bonds, leading to effective passivation of the surface defects of ZnO especially oxygen vacancies. In addition, PCBDMAM incorporation affords effective passivation of   $  Pb_{I}  $   and   $  I_{Pb}  $   antisite

defects within the atop   $  CsPbI_{2.25}Br_{0.75}  $   perovskite layer as well via coordination bonding between the N atoms of PCBDMAM with   $  Pb^{2+}  $  . As a result, a champion PCE of 17.04% is obtained for the regular-structure planar   $  CsPbI_{2.25}Br_{0.75}  $   PSC device, surpassing that of the control device (15.44%). Moreover, after PCBDMAM incorporation, PSC device maintains   $  \sim80\%  $   of its initial PCE after 600 h heating at 85 °C hot plate in   $  N_{2}  $   atmosphere, whereas a rapid degradation of PCE to   $  \sim62\%  $   after 460 h heating is observed for the control device under identical conditions. Hence, PCBDMAM incorporation benefits dramatic improvements of both efficiency and thermal stability of PSC device. Our synchronous defect passivation strategy paves the way for high-efficiency and thermal stable all-inorganic   $  CsPbI_{3-x}Br_{x}  $   perovskite solar cells.
