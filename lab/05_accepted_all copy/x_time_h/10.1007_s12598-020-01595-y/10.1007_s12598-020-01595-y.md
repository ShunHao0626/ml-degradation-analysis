# Cross-linkable fullerene interfacial contacts for enhancing humidity stability of inverted perovskite solar cells

Ming-Wei An, Zhou Xing, Bao-Shan Wu, Fang-Fang Xie, Shan-Yu Zheng, Lin-Long Deng  $  ^{*}  $    $  ^{ID}  $  , Xu Wang, Bin-Wen Chen, Da-Qin Yun, Su-Yuan Xie  $  ^{*}  $    $  ^{ID}  $  , Rong-Bin Huang, Lan-Sun Zheng

Received: 10 June 2020/Revised: 25 August 2020/Accepted: 9 September 2020  
© GRINM Bohan (Beijing) Publishing Co., Ltd 2020

Abstract In situ cross-linking encapsulation has been demonstrated to be an efficient strategy for enhancing the humidity stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). In this study, a novel cross-linkable fullerene derivative, namely 1-(p-benzoate-(p-methylvinylbenzene)-indolino[2,3][60]fullerene (FPPS), was readily synthesized from commercially available building blocks in two steps. This FPPS was employed as an interfacial modifier on perovskite surfaces in inverted planar p-i-n PSCs. Owing to the fast interfacial charge extraction and efficient trap passivation, PSCs based on the cross-linked FPPS (C-FPPS) exhibited excellent performance. The PSCs had a top-performing power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 17.82\% $  with negligible hysteresis, compared to the control devices without C-PFFS  $ (16.99\%) $ . Moreover, the strong water resistance of the C-FPPS interfacial layer distinctly enhances the

ambient stability of PSC devices, exhibiting a  $  t_{80}  $  (the time required to reach  $  80\%  $  of the initial PCE) of  $  300\mathrm{h}  $  under high-humidity conditions. This significantly surpasses the control devices, whose  $  t_{80}  $  was only  $  130\mathrm{h}  $ . These results demonstrate that cross-linkable fullerene derivatives can be promising interfacial materials for designing high-efficiency, hysteresis-free, air-stable PSCs.

Keywords Perovskite solar cells; Cross-linkable fullerene; Fullerene interlayer; Power conversion efficiency; Humidity stability

# 1 Introduction

Organic–inorganic halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have drawn attention from both scientific and industrial communities due to their intriguing photo-physical properties, easy preparation process, and their ability to increase power conversion efficiency (PCE) from 3.8% to 25.2% [1–5]. PSCs with a planar architecture have shown great promise due to their simple structure and easy fabrication of flexible devices. They can be divided into two types: the conventional n–i–p and inverted p–i–n structures [6–8]. Commonly, poly(3, 4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrene sulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS) and (6, 6)-phenyl-C  $  _{61}  $  butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) are fabricated as the hole transport layers (HTLs) and electron transport layers (ETLs) of the p–i–n PSCs, respectively [9, 10]. Owing to low-temperature solution-process preparation, high stability, and much weaker hysteresis behavior, the p–i–n architecture is compatible with flexible devices and roll-to-roll fabrication technology, which are likely future directions for optoelectronic devices [11–13].

However, the instability induced by the properties of perovskite and interfacial issues within devices discourage commercialization of PSCs   $  [14–16]  $  . One approach to these problems, optimizing the composition of perovskite (such as mixed cations of formamidine (FA)/methylamine (MA)/Cs and mixed halides of iodide/bromide/chloride), significantly improves the humidity stability of the perovskite   $  [17–21]  $  . Alternatively, hydrophobic–interfacial engineering can delay moisture penetrating the perovskite. Options include substituting or modifying the commonly used PEDOT:PSS   $  [22–25]  $  , employing cathode buffer layers (CBLs) between ETLs and electrodes   $  [26, 27]  $  , and depositing a waterproof tunneling interfacial layer at the perovskite/ETL interface   $  [28–32]  $  . Furthermore, cross-linkable fullerene electron transporting materials have been demonstrated as potential substitutes for PCBM in organic photovoltaics and PSCs   $  [33–42]  $  , though rarely employed as perovskite/PCBM interfacial modifiers.

Herein, we demonstrate an in situ interfacial cross-linking strategy to fabricate efficient and stable inverted PSCs using a novel cross-linkable fullerene derivative, 1-(p-benzoate-(p-methylvinylbenzene))-indolino[2,3][60]fullerene (abbreviated as FPPS). This promotes interfacial charge extraction, reducing charge recombination and slowing moisture penetration into the perovskite active layers. A modified device based on cross-linked FPPS (C-FPPS) exhibited the best performance, with a maximum PCE of  $ 17.82\% $ . In contrast, the control device without the C-FPPS interfacial layer only exhibited a maximum PCE of  $ 16.99\% $ . In addition, in comparison with the control devices, the C-FPPS interlayers greatly improve the humidity stability of PSCs, which was demonstrated by high-humidity aging and by directly submerging devices into water.

# 2 Experimental

# 2.1 Synthesis process of FPPS

FPPS was readily synthesized from commercially available building blocks in two steps, as shown in Scheme 1. The 1-(p-benzoate)-indolino[2,3][60] fullerene (FPPA) was prepared via Prato reaction of 4-formylbenzoic acid with  $ \mathrm{C}_{60} $  and an excess of phenyl glycine [43]. The synthesis of FPPS was carried out according to a reported literature procedure [33], involving the reaction of FPPA with (4-vinylphenyl)methanol in the presence of 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-carbodiimide (EDC) and 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP) in o-dichlorobenzene.

# 2.2 Device fabrication

Patterned indium tin oxide (ITO) substrates were ultrasonically treated, successively, in a detergent, deionized water, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol for 15 min. Before the fabrication of polytriarylamine (PTAA) layer, the substrates were treated in a UV ozone chamber for 10 min. The PTAA precursor solution (10 mg·ml  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was prepared by dissolving PTAA powders in chlorobenzene doped with 1 wt% F4-TCNQ, followed by spin coating at 4000 r·min  $  ^{-1}  $   for 30 s and annealing at 100 °C for 10 min. The perovskite layer (CsFAMAPbIBr) was fabricated using methods from previous works [44]. Briefly, the precursor solution was prepared by dissolving FAI (176.6 mg), MAI (33.1 mg), CsI (16.9 mg), PbI  $  _{2}  $   (509.4 mg), and PbBr  $  _{2}  $   (71.6 mg) in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 800 μl), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 180 μl), and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP, 20 μl). The precursor solution was stirred for 12 h and spin-coated at 1300 r·min  $  ^{-1}  $   for 10 s and 5000 r·min  $  ^{-1}  $   for 20 s; 200 μl anisole was dropped onto the substrates 5 s before the end of the program, to act as an anti-solvent. The substrates were annealed at 100 °C for 60 min. For C-FPPS-based devices, FPPS powders with different masses were dissolved in anisole, followed by spin coating at 4000 r·min  $  ^{-1}  $   for 30 s and annealing at 150 °C for 20 min in order to finish the C-FPPS layer. After this, we dissolved 20 mg PCBM powders in 1 ml chlorobenzene and stirred it at 35 °C for 60 min before use. The PCBM layer was dynamically spin-coated at 3000 r·min  $  ^{-1}  $   for 20 s. The hole-blocking layers were prepared by dissolving 1 mg bathocuproine (BCP) powders in 2 ml isopropyl alcohol, followed by spin coating at 6000 r·min  $  ^{-1}  $   for 30 s and annealing at 80 °C for 10 min. Finally, an Ag electrode was thermally deposited under a pressure of 2 × 10  $  ^{-4}  $   Pa.

Current density–voltage (J-V) curves were obtained using a Keithley 2420 source meter. External quantum efficiency (EQE) measurements were carried out using a QEX10 with a 300-W xenon lamp. Maximum power point (MPP) output was tested under continuous illumination using a Shanghai Chenhua CHI650E electrochemical workstation. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) measurements were carried out using a Hitachi S-4800 field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM). Contact angles of water were measured using an SDC-100 optical contact angle tester. Steady-state fluorescence was measured on a F7000 fluorescence spectrophotometer. # 3 Results and discussion

The structural design of FPPS was based on the following considerations: (1) a fullerene adduct with styrene groups increases the intermolecular van der Waals interactions in the solid state; (2) the styrene functional groups tend to react without any initiator to form bonds with the adjacent molecules under thermal activation; (3) C-FPPS has highly solvent- and water-resistant properties. The detailed synthesis procedure for FPPS is described in the supplementary material. Its molecular structure was confirmed by   $  {}^{1}  $  H NMR and   $  {}^{13}  $  C NMR spectra, and also mass spectra, as shown in Figs. S1–S3. The absorption spectra for FPPS in solution and the solid state are shown in Fig. 1a. Compared with the solution state, there exists a slight bathochromic shift to 735 nm in the absorption onset in the solid-state films. The energy levels for FPPS were measured by cyclic voltammetry, as shown in Fig. 1b, showing an onset reduction potential (  $  E_{red}^{on}  $  ) of -0.9 V. The lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy is calculated to be -3.9 eV, and the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy is -5.59 eV, according to the ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) absorption spectra [45]. The thermal stability of FPPS was also characterized by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). As shown in Fig. S4, FPPS is thermally stable above 350 °C, which is stable enough for the fabrication of PSC devices.

The cross-linked FPPS (C-FPPS) was realized by in situ cross-linking of FPPS after thermal curing at 150 °C. To prove the existence of C-FPPS films on top of the perovskite layer, top-view and cross-sectional SEM images and contact angle measurements were performed (Figs. S5–S7). The cross-sectional SEM images of perovskite films with and without C-FPPS modification are displayed in Fig. S5, which clearly shows the interface between C-FPPS and perovskite. As shown in the morphology of perovskite films with and without C-FPPS modification (Fig. S6), the formation of a quasi-continuous C-FPPS film greatly reduces the exposure of perovskite grains. This, in turn, passivates surface defects and improves the moisture stability of perovskite layer [46]. The water contact angles of the perovskite films with and without C-FPPS modification are estimated to be 80° and 49°, respectively (Fig. S7). This indicates superior ambient stability of the PSCs, caused by the greater water resistance of the C-FPPS. Most importantly, this cross-linked network produced a robust and adhesive thin film with sufficient solvent resistance, which was evidenced by the almost unchanged absorption spectra measured before and after rinsing with o-dichlorobenzene (Fig. S8). Notably, the absorption intensity of the non-cross-linked layer is much weaker after rinsing with o-dichlorobenzene, indicating that without thermal curing, this thin layer might be totally washed out by the following spin coating process. In addition, the vibrational stretching of the vinyl groups at 1627 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  in FPPS disappeared in the IR spectrum after thermal treatment, again proving the occurrence of cross-linking (Fig. S9).

As shown in Fig. 2a, inverted planar heterojunction PSCs with the configuration of ITO/PTAA/CsFAMAPbIBr/with or without C-FPPS/PCBM/BCP/Ag were fabricated. A solution of FPPS was spin-coated on top of the perovskite, followed by thermal curing at  $ 150^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 20\mathrm{min} $  to trigger in situ polymerization. Operational stability has been an Achilles' heel for commercial application of PSCs, due to the intrinsic and extrinsic issues of the devices. In this work, the ambient stability of the C-FPPS-based devices was studied, compared to that of control devices without the C-FPPS interfacial layer. The stability test of unsealed devices (eight devices for each condition) was carried out under ambient conditions (dark, room temperature (RT), RH = 50–60%). The C-FPPS-based devices showed a   $  t_{80}  $   of 300 h, while the control devices last only 130 h under the same conditions (Fig. 4). As demonstrated in Fig. S17, we immersed the two devices in water directly. The device without a C-FPPS interfacial layer corroded rapidly within 5 s, while the C-FPPS-based device was kept nearly undamaged for 30 s. The excellent water resistance of C-FPPS, as discussed above (Fig. S7), leads to enhanced humidity stability in C-FPPS devices.

# 4 Conclusion

In summary, a cross-linkable fullerene derivative FPPS was introduced into inverted planar PSCs as an interfacial material to enhance photovoltaic performance and device stability. PSCs based on C-FPPS exhibited an optimal PCE of 17.82% with negligible hysteresis. This is mainly attributed to higher electron mobility, faster interfacial charge extraction and transfer, and more efficient trap passivation on the perovskite surface. Moreover, benefiting from the stronger water-resistant ability, the C-FPPS-based devices showed enhanced stability with a   $  t_{80}  $   of 300 h in ambient air, more than double that of the control devices. This work demonstrates that cross-linkable fullerene derivatives can be promising interfacial materials for high-efficiency, hysteresis-free, air-stable PSCs.
