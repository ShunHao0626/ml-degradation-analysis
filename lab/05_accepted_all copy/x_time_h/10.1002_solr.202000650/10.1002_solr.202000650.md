# Abstract

Passivation approaches of perovskite surface are key to improve the light stability of the perovskite solar cells. However, the passivation strategy is still required to enhance the durability of the perovskite layer. Here, a promising passivation concept is demonstrated by applying a fluorinated agent on the perovskite layer for light stability improvement. Such fluorinated passivation agents could prevent the formation of  $ \mathrm{Pb}^0 $  at the perovskite surface resulting in suppressing a defect-induced recombination and improving the durability of the perovskite solar cells. As an additional benefit, the fluorinated passivation agent increases the  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  which improves the photovoltaic performance of the perovskite solar cells. Consequently, with a fluorinated passivation agent, the perovskite maintains a power conversion efficiency of  $ 95\% $  after  $ 300\mathrm{h} $  of light illumination. It was found that the fluorinated passivation material of  $ 1H,1H,2H,2H- $  perfluorooctyltriethoxysilane (PFOTES) can improve the stability of the perovskite solar cells.

# 1. Introduction

Since the photovoltaic properties of perovskite solar cells started to be acknowledged,  $  ^{[1-4]}  $   considerable efforts have been made in their performance development reaching over 25% of power conversion efficiency.  $  ^{[5-8]}  $   The metal halide perovskite has significant characteristics of optoelectrical and semiconductor properties such as tunable bandgap,  $  ^{[9-12]}  $   high absorption coefficient,  $  ^{[13,14]}  $   and long carrier diffusion length.  $  ^{[15-18]}  $   However, the light stability of the perovskite semiconductor is the key agenda for the research field to further improve the durability of the perovskite solar cells.

To improve the light stability of the perovskite solar cells, several passivation materials have been applied such as metal halide, $ ^{[19-22]} $  chloride, $ ^{[23-26]} $  poly(3-hexylthiophene), $ ^{[27]} $  poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA), $ ^{[28,29]} $  ammonium salt, $ ^{[30-36]} $  and poly(4-vinylpyridine) (PVP). $ ^{[37,38]} $  The passivation molecules need to be designed considering the functional groups, which were reported for example carboxyl, ammonium, aromatic, phosphate, and sulfonic group. $ ^{[39,40]} $  The triethoxysilane group has silicon-oxygen bonding that can interact with the perovskite, thus passivation agents could be deposited homogeneously on the substrate. $ ^{[41]} $  However, it is still required to investigate how to improve the light stability of perovskite solar cells. Thus, an in-depth passivation strategy is needed to achieve further light stability beyond the current state of the art.

Fluorinated-based molecules are promising passivation materials to improve the light stability of the perovskite solar cells. The light-induced degradation is due to the cation disengagement from the perovskite, which results in   $  Pb^{0}  $   formation.  $  ^{[42,43]}  $   The first-principle calculations predict that vacancies defects have low formation energies and can form shallow states in the forbidden energy band.  $  ^{[44]}  $   Such   $  Pb^{0}  $   causes the increase in the non-radiative pass way, quench the photoluminescence, and increase the defect density, resulting in a decrease in the photovoltaic properties.  $  ^{[45]}  $   Since the fluorine can work as a barrier layer,  $  ^{[46,47]}  $   there is a possibility to prevent the cation disengagement

In this study, we demonstrate that a fluorinated passivation agent could enhance the light stability of the perovskite solar cells. The maximum power point tracking and photoluminescence spectroscopy were performed for the perovskite solar cells with and without the passivation agent. Here we propose a passivation strategy to enhance the light stability of perovskite solar cells. As an added benefit, the fluorinated passivation agent can improve device performance. These experimental results show a design strategy for the passivation of perovskite solar cells.

# 2. Results and Discussion

Our proposed passivation strategy that protects the perovskite layer with a fluorinated molecule by using  $ 1H,1H,2H,2H $ -perfluorooctyltriethoxysilane (PFOTES) is shown in Figure 1a,b. The PFOTES molecule consists of a fluorinated alkyl chain incorporated with ethoxy silane. Since the C-O bonding in the ethoxy silane generate dipoles because of the negatively charged O and positively charged C, such a dipole could possibly lead to defect passivation on the perovskite surface. The ethoxy silane functional groups enable the facile deposition of the fluorinated passivation agent onto the perovskite layer.

To confirm the existence of the passivation agents on the perovskite layer, we performed a 2D nano-secondary ion mass spectrometry (2D SIMS) with a helium ion microscope (HIM).  $  ^{[48,49]}  $   It was shown previously that the HIM-SIMS is a particularly powerful tool to provide a high-resolution and high-sensitivity characterization of perovskite materials.  $  ^{[50-52]}  $   The HIM-SIMS results are shown in Figure 2. The PFOTES was deposited on the perovskite layer by spin-coating a solution (10 vol%). Figure 2a,b shows fluorine and silicon distribution mapping of the PFOTES on the perovskite layer. Fluorine (  $  ^{19}F^{-}  $  ) and silicon (  $  ^{28}Si^{-}  $  ) ions are in blue and cyan color, respectively. These two elements were observed on the perovskite layer with a relative homogeneity, which indicates the existence of PFOTES on the perovskite layer. To verify for possible artifacts, the perovskite without PFOTES was analyzed by SIMS (Figure S1). Here, we detected very low SIMS signal intensities for fluorine and silicone, which, considering the very high sensitivity of the SIMS technique, can be related to a background created by some fluorine and silicon contaminations on the sample or in the chamber. Further investigation of the passivation agent was completed by using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) as shown in Figure 2c,d. Comparisons are made with and without PFOTES on the perovskite layer. Figure 2c shows the F 1s peaks with and without PFOTES on the perovskite layer. The spectrum with PFOTES shows the peak at 688.3 eV which corresponds to fluorine.  $  ^{[53]}  $   Similarly, the sample with PFOTES shows the peak at 103.0 eV that is consistent with Si 2p,  $  ^{[54-56]}  $   while the sample without PFOTES does not show a peak (Figure 2d). Despite that PFOTES was detected by XPS, it can be too thin to observe the precise thickness by SEM (Figure S2). The top-view SEM image shows that the perovskite morphology is not influenced by the PFOTES deposition (Figure S2). From these results, it was established that the PFOTES could be deposited on the perovskite layer.

The photovoltaic performance was measured to find out the effect of the PFOTES passivation agent shown in Figure 3a,b. The best  $ I - V $  curves and photovoltaic properties are shown in Figure 3a. By applying the PFOTES passivation agent, open-circuit voltage  $ (V_{\mathrm{OC}}) $  was improved from 1.058 to  $ 1.098\mathrm{V} $ . Other photovoltaic parameters are shown in Figure S3 and S4. This  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  improvement can be due to the dipole generated by C-O bonding in the passivation agents that can lead to defect passivation on the perovskite surface. It was reported that the oxide in the passivation layer can interact with the perovskite surface to enhance the photovoltaic performance.[54] It could be considered that the same effect may have occurred with the PFOTES passivation agent. To investigate the effect of the PFOTES passivation agent on light stability test, we measured maximum power point tracking (MPPT) with and without PFOTES (Figure 3c). Samples were illuminated with the 1 sun  $ (100\mathrm{mW} / \mathrm{cm}^2) $  intensity, an atmosphere of  $ 100\% $  nitrogen, and  $ 0\% $  humidity. After  $ 300\mathrm{h} $ , the normalized efficiency with PFOTES was maintained at  $ 95\% $  of the initial efficiency. While in the case of perovskite solar cells without PFOTES, it decreased by  $ 15\% $  during the light stability test. In addition, we measured the stability of the solar cells in air ambient (RH  $ 30\% $ ) in dark. The conversion efficiency of the solar cells with passivation could maintain  $ 98\% $  at  $ 300\mathrm{h} $  from the initial efficiency, while control cells decreased by  $ 96\% $  (Figure S5). This result could be due to the fluorinated alkyl chain in the PFOTES molecule, which could protect the perovskite absorber from the moisture in the ambient air. These results indicate that the PFOTES passivation agent could enhance the durability of the perovskite solar cells.

To investigate how the PFOTES passivation could enhance the light stability of perovskite solar cells, steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra, PL decay, and X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern were measured by comparing before and after light soaking for  $ 300\mathrm{h} $  (Figure 5). Samples were kept under the 1 sun  $ (100\mathrm{mW/cm}^2) $  illumination and an atmosphere of  $ 100\% $  nitrogen with  $ 0\% $  humidity. The samples were structured as a Glass/Perovskite/PFOTES and a Glass/Perovskite for PL measurement, while FTO glass substrate was used for XRD instead of normal glass. In the case of the Glass/Perovskite/PFOTES (Figure 4a), the intensity of the emission peak did not change significantly before and after light soaking. # 3. Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrated that PFOTES can improve the light stability of perovskite solar cells and characterized the fluorinated based passivation materials. It was found that the passivation agent can suppress   $  Pb^{0}  $   at the perovskite surface after light soaking, which prevents an increase of the recombination pathway and results in improving the light stability of the perovskite solar cells.

# Materials

1H,1H,2H,2H-perfluorooctyltriethoxysilane (PFOTES) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. To fabricate the perovskite layer, methylammonium bromide and formamidinium iodide were supplied from GreatCell Solar Ltd. Lead iodide and lead bromide were supplied from TCI Co. Ltd. Cesium iodide was obtained from abcr GmbH. FTO glass substrate (TEC-9AX) was purchased from NSG group. Titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetylacetonate) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Titanium oxide nanoparticle (PST30NRD) was supplied from GreatCell Solar. Tin chloride (IV) was purchased from Acros Organics. Bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt and cobalt-complex (FK209) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All of the purchased chemicals were used as received without further purification.

# Device fabrication

After cleaning the FTO glass by sonication in ethanol and deionized water, then a blocking titanium oxide (TiO $ _2 $ ) layer was formed on the FTO glass by spray pyrosis deposition with a TiO $ _2 $  precursor solution (1 mL of titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetylacetonate) in 15 mL ethanol) at 500 °C. After that, a mesoporous TiO $ _2 $  layer was deposited on the blocking TiO $ _2 $  layer by spin-coating (5000 rpm, 30 s) with a TiO $ _2 $  nanoparticle solution (1 g in 11.8 g of ethanol), and annealed at 500 °C for 30 min. Then, a tin oxide (SnO $ _2 $ ) layer was deposited on the mesoporous TiO $ _2 $  layer by spin-coating using precursor solution (0.1 M of tin chloride (IV) in deionized water) and annealed at 190 °C for 1 h in air. After UV/O $ _3 $  treatment, a perovskite layer was deposited on the SnO $ _2 $  layer. The perovskite solution was 1.3 M of (CsPbI $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.1} $ (FAPbI $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.875} $ (MAPbBr $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.125} $  dissolved in DMF:DMSO=4:1 (volume ratio). The perovskite solution was spin-coated on the SnO $ _2 $  layer (two-step program: 1000 rpm for 10 s and 5000 rpm for 30 s). An anti-solvent of chlorobenzene was dropped on the film surface 10 s before finish the spin-coating, followed by annealing at 100 °C for

1 h to form the perovskite layer. After that, PFOTES was deposited on the perovskite layer by spin-coating (4000 rpm, 30 s) using a solution with variable concentration (2—20 vol% diluted in o-xylene). Then, spiro-OMeTAD as a hole-transport layer was formed on the perovskite layer by spin-coating (4000 rpm, 30 s). To prepare the spiro-OMeTAD solution, 80 mg of spiro-OMeTAD was diluted in 1023 μL of chlorobenzene, followed by adding 32 μL of 4-tert-butylpyridine, 19 μL of bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt solution (517 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) in acetonitrile, and 14 μL of cobalt-complex solution (376 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) in acetonitrile. The Au layer (70 nm) was evaporated on the hole-transport layer as a contact electrode layer.

# Characterizations and measurements

The  $ I-V $  measurements were performed by using an Oriel VeraSol solar simulator (Newport Corporation) and a LCE-50 (Centronics) calibrator. The  $ I-V $  measurement was performed from 1.2 to 0 V and from 0 to 1.2 V as reverse and forward scan, respectively, using a mask of  $ 3 \times 3 \mathrm{~mm}^2 $ . The scanning step and speed were  $ 10 \mathrm{mV} $  and  $ 50 \mathrm{mV/s} $ , respectively. UV/VIS spectroscopy was measured by using a Lambda 950S (PerkinElmer, Inc.). Photoluminescence emission spectra were obtained with LS-55 fluorescence spectrometer (PerkinElmer, Inc.) and an excitation wavelength of  $ 610 \mathrm{~nm} $ . Time-resolved photoluminescence spectroscopy was performed by Fluorolog TCSPC with an excitation wavelength of  $ 640 \mathrm{~nm} $  (HORIBA, Ltd.). SEM image was measured by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FEI, Teneo SEM). The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed by a VersaProbe II (Physical Electronics Inc) with a monochromator and source of Al-Kα 1486.6 eV. The spectrum was referenced using the C-C bound component of the adventitious carbon. The maximum power tracking and the stability test were performed by keeping samples under the 1 sun ( $ 100 \mathrm{mW/cm}^2 $ ) illumination with  $ 100\% $  nitrogen atmosphere and  $ 0\% $  humidity at  $ 25^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ . Structural secondary electron micrographs and chemical Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry maps of the perovskite photon absorber were carried out at a beam energy of 15-20

# HIM-SIMS

Chemical and secondary electron imaging of the perovskite surface was completed with a helium ion microscope coupled to a magnetic sector secondary ion mass spectrometer (HIM-SIMS). Mass-filtered SIMS micrographs (512 x 512 pixels) were acquired for the detection of  $ ^{28}\mathrm{Si}^+ $   $ (\mathrm{m/z} = 28) $  and  $ ^{19}\mathrm{F}^{-} $   $ (\mathrm{m/z} = 19) $ . In negative polarity, elemental SIMS maps were recorded at a beam energy of 15 keV and with a beam current of 7 pA covering a field of view of  $ 8\times 8 - 10\times 10~{\mu\mathrm{m}}^2 $  and a dwell time of 3.5 ms/pixel. In positive polarity, chemical SIMS maps were carried out with a beam energy of 20 keV and a beam current of 1.5 pA. For the image acquisition parameters in positive polarity, a dwell time of 1.5 ms/pixel for a field of view of  $ 10\times 10~{\mu\mathrm{m}}^2 $  was used and a dwell time of 2 ms/pixel for a field of view of  $ 12\times 12~{\mu\mathrm{m}}^2 $  was used.
