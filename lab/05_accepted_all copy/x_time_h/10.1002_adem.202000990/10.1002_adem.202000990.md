# Morphology and Crystallinity Amelioration of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  Perovskite in Virtue of PbI  $  _{2}  $  Thermal Absorption Drifted Performance Enhancement in Planer n–i–p Solar Cells

Bhanu Pratap Dhamaniya, Amit Kumar, Ganesh N., Priyanka Chhillar, Anaranya Ghorai, Krishna Priya Ganesan, Suresh E. Puthanveettil, Narayan K. S., and Sandeep Kumar Pathak*

Sequential deposition route is widely investigated in fabricating perovskite thin films for state-of-the-art perovskite photovoltaics. However, concerns such as lower morphological control, phase purity, and remnant unreacted salts methylammonium iodide (MAI and   $  PbI_{2}  $  ) are raised, which can significantly deteriorate optoelectronic properties, hence the operational durability of the devices. Herein, a facile two-step method to prepare high-quality perovskite thin films with reproducibility is reported, as-spun   $  PbI_{2}  $   is annealed at varying thermal input under controlled rate, and a trend in converted perovskite film properties is noted. Specifically,   $  PbI_{2}  $   thin film annealed at 200 °CC results in 20x intensified crystallinity with pinholes free and a subsequent reduction in the crystal microstrain. In addition, it provides higher surface roughness to load more MAI [in iso-propyl alcohol (IPA)]; therefore, a higher perovskite conversion is achieved. This method enables a significant efficiency enhancement in the treated sample (Pero@  $  PbI_{2}  $  -200 °C) as compared with controlled film; it retains around 90% initial efficiency after 384 h of ambient exposure. Furthermore, a facile intermediate solvent treatment method to gain the complete conversion of   $  PbI_{2}  $   into perovskite is also reported. This study highlights the importance of morphological control in governing optoelectronic properties, hence the efficiency and stability of perovskite solar cells.

In addition, this route also features other advantages such as 1) robust fabrication process due to implication of inorganic layer, 2) comparatively more robust to moisture during fabrication and in outdoor.  $  ^{[21]}  $   3) flexibility to adapt the fabrication method for each layer, and 4) flexibility to optimize each layer separately.  $  ^{[22,23]}  $   The quality of the perovskite fabricated from the sequential deposition method greatly depends on the underneath lead iodide layer and the method of conversion into perovskite. Among the two major perovskite conversion strategies, the first one demonstrated a dip-coating method where lead iodide layer is immersed into methylammonium iodide (MAI) solution.  $  ^{[24]}  $   This solution dipping technique is associated with challenges in achieving perfect conversion because slightly lower and higher dipping time can lead to incomplete conversion and perovskite leaching, respectively.  $  ^{[25]}  $   The second method, where MAI solution is spin-coated on the lead iodide layer, provides greater flexibility to optimize the process.  $  ^{[26]}  $   In both the conversion techniques, intergranular voids in   $  PbI_{2}  $   play a crucial role by offering adequate space for volume expansion—originated due to crystallographic reconstruction of   $  PbI_{2}  $   into   $  MAPbI_{3}  $  .  $  ^{[27,28]}  $

Although the two-step deposition route is proven as the more flexible, controlled, and reliable route for lab-scale and large-area perovskite solar cell fabrication, there have been some loopholes in the quality of films fabricated using this technique. The perovskite film suffers from poor surface coverage, lots of grain boundaries due to smaller grain size, and higher surface roughness due to grain shape irregularity, therefore degrading the optoelectronic quality.  $  ^{[29-31]}  $   Several approaches have been submitted to address the aforementioned issues by using additives in the precursor solution,  $  ^{[28,32]}  $   solvent engineering,  $  ^{[33-35]}  $   surface treatment,  $  ^{[36]}  $   changing the composition of the precursors,  $  ^{[37]}  $   and so on. For example, Li et al.  $  ^{[38]}  $   and Kim et al.  $  ^{[19]}  $   have used dimethyl sulfoxide-based complexes for catalytic treatment and mediator extraction treatment to improve the performance of perovskite. Li and co-workers have reported the addition of weak coordination additive (ACN) that improves the grain size and surface morphology.  $  ^{[28]}  $   Dong et al.  $  ^{[39]}  $   used 4-tert-butylpyridine additive in MAI solution which adds to large grains and higher crystallinity of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   thin films. Sun and co-workers, in their reports, have used an antimony-doped SnO  $  _{2}  $   nanorods in place of a conventional mesoporous layer to reach an efficiency of 20.1%.  $  ^{[40]}  $   To boost the performance of perovskite device, one such most effective approach is improving the morphology and surface quality of the lead iodide film.  $  ^{[41,42]}  $   A lead iodide film with large grains, ordered structure, and tight packing will produce perovskite with high crystallinity and lower number of grain boundary defects.

In this article, we report an efficient way to improve the perovskite film quality in two-step fabrication process by improving the morphology of lead iodide precursor layer through temperature-controlled crystallization process. By doing so, we found a more than threefold enhancement in the efficiency of the treated sample as compared with pristine sample. Our degradation studies also report an exceptional improvement in the environmental stability of the perovskite solar cell. We observed that this temperature profiling of lead iodide film leads to a highly crystalline, pinhole-free film with a 2.5 time increase in the crystallite size and around  $ 60\% $  reduction in the lattice microstrain. In this report, we have demonstrated how the improvement in the

structural and morphological parameters of underneath precursor layer led to a perovskite with amplified optical properties and improved carrier lifetime. Although a report  $  ^{[43]}  $  has been published on a fraction of similar interest during the course of preparation of this article, our study put forward a broader concept with more comprehensive approach and in-depth explanations of the matter using structural, microscopic, and electrical analysis. To counter the unreacted lead iodide in the film, a facile approach has also been illustrated to avoid residual  $  PbI_{2}  $  and obtain complete conversion of highly crystalline lead iodide film into perovskite.

# 2. Design and Fabrication of Perovskite Film

In this study, improvement in the morphology and crystallinity of the lead iodide film was achieved by controlled annealing protocols.   $  PbI_{2}  $   films were annealed at high temperatures as per the profile shown in Figure 1a. The temperature was increased gradually to 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 °C for 30 min including ramp. Films were allowed to cool down at ambient rate after annealing at the aforementioned temperatures. A reference control film was prepared from the conventional route by direct annealing as spin-coated   $  PbI_{2}  $   film at 70 °C for 30 min.   $  PbI_{2}  $   thin films annealed at various temperatures ranging from 70 to 300 °C are hereafter referred to as   $  PbI_{2}-K^{\circ}C  $ , where K denotes temperature value. Perovskite was converted from these films through the infiltration of MAI as explained earlier and hereafter named as Pero@  $  PbI_{2}-K^{\circ}C  $ .

Morphologies of the   $  PbI_{2}-K^{\circ}C  $   films were studied using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis. As shown in Figure 2a, lead iodide film prepared from the conventional method (direct annealing at   $  70^{\circ}C  $  ;   $  PbI_{2}-70^{\circ}C  $  ) gives very poor surface properties with incomplete surface coverage having a large number of voids. Also, the grains have very irregular morphology with no proper shape and size. A striking change in the surface characteristic was observed for the high-temperature annealed   $  PbI_{2}-K^{\circ}C  $   films. Although   $  PbI_{2}-100^{\circ}C  $   film has numerous pinholes, still the morphology looks much better as compared with   $  PbI_{2}-70^{\circ}C  $   film in terms of better surface coverage and grain regularity. As the annealing temperature of the film is ramped, grains start enlarging and relaxing providing a compact, crystalline, pinhole-free, and large grain lead iodide film, as shown in Figure 2d, for   $  PbI_{2}-200^{\circ}C  $   case.

The large grains in the film can be accounted to the impact of annealing temperature on nucleation and growth process of lead iodide films. During the heterogeneous nucleation of   $  PbI_{2}  $   on the substrate, (  $  \Delta G  $  ), Gibbs formation energy increases in the negative with rise in the annealing temperature of the substrate and approaches toward optimum conditions for stable nucleation and crystal growth thereon. As reported by Gao and Yang, an increase in substrate temperature also affects the degree of supersaturation leading to a large and compact grain growth.  $  ^{[53,54]}  $     $  PbI_{2}  $   crystallites are associated with unsaturated bonds (dangling bonds) at the interfaces and can act as the active sites in the merging process during crystal growth. Further increase in the crystallization temperature (  $  PbI_{2}-250\;^{\circ}C  $   film) supports in improving the grain size but increases the irregularity in the grains and follows a layered type structure with very high crystallinity. # 3.2. Photophysical and Optoelectronic Characterization

Figure 4b shows the absorption spectrum of all the perovskite films prepared from high-temperature annealed   $  PbI_{2}  $   films and a reference film (Pero@  $  PbI_{2}\cdot70^{\circ}C  $  ). All the films show an absorption edge rising at around 770 nm designating perovskite absorption. From Figure 4b it is evident that Pero@  $  PbI_{2}\cdot200^{\circ}C  $   film has better absorption of the visible spectrum as compared with the reference perovskite film converted from   $  PbI_{2}\cdot70^{\circ}C  $   (though Pero@  $  PbI_{2}\cdot200^{\circ}C  $   film has a comparatively higher amount of residual lead iodide).

This uptake in the absorbance value can be credited to large and coalescence grains, higher crystallinity, and complete surface coverage attained by the perovskite film.  $  ^{[61]}  $   We noted a relative low absorption for the perovskite prepared from   $  PbI_{2}\cdot250/300\ ^{\circ}C  $   films that can be due to large pinholes, intergrain gaps, and incomplete surface coverage of the films.  $  ^{[62]}  $

The electronic quality of the perovskite films was evaluated by measuring the steady-state and time-resolved PL (SSPL and TRPL) spectrums, as shown in Figure 4c,d, respectively. We note that the perovskite films prepared over bare glass substrate for SSPL and TRPL. PL spectra were measured by illuminating perovskite films with a Xenon lamp at an excitation wavelength of 520 nm. Apparently from Figure 4c, the emission intensity of the perovskite films prepared from   $  PbI_{2}-200\ ^{\circ}C  $   and   $  PbI_{2}-250\ ^{\circ}C  $   films is an order of magnitude higher than   $  PbI_{2}-70\ ^{\circ}C  $   film. # 3.3. Comprehensive Perovskite Conversion Using Polar Aprotic Solvent Treatment

As discussed earlier, we observed that high-temperature annealing of lead iodide might lead to incomplete conversion due to the boost in the crystallinity of the film (as shown in Figure S3, Supporting Information). Although the increase in the surface roughness and surface area helps in better infiltration of MAI ions, a small residue of   $  PbI_{2}  $   can be spotted in the perovskite film. To address this issue and to improve the reproducibility of the perovskite, we have implemented N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) treatment as the intermediate step in the fabrication of perovskite. An illustration of the process is shown in Figure S4, Supporting Information. Although the use of DMF has been reported earlier as an additive in MAI solution, here DMF treatment is done on the crystallized   $  PbI_{2}  $   film as an intermediate step to avoid any possibility of perovskite dissolution due to DMF content.

In this intermediate step,   $  8 \mu L mL^{-1}  $   of DMF/iso-propyl alcohol (IPA) solution was drenched on the lead iodide film just before the second step (MAI coating). This small concentration of DMF helps in loosening up the lead iodide crystallinity and led to an effective MAI infiltration. XRD patterns in Figure S5a, Supporting Information, prove that this intermediate treatment of   $  PbI_{2}-200^{\circ}C  $   film with DMF/IPA solution results in complete conversion into perovskite. We observed an increase in the absorption intensity of the treated film (Figure S5b, Supporting Information) that can be correlated to the complete conversion and a small improvement observed in emission intensity. A closer look at the absorption spectra depicts that the perovskite film prepared without DMF treatment shows a small absorption onset around 500 nm corresponding to lead iodide absorption. However, this onset is not spotted in the countercase confirming the complete transformation of lead iodide into perovskite. Morphology of the perovskite prepared from treated   $  PbI_{2}  $   shows a compact film with high crystal quality (Figure S5c, Supporting Information).

# 3.4. Photovoltaic Characterization

Perovskite solar cells in n–i–p configuration (Figure 5a) were fabricated to measure the photovoltaic performance of the perovskite prepared from PbI $ _2 $ -K °C films.  $ J $ - $ V $  curves in Figure 5b reveal the correlation in device performance with respect to the annealing temperature of PbI $ _2 $  film. The device prepared from the standard Pero@PbI $ _2 $ -70 °C film shows a very low power conversion efficiency (PCE), 4.27%, as a result of very low short-circuit current density ( $ J_{sc} $  = 8.66 mA cm $ ^{-2} $ ) and low open-circuit voltage ( $ V_{oc} $  = 0.74 V). As the annealing temperature of lead iodide film increases, the efficiency of the perovskite devices prepared thereof increases. Perovskite from PbI $ _2 $ -100 °C and

$ \mathrm{PbI}_2 - 150^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  shows a drastic improvement in the current density and significant uptake in the  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  leading to improved efficiencies of  $ 6.67\% $  and  $ 10.73\% $ , respectively, as tabulated in Table 1. The highest efficiency of  $ 13.51\% $  is exhibited by Pero@  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 - 200^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  film as a result of highest  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  (0.98 V) and highest short-circuit current density  $ J_{\mathrm{sc}} $  (18.47 mA cm $ ^{-2} $ ). These superior numbers for voltage and fill factor (FF) are obtained due to the enhanced morphology of the film, as shown in Figure 3. Large grains and pinhole-free morphology help in blocking the shunt pathways leading to remarkable voltage and FF. As the annealing temperature of lead iodide is increased further (Pero@  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 - 250^{\circ}\mathrm{C}/ $   $ 300^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  films), the FF starts falling (see Table 1) and the performance starts downgrading, showing the efficiencies of  $ 11.73\% $  and  $ 10.09\% $ , respectively. This trend can be attributed to the deterioration in the morphology of the perovskite film as explained in the previous section. The loose packing and cracks in the film result in lowering the voltage and FF.

To study the effect of solvent treatment on the device, perovskite solar cells prepared from untreated and treated   $  PbI_{2}\cdot200\ ^{\circ}C  $   film is shown in Figure S5d, Supporting Information. We observed that despite a little rise in the   $  J_{sc}  $  , the efficiency of perovskite film prepared from DMF-treated   $  PbI_{2}  $   declined to a value of 12.0% due to a drop in   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF. This suggests that the remanent   $  PbI_{2}  $   was, maybe, present at the bottom of the film instead of grain boundaries and was acting as a passivating layer to avoid cross-carrier recombination.  $  ^{[70]}  $   Or, it may be due to arising out of some shunting paths in the perovskite, hence lowering the   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF.  $  ^{[71,72]}  $   Further studies are required to precisely understand the crystal growth mechanism when polar aprotic solvent something like DMF is introduced and how it governs the mechanism.

# 3.5. Device Stability Test

The stability of the devices has been tested against relative humidity of 55–65%. Figure 5c–f clearly shows that perovskite film prepared from   $  PbI_{2}-200^{\circ}C  $   has much higher stability as compared with standard   $  PbI_{2}-70^{\circ}C  $   film. We recorded around 50% drop in the PCE for Pero@  $  PbI_{2}-70^{\circ}C  $   film within 72 h of moisture exposure, whereas only 10% degradation is seen for Pero@  $  PbI_{2}-200^{\circ}C  $   film even after 384 h of exposure. This remarkable improvement in the stability may be attributed to high crystallinity, large and coalescence grains, with very few grain boundaries providing resistance to moisture ingress.  $  ^{[73]}  $   We observed that Pero@  $  PbI_{2}-300^{\circ}C  $   film is a little less tolerant to moisture (around 35% drop is 384 h) compared with Pero@  $  PbI_{2}-200^{\circ}C  $   film (though it is still much more stable than Pero@  $  PbI_{2}-70^{\circ}C  $   film), as more intergranular space and cracks in the film might have given the way for moisture attack. It is apparent from the J-V curves and stability comparison graph that the perovskite film prepared from 200 °C annealed lead iodide shows the highest performance in terms of efficiency (13.51%) and stability compared with the other counterparts.

# 5. Experimental Section

Materials: Lead iodide (PbI $ _2 $ , Sigma-Aldrich 99.95% pure), dimethylformamide (DMF, anhydrous, Sigma-Aldrich), hydroiodic acid (HI, Alfa Aesar), methyl ammonium iodide (MAI, GreatCell Solar), isopropyl alcohol (IPA, anhydrous). All chemicals received were used without further purification.

Perovskite Film Fabrication: Glass substrates were cleaned using a 2% Hellmanex soap solution than consecutively rinsed with deionized water (DI) water, acetone, and IPA followed by sonication in acetone and IPA for 15 min each. The substrates were dried using nitrogen flushing and treated under oxygen plasma for 10 min before perovskite fabrication. The precursor solution was prepared by dissolving lead iodide in DMF (0.7 M), 10 μL of HI was added to get a transparent PbI₂ precursor solution. An 80 μL of this solution was spin-coated on the glass substrates at 3000 rpm for 30 s followed by subsequent annealing at 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 °C. Hot plate temperature was slowly increased from 70 °C to high temperatures and allowed to cool naturally. The controlled film was annealed at 70 °C. MAI in IPA solution (20 mg mL⁻¹) was prepared and spun on the lead iodide-coated precursor substrates at 2000 rpm for 60 s followed by annealing at 100 °C for 30 min.

Device Fabrication: FTO-coated glass substrate was cleaned using Hellmanex (2% in water, by volume) solution. The substrates were sonicated in acetone, isopropyl alcohol, and Milli-Q water for 2 min each. FTO substrate was further treated with oxygen plasma for 10 min.

Device Fabrication—Electron transport layer: Tin (IV) chloride pentahydrate was dissolved in IPA (17.5 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) and stirred for 30 min. The solution was then spin-coated on cleaned FTO substrate at 3000 rpm for 30 s followed by drying at 100 °C for 10 min and annealing at 180 °C for an hour.

The perovskite layer was fabricated as described earlier.

Device Fabrication—Hole Transport Layer: Spiro-OMeTAD was dissolved in anhydrous chlorobenzene (85   $  \mu  $  L mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ). A solution containing 4-tert-butylpyridine (33   $  \mu  $  L) and a solution of 500 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   bis(trifluoromethane)-sulfonimide lithium salt in acetonitrile (20   $  \mu  $  L) was further added. The solution was spin-coated at 2500 rpm for 25 s. Thick gold electrodes (80 nm) were deposited by thermal evaporation under   $  <9\times10^{-6}  $   Torr pressure.

Characterization Techniques: Perovskite films were characterized by Zeiss EVO 18 Scanning Electron Microscope for morphology analysis. XRD measurements were performed using Rigaku ultima IV diffractometer system with Cu Kα radiation source at the scan rate of   $  5^{\circ} min^{-1}  $   and step size of   $  0.02^{\circ}  $  . Absorption spectroscopy was performed using Perkin-Elmer Lambda 1050 spectrophotometer. PL data were collected using Horiba instrument. AFM measurements were recorded using Bruker's Dimension Icon instrument.
