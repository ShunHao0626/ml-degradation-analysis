# Abstract

The defects within perovskite films are the fatal roadblock limiting the performance of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Herein, we develop a patching strategy to obtain high-quality perovskite film using bifunctional trifluorophenylacetic acid (TFPA). Theoretical calculation and experiments reveal that the -COOH groups in TFPA effectively passivate the deep-energy-level defects of perovskite by combining with Pb clusters on the perovskite grain surfaces to enhance the efficiency of PSCs, and hydrophobic benzene groups containing fluorine in TFPA are exposed to improve the stability of PSCs. The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs with TFPA is enhanced from  $ 22.95\% $  to  $ 24.56\% $ . Most importantly, we successfully fabricated flexible PSCs using TFPA with the high PCE of  $ 22.65\% $ . The devices with TFPA maintain  $ 93.22\% $  of the initial efficiency MPP under continuous irradiation for  $ 10.5\mathrm{h} $ , while the devices without TFPA only retain  $ 82.22\% $  of the initial efficiency under the same conditions for just  $ 5\mathrm{h} $ . Meanwhile, the unsealed devices with TFPA hold  $ 93.59\% $  of the initial efficiency when stored in air for  $ 3912\mathrm{h} $ .

# 1 | INTRODUCTION

In just a few years, perovskite materials have become a rising star in optoelectronics due to their advantages, such as adjustable band gap, high light-absorption coefficient, long carrier-diffusion length, and so on.  $  ^{1-3}  $   In particular, the certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of the organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cell (PSC) has been rapidly increased to 25.8%,  $  ^{4}  $   higher than all other types of thin-film solar cells. However, there are some emerging challenges with the development of PSCs. One is fabricating large-scale cells, which is limited by the fabrication technology for perovskite devices.  $  ^{5-8}  $   Another is the long-term stability, which is affected by the defects within the perovskite absorber and is also dominated by humidity.  $  ^{9-12}  $

There are three types of vacancy defects in perovskite films, namely, the organic vacancy (  $  V_{org}  $  ), metal vacancy (  $  V_{m}  $  ), and halogen vacancy (  $  V_{ha}  $  ) defects.  $  ^{13}  $   The evidence has demonstrated that passivation engineering is an effective strategy to reduce the defects. So far, the passivation materials have mainly included Lewis acids, Lewis bases and ionic liquids. Lewis acids can form coordination or ionic bonds with uncoordinated halides such as   $  PbI_{3}^{-}  $  to eliminate the corresponding defects, thus reducing nonradiative recombination to increase the carrier lifetime. Metal salt-based Lewis acids such as NaF,  $  ^{14}  $     $  Y-Th_{2}  $  ,  $  ^{15}  $   and triphenylphosphine oxide (TPPO)  $  ^{16}  $   have been added into perovskite precursors as additives and have been proved to be effective in passivating anion vacancies in perovskite films. Lewis bases such as C=O,  $  ^{17-21}  $     $  S=O  $  ,  $  ^{22,23}  $   and   $  -NH_{2}  $    $  ^{17,18,24,25}  $   are often used as electron donors to combine with uncoordinated lead ions and organic cations, and they are all considered to interact with   $  Pb^{2+}  $   to stabilize the perovskite structure and passivate defects. In addition, ionic liquids (IL) generally exist in liquid form at room temperature and are often used as additives in perovskite precursor solutions due to their high ionic conductivity and stability. For instance, 1,3-dimethyl-3-imidazolium hexafluorophosphate (DMIMPF  $  _{6}  $  ) IL,  $  ^{26}  $   1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium hydrogen sulfate EMIMHSO  $  _{4}  $   IL,  $  ^{27}  $   butylammonium acetate (BAAc) IL,  $  ^{28}  $   formamidine acetate (FAAc),  $  ^{29}  $   dimethylammonium (DMA),  $  ^{30}  $   and alkali salts  $  ^{31}  $   have been employed as additives in perovskite precursor

solutions to passivate defects in perovskite films. Therefore, the primary task is to find suitable additives to realize a reduction in the number of defects, which could improve the quality and stability of perovskite films.

Humidity is one of the most important factors affecting the device stability because water would erode the perovskite film. Consequently, it is essential to improve the hydrophobic properties of the perovskite films. Fluorinated compounds are commonly used as the modification layers to improve the hydrophobicity of perovskite films. Wang et al.  $  ^{32}  $  found that the fluoride group can improve the hydrophobicity of perovskite film, thus improving the overall stability of PSCs. Subsequently, 2,3,4,5,6-pentafluorobenzyl phosphonic acid (PFBPA)  $  ^{33}  $  and F-PEAI  $  ^{34,35}  $  are employed to modify perovskite films to successfully improve their humidity stability.

Here, we use 2,3,5-trifluorophenylacetic acid (TFPA) as a bifunctional additive, which could not only reduce the defects within the perovskite absorber due to combination with lead and halogen vacancies but also improve the stability of PSCs owing to the strong hydrophobicity of TFPA. Theoretical calculation and experimental characterizations reveal that —COOH in TFPA interacts with lead ions, and obtuse-angle halogen anion vacancies to reduce the defects in perovskite films. As a result, the efficiency is up to 24.56% for a rigid device and 22.65% for a flexible cell when using TFPA additive, which is one of the highest efficiencies for flexible perovskite devices. The optimized device based on TFPA without encapsulation maintains 93.22% of the initial efficiency after 10.5 h of tracking at the maximum power point (MPP) under continuous irradiation (  $  \sim  $  62% humidity). The unsealed device with TFPA retains 93.59% of its initial efficiency after storage in the air for 3912 h, while the comparison without TFPA device retains only 46.69% of its initial value under the same conditions. The outstanding stability originates from the better hydrophobicity of TFPA, which combines with perovskite to prohibit the moisture permeation.

We investigated different TFPA concentrations added into perovskite precursor solution. The J-V curves and parameters are shown in Figure S7 and Table S1. It is found that the short-circuit current density (  $  J_{sc}  $  ) is almost same regardless of the TFPA concentration, which is caused by the similar absorption, as shown in Figure S8. When the concentration of TFPA is >4 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  , the efficiency sharply decreases owing to poor solubility in the perovskite precursor solution.

Figure 4A shows the steady-state photoluminescence (PL) of perovskite films with and without TFPA deposited on glass substrates. It is apparent that the PL intensity of the perovskite film with TFPA is much higher than that of pristine perovskite film. The strong PL intensity demonstrates the weak radiative recombination,  $  ^{41,42}  $  which is due to the decreased defects when using TFPA (Figures 1A and S5). Figure 4B shows their corresponding time-resolved PL (TRPL) spectra, and their fitting parameters are listed in Table S3. In general, the fast-decay lifetime (  $  \tau_{1}  $  ) and slow-decay lifetime (  $  \tau_{2}  $  ) come from the nonradiative recombination at the interface and radiative recombination in the bulk perovskite, respectively.  $  ^{43,44}  $  When using TFPA,  $  \tau_{2}  $  significantly increases to 4255.93 from 2098.53 ns, implying that the radiative recombination is effectively suppressed. In addition, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements are performed to study the charge transport and recombination process in the device. The Nyquist plots of PSCs with or without TFPA are shown in Figure S10, and the fitting data are summarized in Table S4. The charge-transport resistance (  $  R_{tr}  $  ) decreases from 540.3 to 55.6  $  \Omega  $ , and the recombination resistance (  $  R_{rec}  $  ) increases to 1218.0 from 1099.0  $  \Omega  $  after employing TFPA. These results demonstrate that the charge recombination is significantly reduced due to the decrease of the defect density in the perovskite by TFPA, which is consistent with the large  $  V_{oc}  $  and FF.

Figure 4C shows that   $  V_{oc}  $   varies linearly with the light intensity. In general, the deviation between the slope and the value of (kT/q) reveals the degree of trap-assisted recombination.  $  ^{45-49}  $   The device with TFPA shows the smaller slope of 1.19 compared with the slope of 1.48 for the pristine perovskite, demonstrating less trap-assisted recombination with TFPA, which is in good agreement with the experimental value (Figure S5) and calculation result (Figure 1A). It is obvious that the device with TFPA shows the higher   $  V_{bi}  $ , which produces the large   $  V_{oc}  $ .

The transient absorption decays (TA) were measured to systematically investigate the dynamics of charge transfer in the bulk perovskite and at the different

interfaces. Figure 4D–F show the TA spectra of perovskite with and without TFPA deposited on different substrates, including glass, FTO/ETL, and FTO/ETL/perovskite/HTL. The perovskite film with TFPA on glass exhibits the larger   $  \tau_{ave}  $   of 60.71 ns compared with perovskite without TFPA (49.07 ns), further indicating the effective passivation. The above results verify again the defects of the perovskite have been efficiently passivated by TFPA, in excellent accordance with the calculated and other experimental results.

# 3 | CONCLUSION

In conclusion, TFPA was developed as an additive to effectively passivate the defects of perovskite for high performance of PSCs. Calculation and experimental results reveal that —COOH groups in TFPA combine with Pb-cluster defects on the FAI-terminated surface to reduce the defects, and benzene groups containing fluorine in TFPA are exposed, leading to much greater hydrophobicity of the treated perovskite film. As a result, due to the uniform perovskite films, the PCE of PSCs increases from 22.95% to 24.56% when using TFPA, and the high efficiency of 22.65% for flexible PSCs is obtained, which is among the highest efficiencies of flexible perovskite devices. More importantly, the stability of PSCs is significantly improved when using TFPA. Under continuous illumination, the efficiency of an unsealed device with TFPA is 93.22% of the initial value after MPP tracking for 10.5 h, while the device without TFPA only maintained 82.22% of the initial efficiency after just 5 h. When the unsealed devices were stored in air for 3912 h, the efficiency was 46.56% of the initial value. The devices with TFPA hold 93.59% of the initial efficiency under the same conditions. We believe this development of passivating additive will accelerate the transition of perovskite devices to commercialization.
