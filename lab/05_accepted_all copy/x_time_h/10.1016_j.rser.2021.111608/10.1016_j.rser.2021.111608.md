In the past decade perovskite solar cells have received immense attention and an astounding advance in terms of power conversion efficiency is achieved. The best achieved power conversion efficiency for single junction device is around 25 % which is comparable to the well-established and commercialized silicon solar cell technology. The poor lifetime and stability of these devices remain a major bottleneck, which needs to be resolved quickly as these factors play a significant hurdle towards commercialization of this technology. Apart from developing new stable perovskite material and optimizing the device architecture, a robust barrier/encapsulation with high barrier performance materials is one of the best ways to address the poor lifetime/stability problems encountered in these devices. Polymer based encapsulation methodology can satisfy both the needs of maintaining a balance between processing cost and device lifetime. In this review article an extensive outline on the degradation mechanisms commonly seen in perovskite solar cells is first accounted. In depth analysis about the various requirements an encapsulant material must satisfy and different types of encapsulant materials that were used earlier as an encapsulant in perovskite solar cells are explained. Various techniques that are commonly adopted for the encapsulation procedure is discussed briefly. This review is concluded by mentioning few promising strategies in encapsulation which could help in improving the overall device stability and lifetime of perovskite solar cells.

The need for a robust and proven technology to convert renewable energy to electricity is more demanding and, in this context, solar photovoltaic (PV) technology has emerged as a key technology towards facile production of electricity. Solar energy is the most abundant, clean, and easiest to handle renewable energy. In the year 2019 about 3% of world's total electricity was produced using solar PV technology [1]. Silicon PV technology is the market dominant (95% in 2019) and most mature of all the PV technologies available today [2]. It has many significant advantages like relatively high module power conversion efficiencies (20–21%), acceptable lifetime (20–25 years), the feedstock material is abundantly available and also it is non-toxic [3,4]. Even though many significant advancements have been made in the silicon solar cells production and the PV unit prices are continuously brought down, the overall cost for generating PV electricity is still relatively high compared to traditional coal-based power generation [5]. Moreover, a high vacuum, high temperature (up to 1400 °C), handling of toxic and pyrophoric materials during the production and a precise doping of atoms is required for making a simple silicon solar cell [5]. In this context, researchers are always on the lookout for a new material which can be an alternative to silicon with the following attributes - comparatively cheaper to crystalline silicon, can be relatively straightforward to process and most importantly show similar performance (efficiency wise). To this effect, metal halide-based perovskite materials have gained a significant interest among the research community in the last 6–7 years [6–9].

Perovskite based solar cell technology is the fastest developing PV technology which has the potential to compete or at least complement the silicon PV technology as of today. The salient features that makes this technology quite attractive are as follows a) can be processed by low cost wet chemical solution method, b) can be deposited on both rigid and flexible substrates, c) light weight, and d) can be used as a top cell in combination with silicon solar cells on a tandem cell configuration towards achieving high efficiency beyond 30 % [10]. Perovskite solar cells (PVSC) can be fabricated using solution processable methodologies like spin coating, dip coating and spray coating from chemicals that are easily available like organic (methylammonium, formamidinium) or inorganic (cesium, rubidium) halides and lead iodide. The precursor materials that are required for making these solar cell absorber are readily soluble in common organic solvents and this fact enables high volume throughput manufacturing techniques like roll-to-roll (R2R) [11,12]. Printable solar cells come under the category of printed electronics. These types of solar cells can be made on a variety of substrates using organic or organic – inorganic hybrid inks and metal electrodes. For Printing and scaling up of solution processable solar cells the most common techniques used are roll-to-roll (R2R) method, blade coating, slot-die coating, inkjet printing and spray coating [13,14].

Organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite materials are a class of novel semiconductor material that shows superior light harvesting capability. It has the general formula of   $  ABX_{3}  $  , in which A is a larger monovalent cation such as methylammonium (  $  MA^{+}  $  ), formamidinium (  $  FA^{+}  $  ) or cesium (  $  Cs^{+}  $  ), B is a smaller divalent metallic cation such as lead (  $  Pb^{2+}  $  ) or tin (  $  Sn^{2+}  $  ) and X is a halide anion (iodide (  $  I^{-}  $  ), bromide (  $  Br^{-}  $  ) or chloride (  $  Cl^{-}  $  ) [15]). A very commonly used organic-inorganic perovskite material, methyl ammonium lead iodide, exhibits excellent photophysical and electrical properties, such as high absorption coefficient [16,17], low exciton binding energy [18,19], long charge carrier diffusion length [20,21] and easy band gap tunability through simple solution processable techniques [22]. Low activation energy (56.6–97.3 kJ mol  $  ^{-1}  $  ) for nucleation and crystallization energy of these perovskite materials, renders them a viable candidate for low temperature high volume throughput manufacturing techniques [23], and it is worth mentioning here that these values are much lower than that of polycrystalline silicon (280–470 kJ mol  $  ^{-1}  $  ) [24].

The above-mentioned advantages of perovskite materials have led to an increased interest in both academic and industrial research.

Consequently, a substantial development towards their performance i.e. power conversion efficiency (PCE) has been achieved. The PCE of perovskite solar cells have increased from 3.8 % to 25.5 % in less than a decade [25]. A respectable increase in the efficiency of PVSCs irrespective of size - from small area cells (  $  \sim  $  0.1 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ), large area cells (  $  \sim  $  1 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ) and mini modules (  $  \sim  $  >10 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ) has been observed [26]. Impressive power conversion efficiency close of 25 % achieved in small active area makes it suitable and worthwhile to take this from the device level research to product level research. These recent developments have led to silicon-perovskite tandem devices [27,28] and commercialization of perovskite solar cells by companies like Oxford PV, Saule Technologies etc. [29]. These devices also find other interesting applications in the areas of flexible electronics and building integrated photovoltaics (BIPV) [30–32].

In this juncture, it is worth mentioning that the commercialization does not purely depend on achieving high PCEs only, but it must be cost effective also. The main requirements for a cost effective power generation using photovoltaic systems are a) minimal system cost, b) high initial performance, and c) performance loss should be minimal in the long run over 20–25 years (see Fig. 1) [33].

The main obstacles that prevent the successful commercialization of perovskite solar cells are as follows a) the instability of the absorber material in ambient conditions, b) lead toxicity, and c) lack of long-term device stability. The commonly used photoactive layer or light absorber in PVSC, methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ) is prone to photodegradation and also the degradation is enhanced in the presence of moisture and oxygen [34]. In this context, it is worth mentioning here that the silicon based solar cells can maintain their performance over many years of operation with a very minimal performance loss over their lifetime.

Song et al. have carried out a techno-economic analysis for manufacturing perovskite solar modules using feasible low-cost materials and by assuming that the modules are operating at 16% PCE, Song et al. have estimated that the overall manufacturing cost will be around \$31.7 per m $  ^{2}  $  and the minimum sustainable price (MSP) will be \$0.41 per W $  _{p}  $  [35]. The above-mentioned values are indeed very promising and to realize the commercialization of PVSCs the following aspects needs to be addressed quickly - instability of the photoactive layer in the presence of oxygen, moisture, and UV light. # 2. Reliability issues in perovskite solar cells

The active layer in the perovskite solar cell is well known to be unstable in air and it is also known to undergo photocatalytic and photochemical reaction when exposed to sunlight which causes photo degradation   $  [56–59]  $  . The temperature dependent photodegradation is a serious threat to PVSCs commercialization since during real time operation these solar cells are subjected to high illumination conditions in the presence of air and moisture which increases the rate of degradation. Perovskite degradation can occur through two main mechanistic pathways a) intrinsic degradation, and b) extrinsic degradation and these processes can occur both in the photoactive material and in the device. Broadly, intrinsic properties such as structural properties, phase stability and defect tolerance in the perovskite crystal affects the overall stability. External factors like moisture, oxygen, temperature, and UV-light illumination also plays a major role in the degradation process.

# 2.1. Intrinsic stability of perovskite

The formation of perovskite structure is governed by the Goldschmidt tolerance factor, t [60].

$$
t = \frac {R _ {A} + R _ {B}}{\sqrt {2 (R _ {X} + R _ {B})}}
$$

(1)

where   $  R_{A}  $   is the atomic radius of the A cation,   $  R_{B}  $   is the atomic radius of the B cation and   $  R_{X}  $   is the atomic radius of the X anion. In the simplest form,   $  ABX_{3}  $   perovskites consists of a hexa-coordinated metal cation (B) occupying the octahedral centers which share their halide ion (X).

corners, (A) cation fills the voids left by every eight of those octahedral located at the corners of a classical cubic cell. When the tolerance factor is greater than 1 the crystal will be hexagonal or tetragonal, between 0.9 and 1 will give raise to a cubic structure, between 0.71 and 0.9 orthorhombic or rhombohedral structures will be formed, and tolerance factor less than 0.71 will not result in a perovskite structure [60]. In the case of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ , cubic perovskite, MA and Pb donates one and two electrons respectively to three I ions resulting in the observed band gap of  $ 1.5\mathrm{eV} $  between the unoccupied 'B' cation's (Pb) p-orbital and occupied X anion's (I) p-orbital [61].

Wide variety of perovskite materials can be formed by mixing organic and inorganic cations along with halide anions and the bandgap of these materials can be widely tuned from visible to near infra-red region quite easily. The cubic phase of the perovskite crystal is greatly influenced by temperature, by increasing the temperature, above  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  methylammonium lead halide perovskite undergoes phase transition from orthorhombic to tetragonal and then to cubic [62], whereas cesium lead halide perovskite undergoes phase transition, above  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ , from orthorhombic to cubic [63]. The details of the phase transition temperature are given in Table 1. Proper composition of materials and a perfect phase transition temperature are the necessary criterion any perovskite material must satisfy to find suitable application as absorber/photo active material in a solar cell.

Formation of pure cubic phase of perovskite requires a correct composition of starting materials and precise annealing temperature. The prepared film should stay homogenous during the operation of solar cell and for that it should be energetically favorable to form a stable cubic phase and presence of any traces of other phase materials will have serious impact on the solar cell performance. As the formation energies of these materials are generally low, there is always a variation that is observed in the ratio of active materials between the final composition of the film and the initial solution that is used as a starting material [68]. Altering the growth conditions will change the formation energy and this will lead to defect formation in the bulk and in the surface. In the formation of defects, the ratio of 'B' site cation to 'X' site anion plays a major role, while the 'A' site cation helps to maintain the structural stability [69]. As the perovskite is a highly crystalline material the defects in the bulk and in the surface varies a lot. The surface with a broken unit cell are termed as defect points or point defect [70]. This kind of defects have very low formation energy and hence the degradation of the material starts from these defect sites and hence it becomes imperative to properly tune the composition, annealing temperature and the additives that are used to make a pure single phase perovskite material.

2.2.1. Illumination (photo stability)

It has been proven that the light illumination on  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  will result in the formation of iodine vacancies [57]. Kim et al. stated that the holes that are generated by the photon absorption will oxidize the iodine atoms in the lattice which further leads to the formation of neutral interstitials and iodine vacancies, this neutral iodine because of its smaller size can easily migrate from the lattice and can easily fit in the interstitial site.

$$
I _ {I} ^ {X} + h ^ {+} \rightarrow I _ {i} ^ {X} + V _ {I} ^ {+}
$$

(2)

This free iodine vacancies results in reduction of the activation energies which further paves path for faster degradation [75]. The neutral iodine is easily removed via sublimation or other chemical reactions. The degradation process not only starts from the active layer, but it also highly depends on the device architecture [34,45]. A recent study reported by Leijtens et al. revealed that the PVSC with mesoporous  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  layer as electron transport layer (ETL) is susceptible to degrade under UV light [76]. Leijtens et al. have carried out device degradation analysis with and without encapsulation coupled with UV filter. Degradation studies were carried out under 1 sun AM 1.5G illumination for  $ 5\mathrm{h} $ . Encapsulated device without UV filter showed faster degradation. The rapid decrease observed in the power conversion efficiency value indicated that the mesoporous  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  degrades quite rapidly under UV light. This degradation is mainly attributed to the desorption of oxygen molecules adsorbed on the surface of  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  shown in Fig. 2. Also, the mesoporous  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  can easily extract electrons from  $ \mathrm{I}^- $  ion in the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  crystal, resulting in the formation of Iodine  $ (\mathrm{I}_2) $ , which further reconstructs the perovskite crystal [77].

2.2.2. Temperature (thermal stability)

Changes in the crystal structure of perovskites due to temperature is a well-known phenomenon [62]. From Table 1 we can see that the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  undergoes phase transition from tetragonal to cubic around  $ 54 - 56^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . To ascertain whether the solar cells can be employed for real time applications the devices are first subjected to high temperature test  $ (85^{\circ}\mathrm{C}) $  according to the prescribed standards provided by International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC 61215) [78]. Conings et al. have reported earlier that temperature above  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  can result in the decomposition of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  and the decomposition is observed irrespective of the conditions (dry nitrogen, dry  $ \mathrm{O}_2 $  and at dark with  $ 50\% $  relative humidity). A continuous exposure of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  films at  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $   $ (0.093\mathrm{eV}- $  energy estimated for  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C}) $  will start to degrade the films because the formation energy of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $   $ (0.11 - 0.14\mathrm{eV}) $  lies closely to the testing temperature of  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $   $ (0.093\mathrm{eV}) $  [79]. At higher temperature, the degradation of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  occurs in both reversible and irreversible ways as explained below

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathrm {CH_ {3} NH_ {3} PbI_ {3} (S)} \leftrightarrow \mathrm {PbI_ {2} (S)} + \mathrm {CH_ {3} NH_ {2} (g)} + \mathrm{HI(g)} \\ \mathrm {CH_ {3} NH_ {3} PbI_ {3} (S)} \leftrightarrow \mathrm {PbI_ {2} (S)} + \mathrm {CH_ {3} I(g)} + \mathrm {NH_ {3} (g)} \end{array} \tag {4}

(3)

To withstand higher temperature (above 85 °C), a superior thermal resistant material should be used as absorber because thermal stability is a very important parameter for a number of reasons: (1) all perovskite absorber films and charge transport layers (CTL) needs thermal annealing during the device fabrication, (2) in most cases higher than 100 °C is required for encapsulation processes for short periods of time, (3) during real time application in hot climatic conditions, photovoltaic devices can easily reach 65 °C, and finally (4) most of the durability tests are usually done in higher temperature. The accelerated aging/weathering test standards for measuring the stability of perovskite solar cells is given by International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC-61215) [80] and the International Summit on Organic Photovoltaic Stability (ISOS) protocols recently [81]. 2.2.4. Oxygen (photooxidative stability)

The perovskite materials are fairly stable to oxygen while stored in dark (see Fig. 3), but these materials tend to rapidly decompose in the presence of light and oxygen [73]. Pearson et al. proposed that the photogenerated electrons in the absorber enables a reaction between the methylammonium organic cation in the perovskite and a superoxide, a process that results in the decomposition of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  into methylamine, lead iodide, iodine, and water as shown below [83]:

$$
\mathrm{CH} _ {3} \mathrm{NH} _ {3} \mathrm{PbI} _ {3} + \mathrm{O} _ {2} ^ {\bullet -} \rightarrow \mathrm{CH} _ {3} \mathrm{NH} _ {2} + \mathrm{PbI} _ {2} + 1 / 2 \mathrm{I} _ {2} + \mathrm{H} _ {2} \mathrm{O}
$$

(6)

In PVSCs not only the active layer, even the charge transport layers are also susceptible to degradation through oxidation in ground and excited states   $  [84]  $  . Generally, charge transport layers are prepared from either organic small molecules and polymers or inorganic materials such as metal oxides. Oxidation of these materials lowers the charge carrier mobility which ultimately affects the performance of the device. From the above discussion, it is apparent that the appropriate choice of perovskite composition, suitable device architecture and electrode design is paramount for the long-term stability of perovskite solar cells. In this context, to protect the PVSCs devices without compromising on the power conversion efficiency, post-encapsulation methodology is recognized as an effective strategy to tie up both performance and long-term stability   $  [55,85,86]  $  .

# 3. Encapsulation

For achieving better device lifetime, encapsulation plays an important role by preventing the ingress of moisture and oxygen into the device. In the case of silicon PV modules an encapsulant resin with glass, an edge sealant and a back sheet is commonly used (see Fig. 4) [54]. For a device like organic light emitting diode (OLED) fabricated on glass or flexible substrate, encapsulation is usually done using cover glass slip and UV epoxy sealing. This type of encapsulation showed considerable improvement in the lifetime of these devices [87]. A good encapsulant material should have the following qualities a) able to hold the layers/stacks of the device, b) good insulation property, c) should possess good light transmittance, and d) block the ingress of moisture and oxygen into the device [88]. Since encapsulation plays an important role in the photovoltaic module many academic research groups and industries like Dow Corning, Du Pont, 3 M, and Stevens Urethanes, etc. are very actively working in this research area [89].

# 3.1. Encapsulation requirements

Encapsulation methodology in silicon photovoltaics is well matured and the requirements that an encapsulant material must satisfy are less stringent compared to the encapsulants used in organic electronics. As the device degradation is still a complex phenomenon to understand and address in perovskite solar cells, an encapsulation of the whole device seems to be the most appropriate way to aid in faster commercialization of these devices. Encapsulant materials used in perovskite solar cells must be chemically compatible with all the layers of the solar cell and hence must be carefully chosen keeping in mind the more complex and specific degradation mechanisms that takes place in these solar cells. 3.1.2. Oxygen and water vapor transmission rate

Prevention of moisture ingress is paramount to improve the lifetime of the perovskite solar cells and for this a glass and an encapsulant along with an edge sealant is often used as this helps in blocking the moisture ingress through the edges. The optimum level of WVTR is  $ 10^{-4} \cdot 10^{-6} $  gm $ ^{-2} $ day $ ^{-1} $  and OTR is  $ 10^{-3} \cdot 10^{-5} $  cm $ ^{3} $ m $ ^{-2} $ day $ ^{-1} $ atm $ ^{-1} $  [88,92,93]. The water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) can be measured by techniques like calcium test (measurement of resistance of encapsulated metal), desiccant method (ASTM E96), AMETEK MOCON permeation testing (ASTM F1249) [94]. Oxygen transmission rate (OTR) can be determined by similar methods (ASTM D3985, F1927). Assuming that the diffusivity of water vapor and oxygen obeys Fick's law (diffusivity is independent of concentration), the general formula for measuring transmission rate is given as

$$
W V T R = \frac {D C s}{l} \left[ 1 + 2 \sum_ {n = 0} ^ {n} (- 1) ^ {n} e ^ {\left[ - \frac {D n ^ {2} \pi l}{l ^ {2}} \right]} \right]
$$

where D is the diffusivity, t is the time,  $  C_s  $  is the surface saturation concentrations, and l is the film thickness. WVTR and OTR is a function of temperature and normally diffusivity, D increases with increasing temperature. The transmission rates are greatly accelerated by temperature and in particular high transmission rates at high temperatures results in rapid mass transport processes [95].

Due to the presence of different functional groups and different chemistry involved in the preparation of encapsulants, the transmission rates (TR) varies a lot. The more polar a polymer, the more hydrophilic it is and hence higher is the moisture vapor transmission rate [96]. The thermoplastic olefin (TPO) with a very non-polar polymer show the lowest TRs and silicone on the other side, with the highly polar Si–C bond shows highest TRs [96].

3.1.3. Mechanical requirements

Encapsulants used in a photovoltaic module must compensate the mechanical stress that develops over time in the solar cell during the thermal cycling process. In perovskite solar cells, annealing at higher temperature (  $  >80\ ^{\circ}C  $  ) is often required to form proper perovskite film on TCO coated glass or polymeric substrate and this results in the buildup of mechanical stress between the layers. The coefficient of thermal expansion is low for glass (in the order of   $  \sim10\times10^{-6}\circ C^{-1}  $  ) and inorganic materials, but the perovskite layer, organic and polymeric inter layers are always subjected to mechanical stress when they are cooled down. This results in the buildup of tensile stress in between the layers which further results in delamination, cracking and because of this ingress of moisture takes place which ultimately causes degradation. To overcome these drawbacks the elastic modulus of the encapsulant material must be low enough to reduce the stress in the layers.

Temperature-dependent storage or elastic modulus is an important factor that needs to be kept in mind when a new encapsulant material is synthesized, a drop in storage modulus with an increase in temperature

usually indicates a phase change; crystalline to amorphous, or in other words glass transition temperature. For the PV modules, typical thermal cycling tests is carried out in the temperature range between  $ -40^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  to  $ +85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  and any phase change around  $ -20^{\circ}\mathrm{C} - 10^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  leads to build up of shear stress between the layers which leads to breakage of the solar cells [49]. Tensile strength greater than  $ 20\mathrm{Mpa} $  is typically required to withstand any objects falling (example - hail) on the device. For commercialization purposes, the whole encapsulant system which comprises of encapsulant and glass must pass a standard hail impact test (FM 44787). Further, the encapsulant material should also possess a high creep resistance. Cross linked polymers tend to show higher creep resistance than non-cross linked ones [96]. Cross linking helps to hold the stack of layers and helps in slowing down the migration of moisture and oxygen into the device from the atmosphere.

3.1.4. Electrical requirements

Encapsulant material in general should be an electrical insulator to minimize the leakage current. Volume resistivity is the quantification of the material to oppose the electrical current flow and it is directly related to the stability of a solar cell. In case of silicon solar cells, low resistance in encapsulant materials has been linked to electrochemical corrosion and potential induced degradation (PID) [97] and hence a high surface and volume resistant material is required for encapsulation application.

3.1.5. Chemical requirements

Chemical requirements include inertness to chemicals at higher temperature, water absorption, adhesion to substrate and ease of lamination. Encapsulant material should not react with any chemical and to check the suitability of an encapsulant whether it satisfies this aspect or not usually immersion or spraying of chemicals on copper (Cu) coupons covered with encapsulant material is carried out. If no reaction occurs on the coupons at  $ 90^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  then one can safely say that the encapsulant is chemically inert [98]. In the case of perovskite solar cells all the layers are generally fabricated by wet chemical coating process and hence there may be a chance of residual solvents present in the solar cell. This being the case it becomes even more important that the encapsulant material not only should be chemically inert but also should not react with solvent or with any other layers which may cause untoward chemical reaction and thus overall will have a detrimental effect on the performance of the solar cell.

Lamination is an important step in the process of encapsulation, this means the melting point of the encapsulant should be low (typical lamination temperature for commercially available encapsulant materials is 140–160 °C) and the viscosity should be at optimum level at that temperature. For cross-linking polymers, the cross-linking should not start too early to avoid gas bubble formation. During and after cooling, the encapsulant should possess extremely low (<1 %) shrinkage otherwise it may lead to cracking and delamination issues. Adhesion of encapsulants should be done without any treatment on surfaces like glass, silicon, silicon nitride and metal electrodes. The adhesion to glass before weathering should be in the range of 4–7 N/mm [99]. After damp heat testing, the adhesion can be as low as 1 N/mm. The adhesion is measured by standard peel-off test: either 90° peel test or 180° peel test (ASTM D 903). Because of all these requirements, the selection, development and integration of high-barrier encapsulation films in the area of organic electronics remains very challenging and remains an interesting topic for further research and development [100].

4.1.2.5. Poly (methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) as encapsulant in PVSC. PMMA is an acrylic based polymer with great flexural strength, transparency, and UV tolerance. This polymer has been used as an encapsulant in perovskite solar cells to understand the degradation mechanism   $  [116]  $  . In 2015 Li et al. made wearable double-twisted fibrous perovskite solar cell with single walled carbon nano tubes encapsulated by PMMA. The encapsulated device was able to withstand 1000 bending cycles without delamination and retained 90 % of its initial power conversion efficiency after 96 h of air exposure (see Fig. 13 (a))   $  [129]  $  . Han et al. encapsulated a perovskite device by spin coating graphene oxide mixed with PMMA. Han et al. observed that the devices encapsulated only by PMMA starts to degrade after 100 h in humid conditions (75 % RH) whereas the devices encapsulated with PMMA + graphene oxide retained 90 % of its initial PCE even after 500 h and the devices were able to retain 80 % of its initial PCE after keeping at 85 °C for 96 h (see Fig. 13 b))   $  [130]  $  .

PMMA has been used as an interlayer   $  [131,132]  $   and passivation layer   $  [133]  $   to increase the device lifetime.

4.1.2.6. Thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) as encapsulant in PVSC. TPU is a block copolymer consisting of alternating hard and soft segments. TPU is made by reacting polyols (short chain and long chain diols) with diisocyanates. By changing their ratio, the structure and properties can be varied widely. This polymer is used in PV modules where a higher adhesion is required. TPU is the only encapsulant that adheres well on polyester without any surface treatment [125]. TPU has been widely used as an encapsulant in almost all electronic devices [134]. Due of its chemical nature it can be used as a film encapsulant [135], or even as resin [136]. In the year 2015 Kaltenbrunner et al. have used polyurethane as an encapsulant material and made ultra-thin perovskite

solar cell [137]. Their devices were quite stable in outside environment.

Fu et al. fabricated a triple mesoscopic HTM free perovskite sub module   $  (10 \times 10 \, \text{cm}^{2})  $   and encapsulated the module using TPU, polyolefin and EVA. The small active area devices were subjected to thermal stress by heating at   $  60 \, ^{\circ}C  $   for 50 h, then at   $  85 \, ^{\circ}C  $   for 50 h, and finally to   $  120 \, ^{\circ}C  $   for 30 h. Fu et al. have observed color change in devices encapsulated by EVA due to the release of acetic acid, but the devices encapsulated by polyolefin and TPU showed no color change. The device encapsulated by TPU showed no performance degradation over 325 h after keeping at   $  85 \, ^{\circ}C  $   in ambient condition as shown in Fig. 14. The sub module retained 97.52 % of the initial PCE after 2136 h after keeping it in outside environment [138].

4.1.2.7. Fluoro polymers as encapsulant in PVSC. Fluoro polymers are high end engineering materials with excellent chemical resistance, heat resistance and electrical resistance. They have been used in all semiconductor-based technologies for their inherent hydrophobic nature and insulating property. Hwang et al. have used polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) as a facile hydrophobic passivation layer on top of the perovskite device which makes it high hydrophobic surface as shown in Fig. 15(a). The device could withstand washing by running water for 60 s and retained  $ 95\% $  of its initial PCE after 30 days in ambient environment as shown in Fig. 15(b) [139]. The use of a luminescent additive along with encapsulant proved to increase the stability and performance. Bella et al. have used Lumogen F Violet 570 as a luminescent downshifting material and added it to the photocurable fluoro polymer solution and later encapsulated the device by spin coating followed by UV curing. The device was coated in different (front and back) configurations. Both the front/back coated devices showed improved performance and no loss in PCE was observed even after 180 days (the devices were kept under Argon atmosphere for the first 90 days and in air atmosphere at  $ 50\% $  RH for the other 90 days under continuous UV irradiation). During the outdoor stability test, the front/back coated devices retained  $ 90\% $  of its initial PCE even after 5 weeks [140].

Wu et al. had coated carbon tetrafluoride  $ (\mathrm{CF_4}) $  plasma on top of a perovskite device. The fluorination treatment was found to improve the hydrophobic nature of the film. The  $ \mathrm{CF_4} $  coated device showed excellent stability even after dipping in water as shown in Fig. 16. The devices retained  $ 70\% $  of its initial PCE after  $ 100\mathrm{h} $  of continuous Xenon lamp (AM 1.5G, 1 sun) illumination in ambient air (21% RH) [141].

4.1.3. Multi layered thin film encapsulation in PVSC

Thin film encapsulation (TFE) is one of the most promising technology in preventing moisture and volatile material ingress into the device [153]. TFE is based on a single or multi-layer stack of film, made of alternating inorganic and organic layers. The organic or polymeric films used in encapsulation are homogeneous in thickness aspect, but the inorganic films or layers used are typically of metal oxides and act as barriers for vapors/moisture. The inorganic layer shows extreme barrier properties, but they tend to have more defects and irregularities which paves a way for moisture and oxygen to enter in long run and their properties depend on thickness of the film [154]. To address this issue, organic or polymer interlayers are used in order to improve the mechanical properties of the stack (flexibility without delamination) and to limit the moisture permeation and have a WVTR of below  $ 10^{-5} $

# 5. Issues in encapsulant materials and strategies for improvement

A standard glass-polymer-glass encapsulation is the most used method in the field of photovoltaics. In this method the top and bottom are perfectly protected with glass, but the edges are more vulnerable. Through the edges ingress of moisture can happen and hence a proper edge sealant must be employed to completely safeguard the device. In case of flexible devices, the encapsulation architecture plays a major role in stability. UVC epoxy is mostly available in liquid form containing the monomers, initiators, stabilizers and trace amount of moisture and oxygen. These materials should not react with the perovskite layers. One of the major problem with epoxy is outgassing of moisture [192], either a degassing process or use of desiccant material is required when using epoxy as encapsulant. Alternatively, thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) has many advantages over other polymeric materials such as good weatherability, good optical properties, good mechanical strength and adhesion property, but TPU have nitrogen and oxygen in backbone as urethane bond (R-NH-COOR'). A careful selection of raw materials (polyols and di-isocyanates) and correct formulation can prevent this hydrolysis problem in TPUs. Solute content in PMMA can damage the spiro-OMeTAD which is a commonly used hole transport material in PVSC and leads to performance loss [132]. PET have greater advantage in materials cost, but it has low weathering resistance compared to other encapsulant materials. Fluoro polymers show great weatherability and hydrophobic nature but it shares a disadvantage of higher price. This disadvantage can be overcome by using modern coating techniques which require little quantity of materials [96]. In the case of thin film encapsulant with many layers if one or more layer is thicker than the other, the coefficient of thermal expansion will be different for all layers. Due to this easily stress can build up which gives rise to delamination and cracking in the weakest layer [193]. Also, the TFE edges should be completely sealed to prevent volatilization of species from PVSC. Instead of using polymeric materials (like PMMA, Polystyrene (PS), PEG, PVP, PVA) as encapsulants they have been used as additives during the perovskite absorber film formation. This kind of additive engineering using polymers have passivated the absorber and increased the device performance and its stability [133,172,194–196].

One of the major considerations for encapsulation strategy used in PVSC is to avoid leakage of toxic substances (lead, cesium) into the environment during their lifetime. Jiang et al. did a lead leakage study of encapsulated perovskite solar module damaged by metal ball used for the standard hail impact test, FM 44787. To find the lowest lead leakage from the device, Jiang et al. had encapsulated the device in three different methods; 1) cover slip method with UV resin, 2) top covered with glass and bottom with surlyn covered with glass UV resin, and 3) top covered with glass and self-healing polymer, bottom covered with glass UV resin. The PVSC modules were kept at  $ 45^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  and water was made to drip on it to mimic raining process. Jiang et al. have also studied the effect of four different weather conditions on the lead leakage through different encapsulation methods. In the different weather condition study, the self-healing polymer encapsulated device showed very less leakage because during the sunny time the polymer repairs its damage on its own and greatly reduces the lead leakage rates from 30 to  $ 0.08\mathrm{mg}\mathrm{h}^{-1}\mathrm{m}^{-2} $  compared to bottom encapsulated perovskite solar

modules [197]. To address this issue more research on lead free perovskites as absorber material is the need of the hour [198,199].

# 6. Conclusions and outlook

Significant progress has been made in the perovskite photovoltaic technology in the last decade and now they are at a stage where they can complement both thin film and Si wafer based PV modules in tandem configuration to improve the power conversion efficiency, if not compete with market dominant silicon PV technology. The power conversion efficiency of perovskite cells has increased tremendously in the recent years, but the poor stability issues persist which hampers these devices from commercial applications. Higher device lifetime has become mandatory and to achieve this encapsulation has become as effective method to prevent the permeation of moisture and oxygen, which plays a significant role in the degradation of the device. In the initial part of this review, the degradation mechanisms observed in PVSCs is concisely explained. The usage of different materials (organic and inorganic) as encapsulants along with different strategies that are employed have been explained in detail in this review. The new strategies adopted along with novel polymeric materials when used as encapsulant has shown to improve the device lifetime by many folds. The issues with the materials currently in use and the strategies to improve the encapsulation are also discussed in detail in this review. In summary, developing new range of materials with great permeation property, optimizing the encapsulation strategy without losing the device performance and ready to commercialize technique is the key research area that needs immediate attention to improve the overall lifetime of perovskite based devices.
