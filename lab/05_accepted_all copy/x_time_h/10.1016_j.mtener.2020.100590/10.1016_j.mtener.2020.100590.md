# Abstract

The decent power conversion efficiency ( $ \sim 20\% $ ), ultra-low cost and one-year stability make carbon-based perovskite solar cells (C-PSCs) one of the most promising candidates for commercialization in the field of perovskite photovoltaics. However, the stability of C-PSCs still lags behind that of the commercialized silicon and thin-film photovoltaics. Although there have been many reviews of the long-term stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs), none has specifically focused on the long-term stability of C-PSCs. Herein, we summarize the environmental risks and strategies for ensuring the long-term stability of C-PSCs, highlighting also the various contributions of our research group. The most important factors affecting long-term stability are analyzed according to the environmental risks (i.e. moisture, oxygen, light, and thermal stress) of device degradation. The proposed strategies chiefly center around improving the intrinsic stability and strengthening encapsulation. In our conclusions, we also present an outlook on future work concerning stability improvement and commercial development of C-PSCs. Our work provides a clear guideline for future research on C-PSCs.

Keyword: Carbon-based perovskite solar cells; environmental risks and strategies; perovskite solar cells; stability

# 1. Introduction

To date, perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have achieved a certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 25.5% [1] due to their excellent optical and electronic properties such as an excellent light absorption coefficient, high external quantum efficiency, and long carrier diffusion length [2-4]. However, two main issues have thus far precluded its commercialization. One is the cost issue as a result of the utilization of noble metals (such as gold or silver) as back electrodes, which are expensive, rare, and energy-intensive. The other is the long-term stability issue that stems from the deterioration of charge transport materials and the degradation of perovskite (PVK) materials, including (1) rapid decomposition induced by water [5-7], (2) oxidation triggered by oxygen molecules and accelerated by light [8,9], (3) light-induced cation segregation and halide segregation [10], (4) nonphotoactive phase transformation induced by high temperature [11,12], and (5) reaction with the migrated metal ions generating metal halides [10,13,14]. To overcome these problems, several strategies have been proposed, including utilizing stable charge transport materials [15,16], performing composition engineering of PVK [17], and replacing noble metal electrodes with such alternatives as MXene [18], carbon [19], Al [20], Cu [21], and Ni [22], etc. Carbon materials have some distinct advantages, e.g. low cost, abundant sources, robust hydrophobicity as sealant to resist PVK decomposition induced by moisture, and high chemical stability to avoid PVK degradation induced by the migration of metal ions [19,23,24]. It is thus considered to be the most promising material for replacing noble metal electrodes [25-27]. To date, carbon-based PSCs (C-PSCs) have achieved a PCE of ~20% and one-year stability, exhibiting good durability and excellent long-term stability compared with their noble metal-based counterparts [28-31].

Although C-PSCs are recognized as the most promising candidate for commercialization, the stability of C-PSCs significantly lags behind the expected 25-year lifespan of the commercial photovoltaics. It is thus imperative to carry out

# 2. Environmental risks

During fabrication, storage, and operation, C-PSCs are inevitably influenced by the ambient environment. The instability of C-PSCs is primarily a result of the decomposition of PVK materials and the deterioration of charge transport materials. No matter whether encapsulated or not, degradation is linked closely to moisture, oxygen, light, thermal stress, and, importantly, their synergistic effects under atmospheric conditions. A brief overview of each factor is as follows:

(1) Moisture. Moisture can easily permeate into the bulk of PVK and produce

Journal Pre-proof

PVK hydrate. The strong hydrogen bonds between water molecules and organic cations consequently weaken the chemical bond between the cation and   $  PbX_{6}^{-}  $   (X=I, Br, or Cl), resulting in rapid deprotonation of the organic cation and thus degradation of the PVK – especially in the presence of extrinsic stresses, e.g. thermal stress or electric fields [5-7]. Furthermore, the use of hygroscopic dopants in hole transport materials (HTMs) can also induce moisture degradation of PVK [40-42]. Usually, moisture is recognized to be detrimental to PVK. However, it could also play a positive role in the crystallization of PVK. Appropriate moisture interference can greatly improve the film quality, grain size, carrier mobility, and stability [43,44].

(2) Oxygen. Oxygen can oxidize both metal oxide charge transport materials (especially  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ ) and PVK photoabsorbers, causing a drop in the performance of PSCs.  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  is notorious for readily combining with atmospheric oxygen, in turn generating superoxides (i.e.  $ \mathrm{O}_2^- $ ) particularly under ultraviolet (UV) irradiation. These superoxides subsequently participate the oxidative decomposition of PVK [45,46]. The direct effect of oxygen on PVK is due to the formation of peroxides or superoxides as well, which can react with the organic  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_2^+ $  ( $ \mathrm{MA}^+ $ ) and thus quicken the decomposition of PVK [45]. In particular, the combined effect of light and oxygen can lead to the decomposition of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ , generating  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ ,  $ \mathrm{I}_2 $ ,  $ \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} $ , and gaseous  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_2 $  [8,9]. However, interfacial modification molecules containing oxygen groups can reduce the interfacial defects and trap states, leading to a high e□ciency and stability [47]. For all-inorganic PVK, the high-temperature annealing in dry air can dissociate the oxygen molecule absorbed at its halide vacancies to oxygen atom. The resultant oxygen atom can significantly reduce the defect density and nonradiative recombination of PVK by a stronger electron-coupling with the valence band [48].

(3) Light. Besides the synergistic decomposition of light and oxygen, light-induced decomposition of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  can occur even in the absence of oxygen. Under light illumination, when photons receive much higher energy than the bandgap of PVK compounds, electrons in the valence band are excited by such high-energy photons (approximately  $ >3\mathrm{eV} $ ) can antibond orbitals of  $ \mathrm{NH}_3 $  [49]. As a result, the N-

H bonds in   $  MA^{+}  $   can become dissociated and form gaseous   $  CH_{3}NH_{2}  $   as well as   $  H_{2}  $  . In addition, excess carriers in PVK and the capture of photogenerated electrons in the conduction band can accelerate these chemical reactions in the presence of oxygen. Besides the chemical reactions, light-induced cation segregation and halide segregation can also significantly reduce the stability of PVK materials [10].

(4) Thermal stress. The thermal degradation of C-PSCs can be attributed to the following three reasons. First, the low thermal conductivity of PVK materials precludes rapid transfer of heat to the surrounding environment when experiencing high temperatures. Excessive heat accumulation results in significant thermal stress, which typically leads to the degradation or nonphotoactive phase transformation of PVK [12,50]. This problem is exacerbated when thick carbon layers (  $  \geqslant  $  10   $  \mu  $  m) are used as back electrodes [51]. Second, the chemical reaction between MA  $  ^{+}  $  cations of PVK and carbon atoms of carbon layers can degrade the PVK photoabsorbers [52]. Third, some organic HTMs are unstable and prone to high-temperature decomposition, e.g. 2,2',7,7'-Tetrakis[N,N-di(4-methoxyphenyl)amino]-9,9'-spirobifluorene (spiroOMeTAD), resulting in performance deterioration of the devices [53].

Generally, while the degradation of C-PSCs during operation is multifactorial, device resistance to light- and thermal-induced decomposition essentially depends on the intrinsic stability of functional materials [41,54]. In contrast, device resistance to moisture- and oxygen-induced decomposition depends more on the encapsulation techniques in addition to the intrinsic properties of the materials [55,56]. To minimize the environmental risks (i.e. moisture, oxygen, light, and thermal stress) that can cause device degradation, strategies for improving the long-term stability of C-PSCs can thus be divided into two distinct categories: (1) improving the intrinsic stability, and (2) strengthening encapsulation. Below, these two strategies are summarized.

# 3. Improving intrinsic stability

To improve the long-term stability of C-PSCs, one of the most important strategies is to enhance the intrinsic stability of the relevant components. Using stable materials can not only significantly reduce the risk of device decomposition, thus

extending lifespan, but also bring down the cost of device encapsulation. Generally, C-PSCs consist of transparent conductive oxide (TCO) substrate/electron transport material (ETM)/PVK photoabsorber/carbon electrode (CE). In some cases, HTMs are incorporated into the device structure. Depending on whether HTMs are utilized, C-PSCs are classified as either HTM-free C-PSCs or HTM-based C-PSCs. # 3.1 TCO substrates

The commonly used TCO glass substrates for C-PSCs include indium-doped tin oxide (ITO) glass and fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass [57,58]. Since TCO glass substrates have excellent mechanical, chemical, and thermal stability, negative effects on the stability of devices are usually negligible. However, ITO showed slightly worse stability than FTO. Reports showed that a number of large and small highly-resistive regions or spots appeared on the surface of ITO after exposure for  $ 886\mathrm{h} $  in damp-heat environment  $ (85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  and  $ 85\% $  relative humidity (RH)) [59,60]. The damp and heat triggered hydrolysis of the oxides at some sporadic "weak" spots. Subsequently, spreading of the hydrolyzed regions caused discontinuity of electrical path, hydrolysis of the oxide/glass interface, and finally formation of insulating areas.

In the case of C-PSCs, substrate selection must take special factors into consideration. Generally, ITO glass exhibits higher transmittance in the visible light range, as well as higher conductivity and lower surface roughness than FTO glass. High-efficiency and planar C-PSCs are thus more likely to use ITO glass substrate. For example, Zhou et al. fabricated planar structure C-PSCs using ITO glass substrate [61]. The PCE of the resultant device exceeded  $ 16\% $ . Meanwhile, the device without encapsulation retained  $ 80\% $  of its initial PCE after a maximum power point (MPP) tracking of  $ 505\mathrm{h} $  in ambient air. Compared to ITO glass, however, FTO glass is cheaper and has greater resistance to high temperatures and pressure. Since the cost of FTO glass is lower, it is more frequently used as substrate than ITO glass [32,36]. More pertinently, FTO glass is more suitable than ITO glass given the high temperature sintering during ETM fabrication and the mechanical pressure of CE deposition [30,62,63]. Wei et al. hot-pressed a flexible carbon film on  $ \mathrm{FTO / TiO_2 / PVK} $  substrate under  $ 0.25\mathrm{MPa} $  pressure, with the resultant device achieving a PCE of  $ 13.53\% $  [62]. After storage for  $ 20\mathrm{d} $  in air, the PCE of the device without encapsulation dropped only by  $ 5\% $ . Our group reported the fabrication of Coal-based CE by hot-pressing the carbon layer on  $ \mathrm{FTO / TiO_2 / PVK} $  substrate under  $ 0.4\mathrm{MPa} $  pressure [63]. Zhang et al. press-transferred a self-adhesive carbon film on  $ \mathrm{FTO / TiO_2 / PVK} $

# 3.2 Selection of ETMs

A suitable ETM for highly-efficient and long-term stable C-PSCs should fulfill the following criteria: (1) high visible light transmittance to increase the light harvesting efficiency of the PVK photoabsorbers; (2) high electron mobility to achieve effective electron extraction and transfer at the ETM/PVK interface; and (3) good UV stability to reduce the oxidative decomposition of PVK [15,16]. To date, the reported ETMs for C-PSCs contain  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ , fullerene  $ (\mathrm{C}_{60}) $ ,  $ \mathrm{ZnO} $ ,  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ , and  $ \mathrm{Nb}_2\mathrm{O}_5 $ . The effects of ETMs on the stability of differently-structured devices are summarized below.

# 3.2.1 ETMs for monolithic structures

Due to its excellent chemical stability and high light transmittance in the visible light spectrum [64-66],  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  is widely used as ETM in C-PSCs. For monolithic structures,  $ \mathrm{m - TiO_2} $  has the dual function of acting as mesoporous scaffold and ETM, as shown in Fig. 2a-b. Thus, it is indispensable in C-PSCs with a monolithic structure. Because the thickness of  $ \mathrm{m - TiO_2} $  can significantly influence the depletion width at the ETM/PVK interface, it is usually thicker in HTM-free C-PSCs with a monolithic structure (usually  $ \sim 1\mu \mathrm{m} $ ).

In 2013, Ku et al. first reported on monolithic C-PSCs [19]. The triple mesoporous scaffold consisted of  $ 1\mu \mathrm{m} $   $ \mathrm{m - TiO_2} $ ,  $ 1\mu \mathrm{m} $   $ \mathrm{m - ZrO_2} $ , and  $ 10\mu \mathrm{m} $  mesoporous CE, which were printed layer by layer. Finally, the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  (MAPbI₃) PVK precursor was drop-casted on the top of the CEs and infiltrated in the triple mesoporous scaffold, as shown in Fig. 2a. Due to the uniform distribution of MAPbI₃ in spheroidal graphite and  $ \mathrm{m - TiO_2} $  layers, this device achieved a PCE of  $ 6.64\% $ . After storage for  $ 840\mathrm{h} $  under a dry air atmosphere in the dark, the devices without encapsulation sustained almost no decrease in PCE (Fig. 3a), exhibiting much better long-term stability than their Au electrode counterparts. Liu et al. prepared dual-sized TiO₂ nanoparticles as scaffold layers for monolithic structures and adjusted their thickness to  $ 712\mathrm{nm} $  [67]. This both improved the

# 3.2.2 ETMs for layer-by-layer structures

In C-PSCs with a layer-by-layer structure (Fig. 2c-d), the selection of ETMs is varied. Compact  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  ( $ \mathrm{c - TiO_2} $ )/ $ \mathrm{m - TiO_2} $  composites are commonly used, with the thickness of  $ \mathrm{m - TiO_2} $  ranging from  $ \sim 100 $  to  $ \sim 600 $  nm. Although the presence of  $ \mathrm{m - TiO_2} $  can boost the efficiency of the device, it is not essential [69]. For devices with a planar architecture,  $ \mathrm{m - TiO_2} $  is not used, and  $ \mathrm{c - TiO_2} $  can be replaced with  $ \mathrm{C_{60}} $ ,  $ \mathrm{ZnO} $ ,  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ , or  $ \mathrm{Nb_2O_5} $ .

In 2014, our group reported C-PSCs based on a  $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / MAPbI_3} $  heterojunction, achieving a PCE of  $ 9.08\% $  [23]. The C-PSCs without any encapsulation kept their initial PCE after storage for over  $ 2000\mathrm{h} $  under an air atmosphere in the dark, as shown in Fig. 3b. Zhang et al. optimized the thickness of the  $ \mathrm{m - TiO_2} $  layer to  $ 630~\mathrm{nm} $  for C-PSC as shown in Fig. 3c, achieving a PCE of  $ 8.31\% $  [70]. And the device without encapsulation showed a slight increase in PCE after storage for  $ 800\mathrm{h} $  in air. With  $ \mathrm{m - TiO_2} $  nanoparticles, it is difficult to achieve a fast charge transport and thick PVK absorber layer simultaneously. Therefore, Zheng et al. and Liu et al. designed  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  ETMs with different structures for C-PSCs [71,72]. The former came up with  $ \mathrm{m - TiO_2} $  nanobowl (NB) arrays as ETMs, with the PCE of the device without encapsulation only dropping by  $ 8\% $  after storage for  $ 45\mathrm{d} $  in air at room temperature

Importantly, the device without encapsulation showed no decline in performance after storage for 3000 h in ambient air. Even though   $  TiO_{2}  $   is widely used as ETM in C-PSCs, it has certain disadvantages. First, it is instable when exposed to UV. The photocatalytic activity of   $  TiO_{2}  $   is closely related to the oxygen vacancies on its surface. Under continuous UV illumination, oxygen molecules react with the surface oxygen vacancies on   $  TiO_{2}  $ , creating superoxides. Eventually, the adjacent contact layer including PVK is oxidized and decomposed by the superoxides [45,46]. Second,   $  TiO_{2}  $   has a low electron mobility (  $  \sim1~cm^{2}\cdot V^{-1}\cdot s^{-1}  $  ) compared to PVK materials (  $  24.81~cm^{2}\cdot V^{-1}\cdot s^{-1}  $  ) [73]. Third,   $  TiO_{2}  $   has a high defect density [74]. Fourth, the fabrication of   $  TiO_{2}  $   is highly energy consuming as it requires a high-temperature (  $  \sim500^{\circ}C  $  ) sintering step [75]. Therefore, to obtain highly efficient and highly stable C-PSCs with a layer-by-layer structure, it is essential to replace   $  TiO_{2}  $   with materials characterized by high UV stability, low-temperature processability, and good electron mobility.

Compared with   $  TiO_{2}  $  ,   $  C_{60}  $   boasts high electron mobility (1.6 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ·V  $  ^{-1}  $  ·s  $  ^{-1}  $  ) [76] and high electron affinity [77]. And the   $  C_{60}  $  -based PSCs usually demonstrate a slighter hysteresis phenomenon and have excellent UV photostability [78,79]. These advantages make   $  C_{60}  $   an extremely promising alternative for   $  TiO_{2}  $  . Ahn et al. fabricated C-PSCs using   $  C_{60}  $   as ETM [80]. Owing to the reduced charge trapping at PVK/  $  C_{60}  $   interface, the   $  C_{60}  $  -based devices exhibited superior stability compared to the   $  TiO_{2}  $  -based counterparts under continuous 100 mW·cm  $  ^{-2}  $   illumination. A PCE of 15.38% was achieved in   $  C_{60}  $  -based devices. Moreover,   $  C_{60}  $   is a kind of stable carbon material, which presents far more superior photo and moisture stability than   $  TiO_{2}  $  . In addition to   $  C_{60}  $  , another viable alternative to   $  TiO_{2}  $   is ZnO. Despite having similar physical properties as   $  TiO_{2}  $  , the electron mobility of ZnO is far superior (205~300   $  cm^{2}\cdot V^{-1}\cdot s^{-1}  $  ) [83]. In addition, ZnO can be deposited by spin-coating and annealed at low temperature (<150°C) [84,85]. In 2015, our group for the first time reported C-PSCs that used ZnO as ETM, achieving a PCE of 8.07% [86]. Unfortunately, the ZnO-based devices suffered from poor stability (500 min in air), which were attributed to the high-density defects at the ZnO/PVK interface. To solve this problem, Shirazi et al. devised a composite ZnO/Al-doped ZnO layer [87]. Although this did not significantly improve the PCE of the device (8.23%), it made a big difference with regards to long-term stability. After storage for 15 d in ambient air without encapsulation, the PCE of the device retained 95.3% of its initial value. Yang et al. also used ZnO as ETM, obtaining C-PSCs of greater stability [88]. The champion device without encapsulation kept >90% of its initial PCE after storage for 1200 h in ambient air with 25~28°C and 45%~60% RH.

# 3.3 PVK photoabsorbers

In both monolithic and layer-by-layer structures, the instability of the devices can largely be attributed to the degradation of PVK materials due to its unique structure and properties. The PVK materials, especially the three-dimensional (3D) organic-inorganic hybrid PVK, have many shortcomings with regards to stability-related issues: (1) they easily dissolve in water, and frequently suffer from oxidative decomposition [5,6,8,9]; and (2) the weak bonds within PVK compounds or feeble interactions with the adjacent contact layers can be broken under continuous illumination and/or thermal stress [10-12]. To tackle these issues, composition engineering has emerged as a key strategy – a composite of various approaches such as the incorporation of mixed cations and/or mixed halides, the introduction of additives and dopants, the application of two-dimensional (2D) PVK, and the utilization of all-inorganic PVK [17]. In addition, deep-level defects in the bulk of PVK films are usually considered as the Shockley-Read-Hall nonradiative recombination centers, thus resulting in charge trapping and scattering, and finally leading to inferior device PCE and instability. Therefore, rational adjustment of PVK crystallinity and morphology is equally important for enhancing the intrinsic stability. For C-PSCs with a monolithic structure, in particular, the penetration of the PVK precursor is crucial [97]. This is not only because the precursor needs to pass through the micron-thick mesoscopic layers to make contact with  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ , but also because the pore filling of the precursor has an important influence on the crystallization of PVK. As for C-PSCs with a layer-by-layer structure, PVK often encounters a large amount of corrosive solvent from carbon paste or wet carbon film [23,98], requiring highly-stable, dense, and smooth PVK films. The effects of PVK photoabsorbers on the stability of differently-structured devices are summarized below.

# 3.3.1 PVK for monolithic structures

For monolithic structures, the purpose of composition engineering is to tune the optoelectronic properties, improve the crystallinity and morphology, facilitate the

penetration, and enhance the intrinsic stability of the PVK photoabsorbers [97,99,100].

# 3.3.1.1 3D organic-inorganic hybrid PVK

Up to now, most of the publications of C-PSCs use 3D organic-inorganic hybrid PVK as photoabsorbers. This is because of their unique properties, e.g. high light absorption coefficient, suitable bandgap, bipolar charge transport, long carrier diffusion length and lifetime, and surprisingly high tolerance to defects. In early PSCs,  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  was the most commonly used PVK photoabsorber. Ku et al. reported the first monolithic C-PSC using  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  as PVK photoabsorber [19]. However, incomplete infiltration of PVK crystals in the  $ \mathrm{ZrO_2 / TiO_2} $  mesoporous layer was responsible for a substantial amount of voids, resulting in a low PCE of  $ 6.64\% $  and just  $ 840\mathrm{h} $  of stability in the dark.

To further improve the efficiency and stability, Han and his coworkers further studied the effects of CEs [101], insulating layer [102], particle size of  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  [103] and solvents of PVK precursor [104]. In addition, they also explored the effect of functional additives [105,106] and composition engineering [107] of the 3D PVK on efficiency and stability of C-PSCs. They employed  $ \mathrm{NH_4Cl} $  as an additive in the PVK precursor to assist its crystallization [106]. The synergy effect of  $ \mathrm{NH_4Cl} $  and moisture induced the generation of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{X}\cdot \mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{PbX}_3(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O})_2 $  (X=I or Cl). This intermediate effectuated the preferentially orientated crystallization and entirely pore-filling of PVK photoabsorber in the triple mesoporous scaffold. Therefore, the champion device achieved a PCE of  $ 15.6\% $ , and it retained  $ 96.7\% $  of the initial PCE for over 130 d in ambient air. They also utilized biguanide hydrochloride as an additive in  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ , resulting in the out-of-plane crystal orientation of PVK grains along the (001) and (002) crystal planes [108]. This C-PSC delivered a champion PCE of  $ 16.35\% $ , with unsealed device retaining  $ 98\% $  of its initial PCE after 2500 h storage in air ( $ 50 \pm 5\% $  RH). They reported an in-situ single-crystal transformation by a gas-solid interaction between  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  single crystals and methylamine gas flow, accomplishing fully  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  infiltration into the

As a result, the PVK mainly distributed in the m-  $  TiO_{2}  $   and m-  $  ZrO_{2}  $   layers and realized large crystals and entirely pore filling. Moreover, Zhang et al. fabricated  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3(\mathrm{SrCl}_2)_{0.1} $  PVK using a novel and eco-friendly  $ \mathrm{SrCl}_2 $  as an inorganic source for the precursor [111]. Since the introduction of  $ \mathrm{Cs^{+}} $  reduces the effective radius of cations and suppresses the halide segregation, the inherent stability of the mixed PVK is significantly enhanced. The encapsulated device maintained  $ 91\% $  of its initial PCE after storage for  $ 1020\mathrm{h} $  at  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  and  $ 40\% \sim 60\% $  RH in the dark.

In comparison with 3D PVK, the bulky organic cations in 2D PVK demonstrate higher moisture stability, thermal stability, and a significantly lower ion migration/phase transition. However, the horizontal arrangement of the organic layer severely hinders the transport of carriers, resulting in a low PCE. As an alternative

In 2014, Mei et al. first synthesized a novel mixed cation PVK  $ (5\text{-AVA})_{\mathrm{x}}(\mathrm{MA})_{1 - \mathrm{x}}\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  by partially replacing  $ \mathrm{MA^{+}} $  with  $ 5\text{-AVA}^{+} $   $ (5\text{-AVA}^{+} = 5\text{-aminovaleric acid cation}) $  [97]. The introduction of  $ 5\text{-AVA}^{+} $  enhanced the penetration of the precursor, produced better pore filling in the triple mesoporous scaffold, and improved the crystallinity and morphology of PVK crystals, resulting in a longer exciton lifetime and higher stability. The champion device achieved a certified PCE of  $ 12.8\% $  and showed no degradation after exposure for  $ >1000\mathrm{h} $  in ambient air under full sunlight.

Since the addition of  $ 5\mathrm{-AVA^{+}} $  to  $ \mathrm{MAPbI_3} $  can bring about better penetration, improve the crystallinity and morphology, and enhance the intrinsic stability, much work has been done on this promising approach. The relevant findings are summarized according to the following categories: (1) stability of laboratory-scale cells in different test environments; (2) stability of large-scale carbon-based perovskite solar modules (PSMs); (3) stability improvement mechanism.

First, for laboratory-scale cells, Li et al. investigated the stability of   $  (5-\mathrm{AVA})_{\mathrm{x}}(\mathrm{MA})_{1-\mathrm{x}}\mathrm{PbI}_{3}  $  -based C-PSCs in different test environments [113]. Despite retaining 80% of their initial PCE for 1000 h in the dark at both RT and 65°C, the devices deteriorated notably under continuous illumination at open-circuit conditions. And MPP tracking and LED illumination results also displayed the slower degradation of devices. Nonetheless, outdoor operation showed the natural day/night cycling stability of the monolithic C-PSCs.

# 3.3.2 PVK for layer-by-layer structures

For layer-by-layer structures, composition engineering intends to optimize optoelectronic properties, generate the high-quality films, and improve the intrinsic stability of the PVK photoabsorbers [124-126]. Moreover, in the past several years, the PSC field has shifted much more attention on extending the long-term operational stability of PSCs. In addition to manipulating the composition of 3D organic-inorganic hybrid PVK, two promising strategies for enhancing the stability involve in the utilization of 2D PVK and inorganic PVK.

# 3.3.2.1 3D organic-inorganic hybrid PVK

In the early development of C-PSCs, performance and stability greatly depended on the quality of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbX}_3 $  (X=I, Br, or Cl) film. Chen et al. prepared a dense and uniform  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  film via scalable electrodeposition of  $ \mathrm{PbO}_2 $  followed by HI and MAI chemical bath (Fig. 5a), with the resultant C-PSC achieving a PCE of  $ 10.19\% $  [124]. The device without encapsulation maintained  $ 98\% $  of its initial PCE after storage for  $ 30\mathrm{d} $  in ambient atmosphere. They also prepared uniform, even, and smooth  $ \mathrm{MAPbBr}_3 $  film by adding non-polar cyclohexane into the MABr isopropanol solution [126]. Owing to the stable cubic phase structure of  $ \mathrm{MAPbBr}_3 $ , the C-PSC without encapsulation showed no decline in PCE after storage for  $ 90\mathrm{d} $  in dry air with  $ 25\sim 30^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  and  $ 10\% \sim 20\% $  RH, and retained  $ \sim 90\% $  of its initial PCE after storage for  $ 15\mathrm{d} $  under thermal stress (air,  $ 80^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ , and  $ 50\% \sim 85\% $  RH). Yang et al. adopted  $ \mathrm{NH_4Cl} $  as an additive to improve the quality of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  film, with the PCE of the resultant device reaching  $ 13.26\% $  [88]. After storage for  $ 1200\mathrm{h} $  in ambient air, the device without encapsulation maintained  $ 91.3\% $  of its initial PCE. Liu et al. synthesized a  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{MAPbI}_x\mathrm{Br}_{3 - x} $  stacking layer by in situ growing  $ \mathrm{MAPbBr}_3 $  on the top of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  layer [127]. The PVK stacking layer effectively suppressed charge interfacial recombination and electron reverse transfer (Fig. 5b), allowing for a PCE of  $ 16.2\% $ . The device without encapsulation kept  $ \sim 85\% $  of its initial PCE after storage for  $ 55\mathrm{d} $  in dark ambient conditions, as shown in Fig. 5c.

Yet, the  $ \mathrm{MAPbX}_3 $  (X=I, Br) PVK compounds have a number of drawbacks, e.g.

# 3.3.2.2 2D/3D mixed-dimensional PVK

An effective approach to strengthen the moisture stability of PVK is to introduce moisture-resistant 2D PVK components into 3D PVK compounds. Zhou et al. incorporated the short carbon-chain  $ \mathrm{EA^{+}} $  ( $ \mathrm{EA^{+}} = $  ethylammonium cation) into 3D  $ \mathrm{MAPbI_3} $ , constructing a 2D/3D hybrid  $ (\mathrm{EA})_2(\mathrm{MA})_5\mathrm{Pb}_6\mathrm{I}_{19} $  PVK [134]. The resultant C-PSCs without any encapsulation exhibited excellent long-term stability, thermal stability and photostability. They retained  $ 93\% $  of the initial PCE for  $ 2160\mathrm{h} $  in ambient air (Fig. 5e),  $ 80\% $  of their initial PCE for over  $ 100\mathrm{h} $  during continuous heating at  $ 80^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ , and  $ 92\% $  of their initial PCE after an MPP tracking of over  $ 300\mathrm{h} $ . Li

# 3.3.2.3 All-inorganic PVK

In contrast to the impressive photoelectric properties, organic-inorganic hybrid PVK presents significant disadvantages in terms of stability due to the hygroscopic and volatile properties of the organic cations. In recent years, cesium-based all-inorganic metal halide PVK has emerged as e·cient and thermally stable photoabsorbers, which promise to deal with the instability issues by replacing the organic species with all-inorganic species.

With cubic phase and a bandgap of  $ 1.73\mathrm{eV} $ ,  $ \alpha $ -CsPbI $ _3 $  has favorable energy level, which is a prominent candidate of photoabsorbers among all-inorganic halide PVK [137,138]. However, CsPbI $ _3 $  requires a high temperature to sustain its cubic  $ \alpha $ -phase [139]. If not, it will rapidly convert to a yellow orthorhombic nonperovskite phase, thus resulting in a poor performance. Incorporation of dopants or additives in CsPbI $ _3 $  precursors is an effective strategy to enhance its phase stability. Xiang et al.

A champion PCE of 5.18% was obtained for HTM-free C-PSCs. After storage for 37 d in air atmosphere at RT, the device retained 93% of the initial PCE. At the same time, they replaced PbI $ _2 $  with HPbI $ _3 $  in the one-step deposition of  $ \alpha $ -CsPbI $ _3 $  to create tensile lattice strain, thus stabilizing large  $ \alpha $ -CsPbI $ _3 $  crystals in dry air [141]. The resultant C-PSCs achieved a champion PCE of 10.7%. After storage for 70 d in air, the device without encapsulation displayed no PCE decline. Partial substitution of PbI $ _2 $  with InI $ _3 $  not only optimized the energy level but also suppressed the defects related with Pb species, resulting in a champion device with PCE up to 12.04%. Zhu et al. found that CsI methanol solution could assist the formation of full-coverage and pure-phase   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   film [146]. The corresponding C-PSCs yielded a maximum PCE of 9.16%. # 3.4 Role of HTMs

For C-PSCs, the advantageous role of HTMs is reflected in two aspects: (1) HTMs allow much more favorable energy level alignment between PVK and CEs, not only improving hole extraction and transfer but also suppressing the reverse transfer of electrons [26,38]. Therefore, if high efficiency is the goal, researchers are likely to prepare HTM-based C-PSCs. (2) Using hydrophobic, chemically-stable, and thermally-stable HTMs could provide extra protection for C-PSCs against degradation induced by environmental stress [80,161]. The effects of HTMs on the stability of differently-structured devices are summarized below.

# 3.4.1 HTMs for monolithic structures

For HTM-based C-PSCs with a monolithic structure, the HTM together with  $ \mathrm{m - TiO_2} $ ,  $ \mathrm{m - ZrO_2} $  and CE form the quadruple mesoscopic scaffold, as shown in Fig. 2b. HTMs are deposited by screen-printing the paste followed by sintering at high temperature ( $ \sim 500^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ ) [162,163]. The particle size and thermal stability of HTMs are thus crucial. Due to its suitable particle size, good thermal stability, low cost, and wide bandgap, NiO has thus far proven to be an irreplaceable HTM for monolithic C-PSCs. Besides, spinel  $ \mathrm{Co}_3\mathrm{O}_4 $  has also been shown to be an effective HTM.

In 2015, Xu et al. reported the usage of NiO as HTM (Fig. 6a) to suppress charge recombination and facilitate hole extraction in monolithic C-PSCs, with the resultant device achieving a PCE of  $ 14.9\% $  [162]. The device without encapsulation maintained over  $ 93\% $  of its initial PCE after storage for  $ 1000\mathrm{h} $  in ambient atmosphere, and kept  $ 80\% $  of its initial PCE for  $ >1000\mathrm{h} $  when exposed to an atmospheric temperature of  $ 60^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ , as shown in Fig. 6b. However, the device without encapsulation lost about  $ 80\% $  of its initial PCE after light illumination for  $ 150\mathrm{h} $ . Thereafter, Cao et al. further used NiO as HTM and replaced  $ \mathrm{m - ZrO_2} $  with  $ \mathrm{m - Al_2O_3} $  for mesoscopic C-PSCs, obtaining a PCE of  $ 15.03\% $  [163]. The device without encapsulation exhibited ambient and thermal stability for  $ 1000\mathrm{h} $ . Under continuous illumination, the encapsulated device kept over  $ 75\% $  of its initial PCE for  $ 1000\mathrm{h} $  in air.

# 3.4.2 HTMs for layer-by-layer structures

Generally, the HTMs used for conventional n-i-p PSCs are likely suitable for C-PSCs with a layer-by-layer structure as well. But some HTMs are unsuited due to the following disadvantages: (1) thermal and chemical instability. When carbon paste is used to deposit CEs, the solvent in the paste and the subsequent heating can decompose the unstable HTMs, e.g. spiro-OMeTAD [53]. Moreover, some organic HTMs are susceptible to react with the migrated halide anions, resulting in declining device performance [177]. (2) Dopant hygroscopic nature. The usage of hygroscopic dopants can trigger moisture degradation of PVK [40-42]. Therefore, only chemically and thermally stable dopant-free HTMs have the potential for fabricating long-term stable C-PSCs. For layer-by-layer structures, HTMs are sandwiched between the PVK and CE in most cases, as shown in Fig. 2d. The reported HTMs include spiro-OMeTAD, poly(3-hexylthiophene) (P3HT), copper phthalocyanine (CuPc), cuprous thiocyanate (CuSCN), CuS, NiO, and  $ \mathrm{Cu(Cr,M)O_2} $  ( $ \mathrm{M = Ba^{2+}} $ ,  $ \mathrm{Ca^{2+}} $ , or  $ \mathrm{Ni^{2+}} $ ).

Although the PCE of devices based on spiro-OMeTAD is usually satisfactory, the devices often undergo degradation accelerated by spiro-OMeTAD's thermal instability, chemical instability, and dopant hygroscopic nature, especially under illumination and thermal stress. Gholipour et al. dropped a small amount of spiro-OMeTAD solution into carbon cloth electrodes, with the resultant device achieving a PCE of 15.29% [177]. After an MPP tracking of 115 h at 85°C in a N₂ atmosphere, the device maintained ~50% of its initial PCE. Aitola et al. infiltrated single-walled carbon nanotube (SWCNT) networks with an appropriate amount of spiro-OMeTAD [24]. They achieved a PCE of 16.6%, and an MPP tracking of 140 h was carried out in a N₂ atmosphere. Zhang et al. spin-coated a layer of spiro-OMeTAD film prior to depositing carbon film [30]. The champion device obtained a high PCE of 19.2% and maintained over 94% of its initial PCE after an MPP tracking of 80 h at 20°C in a N₂ atmosphere.

By increasing interstitial oxygen, the hole extraction at PVK/CE interface was greatly enhanced. By further doping   $  Sm^{3+}  $   ions into   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $  , a maximum PCE 10.79% was achieved. The unencapsulated   $  \mathrm{Cu}(\mathrm{Cr},\mathrm{Ba})\mathrm{O}_{2}  $  -based C-PSCs maintained nearly 100% of their initial PCE at an air atmosphere after either 80% RH storage for over 60 d, or 80°C annealing for over 40 d, or light illumination for 7 d. # 3.5 Various CEs

Carbon materials have a proper work function of around  $ -5.0\mathrm{eV} $ , which is much closer to that of Au  $ (-5.1\mathrm{eV}) $ . Moreover, CEs have the following advantages: (1) low cost and abundant sources; (2) robust hydrophobicity that can resist PVK decomposition induced by moisture; and (3) high chemical stability that can withstand PVK degradation induced by the migration of metal ions [19,23,24]. Therefore, using CEs to replace noble metal electrodes is an indispensable strategy for future commercial production of PSCs. The commonly used carbon materials include graphite [105,195], carbon black [196,197], CNTs [181,198], and biomass carbon [63,199]. Typical preparation methods of CEs include screen-printing [101,102], painting [124,125], doctor-blading [23,86], press-transfer [30,62], and lamination [200,201]. The effects of CEs on the stability of differently-structured devices are summarized below.

# 3.5.1 CEs for monolithic structures

For monolithic structure, carbon paste is the only choice for preparing CEs. Carbon paste is a mixture of carbon materials, dispersants, binders and solvents. The dispersants can uniformly disperse the carbon materials in the solvents and prevent them from sedimentation and agglomeration. The binders can enhance the interconnectivity of carbon particles and the adhesive force of carbon layers on underlying substrates, resulting in an ohmic contact and efficient charge transfer. They should be sufficiently dissolved in solvent to ensure the homogeneity of carbon paste. The stability of monolithic C-PSCs is negligibly influenced by dispersants, binders and solvents, because they would disappear after the high-temperature (400~500°C) sintering step [123]. Conversely, the stability of monolithic C-PSCs is closely related to the selection of carbon materials, including single, binary or multiple carbon materials.

First, the selection of carbon material is crucial for carbon paste consisting of single carbon material. The favorable characteristics of the carbon material, namely,

Mali et al. found cross-linked network of aloe vera plants and calcined its gel to prepare Aloe Vera Carbon (AV-C) for HTM-free C-PSCs, with the resultant device achieving a PCE of  $ 12.58\% $  [199]. The device without encapsulation retained  $ 80\% $  of its initial PCE after storage for  $ 1000\mathrm{h} $  in ambient atmosphere with  $ >65\% $  RH, as shown in Fig. 8a. Liu et al. first deployed a thin film ( $ \sim 1.8~{\mu\mathrm{m}} $ ) of SWCNTs as back contact for a monolithic structure, achieving a PCE of  $ 12.7\% $  and observing good stability for  $ 300\mathrm{h} $  in dark ambient conditions [203]. Luo et al. fabricated a novel device based on a substrate/NiO(PVK)/ $ \mathrm{Al_2O_3} $ (PVK)/ $ \mathrm{SnO_2@CNTs} $ (PVK) structure, attaining a PCE of  $ 14.3\% $  [204]. The device retained over  $ 88\% $  of its initial PCE for  $ 550\mathrm{h} $  under full light soaking in a  $ \mathbf{N}_2 $  atmosphere.

Second, a binary mixture of graphite and carbon black is often used to prepare carbon paste. The size of graphite and the ratio of graphite and carbon black play a key role in determining the mesoporous structure and electrical conductivity of CEs [101,205]. The suitable pore size of CEs is beneficial for the infiltration of PVK precursor, facilitating intimate interfacial contact of PVK and CEs. This will help to reduce interfacial degradation and improve device stability. In 2013, Ku et al. firstly screen-printed spherical graphite and carbon black paste to prepare CEs for PSCs [19]. This C-PSC without any encapsulation presented high stability for  $ 840\mathrm{h} $  under dark condition in dry air at RT. In 2015, Zhang et al. systematically optimized the size of graphite, work function, homogeneity, average pore size and the square resistance of CEs [101]. Then, the mixture of graphite and carbon black was extensively utilized to prepare CEs for monolithic structure [19,31,97,100,111-113,117,121,168]. And the stability of the related devices was also widely investigated. To date, for laboratory-scale cells, a maximum PCE of  $ \sim 17\% $  has been achieved [112]. The monolithic C-PSCs can be easily fabricated by screen-printing, and they are more suitable for large-scale production. The carbon-based mini-module (10~200 cm $  ^{2}  $ ) has reached a PCE of 12.87% so far, but the fabrication of large-scale module (>200 cm $  ^{2}  $ ) is rarely reported. For large-scale monolithic module, the worst subcell will determine the overall performance due to the series connection. Therefore, the fabrication of each subcell is crucial for ensuring the overall performance. The strategies based on laboratory-scale cells and mini-modules will provide important insights into improving the performance of large-scale carbon-based module. On the one hand, the fabrication of defect-free films is of great importance for ensuring the performance of individual cells. Optimizing the settings related to print speed and angle of screen separation enables the defect-free printing of large-scale modules. On the other hand, the PCE loss of monolithic C-PSCs mainly derives from the inferior interfacial contact between PVK and triple mesoporous scaffold [36,38], which gives rise to the poor transfer and serious recombination of carriers [37]. Therefore, interfacial engineering is an effective approach to improve the efficiency, including optimization of the mesoporous scaffold and optimization of PVK precursor. For mesoporous scaffold, optimizing the particle size of m-TiO $  _{2}  $  can largely facilitate the contact between PVK and ETM [103,210]. Optimizing the thickness of m-ZrO $  _{2}  $  [102] or using other insulating layers (Al $  _{2}  $ O $  _{3}  $  or SiO $  _{2}  $ , for instance) instead of m-ZrO $  _{2}  $  [211,212] can achieve both the perfect electrical isolation of ETM/CEs and the beneficial penetration of the PVK precursor. Optimization of the recipe of carbon paste could obtain a more uniform carbon layer and avoid the formation of dense flakes, which can hinder PVK infiltration [213]. In view of the limited hole selectivity of carbon materials, usage of suitable HTMs can further facilitate the hole extraction and transfer between PVK and CEs. For PVK precursor, compositional engineering [97,109,112], solvent engineering [110], additive engineering [106,108], and post-treatment strategies [68,214] have been extensively investigated to enhance its penetrability and pore filling capability. Slot-die coating and inkjet printing methods

Precisely patterning the  $ \mathrm{c - TiO_2} $  layer [115] or reducing its thickness [215] can effectively improve the interconnection between adjacent cells and thus reduce the contact resistance of the carbon/TCO interface. Designing the metal grid in the TCO could achieve the same goal [216]. Filling the carbon mesopores with highly conductive polymers or metallic nanoparticles in interconnection regions may reduce the resistance of the carbon/TCO interface. In addition, optimizing the scribing method [115,217] play a key role in reducing the contact resistance of the carbon/TCO interface as well.

The CEs can be fabricated from (self-made or commercial) carbon paste, conductive carbon cloth, CNTs network, graphene, or a separate carbon film.

For highly efficient and stable C-PSCs, the selection of carbon materials of carbon paste is extremely important. The outstanding CEs need not only high conductivity but also good interfacial contact with PVK films, which can effectively protect the PVK from moisture and oxygen degradation. Besides carbon materials, the binders and solvents of carbon paste are equally important, because they are related to the final morphologies of CEs. The homogeneous, dense, and PVK-friendly CEs could contribute to enhance the stability of the device. The following requirements need to be satisfied. First, the solvents should sufficiently dissolve the binders to ensure the homogeneity of carbon paste. Second, the binders and solvents in carbon paste should not degrade the PVK film. Third, the binders should avoid hygroscopicity to induce PVK degradation. Consequently, to minimize the impact of the post-deposited CEs on the PVK films, other forms of CEs have been investigated vastly.

(2) Carbon cloth

Carbon cloth and carbon fiber are widely used as catalysts or counter electrodes in dye-sensitized solar cells, lithium batteries, and supercapacitors. Gholipour et al. deployed a low-cost carbon cloth electrode as a hole collector composite and degradation inhibitor for PSCs   $  [177]  $  . The resultant device achieved a PCE of 15.29%. (4) Graphene

Graphene is a two-dimensional material with hexagonal honeycomb lattice, which presents excellent photoelectric and mechanical properties. It displays a broad application prospect in the field of photovoltaics. In 2015, You et al. reported the fabrication of semitransparent C-PSCs by laminating the multilayer graphene (MG) as top transparent electrodes, which showed high transparency under light illumination [201]. # 4. Strengthening encapsulation

To minimize the environmental risks that can trigger device degradation, one of the most effective strategies is to strengthen encapsulation   $  [55,56]  $  . To date, the relevant techniques with regards to C-PSCs fall into two categories: laboratory-scale cells and large-scale carbon-based PSMs. The effects of encapsulation techniques on device stability are summarized below.

# 4.1 Laboratory-scale cells

Laboratory-scale cells can flexibly be encapsulated within a limited active area. A wide array of encapsulation techniques exists, including thin glass+UV-curable resin, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) protective layer, epoxy+Ag-paint protective layer, and epoxy resin protective layer.

In 2015, Cao et al. reported that the C-PSCs sealed with thin glass+UV-curable resin retained more than  $ 75\% $  of their initial PCE for  $ 1000\mathrm{h} $  under continuous light soaking in ambient atmosphere at RT ( $ \sim 25^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ ) and  $ 40\% $  RH, showing excellent stability [163]. Baranwal et al. found that a proper sealing position was required for the devices to avoid rapid degradation under thermal stress, as shown in Fig. 10a [248]. The sealed devices maintained about  $ 90\% $  of their initial PCE for  $ 4500\mathrm{h} $  in ambient atmosphere [52]. Similarly, Ahn et al. used thin glass+UV-curable resin to encapsulate the C-PSCs, with the resultant device keeping up to  $ 80\% $  of its initial PCE after storage for  $ 2200\mathrm{h} $  under 1 sun illumination including UV radiation in ambient air, as shown in Fig. 10b [80]. Additionally, Liu et al. simply sealed the C-PSCs using PDMS [249]. After storage in air for  $ 3000\mathrm{h} $  at  $ 20^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  and  $ 20\% $  RH, the PCE of the device showed a slight increase. Wei et al. elaborately designed an epoxy+Ag-paint protective layer on top of the CE [250]. The devices were tested in different environmental conditions to assess water resistance. They found that the devices maintained stable operation in a watery environment for  $ 80\mathrm{min} $ . Hashmi et al. encapsulated C-PSCs using low-cost epoxy resin [251]. After continuous exposure to

# 4.2 Large-scale PSMs

For large-scale PSMs, the encapsulation techniques are extremely challenging because of the unsuitability of the edge sealing method. Likewise, common encapsulation techniques for silicon or thin-film solar cells are not applicable due to the high-temperature decomposition of PVK materials. The currently reported encapsulation techniques include glass+Pattex silicon, glass+Surlyn polymer, and glass+hot melt polyurethane (PU) film.

In 2017, Hu et al. sealed large-scale PSMs (substrate area:  $ 100~\mathrm{cm}^2 $ ) using glass+Pattex silicon and conducted an outdoor stability test [100]. The encapsulated PSM showed no decay in performance after exposure outdoors at  $ \sim 30^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  and  $ \sim 80\% $  RH. Grancini et al. encapsulated PSMs (substrate area:  $ 100~\mathrm{cm}^2 $ ) using glass+DuPont Surlyn polymer [31]. The module was covered with a thin glass, and the edges were plugged using DuPont Surlyn polymer. Then, an extra layer of epoxy glue was added around the edges of the module. The encapsulated PSM showed no decline in performance for over  $ 10,000\mathrm{h} $  under continuous illumination in air at  $ 55^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . Although the stability of the PSMs was decent, the effects of the encapsulation on device performance were not well studied and discussed in the above-mentioned reports. Recently, Fu et al. systematically investigated the encapsulation process, including laminating temperature, selection of hot melt films, film thickness, and melting temperatures [252]. # 5. Stability testing standards

Recently, the studies on C-PSCs have attracted much attention due to their excellent stability. Usually, it is difficult to compare the stability of C-PSCs from the different publications, because each of them utilizes different standards of the long-term stability test. The main purposes to unify the testing standards lie in evaluating and comparing the real stability of the devices   $  [253]  $  . Therefore, it is greatly important to establish standards for the long-term stability test   $  [253,254]  $  .

First, the statistical data of stability test should be provided. The data from a single cell can only indicate that this cell is stable in a certain environment, which does not demonstrate it is more stable than others. However, the statistical results from a large number of cells are more reliable than a single one. The statistical data from ten or more cells may both provide more reliable results and push the results to a statistical level [253]. If a great number of cells (much larger than ten) are utilized for mathematical statistics, these results would be so complicated that they are very difficult to discuss. Moreover, the statistical samples should select the cells with better PCE parallelity to avoid larger stability discrepancies, thus ensuring the validity of the results. In this review, only  $ 16\% $  of literatures provide the statistical data of stability testing results. Others do not provide a detailed description or only results from a single cell.

Second, lack of quantitative data on testing conditions will make the comparison and analysis of the results more difficult   $  [253,255]  $  . Therefore, it is essential to standardize the testing conditions, including temperature, humidity, atmosphere (air or   $  N_{2}  $  ), dark condition or illumination (including light source type and intensity), indoor or outdoor test, with or without encapsulation, and with or without continuous operation. In this review, almost all results describe the ambient temperature and/or humidity during testing. More than 90% of the tests are performed in air atmosphere, and 10% in   $  N_{2}  $   or Ar atmosphere. 76% of the tests are performed under dark condition, and 24% are tested under illumination. 97% of the tests are performed indoors, and only 3% are tested outdoors. 85% of the tests are without any encapsulation, and 15%

Journal Pre-proof

for encapsulated devices. Less than  $ 10\% $  of the tests display the continuous operation of the devices during MPP tracking, and  $ 90\% $  of the tests do not provide this result.

Third, the stability evaluation should be established step by step. It mainly includes four steps: (dark condition) environmental stability, (dark condition) high temperature and/or high humidity aging stability, light-soaking stability (i.e. continuous light-soaking and interval measurements), and continuous operation stability (i.e. MPP tracking measurements). For laboratory-scale C-PSCs, the evaluation of the high temperature and/or high humidity aging stability should be performed when the environmental stability of the devices reaches a certain timescale. When the high temperature and/or high humidity aging stability reach a certain timescale, the evaluation of the light-soaking stability should be carried out. When the light-soaking stability reaches a certain timescale, the evaluation of the continuous operation stability should be established. For large-scale carbon-based modules, more complicated evaluation should be implemented according to the standards of commercial silicon and thin film photovoltaic (i.e. IEC 61215-1:2016) [256], as shown in Fig.11. In addition, the device lifespan defined according to  $ \mathrm{T}_{80} $  (the time that the device takes for the PCE to drop to  $ 80\% $  of the initial value) is widely recommended as the end of the stability tests [254,255,257].

# 6. Conclusion and Outlook

C-PSCs have achieved a decent PCE of  $ \sim 20\% $  and one-year stability, making them one of the most promising candidates for commercialization. Yet, the stability of C-PSCs greatly lags behind the expected 25-year lifespan of the commercialized silicon and thin-film photovoltaics. It is thus imperative to carry out further studies on the stability of C-PSCs. In this review, we have summarized the environmental risks affecting the long-term stability of C-PSCs and the key strategies for achieving the same. Main factors affecting stability include moisture, oxygen, light, thermal stress, and their synergistic effects under atmospheric conditions. To minimize the environmental risks triggering device degradation, strategies for greater stability can be divided into two aspects: improving the intrinsic stability and strengthening encapsulation. With regards to the former, multiple strategies should be applied in C-PSCs, including (1) choosing damp and heat stable TCO under the premise of high visible light transmittance and conductivity, (2) using ETMs with strongly chemical stability, high electron mobility, and non-photocatalytic activity, (3) adjusting the intrinsic stability and crystallinity of PVK to prepare strong hydrophobic and defect-free PVK photoabsorbers, (4) using hydrophobic, chemically-stable, thermally-stable, and high hole mobility HTMs, and (5) selecting CEs with high conductivity, good interfacial contact, strong chemical stability, and no damage to PVK. With regards to the encapsulation, various techniques for laboratory-scale cells and large-scale PSMs should be devised. In addition, unifying standards of stability test is an imperative task in PVK research communities, which will help to evaluate and compare the real stability of the devices.

Considering further commercialization, the following work should be concerned. First, all-inorganic lead-free PVK materials should be extensively investigated. Optimizing bandgap, preparing uniform and defect-free film, and enhancing the intrinsic stability of inorganic PVK will further contribute to improve the PCE and stability of all-inorganic C-PSCs. In view of the environment and health hazards of Pb toxicity, research on all-inorganic lead-free PVK requires more intense efforts. Second,

Journal Pre-proof

to overcome the instability without compromising the efficiency, defect passivation is an effective strategy for C-PSCs. On the one hand, the quality of PVK polycrystalline film is closely associated with its stability. The rational adjustment of PVK crystallinity and morphology could enhance its intrinsic stability. Development of functional molecules that can anchor the uncoordinated halides and cations without disrupting charge excitation and transport in PVK needs much more attention. On the other hand, interfacial passivation materials and methods should be investigated to create the seamless and highly continuous contact between adjacent layers, reducing the interfacial defects and trap states. The design of dual-function molecules of Lewis base and Lewis acid may effectively passivate both types of positively and negatively charged defects simultaneously. Post-treatment of C-PSCs should be also studied to minimize interfacial defects. These approaches will help to improve the stability of the C-PSCs against environmental stress. Furthermore, it is of great importance to develop defect passivation processes that is applicable for large-scale production. Third, the investigations of scalable fabrication and coating methods for large-scale PSMs should be encouraged. In-depth understanding of nucleation and crystal growth dynamical processes will help to reproducibly deposit smooth and pinhole-free films with large grains, high-crystallinity, and uniform stoichiometry across large-scale substrates. To achieve this goal, development of favorable precursor solutions is a key strategy. The scalable coating methods include blade coating, slot-die coating, spray coating, inkjet printing, screen printing, electrodeposition, and vapor-phase deposition. These techniques should be extended to accomplish the scalable fabrication of large-scale carbon-based modules. Fourth, the exploration on the PSM encapsulation should be emphasized. Future research directions will include exploring low-cost and reliable encapsulation materials as well as developing encapsulation techniques to minimize its influence on PSM performance.

Although carbon-based perovskite solar cells (C-PSCs) are recognized as the most promising candidate for commercialization, the stability of C-PSCs significantly lags behind the expected 25-year lifespan of the commercial photovoltaics. It is thus imperative to carry out further investigation on the stability of C-PSCs. For C-PSCs, interfacial engineering, materials, methods, and efficiency improvement have already been summarized. But a comprehensive account of the stability of C-PSCs is conspicuously absent. Herein, we summarize the environmental risks and key strategies for long-term stability of C-PSCs.
