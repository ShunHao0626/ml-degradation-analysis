# Managing Defects Density and Interfacial Strain via Underlayer Engineering for Inverted   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   Perovskite Solar Cells with All-Layer Dopant-Free

Dongwei Han, Siwei Yi, Quan Yuan, Xiaoxuan Tang, Qianwen Shu, Qiaoyan Li, Feng Wang, Dong-Ying Zhou, and Lai Feng*

Inorganic perovskite   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   has advantages of excellent thermal stability and reasonable bandgap, which make it suitable for top layer of tandem solar cells. Nevertheless, solution-processed all-inorganic perovskites generally suffer from high-density defects as well as significant tensile strain near underlayer/perovskite interface, both leading to compromised device efficiency and stability. In this work, the defect density as well as interfacial tensile strain in inverted   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite solar cells (PeSCs) is remarkably reduced by using a bilayer underlayer composed of dopant-free 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-dip-methoxyphenylamine)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD) and copper phthalocyanine 3,4',4'',4'''-tetrasulfonated acid tetrasodium salt (TS-CuPc) nanoparticles. As compared to control devices with pristine Spiro-OMeTAD, devices based on Spiro-OMeTAD/TS-CuPc exhibit remarkably improved photovoltaic performance and enhanced thermal/humidity stability due to the better perovskite crystallization, improved interfacial passivation, and hole-collection as well as efficient interfacial strain release. As a result, a champion efficiency of 14.85% can be achieved, which is approaching to the best reported for dopant-free and inverted all-inorganic PeSCs. The work thus provides an efficient strategy to simultaneously regulate the defects density and strain issue related to inorganic perovskites.

# 1. Introduction

All-inorganic perovskites (i.e.,   $  CsPbX_{3}  $  , X = I, Br) have been considered as promising absorbers for constructing the top layer of tandem solar cells due to their wide bandgaps, excellent

variety of strategies have been developed for defect control. Typically, doping engineering has been adopted for preparing high-quality inorganic perovskite films,  $  ^{[18-20]}  $  which, however, unfavorably increases the complexity of device fabrication. On the other hand, as perovskite quality is highly associated with the condition or nature of underlayers, modification of the underlayer has been considered as an alternative approach toward high-quality perovskite film. Nevertheless, so-far employed underlayer for inorganic perovskite is limited to conventional carrier transport materials such as  $  TiO_{2}  $ ,  $  SnO_{2}  $  (for n-i-p type PeSCs),  $  ^{[21,22]}  $  and  $  NiO_{x}  $  (for p-i-n type PeSCs).  $  ^{[7,23]}  $  Very recently, well-designed polymer poly[3-(4-carboxylbutyl)thiophene methylamine] and small molecule 4,4',4'',4'''-(ethene-1,1,2,2-tetrayl)tetrakis(N,N-bis(4-(methylthio)phenyl)aniline) (TPE-S) have been employed as the underlayer and hole transport layer (HTL) for inverted inorganic PeSCs, which afford excellent device performance with record PCEs.  $  ^{[24,25]}  $  Nevertheless, these hole transport materials (HTMs) are lab-synthesized and not commercially available, which limits their wide applications. The new underlayer as well as HTL based on ingenious design with commercially available materials is highly desired.

On the other hand, strain issue remains as another challenge for inorganic PeSCs, which includes intrinsic lattice strain and extrinsically induced strain.  $  ^{[26]}  $   Particularly, the latter is mainly induced by thermal expansion mismatch between the perovskite film with high thermal expansion coefficient (  $  \alpha_{TE}  $  :   $  \approx10^{-4}  $   K  $  ^{-1}  $  ) and the underlayer with much lower   $  \alpha_{TE}  $  , such as above-mentioned TiO  $  _{2}  $  , SnO  $  _{2}  $  , or NiO  $  _{x}  $   with   $  \alpha_{TE}  $   of   $  0.1\sim2\times10^{-5}  $   K  $  ^{-1}  $  .  $  ^{[27-29]}  $   In addition, the large temperature gradient (  $  \triangle T  $  ) from perovskite formation temperature (i.e., >160 °C for CsPbI  $  _{2}  $  Br) to room temperature aggravates such mismatch.  $  ^{[19,30]}  $   As a result, serious tensile strain has been observed near the underlayer/perovskite interface, which might induce more interfacial defects as well as intrinsic instability.  $  ^{[31]}  $   To solve this issue, several strategies have been reported and summarized as follows. (i) Employing the underlayer with a high   $  \alpha_{TE}  $   to decrease   $  \triangle\alpha_{TE}  $  ;  $  ^{[31]}  $   (ii) Lowering the formation temperature of perovskite film to reduce   $  \triangle T  $  ;  $  ^{[32]}  $   (iii) Strain compensation by using an underlayer with lower   $  \alpha_{TE}  $   and a top layer with higher   $  \alpha_{TE}  $  .  $  ^{[29]}  $   Nevertheless, how to balance the strain issue with perovskite quality still remains challenging. For instance, lower-temperature thermal annealing usually results in reduced tensile strain but more defects in perovskite film, which is not beneficial for device performance.

2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-dip-methoxyphenylamine)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD) as a conventional and commercial available HTM has been widely used to construct HTL for n-i-p type PeSCs, while it is rarely used for p-i-n type PeSCs probably due to the low light transmission of regularly prepared Spiro-OMeTAD layer with a thickness of   $  \approx  $  200–300 nm (see Figure S1, Supporting Information). Nevertheless, very recently, we found that an alternatively prepared thin Spiro-OMeTAD layer with high light transmission works well as the underlayer and HTL in p-i-n type PeSCs.  $  ^{[33]}  $   In this work, we report an ingenious design for the underlayer in p-i-n type   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   PeSCs, by which a bilayer HTL (denoted as Spiro/TS-CuPc) is facilely prepared by modifying a dopant-free Spiro-OMeTAD layer with copper phthalocyanine 3,4',4'',4'''-tetrasulfonated acid tetrasodium salt (TS-CuPc) nanoparticles. # 2. Results and Discussion

The bilayer HTL based on Spiro-OMeTAD/TS-CuPc (Spiro/TS-CuPc) was prepared using a solution method. Particularly, the TS-CuPc layer was deposited on a dopant-free Spiro-OMeTAD layer from an aqueous solution with optimized concentration (6 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ). To further optimize the deposition condition, the TS-CuPc solution was introduced either during or before the spin-coating process. The obtained HTLs are denoted as Spiro/TS-CuPc-i and Spiro/TS-CuPc-ii, respectively. Their optical images are presented in Figure S2, Supporting Information. Obviously, the Spiro/TS-CuPc-i film is more uniform than the other. Their typical atomic force microscopy (AFM) height images are provided in Figure 1a–c. Obviously, the Spiro/TS-CuPc-ii film shows a rough surface with a high root-mean-square roughness (RMS) of 2.450 nm, which is ascribed to the highly aggregated nanoparticles with size of   $  \approx  $  10–20 nm on the film surface. In contrast, Spiro/TS-CuPc-i film exhibits a highly smooth surface with an ultralow roughness of 0.285 nm, even lower than that (0.608 nm) of pristine Spiro-OMeTAD film, which indicates the “smear effect” of TS-CuPc-i deposition. On the film surface, nanoparticles are well dispersed (Figure S3, Supporting Information), indicating that serious aggregation of TS-CuPc may be efficiently suppressed by using the applied deposition method. To confirm the presence of TS-CuPc-i on Spiro-OMeTAD layer, element mapping images are presented in Figure S5, Supporting Information, illustrating uniform distribution of O, Cu, S, and Na species of TS-CuPc. In addition, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and water contact angles were measured for these HTLs. As shown in Figures 1d–f, DMSO droplet spread immediately upon the contact with the surface of Spiro/TS-CuPc-i, resulting in a much lower contact angle (5.1°) relative to that of pristine Spiro-OMeTAD layer (18.4°) as well as Spiro/TS-CuPc-ii layer (26.9°). The lower contact angle means better wettability toward DMSO, which is beneficial for forming a good contact between HTL and perovskite layer.[25] On the other hand, as illustrated in Figure S6, Supporting Information, the water contact angle is varied in the order of Spiro/TS-CuPc-i > Spiro-OMeTAD > Spiro/TS-CuPc-ii. It indicates that the Spiro/TS-CuPc-i film is more hydrophobic than others presumably due to its much lower surface roughness, which is beneficial for preventing moisture adsorption. In addition, the electronic and optical natures of Spiro/TS-CuPc-i film were probed by means of ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) and UV-Vis transmission spectroscopy, respectively. As shown in Figure S7, Supporting Information, Spiro/TS-CuPc-i and Spiro-OMeTAD films exhibit almost similar work function (WF), indicative of the negligible electronic effect of additional TS-CuPc-i layer. As revealed by the UV-Vis transmission spectra (Figure S1a, Supporting Information), the Spiro-OMeTAD base layer exhibits high light transmission of >80% in the wide spectral range of 420–800 nm and slightly lower transmission of =47–82% in the range of ≈340–420 nm, as compared to that (≈71–82%) of ITO layer. The transmission spectrum of Spiro/TS-CuPc-i bilayer is almost similar to that of Spiro-OMeTAD base layer, in which the transmission between 400 and

600 nm is slightly decreased due to the absorption of the top thin layer of TS-CuPc-i. This result indicates that the Spiro/TS-CuPc-i bilayer is fully transparent, which makes it applicable as underlayer and HTL in inverted PeSCs.

To clarify how perovskite quality relies on the underlayer, three cases of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite films deposited on pristine Spiro-OMeTAD, Spiro/TS-CuPc-i, and Spiro/TS-CuPc-ii were characterized by means of scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As shown in Figures 2a–c, all perovskite films display compact and pinhole-free morphology. Nevertheless, by using Spiro/TS-CuPc-i as underlayer and HTL to replace Spiro-OMeTAD-only layer, the perovskite morphology is obviously improved with larger grain-size up to 700–800 nm and reduced boundary gap (see Figure S8, Supporting Information). Moreover, all perovskite films display similar X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Figure 2d) with two dominant peaks at 14.6° and 29.5°, assignable to the (100) and (200) planes of   $  \alpha  $  -CsPbI  $  _{2}  $  Br. In comparison, more enhanced patterns are observed for the Spiro/TS-CuPc-i/PVK film, indicative of better perovskite crystallinity. Meanwhile, the Spiro/TS-CuPc-i/PVK film also shows enhanced perovskite absorptions in the spectral range of 400–600 nm relative to others (Figure S9, Supporting Information), confirming again the improved perovskite quality. Thus, above results verify that the bilayer underlayer of Spiro/TS-CuPc-i is able to facilitate the formation of high-quality perovskite film.

To confirm the enhanced   $  J_{SC}  $  , external quantum efficiency (EQE) was recorded and provided in Figure 4d. The device with TS-CuPc-i exhibits slightly enhanced EQE values in the spectral range of 300–650 nm, as compared to the control device. The integrated current density is calculated as 14.05 and 15.12 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   for the devices without and with TS-CuPc-i, respectively, in good agreement with the J-V results with an error within 5%. The PCE histograms and corresponding statistical analysis over   $  \approx  $  30 individual devices (see Figure 4c (inset) and Figure S14, Supporting Information) reveal that the devices with TS-CuPc-i show better reproducibility than control devices. Furthermore, the stabilized power output (SPO) under maximum power-point condition is plotted in Figure 4e. The device with TS-CuPc-i delivers SPO of 13.14% for 500 s, much higher than that (8.75%) of the device without TS-CuPc. The difference between SPO and PCE might be ascribed to the hysteresis effect of inverted device,  $  ^{[7]}  $   which is reduced from 38% to 22% upon the introduction of TS-CuPc-i (see Figure 4f and Table S4, Supporting Information).

In order to evaluate the hole-collection-efficiency of TS-CuPc underlayer, steady-state and transient photoluminescence (PL) spectra were measured. As illustrated in Figure 5a, the

perovskite film with Spiro/TS-CuPc-i displays more severely quenched PL as compared to that with pristine Spiro-OMeTAD or Spiro/TS-CuPc-ii, indicative of more efficient hole-collection. Moreover, as revealed by transient PL spectra (Figure 5b), the introduction of Spiro-OMeTAD layer shortens the PL lifetime from 14.0 to 2.7 ns, while the film with additional TS-CuPc-i displays an even shorter lifetime of 1.9 ns. This result clearly confirms that the introduction of additional TS-CuPc-i layer enhances the hole-collection efficiency of HTL.

To further verify the passivation effect of TS-CuPc, PL was recorded from both sides of glass/HTL/PVK film without or with TS-CuPc-i. As illustrated in Figure S15, Supporting Information, an evident blue-shift (  $  \approx  $  13 nm) from the perovskite side to glass side is observed in the case of the film with TS-CuPc-i, while negligible shift is seen for the control film. This result thus points to fewer defects at the Spiro/TS-CuPc-i/PVK interface, indicative of more efficient passivation effect of TS-CuPc than pristine Spiro-OMeTAD. To confirm this result, electroluminescence measurements were also conducted for the two films with typically a forward bias of 1.0 V, where the charges are directly injected through the contact. As shown in Figure 5c, the film with TS-CuPc-i displays a much stronger emission at around 650 nm, as compared to that of control film. As the injected charges do not recombine in the bulk of perovskite film, the enhanced emission usually points to reduced interfacial charge recombination, which indicates again reduced interfacial defects upon the introduction of TS-CuPc-i.  $  ^{[36,37]}  $

To better understand the interaction between TS-CuPc-i and perovskite, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was performed on thin perovskite films, which could be prepared by depositing a diluted precursor solution (  $  \approx  $  20 times) on the Spiro/TS-CuPc-i and Spiro-OMeTAD substrates, respectively, followed by regular thermal annealing.  $  ^{[38]}  $   As shown in Figure 5d, the Pb 4f peaks are shifted by 0.50 eV to lower binding energy upon the introduction of TS-CuPc-i, which might be ascribed to the formation of Pb-O coordination interaction between TS-CuPc and perovskite.  $  ^{[39]}  $   In addition, the I 3d peaks are also shifted to lower binding energy by 0.44 eV (Figure 5e), presumably due to the electrostatic interaction between Na  $  ^{+}  $  cation of TS-CuPc and iodine species in perovskite.  $  ^{[40]}  $   These results may indicate the interfacial passivation effect of TS-CuPc-i layer, which also benefits to the hole extract at the HTL/PVK interface.  $  ^{[41]}  $

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, we designed and constructed a new underlayer, Spiro/TS-CuPc-i, for dopant-free and inverted   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   PeSCs. It was verified that using Spiro/TS-CuPc-i as underlayer and HTL is beneficial for the low-temperature (<150 °C) crystallization of perovskite, hole collection, interfacial defects passivation, and tensile strain release. As a result, the Spiro/TS-CuPc-i based PeSCs demonstrated remarkable device performance with PCE up to 14.85%, outperforming the reference devices based on Spiro-OMeTAD or TS-CuPc-i alone as well as the conventional underlayers,   $  NiO_{x}  $  , PEDOT:PSS, and their combinations with TS-CuPc. In addition, the Spiro/TS-CuPc-i underlayer also evidently enhanced the thermal/humidity stability of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   PeSCs. All these results validate Spiro/TS-CuPc-i as a promising underlayer and HTL for inverted   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   PeSCs. On the other hand, as both Spiro-OMeTAD and TS-CuPc are commercially available, the optimized Spiro/TS-CuPc-i underlayer is easily accessible to all of us. This study thus provides a high-performance and easily accessible underlayer for inverted PeSCs through ingenious design, which might open new avenues to develop inorganic PeSCs.

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials: Spiro-OMeTAD and TS-CuPc were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. and Sigma Aldrich Corp., respectively. Lead iodide (PbI  $  _{2}  $  ) and cesium bromide (CsBr) were from Alfa Aesar. PC  $  _{61}  $  BM and Bphen were from Solarmer Materials Inc. and Luminescence Tec. Co., respectively.

Device Fabrication: ITO-coated glass with sheet resistance of   $  \approx15\ \Omega\ sq^{-1}  $   was cleaned using established method.  $  ^{[31]}  $   The Spiro-OMeTAD HTL was prepared by spin-coating a THF solution of Spiro-OMeTAD on an ITO substrate (5 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ).  $  ^{[33,51]}  $   The Spiro/TS-CuPc HTL was deposited by successively spin-coating an aqueous solution containing 6 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   TS-CuPc on the Spiro-OMeTAD layer under different conditions, followed by a thermal annealing at 150 °C for 10 min (Figure S16, Supporting Information). To prepare perovskite layer, a 1.1 M precursor solution (1 mL DMSO/DMF mixture with v/v of 9/1) containing CsBr (235 mg) and PbI  $  _{2}  $   (507 mg) was spin-coated on the HTL at a speed of 4000 rpm for 30 s, followed by a two-step thermal annealing process (45 °C for   $  \approx30  $   s and then 140 °C for 2 min, see Figure S17, Supporting Information).  $  ^{[52,53]}  $   The bilayer ETL was deposited by spin-coating the Nano-Eu  $  _{2}  $  O  $  _{3}  $   solution (0.1 mg in 1 mL ethanol)  $  ^{[37]}  $   at a speed of 4000 rpm for 40 s, followed by spin-coating the PC  $  _{61}  $  BM solution (20 mg in 1 mL o-DCB) at a speed of 2000 rpm for 40 s. Next, a buffer layer was prepared by spin-coating the Bphen solution (0.5 mg in 1 mL ethanol) at a speed of 2000 rpm for 40 s. Finally, Ag electrodes with thickness of 80–100 nm were vacuum deposited under   $  1\times10^{-4}  $   Torr using a shadow mask. The device area was defined as 4 mm  $  ^{2}  $  .

Characterizations: The  $ J - V $  characteristics of all the as-fabricated devices were recorded with a Keithley 2400 Source Meter with a scan rate of  $ 0.1\mathrm{Vs}^{-1} $  in dark or under AM 1.5G illumination (XES-70S1,

SAN-EI, Japan.). EQE was measured with a QE-R3011 system (Enli Tech. China). SEM images were obtained on a Hitachi instrument (SU8010). AFM images were obtained on a Bruker instrument (Dimension Icon) in tapping mode. XRD at both room temperature and varied temperature as well as GIXRD spectra were recorded using a Bruker D8 Advance instrument with a Cu Kα source (  $  \lambda = 0.154nm  $  ). Each spectrum was obtained by averaging the data from different regions. Contact angle images were captured by an automatic contact angle instrument (DSA100S, KRUSS, GmbH). Optical measurements were conducted with a Shimadzu spectrophotometer (UV2600). Steady-state PL spectra were obtained using a Horiba system (Fluorolog, Jobin Yvon) with   $  \lambda_{ex} = 468 nm  $  . Transient PL spectra were measured using an Edinburgh system (FLS980) using a pump light wavelength of 477 nm and probe wavelength of 760 nm. XPS as well as UPS measurements were performed on an ESCALab250Xi electron spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using a fixed irradiation energy of 2000 eV and a He-I line with the energy of 21.22 eV, respectively. Non-modulated impedance spectroscopy was performed using an electrochemical workstation (CHI 760E) at various forward biases. A 30 mV voltage perturbation was applied over a constant forward applied bias between 0.4 and 0.8 V in the frequency range between 1.0 KHz and 1.0 MHz.
