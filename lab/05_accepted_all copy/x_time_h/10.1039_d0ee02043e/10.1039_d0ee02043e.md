# Polymeric Room-Temperature Molten Salt as Multiple Functional Additive toward Highly Efficient and Stable Inverted Planar Perovskite Solar Cells

Shuangjie Wang, $ ^{a} $  Bowen Yang, $ ^{b} $  Jian Han, $ ^{a} $  Ziwei He, $ ^{a} $  Tongtong Li, $ ^{a} $  Qi Cao, $ ^{a} $  Jiabao Yang, $ ^{a} $  Jiajia Suo, $ ^{b} $  Xuanhua Li, $ ^{a*} $  Zhike Liu, $ ^{c} $  Shengzhong (Frank) Liu, $ ^{c} $  Chao Tang, $ ^{d} $  and Anders Hagfeldt $ ^{b*} $

The inferior power conversion efficiency (PCE) compared to their regular counterparts (n-i-p) and undesirable stability issues of inverted (p-i-n) perovskite solar cells (PSCs) are the foremost issues hindering their commercialization. Here, for the first time, we demonstrate polymeric room-temperature molten salt (poly-RTMS), namely poly(1-vinyl-3-ethyl-acetate) imidazole tetrafluoroborate (PEa) as a novel type of additive to modulate the perovskite crystallization and its electronic properties. The PEa poly-RTMS containing multiple chemical anchoring sites along with strong bonding stability can firmly bond to Pb ion defects at grain boundaries and the interface of perovskite film via coordination bonding, which effectively passivate the electronic defects and enhance the photo-, thermal-, and moisture-stability of perovskite films. As a result, the PEa-modified inverted PSCs show striking performance improvements over the control with the PCE exceeding 21.4% and excellent long-term operational stability, maintaining over 92% of initial efficiency for 1200 hours under continuous full sun illumination at 70-75 °C. This strategy opens a new avenue to modulate the properties of perovskite for optoelectronic applications.

# Introduction

Metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have made tremendous advancements over the past several years.  $  ^{1-6}  $   The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs has enhanced from 3.8% to 25.2% within a very short period.  $  ^{7}  $   Compared with the regular (n-i-p) architecture devices, the inverted (p-i-n) planar PSCs offer simple processes, low-temperature preparation, alleviated hysteresis, and good compatibility with flexible or tandem architectures.  $  ^{8-10}  $   However, the main challenge of inverted PSCs is a comparably low PCE.  $  ^{11-13}  $   The traps existing at both the grain boundaries (GBs) and surface of the photo-active layer would induce trap-assisted nonradiative recombination and serve as vulnerable initiation sites for the external environmental degradation of the inverted PSCs.

Therefore, an efficient and simple passivation method is highly required to simultaneously improve the PCE and prolong the durability of inverted PSCs.

To date, additive-assisted techniques and crystal engineering have been put forward to improve the performance and stability of inverted PSCs. Additive-assisted techniques incorporate nanoparticles,  $  ^{14, 15}  $  metal halide salts,  $  ^{16,17}  $  acids,  $  ^{18-20}  $  fullerene derivatives,  $  ^{21, 22}  $  and polymers to passivate defects and improve the PV performance of inverted PSCs.  $  ^{23-25}  $  On the other hand, crystal engineering provides large-sized crystal growth and high crystallinity to minimize GBs and defects at both grain interior and boundaries via solvent treatment,  $  ^{26-28}  $  gas vaporizing treatment,  $  ^{29, 30}  $  and organic additives.  $  ^{31-34}  $  In both cases, room-temperature molten salt (RTMS, also known as ionic liquid) is a strong candidate to compete against the traditional materials due to its unique physicochemical properties with negligible vapor pressure, remarkable electrochemical properties, various functional ligands, and nonhazardous nature.  $  ^{35, 36}  $  Bai et al. used RTMS as an additive in the precursor solution to obtain PSCs with a PCE of 20.8% and excellent stability.  $  ^{3}  $  Recently, our group developed a self-assembled monolayer of water-

Concomitantly, it causes substantial diffusion and easily migrate in the perovskite film during operation, which does not meet the requirement of future commercialization of PSCs. There are three unique advantages of the as-designed PEa poly-RTMS: (i) the repeating units of poly-RTMS contain multi-site anchored carbonyl groups (C=O) with strong bonding stability (Fig. 1a), which could passivate Pb ion defects on perovskite surface or GBs and form a highly coordinated and long-range perovskite-poly-RTMS composite ordering along the polymer chain; (ii) the presence of the highly coordinated perovskite-poly-RTMS composite obstructs poly-RTMS from leaving GBs under high temperature and continuous light illumination, which pose superiority in practical PSCs operated under harsh environmental stresses; (iii) compared to RTMS, the high decomposition temperature (347 °C) and hydrophobicity of poly-RTMS are critical to enhance the device stability. As a result, the judiciously tailored PEa poly-RTMS improves the PCE of inverted PSCs from 18.16% (champion) to 21.47% (champion) via modulating perovskite crystallization and suppressing defects formation. Notably, the unencapsulated PEa-modified device displays pronounced moisture stability over 5000 h in relative humidity (RH) of 35 ± 5%, and the encapsulated device exhibits superior long-term operational stability by retaining over 92% of the initial performance for 1200 h under continuous light illumination at 70-75 °C.

Through molecular structure analysis, we speculated that the PEa poly-RTMS with flexible long chains as well as multiple functional repeat units (C=O groups) could provide chemical anchoring sites for the passivation of under-coordinated Pb ions in the perovskite film. As proof, we calculated electrostatic potential (ESP) by the density functional theory to specify the electron density distribution in corresponding RTMS monomers (Fig. 1b). The electron-donating groups (positive region) mainly distribute on the alkyl chain linked by the imidazole ring, and the high electron density (negative region) is mainly concentrated on the electron-withdrawing C=O group, possessing favorable condition for coordination with the unsaturated Pb ions (Fig. 1c).

The molecular interactions between the RTMS, poly-RTMS, and triple-cation FAMACs (FA: formamidinium; MA: methylammonium) perovskite film were investigated using   $  {}^{1}  $  H NMR, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). For Bu RTMS, a series of   $  {}^{1}  $  H NMR measurements were conducted in solution (Fig. 1d and Fig. S5, 6). As shown in Fig. 1d, the broad peak around 9 ppm in the mixed solution of FAI and MABr splits into two new peaks after the introduction of Bu RTMS, suggesting that the hydrogen bond is formed between the H atom in the amidinium moiety of FA and N3 atom of the imidazole ring in Bu RTMS (inset in Fig. 1d).  $  ^{39}  $   We have also conducted the   $  {}^{1}  $  H NMR test of the mixed solution of Ea RTMS and PEa poly-RTMS with FAI respectively (Fig. S7), and observed the same results as Bu RTMS, which indicates that hydrogen bonds are also formed in Ea- and PEa-modified films. The FTIR spectroscopy characterization was also performed to study the existing form of Ea RTMS and PEa poly-RTMS in the perovskite film. The FTIR result after the addition of Ea RTMS monomer is illustrated in Fig. 1e. The peak at 1750 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   in the spectrum of the pure Ea RTMS is assigned

to the stretching vibration peak of C=O and is red-shifted to a lower wave number of   $  1746 \, cm^{-1}  $   in the corresponding Ea-modified perovskite films, while a large red shift to a wave number of   $  1743 \, cm^{-1}  $   from   $  1750 \, cm^{-1}  $   is observed for C=O in the PEa-modified perovskite films (Fig. 1f). These results indicate that the unsaturated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   in the perovskite forms bond with C=O group in RTMS or poly-RTMS.

We obtained further evidence for the molecular interactions between perovskite and RTMS or poly-RTMS additives by XPS analysis. The main peaks of Pb   $  4f_{7/2}  $   and   $  4f_{5/2}  $   with saturated coordination are shown in Fig. 1g. The two small peaks next to the main peaks are assigned to metallic Pb due to the highly under-coordinated Pb ions in the perovskite (see the marked dots in Fig. 1g).  $  ^{40}  $   The Pb 4f peaks in XPS of the Bu-modified film shift slightly to lower binding energy although the Bu RTMS has no C=O group. As shown in the ESP calculation result (Fig. 1b), the high electron density (negative region) mainly concentrated on the junction of vinyl group and imidazole ring. The main reason can be attributed to the shared electrons between the vinyl and imidazole ring, leading to an electron-rich conjugated   $  \pi  $  -electron system. Therefore, we speculate that binding energy shift is caused by the formation of weak coordination bond between this negative region with under-coordinated Pb ion defects. Meanwhile, we observed that the Pb 4f peaks in PEa-modified film show a strong shift of 0.6 eV toward lower binding energy but relatively small change for the Ea-modified film (0.3 eV). Moreover, the small peaks of metallic Pb in Ea- and PEa-modified perovskite films almost disappeared compared to the control film. The morphology of the perovskite films and grains were studied by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Top-view, cross-sectional SEM images, and crystal size distributions of perovskite films are displayed in Fig. 2a–d, respectively. The control film shows small grain (concentrated at 200-300 nm) and nonuniform morphology (Fig. 2a), which could be improved by tailoring the molecular structure of RTMS. In comparison with the

control film, the incorporation of Bu RTMS contributes to a uniform morphology with larger grain size of   $  \sim  $  300 nm (Fig. 2b). The grain size of perovskite is further increased to   $  \sim  $  400 and   $  \sim  $  600 nm upon the incorporation of the Ea RTMS (Fig. 2c) and PEa poly-RTMS (Fig. 2d). The large grain size of PEa-modified film is also confirmed by electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) measurement (Fig. S11). Moreover, the RTMS- and poly-RTMS-modified perovskite have particular monolithic, large grains throughout the films, which benefits the longitudinal electron transport.  $  ^{41,42}  $   These results demonstrate that the quality of the perovskite film could be better controlled by adding PEa poly-RTMS due to its low volatility, which could slow down the crystallization rate and lead to large perovskite grains. More importantly, the long chain structure of PEa poly-RTMS prevents its agglomeration during perovskite crystallization (the good film-forming property of PEa poly-RTMS copmared to Ea RTMS has been confirmed in Fig. S12), resulting in a homogenous perovskite film and more delicate GBs than the control film. We have performed energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy of the cross-sectional SEM (SEM-EDX) to clarify the element distribution. As illustrated in Fig. 2e, the distribution of B and F elements (characteristic elements in PEa poly-RTMS) is the same as that of Pb and I elements, which indicates that PEa poly-RTMS exists in the entire perovskite layer. X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra confirm the enlarged grain size observed by SEM measurements (Fig. S13). No obvious peak shift and new peaks are observed in the RTMS- and poly-RTMS-modified perovskite films in XRD, indicating that the additives do not insert into the perovskite lattice.

The distinct change of the PEa-modified perovskite film motivated us to deeply study the unique role of PEa poly-RTMS on the crystallization of the perovskite film by transmission electron microscope (TEM). Fig. 2f exhibits the low magnification TEM image of the PEa-modified film with polycrystalline grains and GBs. A few hundred nanometers of grains and their GBs can be seen from the image. In high-resolution TEM (Fig. 2g-i), perovskite grains show noticeable lattice fringes because of their crystallinity, while the PEa poly-RTMS shows amorphous morphology. Fig. 2g shows the high-resolution TEM of a “bridge” between two adjacent perovskite grains in the dotted circle of Fig. 2f. We thus focused the study of the interior connection (Fig. 2h) and the edge (Fig. 2i) of this bridge. In Fig. 2h, the adjacent region of the amorphous and crystalline phases clearly appeared, and the amorphous region, which can be attributed to PEa poly-RTMS, mainly exist among crystalline perovskite grains. Meanwhile, the magnified image in Fig. 2i exhibits the bonding of crystalline and amorphous phases, forming a perovskite-poly-RTMS connecting bridge between two grains. We summarize the likely role of the PEa poly-RTMS in perovskite crystallization in Fig. 2j. The strong interaction between the PEa poly-RTMS and perovskite film as deduced from FTIR and better film-forming properties inferred from SEM ensure that the PEa poly-RTMS could uniformly functionalize most perovskite crystals and be immobilized rather than getting agglomerated. As the perovskite grains grow, the partial PEa poly-RTMS continue to be distributed in the narrowing

intergranular regions among grains or grain clusters, resulting from the large ion radius of PEa poly-RTMS. Eventually, the continuous long-chained structure of PEa poly-RTMS can fill in the GBs and form three-dimensional skeleton templates to support perovskite grains, as previously reported PMMA-templated nucleation,  $  ^{43}  $  like “a thread with a needle” connecting adjacent perovskite crystals with more poly-RTMS. The unique composition of perovskite-poly-RTMS acting as a connecting bridge described here could minimize electrical decoupling or insulation between the perovskite crystals, which could inhibit the non-radiative recombination in the perovskite film.

We carried out conducting atomic force microscopy (cAFM) studies to provide spatially resolved electronic properties of the studied films. The cAFM measurements have been performed under room light condition and local current mapping of these perovskite films are illustrated in Fig. 3a-d. For the PEa-modified film in Fig. 3d, a large current is presented, compared with control and other RTMS monomer-modified films in Fig. 3a-c. The obvious change in current for the PEa-modified film indicates that the poly-RTMS incorporation could lead to an effective increment in the conductivity of the perovskite film. We conjecture that the large local current of the PEa-modified film is due to the large grain size and less horizontal GBs; on the other hand, the long-chained poly-RTMS can bridge the adjacent perovskite grains effectively, providing a smooth path for charge transfer through GBs.

The improvement of the absorption might result from the better crystallinity and the larger crystal size, which is beneficial to enhance light-harvesting efficiency, as previously reported.  $  ^{[44, 45]}  $   The charge carrier behavior in these perovskite films was investigated by steady-state photoluminescence (PL). Particularly, the PEa-modified film exhibits twofold enhancement as opposed to the control film. We further estimated the trap density of different films by space-charge limited current (SCLC) measurement with a structure of ITO/TiO $ _2 $ /perovskite/PCBM/Ag electrode. As evidenced in Fig. 3g, the dark current–voltage response includes an ohmic-response at low bias, trap-filled limited region at intermediate bias, and a trap-free SCLC region at high bias. All the trap states are completely filled in the trap-filled limited region ( $ TFL $ ). $ ^{47} $  Thus, the trap density can be calculated from the onset voltage  $ V_{TFL} $  corresponding to  $ V_{TFL} = en_t L^2 / 2\varepsilon \varepsilon_0 $ , $ ^{48} $  where  $ e $  is the elementary charge,  $ n_t $  is the trap density,  $ L $  is the thickness of the perovskite layer,  $ \varepsilon $  is the relative dielectric constant of perovskite, $ ^{49} $  and  $ \varepsilon_0 $  is the vacuum permittivity. It can be observed that the trap density is decreased from  $ 1.40 \times 10^{16} $  for control film to  $ 0.89 \times 10^{16} $ ,  $ 6.44 \times 10^{15} $ , and  $ 5.32 \times 10^{15} $  cm $ ^{-3} $  for Bu-, Ea-, and PEa-modified films, respectively, illustrating that the PEa poly-RTMS is more effective in suppressing the appearance of trap states in perovskite films. The carrier mobility can be determined by the Mott-Gurney law corresponding to the  $ J = 9\varepsilon \varepsilon_0 \mu V^2 / 8L^3 $ . $ ^{50} $  The calculated electron mobilities of control, Bu-, Ea- and PEa-modified perovskite films are 0.014, 0.058, 0.148, and  $ 0.290 $  cm $ ^2 $  V $ ^{-1} $  s $ ^{-1} $ , respectively. We also calculated hole mobilities of 0.096 , 0.126, 0.287, and  $ 0.430 $  cm $ ^2 $  V $ ^{-1} $  s $ ^{-1} $  for the control, Bu-, Ea- and PEa-modified perovskite films (Fig. S15), respectively. Compared to hole mobilities, the electron mobilities of these modified films show more obvious improvement. We have also conducted thermal admittance spectroscopy (TAS) to further evaluate the distribution in trap density of states (t-DOS). $ ^{51} $  As shown in Fig. S16, the PEa-modified device exhibits the lowest t-DOS, especially in shallow trap region compared to the control film. Because the shallow traps are mainly concentrated on the GBs, $ ^{52} $  it can be concluded that the PEa poly-RTMS effectively passivates the trap states of GBs.

The corresponding schematic of energy band alignment is illustrated in Fig. S17, and the energy band of these perovskite films were calculated from UV photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS). Fig. 4a and Table 1 present the corresponding photovoltaic parameters of optimized devices with poly-RTMS and RTMS monomers. The dependence of the device performance on the concentration of RTMS or poly-RTMS was investigated, as shown in Fig. S18–20 and Tables S1–3. Apart from efficiency, device stability was examined upon exposure to air, heat, and light conditions. The stability of different devices in dark at room temperature with   $  35 \pm 5\%  $   relative humidity (RH) in air storage were recorded, as shown in Fig. 4d. For the Bu-, Ea- and PEa-modified devices, the PCEs experienced a reduction of about 36%, 23%, and 6% after 5000 h, respectively. The stability is much inferior in control device and about merely 43% of initial efficiency can be maintained after 3600 h. In Fig. S27, the contact angle of water was dramatically increased from   $  58.33^{\circ}  $   (control film) to   $  81.54^{\circ}  $   (PEa-modified film) due to the excellent hydrophobicity nature of the PEa poly-RTMS (Fig. S28), from which we can foresee improved the moisture stability of the PEa-modified device. We tested the thermal stability of the different perovskite films by ageing the samples at   $  85^{\circ}C  $   for 300 h in the ambient air (  $  RH = 40 \pm 5\%  $  ). The photographs and corresponding UV–Vis absorption spectra of the different degraded perovskite films were tracked (Fig. S29). The PEa-modified film retained its original color even after 300 h thermal ageing, as confirmed by nearly unchanged absorption intensity. Meanwhile, as illustrated in Fig. 4e, the encapsulated PEa-modified device exhibited impressively enhanced stability

compared with the control device and retained over 92% of its initial PCE for 1200 h under continuous light soaking at 70-75 °C. The control, Bu-, and Ea-modified devices experienced reductions to about 57%, 80%, and 83%, respectively. This PEa-modified device is one of the most stable devices reported when ageing under continuous light, and thermal condition, as illustrated in Table S4. Although the perovskite GBs are susceptible to the moisture and thermal stress, which in turn triggers the rapid diffusion of various ions and other

species,  $  ^{56}  $  additives with poly-RTMS and RTMS can effectively passivate defects in perovskite to varying degrees. In particular, the modification with poly-RTMS is beneficial to the stability of PSCs in three aspects. First, the long-chained structure of the poly-RTMS could continuously be distributed along the GBs and form three-skeleton templates to support perovskite grains, increase the grain size of perovskite, and decrease the number of GBs (see Fig. 2d). Secondly, the highly coordinated perovskite-poly-RTMS composite was formed at adjacent perovskite crystals, and its bonding strength is stronger than the hydrogen bond between FAI and Bu RTMS as well as the coordination bond formed by unsaturated Pb ions and small molecular Ea RTMS (see Fig. 1d-f), which could avoid PEa poly-RTMS shedding from GBs under light or high temperature. Finally, the PEa poly-RTMS with the strongest hydrophobicity behavior (Fig. S28) and the highest decomposition temperature (Td ≈ 347 °C, Fig. S30) in the three RTMS is conducive to enhance the thermal, light, and water resistance of the corresponding perovskite film.

# Conclusions

In conclusion, the PEa poly-RTMS containing multiple chemical anchoring sites along with strong bonding stability has been designed, synthesized, and employed to improve the performance and stability of inverted PSCs for the first time. Compared to RTMS, the PEa poly-RTMS can act as three-dimensional skeleton templates to form high-quality (uniform and large grain size) perovskite films. Furthermore, the long-chained PEa poly-RTMS with chemical anchoring sites can fill with GBS to passivate the under-coordinated Pb ion defects in perovskite, and the PEa poly-RTMS with

# Synthesis of Bu and Ea RTMS

1 mol of 1-vinylimidazole and 1.5 mol of  $ \mathrm{C_4H_9Br} $  were dissolved in ethyl acetate in a round-bottomed flask, and stirred at  $ 323\mathrm{K} $  for 24 hours. Letting the reaction solution stand for several minutes, it can be seen that the solution in the flask was stratified and the lower layer was crude production of [BuVIm]Br RTMS. The crude production of [BuVIm]Br RTMS was cleaned several times by solvent and the resultant solvent was evaporated under vacuum. Then, the light yellow viscous liquid was dried for 24 hours at  $ 323\mathrm{K} $  under vacuum condition.

Next, [BuVIm]Br RTMS was reacted with  $ \mathrm{NaBF_4} $  at a molar ratio 1:1 and stirred at room temperature for 32 hours

in acetonitrile solution, obtaining a mixture of precipitated white solid and solution. The reaction product was filtered to remove white solids. The remaining solution was washed several times by solvent, and then evaporated under vacuum, and light yellow solution was obtained. Finally, the target product Bu RTMS was achieved by drying the light yellow solution for 24 hours at  $ 323\mathrm{K} $  under the vacuum condition. The Ea RTMS was synthesized according to the Fig. S1 in Supporting Information by adopting the synthetic method of Bu RTMS.

# NMR titration method

Take the example of PEa poly-RTMS, we first fixed the total concentration of the host (PEa poly-RTMS) and the guest  $ (\mathrm{Pb}^{2+}) $ , and adjusted the ratio of host and guest, we have obtained a graph of the change in chemical shift with the proportion of host. The abscissa value in corresponding to the highest point is the coordination ratio, that is, the coordination ratio of C=O (host) to  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  (guest). Secondly, the NMR titrations were conducted with solutions of a constant concentration of host and varying concentrations of guest to calculate the binding constant.[63, 64] We have drawn the Benesi-Hildebrand plot by the change value ( $ \Delta $ ) of the chemical shift of a-H in poly-RTMS versus the reciprocal of the  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  concentration, and obtained the maximum value ( $ \Delta $ max) of the chemical shift change of a-H after the all C=O groups in PEa poly-RTMS were bonded by the  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  from the intercept of the Benesi-Hildebrand plot. Finally, a Scatchard plot of the  $ p/[\mathrm{Pb}^{2+}] $  vs.  $ p $  (where  $ p $  is the  $ \Delta/\Delta $ max) shows that the binding constant of host and guest, which is the average of the slope and intercept in the Scatchard plot. DMSO-d6 is used as as locking solvent for NMR characterization.
