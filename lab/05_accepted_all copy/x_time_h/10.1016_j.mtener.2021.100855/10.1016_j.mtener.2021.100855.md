# Abstract

Methylammonium acetate (MAAc) ionic liquid was recently realized as an environment-friendly solvent enabling one-step deposition of perovskite thin films at ambient conditions with the absence of anti-solvent. The strong hydrogen bonding between the MAAc and perovskite precursor facilitates a prolonged crystal growth, but parasitically restricts mass transport and solvent escape, leading to retarded crystal nucleation and perovskite growth with unintentional defects. Herein, we propose a novel route to accelerate the nucleation via in-situ nanocrystal seeding crystallization. By incorporating excess  $ \mathrm{PbCl}_2 $  in the fabrication of perovskite thin films using MAAc as solvent,  $ \mathrm{MAPbCl}_{3 - x}\mathrm{Ac}_x $  nanocrystals are firstly generated owing to their low formation energy, which can be used as nucleus to regulate perovskite growth. High-quality perovskite thin films were obtained with simultaneously reduced defect density and shallowed trap depth, resulting in significantly enhanced  $ \mathrm{V_{OC}} $  in the corresponding solar cells. As a result, a champion device with efficiency up to  $ 21.26\% $  and improved storage stability for more than 1300 hours were achieved, which provide a feasible route to fabricate high performance perovskite solar cells.

# Introduction

Hybrid organic-inorganic perovskites (e.g.,  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbX}_3 $ , X=Cl, Br, I) have recently attracted a worldwide research interest, owing to their advancing optoelectronic properties such as tunable band gap, high extinction coefficient, high charge carrier mobility, cost-effective manufacturing process [1-4], etc., and the highest efficiency of solar cells based on perovskites (PSCs) has increased linearly from the initial  $ 3.8\% $  to  $ 25.5\% $  in the last few years [5-6]. Even though the perovskites exhibited high tolerance on defect related energy loss [7], the efficiency of PSC was still lagged far behind the thermodynamic limit because some unintentional defects were inevitable to appear in simply solution-processed multi-crystalline perovskites [8-12], which not only restricted the open-circuit voltage ( $ \mathrm{V_{OC}} $ ) of the device [13], but also directly correlated with hysteresis and stability issues [14-15]. Therefore, the trap state passivation or elimination is worthwhile and of great importance to further improve the device performance [16-22].

Crystallographic distortions in terms of element vacancies, interstitials, substitutional impurity as well as grain boundaries are the main defects in the bulk or surface of perovskite thin films, which originally occurs from the fabrication process. It was known from the crystallization dynamics, fast nucleation and prolonged crystal growth process are prerequisites for the deposition of high-quality perovskite thin films, which should advantageously contain enlarged crystal size with improved surface coverage and minimized lattice distortion with preferred crystal orientation. Many strategies have been developed to accelerate the crystal nucleation, for examples, anti-solvent dripping

[23], vacuum flashing [24], high-temperature hot-casting [25], etc., to speed up the solvent escape and reach a fast super-saturation concentration, or to induce intermediate states by generating solvent- $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  via solvent engineering [26], creating additive- $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  complex via incorporating external functional additives [27], or adding some perovskite nanocrystals to seed the perovskite growth. In the meanwhile, some other methodologies were employed to prolong the crystal growth process, for examples, using solvents with gradient boiling points [28], multi-step thermal annealing [29] or annealing at solvent or moisture atmosphere and so on, to retard the solvent exhaustion and prolong the crystal growth, or boosting the surface tension of the crystal grains by small ions doping [30] or light illumination [31] to enhance the possibility of coalescence at the expense of small grains. Therefore, perovskite crystals with grain size in the range from micrometer to millimeter can be designed.

In addition, some other efforts have been focused on defect passivation, and some functional agents have been intentionally incorporated in the perovskite preparation or post-treatment to associate with antisite defects or under-coordinated ions, for examples, squaraine molecules (SQ) with a zwitterionic structure have been used to associate with under-coordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  and passivate Pb-I antisite defects [32], iodofluorobenzene (IPFB) and Lewis bases (such as thiophene) can effectively passivate the defect states on the perovskite surface because of their interaction with insufficiently coordinated halide anions and metal cations [33], neutral sulfonic acid containing cationic quaternary ammonium salt and anionic sulfonate  $ (-\mathrm{SO}_3^-) $  was also employed to effectively passivate defect states [34]. And also, some other additives were incorporated into the perovskite as redox shuttle to oxidize  $ \mathrm{Pb}^0 $  and reduce  $ \mathrm{I}^0 $  [36], or turning the tolerant factor or dimensions to reduce the phase vibration. The aforementioned methods were favorable to design high-quality perovskites, but complicated the fabrication process with limited reproducibility. In addition, the incorporation of external organic or metallic additives might need post-treatments to remove them, or parasitically reduce the conductivity or induce stability issues. Therefore, it is highly desirable to eliminate defect states in a feasible one-step deposition method. Recently, we reported a new route to fabricate high-quality perovskite thin films by using MAAc ionic liquid as solvent instead of traditional toxic and highly coordinating solvents [37-38]. The MAAc can strongly associate with the perovskite precursor via H-bonding and the low vapor pressure of the MAAc enabled a prolonged crystal growth, and the high viscosity of the precursor solution with enhanced surface tension significantly improved the moisture stability of the solution, which facilitated the perovskite fabrication in ambient air condition with the absence of anti-solvent and low precursor solution concentration. # Results and Discussions

Lead halides, such as  $ \mathrm{PbCl}_2 $ ,  $ \mathrm{PbBr}_2 $  and  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ , have different association abilities with MAAc owing to their different electronegativity of the halide elements and different ionization potential in the solvent. Liquid state  $ ^1\mathrm{H} $  nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy was performed to investigate how the lead halides interact with the MAAc in solution. As shown in Fig. 1a, a characteristic peak at 7.97 ppm arising from the amino protons of MAAc [39] was observed in  $ ^1\mathrm{H} $  NMR spectra of the pure MAAc solution, which was shifted to 7.91, 7.42 and 7.35 ppm when the MAAc was mixed with  $ \mathrm{PbCl}_2 $ ,  $ \mathrm{PbBr}_2 $  and  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ , respectively. It was known that the shift of the  $ ^1\mathrm{H} $  NMR signal is an indication of the chemical shifts of the hydrogen bonds. Therefore, the shift of 0.62 ppm on addition of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  indicated a high association ability with the MAAc. In contrast, the  $ \mathrm{PbCl}_2 $  exhibited the weakest coordination ability with MAAc among the three lead halides, suggesting a high diffusivity of the precursor ions in solution, which is favorable for mass transport in the perovskite crystallization.

To further confirm the interaction between MAAc and lead halides, thin films were prepared by spin coating the concentrated solutions of lead halides in MAAc, and X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were carried out on the as-prepared thin films, or annealed at 50 and  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ , respectively. As clearly shown in Fig. 1b when mixing the  $ \mathrm{PbCl}_2 $  with MAAc, two sharp diffraction peaks at  $ 15.67^{\circ} $  and  $ 31.53^{\circ} $ , owing to (110) and (220) planes of  $ \mathrm{MAPbCl}_{3 - x}\mathrm{Ac}_x $  perovskite, respectively, were observed in the as-prepared and high temperature annealed samples, indicating the formation of  $ \mathrm{MAPbCl}_{3 - x}\mathrm{Ac}_x $  perovskites with a relatively low formation energy. Similarly, the  $ \mathrm{MAPbBr}_{3 - x}\mathrm{Ac}_x $

perovskite was evident by two typical diffraction peaks at  $ 15.16^{\circ} $  and  $ 30.26^{\circ} $  as shown in Fig. S1 when mixing  $ \mathrm{PbBr_2} $  with MAAc. However, in case of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ , no typical diffraction peaks for perovskite phase were observed in the as prepared and the low temperature (50 °C) annealed thin films (Fig. 1c). It was known that the MAAc is hardly to be removed at low temperature because of the low vapor pressure and the high hydrogen bonding with  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ , which retarded the perovskite formation and high temperature thermal annealing (100 °C) is demanded to overcome the energy barrier and induce  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_{3 - x}\mathrm{Ac}_x $  perovskite growth. The chemical reaction between lead halides ( $ \mathrm{PbX}_2 $ ) and MAAc can be described as the following formula:

$$
\mathrm{MAAc} + \mathrm{PbX} _ {2} \longrightarrow \mathrm{MAPbX} _ {3 - \mathrm{x}} \mathrm{Ac} _ {\mathrm{x}} (\mathrm{X} = \mathrm{Cl}, \mathrm{Br}, \mathrm{I})
$$

Therefore, by adding lead halides into the perovskite precursor when using MAAc ionic liquid as solvent, the as formed  $ \mathrm{MAPbX_{3 - x}Ac_x} $  perovskite was prone to be firstly generated because of their low formation energy, which can be used as nucleus to seed the perovskite crystallization. According to the aforementioned experimental results, the process of nanocrystal seeding perovskite growth can be speculated in Fig. 1d. In the precursor solution, the  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  is highly bonded with MAAc via hydrogen bonding. After spin-coating and prior annealing (Stage 1), the additives can associate with the solvent and generate  $ \mathrm{MAPbX_{3 - x}Ac_x} $  nanocrystals, which are prone to be formed at low temperature and can be used as nucleus to seed the perovskite growth. Upon to high temperature annealing (100 °C), as the Ac- escapes and I- enters the perovskite lattice, the cubic  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite was eventually formed on the  $ \mathrm{MAPbX_{3 - x}Ac_x} $  nucleus (Stage 2).

additional diffraction peaks other than those from pristine 3D MAPbI $ _3 $  was found, confirming the completed conversion from precursor to perovskite phase and no excess lead halides were retained in the film. Moreover, the intensity of the diffraction peak at  $ 14.2^{\circ} $  was obviously enhanced as the additives were incorporated, indicating the enhancement of preferred crystal orientation along the (110) plane. The microstructures of the corresponding perovskites films with different additives were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images. It was clearly seen in Fig. 2b-e that the grain size of the pristine perovskites is relatively small with numerous boundaries (Fig. 2b). In contrast, the grain sizes were significantly enlarged by adding a small amount of lead halides into the perovskite precursor solution. Through the crystal size distribution histogram (Fig. S3), the averaged crystal size of 170, 250, 300,  $ 330\mathrm{nm} $  were obtained for the pristine perovskite and doped with  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ ,  $ \mathrm{PbBr}_2 $  and  $ \mathrm{PbCl}_2 $ , respectively. The surface morphology was characterized by atomic force microscopy (AFM) in Fig. S4a-d. Compared with the root mean square (rms)

Journal Pre-proof

roughness of the pristine perovskite film (rms=40.85 nm), the addition of the lead halides can smoothen the surface roughness, which decreased the rms to 36.17, 31.92 and 20.59 nm with the addition of   $  PbI_{2}  $  ,   $  PbBr_{2}  $   and   $  PbCl_{2}  $  , respectively. Therefore, the addition of lead halides in the precursor is benefit for the growth of large perovskite crystals with a flat surface, which can be attributed to three synergistic effect. First, the small halide ions (  $  Br^{-}  $  ,   $  Cl^{-}  $  ) in the additives are more diffusive than   $  I^{-}  $  , promoting the mass transport in the perovskite growth [30]. Second, the halides from the additives can facilitate the crystal coalescence. Particularly, the   $  Cl^{-}  $  ions are energetically unfavored to be incorporated into the   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   perovskite crystal lattice due to the large size mismatch between chloride and iodide, and the incorporated   $  Cl^{-}  $  ions are diffusing along the grain boundaries and surface during the perovskite growth, leading to increased surface tension [31] and facilitated crystal coalescence at the expense of small crystals. In addition, the lead halides can react with MAAc and create   $  MAPbX_{3-x}Ac_{x}  $   perovskite to seed the perovskite growth. The accelerated nucleation and prolonged crystal growth facilitated the growth of high-quality perovskites.

The photoluminescence (PL) spectra (Fig. 2g) of the perovskite films deposited on glass substrates were performed to investigate the effect of the additives on the optoelectronic property of perovskite thin films, and it was found that the PL intensity of the perovskite film with additives was distinctly enhanced. Particularly, the thin film prepared with  $ \mathrm{PbCl}_2 $  as additive exhibited the strongest PL signal while the reference perovskite film showed the weakest PL signal, which was ascribed to the presence of numerous defects within the film and the photogenic carriers were captured [40]. As shown in time-

In addition, the charge recombination resistance for the device based on  $  \mathrm{PbCl}_2  $  doped perovskites was significantly higher than that on pristine perovskites as shown in Fig. S12, suggesting reduced charge recombination with the addition of  $  \mathrm{PbCl}_2  $ .

Furthermore, thermal admittance spectroscopy (TAS) measurements were performed to estimate the defect activation energy  $ (E_{a}) $  [17,20,49,50]. In general,  $ E_{a} $  can be estimated from the variation of  $ \sigma_{\mathrm{dc}} $  as a function of temperature (T) as following [51]:

$$
\sigma_ {\mathrm{dc}} = \sigma_ {o} \exp \left[ - \frac {E _ {\mathrm{a}}}{k T} \right]
$$

(5)

where  $ \sigma_{\mathrm{dc}} $  is the frequency-independent conductivity,  $ k $  is the Boltzman constant,  $ T $  is the absolute temperature. The defect activation energy ( $ E_a $ ) demonstrates approximately the depth of the trap state energy level ( $ E_T $ ) related to the valence band conduction energy level ( $ E_{\mathrm{VBM}} $ ) of the perovskite ( $ E_a = E_T - E_{\mathrm{VBM}} $ ) [52,53]. The probability of charge recombination depends on the depth of trap-state, and the space charge in the trap with a shallow level is easier to escape, while in case of high trap level the space charge is prone to fall into the trap during the migration process. Fig. S13a, b showed the temperature-dependent admittance spectra of the devices with and without  $ \mathrm{PbCl}_2 $  additives measured from 20 Hz to 20 MHz. It was calculated that the  $ E_a $  for the pristine device and  $ \mathrm{PbCl}_2 $  doped device are 0.275 and 0.218 eV (Fig. 4b, c), respectively, which means that the trap depth is shallower in  $ \mathrm{PbCl}_2 $  doped device that is

more conducive to the charge transport. Therefore, the incorporation of   $  PbCl_{2}  $   into the perovskite precursor can effectively passivate trap state by simultaneously reduce the trap-state density and shift the trap depth to a shallower one, significantly eliminating non-radiative charge recombination [54].

The carrier transport dynamics at the perovskite and   $  SnO_{2}  $   interface were further investigated by performing femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA) spectroscopy measurements, and the representative time-dependent TA spectra of   $  SnO_{2}  $   / control perovskite and   $  SnO_{2}  $   /   $  PbCl_{2}  $   doped perovskite were shown in Fig. 4d, e. A broad positive band (520 to 700 nm) from transient absorption of excited states [55] was observed and a sharp negative band peaking at   $  \sim750  $   nm was ascribed to the transient photoinduced bleaching combined edge transitions and laser-induced fluorescence (stimulated emission). By comparing the negative bleaching band peak at   $  \sim750  $   nm between the control and   $  PbCl_{2}  $   doped perovskite films, we noticed that the negative peak intensity of the latter was increased because of the reduction of defects states in the perovskite layer. It was known that the charge carriers can quickly funnel or transfer to the lowest energy position upon light exposure, corresponding to the red shift of the instantaneous bleaching peak, which can be demonstrated by   $  \Delta E\left(\Delta E=\frac{1240}{\lambda_{1}}-\frac{1240}{\lambda_{2}}\right)  $   [56,57]. As shown in Fig. 4d, e, the   $  \Delta E  $   of 21.77 and 18.35 meV was observed for the pristine and   $  PbCl_{2}  $   doped perovskite, respectively. This is consistent with the improvement of   $  V_{OC}  $   in   $  PbCl_{2}  $   doped perovskite solar cells. # Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrate a novel route to fabricate high-quality perovskite thin films by employing lead halides  $ \mathrm{PbX}_2 $  (X=Cl, Br, I) as additives when using MAAc as solvent. It is evidenced that the lead halides can associate with the MAAc and generated  $ \mathrm{MAPbX}_{3-x}\mathrm{Ac}_x $  perovskite. The as formed perovskites can be used as nucleus to seed the perovskite growth, resulting in enlarged perovskite crystal size with eliminated defect states. Among them, the incorporation of  $ \mathrm{PbCl}_2 $  exhibits the best quality because of the lowest formation energy of  $ \mathrm{MAPbCl}_{3-x}\mathrm{Ac}_x $  and the most diffusivity of the  $ \mathrm{Cl^-} $  ions.

Journal Pre-proof

Furthermore, the characterization of TAS, EIS, space-charge-limited current and fs-TA spectrum shows that the addition of   $  PbCl_{2}  $   in the perovskite can reduce trap induced non-radiative recombination and significantly improve carrier lifetime, which is benefit to the enhancement of   $  V_{OC}  $   in the corresponding solar cells. Accordingly, the efficiency was significantly improved from 17.80 to 21.26% with remarkable long-term stability.

The hole transport layer was prepared by spin coating the as-prepared hole transport material precursor at 3000 rpm for 30 s, which was prepared by dissolving  $ 73.2\mathrm{mg} $  of spiro-OMeTAD,  $ 17.6~\mu \mathrm{L} $  of Li-TFSI ( $ 520\mathrm{mg/mL} $  in acetonitrile), and  $ 28.8~\mu \mathrm{L} $  of TBP in  $ 1\mathrm{mL} $  of CB. Eventually, an Ag electrode is deposited by vaporizing  $ 80\mathrm{nm} $ -thick of Ag in a high vacuum chamber.
