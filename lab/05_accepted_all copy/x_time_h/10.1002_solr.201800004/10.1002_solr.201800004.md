# Ligand-Free, Highly Dispersed   $  NiO_{x}  $   Nanocrystal for Efficient, Stable, Low-Temperature Processable Perovskite Solar Cells

Jinjin He, Enbing Bi, Wentao Tang, Yanbo Wang, Zhongmin Zhou, Xudong Yang, Han Chen,* and Liyuan Han*

Inverted configuration perovskite solar cells containing a   $  NiO_{x}  $   layer fabricated by using a low-temperature process have yielded highly efficient, flexible devices. However, the instability of these devices has limited their commercial application. Here, we report high-quality, ligand-free   $  NiO_{x}  $   nanocrystals, prepared directly through a facile organic solvent method, that form a stable ink when dispersed in an ethanol solvent. Rigid and flexible devices containing a   $  NiO_{x}  $   layer (active area,   $  1.02\ cm^{2}  $  ) fabricated with this ink at low-temperature achieved efficiencies of 18.49 and 15.89%, respectively. In addition, the devices retain 90% of their initial performance at 500 h in a damp-heat test (  $  85\ ^{\circ}C  $   and 85% relative humidity) due to there being less hydroxyl functional groups and   $  H_{2}O  $   molecules on the   $  NiO_{x}  $   surface, which degrade perovskite. Thus, we are able to successfully synthesize ligand-free   $  NiO_{x}  $   for the low-temperature production of a high-performance, stable perovskite solar cell.

With the current technology, highly efficient, stable PSCs with a   $  NiO_{x}  $   layer fabricated at high temperature can be easily produced.  $  ^{[23-25]}  $   However, producing PSCs at low temperatures by using a solution-processed   $  NiO_{x}  $   layer is very attractive.  $  ^{[26-28]}  $   Although the efficiencies of PSCs produced at low temperature remain poor compared with those of PSCs produced at high

temperature,  $  ^{[25,27]}  $  much effort has gone into developing methods to improve the quality of solution-processed  $  NiO_{x}  $  layers and PSC performance. For example, Shao et al. have synthesized  $  NiO_{x}  $  nanoparticles in water (W-NiO  $  _{x}  $ ) and prepared a W-NiO  $  _{x}  $  layer by using a low-temperature solution process to produce a rigid PSC with a PCE of 16.47%.  $  ^{[29]}  $  Choy et al. have fabricated a W-NiO  $  _{x}  $  film by using the same method to produce a high-performance, flexible PSC with a PCE of 14.5%.  $  ^{[30]}  $  Jaramillo et al. have elucidated the mechanism underlying the stabilization of solution-processed W-NiO  $  _{x}  $  nanoparticles and have used that knowledge to produce a rigid PSC with a PCE of 16.6% and null hysteresis.  $  ^{[31]}  $  Recently, Jen et al. developed a benzoic acid-derivative self-assembled monolayer interfacial modification method that was used to produce rigid and flexible, W-NiO  $  _{x}  $ -based, inverted PSCs with a PCE of 18.4 and 16.2%, respectively.  $  ^{[32]}  $  Unfortunately, the stability of PSCs made by using low-temperature W-NiO  $  _{x}  $  remains inferior to that of devices fabricated with high-temperature-processed charge-transporting layers.  $  ^{[23,32]}  $  One reason for this is that the W-NiO  $  _{x}  $  surface tends to adsorb ligands (e.g., water molecules, residual nitrate, hydroxide, and sodium ions) during the preparation process,  $  ^{[31]}  $  which reduces the stability of the devices. These ligands are difficult to remove from low-temperature processes. Therefore, ligand-free NiO  $  _{x}  $  nanoparticles are needed to improve the long-term stability of low-temperature-processed PSCs.

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted much attention as next-generation photovoltaic devices because of their high power conversion efficiency (PCE) and low fabrication cost.  $  ^{[1-5]}  $   Several structures for these devices have been proposed, with the two most typical being the conventional n-i-p and the inverted p-i-n configurations.  $  ^{[6,7]}  $   Although PSCs with a conventional configuration consisting of an n-type   $  TiO_{2}  $   semiconductor capped with a perovskite absorber and a hole-transporting layer have the highest efficiencies (above 22%).  $  ^{[8]}  $   inverted planar PSCs are attractive because of their simple fabrication process, high stability, and low hysteresis.  $  ^{[9,10]}  $   Inverted PSCs usually comprise layers of poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene): poly(styrenesulfonic acid) (PEDOT:PSS), perovskite, and phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester

(PCBM).  $  ^{[11]}  $   However, the high acidity of PEDOT:PSS may affect device stability.  $  ^{[12,13]}  $   Several inorganic semiconductors such as CuSCN,  $  ^{[14]}  $     $  NiO_{x}  $  ,  $  ^{[15]}  $     $  Cu_{2}O  $  ,  $  ^{[16]}  $     $  VO_{x}  $  ,  $  ^{[17]}  $   and   $  WO_{3}  $    $  ^{[18]}  $   have been suggested as replacements for PEDOT:PSS. Among them,   $  NiO_{x}  $   is a low-cost material with superior thermal and chemical stability. In addition,   $  NiO_{x}  $   enables the production of perovskite solar cells with a high   $  V_{oc}  $   because of its suitable work function and high conduction band edge position.  $  ^{[19-22]}  $

In the present study, we demonstrate a facile organic solvent method to synthesize high-quality, ligand-free   $  NiO_{x}  $   nanocrystals in ethanol (E-  $  NiO_{x}  $  ). When formulated as a nanocrystal ethanol ink, these nanocrystals could be spin-coated onto a substrate to form a smooth, compact E-  $  NiO_{x}  $   film that had good hole extraction capability. An inverted planar perovskite device (active area, 1.02 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ) with E-  $  NiO_{x}  $   as the hole-transporting material exhibited a PCE of 18.49 and 15.89% on a rigid and flexible substrate, respectively. We found that few hydroxyl groups and   $  H_{2}O  $   molecules were absorbed on the E-  $  NiO_{x}  $   nanocrystal surface, which produced a device with high stability; 90% of the initial performance was retained at 500 h in a damp-heat test conducted at 85 °C and 85% relative humidity.

Figure 1 shows a schematic of the process we used to prepare the E-NiO $ _{x} $  nanocrystal. In brief, the E-NiO $ _{x} $  nanocrystals were synthesized by using Ni(NO $ _{3} $ ) $ _{2} $ ·6H $ _{2} $ O and H $ _{2} $ C $ _{2} $ O $ _{4} $ ·2H $ _{2} $ O in ethanol to produce NiC $ _{2} $ O $ _{4} $ ·2H $ _{2} $ O, which was then thermally decomposed to produce E-NiO $ _{x} $ . [33] Transmission electron microscopy analysis of the E-NiO $ _{x} $  nanocrystals revealed that they existed as spherical nanocrystals of diameter 3–5 nm (Figure 2a). A high-resolution transmission electron microscopy imaging of a single nanocrystal (Figure 2a, inset) revealed a periodic lattice structure with an interval of 0.24 nm assigned to the (111) plane, which confirmed the crystalline nature of the E-NiO $ _{x} $  nanocrystal. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis revealed an XRD pattern indexed to the cubic phase (JCPDS 22-1189) (Figure 2b).[34] The sharp, intense peaks in the XRD pattern indicated that E-NiO $ _{x} $  had good crystallinity. Because the chemical composition of the surface of the NiO $ _{x} $  film strongly influences the interfacial energy level alignment with active layers as well as the photovoltaic performance of the final device, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy was used to examine the surface composition of the E-NiO $ _{x} $  nanocrystal (Figure 2c). The X-ray photoelectron spectrum for E-NiO $ _{x} $  in the binding energy range of the Ni 2p $ _{3/2} $  signal could be separated into three distinct peaks: one at 853.8 eV attributed to Ni $ ^{2+} $ , which is associated with the Ni–O octahedral bonding of cubic rock salt NiO[30]; one at 855.7 eV attributed to vacancy-induced Ni $ ^{3+} $  ion or Ni $ _{2} $ O $ _{3} $ ; and a broad peak at 861.1 eV attributed to a shake-up process.[31] The corresponding O 1s spectrum contained two major peaks at 529.3 and 531.0 eV, which were assigned to O bonded within a regular oxide crystal (O $ ^{2-} $ ) and oxygen atoms in positions adjacent to Ni vacancies (O (def)) within the oxide structure, respectively (Figure 2d).[35] A Fourier transform-IR analysis was also performed to examine the chemical groups at the surface of the nanocrystal (Figure 2e). No obvious peaks were observed in the spectrum, an indication that the surface of the E-NiO $ _{x} $  nanocrystal was ligand-free. In contrast, signals at 3448 cm $ ^{-1} $  (-OH), 1630 cm $ ^{-1} $  (H $ _{2} $ O), and 1381 cm $ ^{-1} $  (NO $ _{3}^{-} $ )

were detected in the spectrum for W-NiO $ _{x} $ , an indication that there were water molecules, hydroxyl groups, and other functional ligands on the surface of W-NiO $ _{x} $  nanoparticle. We found that these functional ligands allowed the W-NiO $ _{x} $  powder to produce a stable dispersion in deionized water without the addition of a surfactant or dispersant (Figure S1, Supporting Information). $ ^{[31]} $  When the annealing temperature was increased to 400  $ ^{\circ} $ C to remove surface ligands, the ability of W-NiO $ _{x} $  powder to disperse in water was markedly reduced. In addition, the dispersion of E-NiO $ _{x} $  nanocrystals was observed to remain homogeneous in the solvents examined (ethanol, isopropanol, and water) even after 1 week, whereas W-NiO $ _{x} $  was stable only in water. This was because ethanol molecules chemisorbed on the surface of the colloidal precipitation provided steric repulsion that prevented particle–particle interactions from occurring (Figure 1). $ ^{[36,37]} $  The possibility of chemical bonds forming between particles and inhibiting the formation of nanoparticle agglomerates was thereby reduced. We also found that the weakly agglomerated nanocrystals disintegrated to primary particles, or at least to much smaller agglomerates, when subjected to light mechanical forces such as those produced by ultrasonication. $ ^{[38]} $

To examine the use of E-NiO $ _{x} $  film as a hole transporting layer, an E-NiO $ _{x} $  film was prepared on an indium tin oxide (ITO) substrate by means of spin-coating. Scanning electron microscopy (Figure 3a) and atomic force microscopy (Figure 3b) revealed that the E-NiO $ _{x} $  nanocrystal completely covered the surface of the substrate and that the surface was smooth and compact (roughness, 5.3 nm). In an attempt to further reveal the chemical groups at the surface of NiO $ _{x} $  film, the regained nanocrystals which obtained via heating the corresponding E-NiO $ _{x} $  and W-NiO $ _{x} $  ink droplets at 120 °C for 1 h on the substrates were determined by FT-IR spectrometry (Figure S2, Supporting Information). The peaks associated with E/W-NiO $ _{x} $  films were similar to the pristine E/W-NiO $ _{x} $  nanocrystals, respectively (Figure 1f), indicating the subsequent process did not change the surface properties of the E/W-NiO $ _{x} $  nanocrystals. The quality of perovskite films on E-NiO $ _{x} $  and W-NiO $ _{x} $  substrates were investigated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The average value of perovskite crystal grains on E-NiO $ _{x} $  HTL which is slightly larger than those of the perovskite film on W-NiO $ _{x} $  HTL (350 vs. 300 nm) (compare Figure S3a and Figure S3b, Supporting Information). Moreover, stronger quenching of the steady-state photoluminescence was observed for E-NiO $ _{x} $ /perovskite film compared with W-NiO $ _{x} $ /perovskite film (Figure S4, Supporting Information), an indication that there was more effective charge extraction by the E-NiO $ _{x} $  hole-transporting layer from the perovskite active layer.

We next examined the performance of PSCs containing E-NiO $ _{x} $  as the hole-transporting layer. A cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy image of the device is shown in Figure 5a; the boundaries between the different layers are clearly visible. Figure 5b illustrates the energy band alignment in the device. Here, the valence band edge of the E-NiO $ _{x} $  film was determined from the UV photoelectron spectroscopy spectrum shown in Figure S6, Supporting Information. The valence band edge (-5.28 eV) of E-NiO $ _{x} $  matches well with the valence band edge of MAPbI $ _{3} $  (-5.4 eV), which facilitates hole transfer from the perovskite to E-NiO $ _{x} $ . The effect of the thickness of the E-NiO $ _{x} $  film on device performance was studied. E-NiO $ _{x} $  film cast from 25 mg mL $ ^{-1} $  of E-NiO $ _{x} $  nanocrystal ink produced devices with the

best performance (Figure S7 and Table S1, Supporting Information). The performance of optimized W/E-NiO $ _{x} $ -based PSCs with active areas of  $ \approx $ 1.02 cm $ ^{2} $  is shown in Figure 5c, together with the corresponding photovoltaic parameters. The W-NiO $ _{x} $ -based device yielded a PCE of 17.02% (short-circuit current density [ $ J_{sc} $ ], 21.13 mA cm $ ^{-2} $ ; open-circuit voltage [ $ V_{oc} $ ], 1.06 V; fill factor [FF], 0.76), whereas the E-NiO $ _{x} $ -based device yielded a PCE of 18.49% ( $ J_{sc} $ , 21.88 mA cm $ ^{-2} $ ;  $ V_{oc} $ , 1.07 V; FF, 0.79), exhibiting a high efficiency for a large-active-area, low-temperature processed, inverted-structure PSC. The improvement in the PCE of the E-NiO $ _{x} $ -based device was mainly a result of the increased  $ J_{sc} $  and FF, which was mainly due to the higher transparency and better conductivity of E-NiO $ _{x} $  compared with W-NiO $ _{x} $  (Figure S8, Supporting Information). Thus, the better conductivity of the charge transport layer reduced the transfer resistance and recombination in the charge transport layer, facilitating charge transfer and improving the  $ J_{sc} $  and FF of the device. Furthermore, the integrated photocurrent from the incident photon-to-current conversion efficiency was 21.89 mA cm $ ^{-2} $ , which is close to that from the  $ I-V $  measurement (Figure S9, Supporting Information). Also, the E-NiO $ _{x} $ -based device exhibited negligible hysteresis (Figure S10, Supporting Information). We also fabricated a batch of 50 E-NiO $ _{x} $ -based devices with excellent reproducibility, as demonstrated by the narrow statistical distribution of the photovoltaic metrics (Figure S11, Supporting Information). To demonstrate the viability of a W/E-NiO $ _{x} $  underlayer for flexible optoelectronic applications, we fabricated a PSC on a flexible polyethylene naphthalate/indium tin oxide substrate (Figure 5d). Finally, we examined the long-term stability of the PSCs to further elucidate the influence of surface ligands on stability. To allow comparison, the stability tests were conducted under the same conditions used in the thermal aging test (temperature, 85°C; relative humidity, 85%). The degradation trends of normalized electrical parameters are shown in Figure 6. The normalized PCE of the E-NiO  $  _{x}  $  -based PSC retained 90% of its initial value at 500 h, whereas that of the W-NiO  $  _{x}  $  -based PSC was reduced to almost 40% of its initial value, a clear demonstration that the E-NiO  $  _{x}  $  -based PSC had a much better stability than the W-NiO  $  _{x}  $  -based PSC.

Here, we report the synthesis of high-quality nanocrystalline   $  E-NiO_{x}  $   with good hole-extraction capability through a facile organic solvent method. We found that the surface of the   $  E-NiO_{x}  $   adsorbed few hydroxyl groups or water molecules, and that this property played a key role in increasing the stability of PSCs containing   $  E-NiO_{x}  $   as a hole-transfer layer. As a result, a PCE of 18.49 and 15.89% for rigid and flexible PSCs (active area,   $  1.02\ cm^{2}  $  ), respectively, were obtained. More importantly, these devices showed negligible degradation; they retained 90% of their initial performance at 500 h in a damp-heat test (temperature,   $  85\ ^{\circ}C  $  ; relative humidity, 85%).

# Experimental Section

Materials: All chemicals were used as received. Ni-  $  (\mathrm{NO}_{3})_{2}\cdot6\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O}  $  (  $  \geq98\%  $  ),   $  C_{2}H_{2}O_{4}\cdot2H_{2}O  $  (  $  \geq99.5\%  $  ), and NaOH (  $  \geq96\%  $  ) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. PbI  $  _{2}  $  (99%), N,N-dimethylformamide (anhydrous), dimethyl sulfoxide (anhydrous), titanium isopropoxide (99.99%), and niobium ethoxide (99.95%) were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich. Methylammonium iodide (MAI, 98%) was obtained from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co. Ltd.

Synthesis of W-NiO $ _x $  and E-NiO $ _x $  Nanoparticles: W-NiO $ _x $  nanoparticles were synthesized by using a chemical precipitation method. $ ^{[30]} $  In brief, Ni(NO $ _3 $ ) $ _2 $ ·6H $ _2 $ O (0.5 mol) was dissolved in 100 mL of deionized water under magnetic stirring until a dark-green solution was obtained. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 10 by dropwise addition of NaOH solution (10 mol L $ ^{-1} $ ). After stirring for 10 min and drying at 80 °C overnight, the obtained precipitate was washed twice with deionized water. Finally, the precipitate was calcined at 270 °C for 2 h to afford dark-black W-NiO $ _x $  nanoparticles. The nanoparticles were dispersed in deionized water to form a stable colloidal ink (concentration, 20 mg mL $ ^{-1} $ ).

E-NiO $ _{x} $  nanocrystals were synthesized by using an organic solvent method. $ ^{[33]} $  In brief, Ni(NO $ _{3} $ ) $ _{2} $ ·6H $ _{2} $ O was dissolved in ethanol to a concentration of 0.2 M. Then, the same concentration of NiC $ _{2} $ O $ _{4} $ ·2H $ _{2} $ O solution was slowly added as a precipitator under magnetic stirring until a turbid light-blue solution was obtained. Device Fabrication: Devices were made on prepatterned indium tin oxide/glass or indium tin oxide/polyethylene naphthalate substrate cleaned by sonication in deionized water, ethanol, and then acetone for 15 min each. The cleaned substrates were treated with oxygen plasma for 10 min. Perovskite precursor solution (90 μL) comprising 1.3 M PbI $ _{2} $  and CH $ _{3} $ NH $ _{3} $ I (1:1/n:n): in N,N-dimethylformamide and dimethyl sulfoxide (4:1/v:v) was spread on the W-NiO $ _{x} $  or E-NiO $ _{x} $  film by using a consecutive two-step spin-coating process at 1000 rpm and then 5000 rpm for 12 and 30 s, respectively. A PCBM film was then sequentially deposited by spin-coating 20 mg mL $ ^{-1} $  PCBM/chlorobenzene solution at 1200 rpm for 30 s. After the film was dried at 70 °C for 10 min, 150 μL of a H $ _{2} $ O/methanol mixed solution (1:99 volume ratio) was slowly drop-cast at 6000 rpm for 30 s. The film was dried at 70 °C for 30 min to promote the formation of TiO $ _{x} $ . Finally, a 100-nm-thick layer of Ag was thermally evaporated on top of the device to form the back contact; the active area of this electrode was

fixed at  $ 1.02\mathrm{cm}^2 $ . The device was sealed with a cavity glass coated with UV glue that was solidified by exposure to UV light at  $ 300\mathrm{W} $  for  $ 15\mathrm{s} $ .
