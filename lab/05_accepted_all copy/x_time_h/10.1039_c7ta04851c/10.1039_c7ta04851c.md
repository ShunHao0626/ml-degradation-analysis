# Dual function of interface passivation and n-doping using 2,6-dimethoxypyridine for enhanced reproducibility and performance of planar perovskite solar cells

Youyu Jiang  $  ^{a,b}  $  , Jing Li  $  ^{a}  $  , Sixing Xiong  $  ^{a}  $  , Fangyuan Jiang  $  ^{a}  $  , Tiefeng Liu  $  ^{a}  $  , Fei Qin  $  ^{a}  $  , Lin Hu  $  ^{a}  $  , and Yinhua Zhou  $  ^{a,b,*}  $

Abstract: Reproducibility of high-performance perovskite solar cells (PVSCs) remains a major obstacle. Herein, for the first time, we report the use of 2,6-dimethoxypyridine (2,6-Py) for interface chemistry engineering to fabricate reproducible high-efficient planar perovskite solar cells. The 2,6-Py serves as dual functions: (1) as a Lewis base enabling surface passivation of the Lewis acid traps (e.g., under-coordinated Pb ions) without corroding the perovskite; (2) as a chemical dopant of [6,6]-phenyl- $ \mathrm{C}_{61} $ -butyric acid methyl ester ( $ \mathrm{PC}_{61}\mathrm{BM} $ ) to improve its conductivity and mobility for efficient electron extraction and transport. Thus, through both the surface passivation of the perovskite layer and the doping of electron transporting layer with 2,6-Py, the resulted  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -based planar solar cells outperform the untreated devices with power conversion efficiency (PCE) significantly improved from  $ 15.53\% $  to  $ 19.41\% $ . The device with the dual-function treatment yields effectively improved reproducibility with narrow PCE distribution that around  $ 90\% $  of the statistic devices afford a PCE over  $ 17.50\% $  (about  $ 90\% $  of its champion PCE), and also displays enhanced air stability that maintain nearly  $ 80\% $  of their initial PCEs after  $ 200\mathrm{h} $  in ambient air without any encapsulation.

Keywords: interface chemistry engineering, reproducibility, Lewis base, under-coordinated ion traps, dopant

# 1. Introduction

Solution-processable organic-inorganic hybrid perovskites have currently evolved as a revolutionary material system promising for versatile optoelectronic applications,  $  ^{1-4}  $  such as solar cells, light-emitting diodes and photodetectors, etc. Perovskite solar cells (PVSCs) have rapidly achieved a competitive performance level with a certified PCE value over 22%,  $  ^{5-9}  $  which is approaching that of the commercial inorganic solar cells. However, the issue of poor reproducibility of high-performance PVSCs remains a major obstacle. Besides the crystal growth of perovskite films via solution processing and the fabrication atmosphere,  $  ^{10-12}  $  the difficulty of controlling trap states on the perovskite surface could be another reason for the reproducibility. The trap states lead to charge recombination and deteriorate the device performance. One important origin of such surface defects is the under-coordinated ions (including the halide ions and the metal ions) that have been confirmed by both the theoretical and experimental investigations,  $  ^{13-15}  $  and also observed for other ionic semiconductors (e.g., GaAs).  $  ^{16}  $  During the annealing process (especially the long-time and high-temperature annealing) of the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  films, the halide and/or organic ions can be partially lost from the crystal mainly due to the low thermal stability of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $ , thereby resulting in the under-coordinated Pb atoms with a net positive charge at the crystal surface.  $  ^{17, 18}  $  Additionally, the under-coordinated ions (e.g., the metal ions) in the perovskite would be unstable when exposed to air and/or moisture,  $  ^{19}  $  which is also adverse to device reproducibility and long-term stability.

(chemical structure in Fig. 1a) was chosen as a Lewis base with electron-rich function group N and O, which totally possess five lone pairs of electrons. It can interact with the perovskite via a coordinate bonding (evidenced by NMR results, Fig. 1c), thus providing accessibility to passivate the Lewis acid trap states of under-coordinated Pb ions. In contrast to the previous reported Lewis base pyridine, 2,6-Py is safer to the perovskite. It doesn't corrode the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite films. On the other hand, the electron-donating 2,6-Py is capable of serving as n-dopant of electron-acceptor  $ \mathrm{PC}_{61}\mathrm{BM} $ , leading to an increase in the conductivity by two orders of magnitude up to  $ 10^{-5}~\mathrm{S~cm}^{-1} $  (Fig. 1d). # 2. Results and discussion

To confirm the chemical interaction between 2,6-Py and the perovskite, we firstly performed the liquid state  $ ^{1} $ H NMR measurements. The proton NMR spectra of the three samples were compared in Fig. 1c: deuterated DMF solution with 2,6-Py, with MAPbI $ _{3} $  and with MAPbI $ _{3} $ +2,6-Py (denoted as treated MAPbI $ _{3} $ ). Herein, the MAPbI $ _{3} $  is comprised of CH $ _{3} $ NH $ _{3} $ I and PbI $ _{2} $  with a fixed molar ratio of 1.3:1.3 in 1mL deuterated DMF. The neat MAPbI $ _{3} $  solution shows two major proton peaks with chemical shifts of 7.96 ppm and 2.72 ppm, which is attributed to proton signals of the NH $ _{3} $  and CH $ _{3} $  groups, respectively. With the addition of 2,6-Py into the MAPbI $ _{3} $ , both the signal peaks of NH $ _{3} $  and CH $ _{3} $  downfield-shifted by 0.12 ppm and 0.10 ppm, locating at 8.08 ppm and 2.82 ppm, respectively, and the peak of NH $ _{3} $  became broader. Meanwhile, all proton signals of the -OCH $ _{3} $  group and aromatic ring of 2,6-Py in the treated MAPbI $ _{3} $  shifted toward upfield when compared to those in neat 2,6-Py solution. Additionally, the proton ratios (calculated from the area ratio of the integrated intensities of proton peaks) of CH $ _{3} $ :NH $ _{3} $  and OCH $ _{3} $ :aromatic protons (“7”:“3”+“4+5” in Fig. 1c) in the treated MAPbI $ _{3} $  sample are in accordance with those in the neat samples. Thus, the abovementioned proton chemical shifts in the treated MAPbI $ _{3} $  sample give strong evidence of the presence of the interaction between 2,6-Py and MAPbI $ _{3} $ . Hydrogen bond between CH $ _{3} $ NH $ _{2}^{+} $  and O was previously described to induce change of chemical shift of NH $ _{3} $ . $ ^{22} $  However, this occurrence can be ruled out since its effect on chemical shift of proton peak of NH $ _{3} $  is in the opposite direction to the shift we observe here. Generally, the interaction between a Lewis acid and a Lewis base is a coordinate bond.

To elucidate the passivation effects of 2,6-Py on perovskite surface traps, we applied the steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved PL (TRPL) decay experiments of both pristine and treated MAPbI $ _3 $  films. MAPbI $ _3 $  films here were prepared using a two-step sequential solution deposition approach (see Experimental section). The passivation treatment was performed through spin-coating the Lewis base 2,6-Py onto the perovskite surface (Fig. 1a). Compared with the PL spectra of pristine MAPbI $ _3 $  film (Fig. 2a), the 2,6-Py-treatment increases the PL intensity to about twice of the MAPbI $ _3 $  films. To ensure the accuracy of the comparison, the same MAPbI $ _3 $  film was used for these measurements with and without 2,6-Py treatment. That means the increased PL intensity is not related with the crystal quality of the perovskite, but mainly ascribed to the reduced surface traps, which suggests effective suppression of the recombination of free carriers. $ ^{17,29} $  As a direct evidence of the reduction of charge recombination, the carrier life time determined from a single exponential fits to the PL decays is significantly improved from  $ 69.7 \pm 2.6 $  ns for pristine film to  $ 296.0 \pm 3.5 $  ns for treated MAPbI $ _3 $  film (Fig. 2b). Thus, 2,6-py can effectively passivate the under-coordinated Pb $ ^{2+} $  traps as described in Fig. 2c: the nitrogen atom of 2,6-Py can donate its lone pair electrons to the Pb atom and form Lewis adducts, thus passivating the trap states.

Instability issues remain a key challenge to the commercialization of perovskite solar cells. In our study, we investigated the photo-stability of the perovskite solar cells by simulating the normal working conditions of a solar cell in daily life. The solar cells were working at a constant voltage bias (the maximum power point of  $  J-V  $  curve,  $  V_{mpp}  $ ) under continuous AM 1.5 light illumination. It is found that the steady output PCEs of high-efficient p-i-n planar PVSCs usually decay after a few hundred seconds illumination, $ ^{41-43} $  implying a bad photo-stability. Interestingly, after around a 2 hours test in the N $ _2 $ -filled glove box, the (2,6-Py)/PC $ _{61} $ BM:2,6-Py-based devices still show a stable steady output PCE of about 19.10% and even an observed increased trend of PCE (Fig. 5c). In comparison, the control devices also exhibit a stable PCE of 14.73% but with a slight decrease. Those devices were then stored under ambient conditions (25 °C, 40% relative humidity) to investigate their environmental stability. The evolution of their PCEs as a function of storage time was shown in Fig. 5d and Fig. S8. The (2,6-Py)/PC $ _{61} $ BM:2,6-Py-based devices have a much better ambient stability that can maintain about 80% of their initial PCEs after 200 h without any encapsulation, while only 60% of their initial PCEs was observed for control devices. # 3. Conclusion

In summary, we have demonstrated the dual function of the 2,6-dimethoxypyridine for surface passivation and in the meantime as a dopant of  $ \mathrm{PC}_{61}\mathrm{BM} $  to fabricate highly-reproducible and high-efficiency planar perovskite solar cells. The simple interface engineering approach enables efficient passivation of the undercoordinated-ion surface traps of the perovskite, renders higher conductivity of the  $ \mathrm{PC}_{61}\mathrm{BM} $ , and improves the charge extraction. As a result, the best-performing device based on (2,6-Py)/ $ \mathrm{PC}_{61}\mathrm{BM}:2,6 $ -Py achieved a highest PCE up to  $ 19.41\% $ , much higher than  $ 15.53\% $  of the control devices without any treatment. Even more encouraging is the improved device reproducibility with narrower PCE distributions that most of the statistical efficiencies are greater than  $ 17.5\% $ . Therefore, the new, low-cost and effective 2,6-dimethoxypyridine can be widely used for realizing highly reproducible and efficient perovskite solar cells.

# 4. Experimental section

# 4.1 Perovskite device fabrication.

Methylamine iodide (MAI) and Lead (II) iodide (PbI $ _2 $ ) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. [6,6]-phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PC $ _{61} $ BM) was purchased from Lumitech. All chemicals were used as received with out further purification. ITO-coated glass was sequentially cleaned with DI water, acetone and isopropyl alcohol, followed by the oxygen plasma treatment for 5 min. For NiO $ _x $  layer, nickel acetate precursors (nickel acetate tetrahydrate in monoethanolamine and methoxyethanol solution with weight percentages of 3%) were spin-coating on the ITO substrates at 3000 rpm for 30 s, then annealed on a hot plate at 150 °C for 10 min, and 350 °C for 20 min in air, followed by oxygen plasma treatment for 4 min before further use. After depositing the hole transporting layer, the MAPbI $ _3 $  absorber layer was then deposited by a consecutive two-step spin-coating method; first, 1.3 M PbI $ _2 $  and 0.3 M MAI in anhydrous dimethylformamide (DMF) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (9:1, v/v) was spin-coated onto the ITO/NiO $ _x $  substrates at 6000 rpm for 15 s, then the speed was decreased to 3,700 rpm, and the MAI solution (35 mg mL $ ^{-1} $  in isopropanol) was dripped on the spinning substrates after the start of 3700 rpm. Then, the films were dried on a hot plate at 100 °C for 30 min. Afterward, the PC $ _{61} $ BM solution (20 mg mL $ ^{-1} $  in chlorobenzene) with different doping ratios of 2,6-Py was then deposited on top of the formed perovskite layers. For 2,6-Py surface treatment, the 2,6-Py diluted solution in chlorobenzene (1:100, v/v) was directly spin-coated onto the perovskite crystals at 2000 rpm for 60s. Subsequently, a thin layer (5 nm) of PEI in isopropanol (0.1 wt.%) was spin-

Liquid state  $ ^{1} $ H NMR spectra of the perovskite samples were performed on a Bruker AV400-400 MHz spectrometer in C $ _{3} $ D $ _{7} $ NO. The absorption spectra of the doped PC $ _{61} $ BM films were taken by UV-vis-NIR spectrophotometer (UV-3600, Shimadzu). The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra were measured at room temperature by a LabRAM HR800 Roman Microscope with a 150 W Xe lamp as an excitation source at 450 nm. TRPL decay spectra were carried out using a Delta Flex Fluorescence Lifetime System monitored at 770 nm. AFM morphology of the films was measured through tapping mode on Shimadzu SPM9700 at ambient conditions. The electron mobility of doped PC $ _{61} $ BM films were tested in electron-only device of ITO/ZnO (20 nm)/PC $ _{61} $ BM or doped PC $ _{61} $ BM/Ca (20 nm)/Al (80 nm) by recording dark current-voltage through applying sufficient voltage to form the space charge limited (SCLC) current. The SCLC is described by the equation of  $ J = 9\varepsilon_{r}\varepsilon_{0}\mu V^{2}/8L^{3} $ , Where  $ \varepsilon_{r} $  is the dielectric constant of the PC $ _{61} $ BM (typical value of 3),  $ \varepsilon_{0} $  is the permittivity of free charge,  $ \mu $  is the carrier mobility,  $ V $  is the voltage drop across the device ( $ V = V_{appl} - V_{rs} - V_{bi} $ ,  $ V_{appl} $  is the applied voltage,  $ V_{rs} $  is from contact and series resistance,  $ V_{bi} $  is from built-in voltage),  $ L $  is the thickness of the film.
