# Reducing the Surface Reactivity of Alkyl Ammonium Passivation Molecules Enables Highly Efficient Perovskite Solar Cells

Lingfang Zheng, Lina Shen, Zheng Fang, Peiquan Song, Wanjia Tian, Jingfu Chen, Kaikai Liu, Yujie Luo, Peng Xu, Jinxin Yang, Chengbo Tian, Liqiang Xie,* and Zhanhua Wei*

The non-radiative recombination at the interfaces of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) is a crucial issue that limits the efficiency and stability of the devices. State-of-the-art surface passivation strategies usually utilize alkyl ammonium halides to suppress the non-radiative recombination of PSCs, but their high surface reactivity leads to the transformation into 2D perovskites under working conditions, limiting the passivation effect and the charge transport of PSCs. Herein, a non-halide ionic salt 1-naphthylmethylammonium formate (NMACOOH) is synthesized for surface passivation of perovskite films. In contrast to the traditional 1-naphthylmethylammonium iodide, NMACOOH treatment hinders the formation of 2D perovskite and forms a thermally stable  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ -NMACOOH adduct on the perovskite surface. Surface characterization reveals that  $ \mathrm{NMA}^+ $  can passivate the cation vacancies of the 3D perovskite while  $ \mathrm{HCOO}^- $  passivates the metallic  $ \mathrm{Pb}^0 $  and halide-vacancy defects. Therefore, the non-radiative recombination of PSCs is dramatically suppressed and a high open-circuit voltage of  $ 1.19\mathrm{V} $  is obtained. Finally, PSCs with high efficiency of  $ 24.75\% $  and improved long-term stability ( $ 98\% $  of the initial efficiency after  $ 1800\mathrm{-h} $  storage) are obtained. Moreover, the NMACOOH-passivated devices also show robust operational stability, retaining  $ 83\% $  of the initial efficiency after working for  $ 658\mathrm{h} $  under continuous one-sun illumination.

However, due to the high reactivity of PEAI, the salt state of PEAI on the perovskite surface is thermally unstable, which can be easily converted to 2D perovskite. It was reported that tailoring the structure of the bulky cation can reduce the reactivity of the alkyl ammonium salt. Nazeeruddin et al. proposed the use of the ortho-(phenylene)di(ethylammonium) iodide (o-PDEAI  $  _{2}  $  ) to effectively increase the formation energy of 2D perovskite and obtained 23.9%-efficient devices with enhanced long-term stability under ambient environment, elevated temperatures, and light soaking conditions.  $  ^{[45]}  $   Gao et al. selected a bulky fused-ring aromatic ammonium salt, 1-naphthylmethylammonium iodide (NMAI), as a perovskite surface passivator. NMAI post-treatment primarily maintained NMAI salt on the perovskite surface but there was also a small amount of low-dimensional perovskite formed.  $  ^{[11]}  $   Yang et al. designed an organic salt with a large π-conjugated polycyclic aromatic tail, namely 2-(pyren-1-yl)ethan-1-aminium iodide (PREA).  $  ^{[12]}  $   The results indicated that PREA can maintain the salt state and form vertical assembly on the surface of perovskite, improving the film hydrophobicity and performance of devices. To date, the strategies used to prevent ammonium salts from forming 2D perovskite on the surface of 3D perovskite include skipping the annealing process after depositing the passivation layer and increasing the size of the bulky cation of ammonium salts.

Recently, the role of non-halide molecular anions in fabricating highly efficient perovskite solar cells has been studied.  $  ^{[46-48]}  $   For instance, compared to halogen ions, non-halide molecular anions have stronger coordination effects with Pb.  $  ^{[48]}  $   Partial substitution of halogen ions by non-halide molecular anions can enhance the film quality and improve the PCE and stability of the devices.  $  ^{[49-52]}  $   We suppose that the interaction strength between the anion of the alkyl ammonium salt and   $  Pb^{2+}  $   is a key factor for the reactivity of the salt since the anion will compete with   $  I^{-}  $  to form coordination bonds with   $  Pb^{2+}  $   on the perovskite surface. If the interaction between the anion and   $  Pb^{2+}  $   is strong enough, it will inhibit the formation of Pb-I octahedra and therefore no 2D perovskite can be formed.

Herein, we selected HCOO  $  ^{-}  $  , with stronger coordination ability with Pb  $  ^{2+}  $   than halogen,  $  ^{[48]}  $   as the anion to reduce the surface reactivity of the alkyl ammonium salt on the 3D perovskite. Lead ions are expected to preferentially coordinate with HCOO  $  ^{-}  $   at the surface and prevent forming Pb–I octahedrons, thus inhibiting the transformation of the ammonium salt into 2D perovskite. We first synthesized the non-halide ionic salt of 1-naphthylmethylammonium formate (NMACOOH) and studied its passivation effect on the formamidinium-based perovskite. Based on the X-ray diffraction (XRD) and wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) measurements, we found that compared with the traditional NMAI, NMACOOH treatment hindered the formation of 2D perovskite and formed stable PbI  $  _{2}  $  -NMACOOH adduct on the perovskite surface. XPS characterization revealed that NMA  $  ^{+}  $   can passivate the cation vacancies of the 3D perovskite while HCOO  $  ^{-}  $   can passivate the metallic Pb  $  ^{0}  $   and halide-vacancy defects. Therefore, the non-radiative recombination within the device was dramatically suppressed and the open circuit voltage was significantly increased. Finally, the PCE of the best device after NMACOOH passivation reached 24.75%, and the V  $  _{OC}  $   was as high as 1.19 V, which was much higher than

that of the control device with a PCE of 22.46% and   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.10 V. The voltage loss of PSCs based on a 1.55-eV perovskite absorber was only 0.36 V. In addition, the NMACOOH-passivated device also showed improved stability, maintaining >98% of the initial efficiency after 1800 h of storage in the air with   $  \approx  $  10% humidity. Moreover, the NMACOOH-passivated PSCs showed much improved operational stability, retaining 83% of their initial efficiency after 658 h following the maximum power point (MPP) tracking under one sun illumination while the control PSC retained 80% of their initial efficiency after 45 h.

# 2. Results and Discussion

NMACOOH was synthesized by the acid-base neutralization reaction between 1-naphthylmethylamine and formic acid (see Supporting Information for details).  $  ^{[53]}  $   To confirm the structure of the ammonium salt, we characterized the product by   $  {}^{1}  $  H NMR in DMSO-  $  d_{6}  $   (Figure S1, Supporting Information). The   $  {}^{1}  $  H NMR signals of the amino group displayed a downfield shift after the reaction (from   $  \delta = 1.87  $   ppm to   $  \delta = 8.36  $   ppm). Concurrently, owing to the increased electronegativity of N, the H signal on the vicinal C slightly shifted to a lower field. These results indicated that the amino group was protonated and NMACOOH was generated.

We then passivated the surface of the prepared 3D perovskite films using the synthesized NMACOOH. Perovskite films were deposited by a typical two-step method in this work and an n-ip device structure of glass/ITO/SnO $ _2 $ /3D perovskite/passivation layer/Spiro-OMeTAD/Au was used (Figure 1a), where ITO is indium tin oxide and Spiro-OMeTAD is

2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-di-p-methoxyphenyl-amine)-9,9'-spirobifluorene. The passivation layer was spin-coated on the surface of the prepared films without further annealing. To demonstrate the presence of NMACOOH on the treated films, we performed Fourier-transformed infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy testing and the results are shown in Figure S2 (Supporting Information). We can observe that the NMACOOH-treated perovskite film showed IR peaks belonging to the out-of-plane C-H bending vibration on the naphthalene ring in the range of 774–803 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  , which proved the existence of NMACOOH on the perovskite film.  $  ^{[11]}  $

To confirm whether NMACOOH post-treatment changed the structure of perovskite films and to determine the state of NMACOOH on perovskite films, we performed XRD and GIWAXS characterizations. The XRD results in Figure S3a (Supporting Information) showed that the NMACOOH-treated film had similar diffraction peaks that belonged to perovskite as the control film, which indicated that the NMACOOH treatment did not change the lattice parameters of the pristine perovskite. For the diffraction of   $  PbI_{2}  $  , the peak intensity decreased to a certain extent but the diffraction angle of the residual   $  PbI_{2}  $   was not changed after the treatment. The enlarged XRD patterns in Figure S3b (Supporting Information) further showed that NMACOOH treatment led to the formation of the new phase with a diffraction angle of 5.91°. These results indicated that after NMACOOH treatment, the residual   $  PbI_{2}  $   was partially converted into a new phase (5.91°). We also compared the NMACOOH-treated perovskite with that treated with the traditional NMAI. XRD/GIWAXS results in Figures S3 and S10 (Supporting Information) indicated that a mixture of NMAI and 2D perovskite of  $ \mathrm{NMA}_2\mathrm{PbI}_4 $  can form on the 3D perovskite surface, which is consistent with the previous report.[11] This means that only enlarging the size of the cation by replacing the benzene ring with the naphthalene was not sufficient to inhibit the formation of 2D perovskite. The line cut of the GIWAXS data of the NMAI salt ( $ q = 0.45^{\circ} $ ) as a function of the azimuth angle (Figure S9b, Supporting Information) also showed an out-of-plane crystal orientation of the formed NMAI.

The passivation effect of NMACOOH and the thermal stability of the NMACOOH-treated film are expected to be better than that of the NMAI-based ones since it hindered the transformation of the molecule into a 2D phase at elevated temperatures. According to the previous report,  $  ^{[54]}  $  the residual  $  PbI_{2}  $  on the perovskite surface will be further reduced to atomic lead under the illumination of light or X-ray, forming deep-level defects. After the NMACOOH treatment, the  $  HCOO^{-}  $  anion can interact with the residual  $  PbI_{2}  $  on the surface, which may suppress the formation of metallic  $  Pb^{0}  $  and reduce the non-radiative recombination loss. Moreover, the  $  HCOO^{-}  $  anion of the NMACOOH:  $  PbI_{2}  $  complex can strongly interact with undercoordinated  $  Pb^{2+}  $  of the 3D perovskite surface due to the Lewis base property of the anion, and the carbonyl group of the anion can form hydrogen bonding with the organic cations of  $  FA^{+}  $  and  $  MA^{+}  $ .  $  ^{[48]}  $

To verify the above-mentioned chemical interactions, we investigated the surface chemical state of perovskite thin films by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis. As shown in Figure 1d, the XPS peaks at 138.5 eV and 143.3 eV were identified as Pb   $  4f_{7/2}  $   and Pb   $  4f_{5/2}  $  , respectively. Compared with the pristine perovskite film, the NMACOOH-treated film exhibited the absence of the two adjacent peaks at 136.9 and 141.7 eV, which were considered to be the signal of the highly undercoordinated lead ions with metallic properties.  $  ^{[55]}  $   These results confirmed the inhibition of the formation of Pb  $  ^{0}  $   by the NMACOOH passivation.

This is also consistent with the XPS results.

The passivation effect of NMACOOH on the 3D perovskite was summarized in Figure 1a. HCOO  $  ^{-}  $  interacts with uncoordinated Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  on the surface, inhibiting the production of metallic Pb  $  ^{0}  $  and the formation of deep-level defects. HCOO  $  ^{-}  $  further interacts with organic cations on the film surface to form hydrogen bonds, which may suppress the escape of the volatile cations and improve the stability of the device. The cation and non-halide molecular anion simultaneously passivate the oppositely charged defects in the film, reducing the non-radiative recombination centers on the surface.

Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) tested in an integration sphere was used to better understand the effect of NMAI and NMACOOH treatment on the charge carrier recombination of perovskite films. As shown in Figure 2b, the NMACOOH-treated film exhibited the strongest fluorescence intensity, which indicated that NMACOOH had the best passivation effect. We further investigated the influence of NMAI and NMACOOH treatment on the interfacial energy level alignment using UV photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS, Figure 2d), and Figure 2e displayed the corresponding energy-level diagrams. Compared to the control with a valence band maximum (VBM) of -5.78 eV and the NMAI-treated film with a VBM of -5.84 eV, the NMACOOH-treated film showed a shallow VBM of -5.63 eV, which was much closer to that of Spiro-OMeTAD, indicating reduced energetic mismatch at the perovskite/HTL interface.

To better understand the mechanism of why NMACOOH treatment improved the device performance, we studied the charge carrier behavior and the defect physics of the devices. As shown in the Mott-Schottky curves (Figure 3a), the built-in voltage  $ (V_{\mathrm{bi}}) $  of the control, NMAI-

treated, and NMACOOH-treated devices were 0.90, 0.98, and  $ 1.00\mathrm{V} $ , respectively. A higher built-in voltage was expected to facilitate carrier separation and reduce charge accumulation, which can contribute to a higher  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $ . To further investigate charge separation and recombination of the device under illumination, we measured the dependence of  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  on the incident light intensity and plotted  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  versus the logarithmic light intensity (Figure 3b). The fitted slopes for the control, NMAI-treated, and NMACOOH-treated devices are 1.90, 1.89, and  $ 1.37\mathrm{k_B}T / q $ , respectively. The

decreased ideality factor from   $  \approx2  $   to close to 1 indicated the suppression of Shockley-Read-Hall recombination within the device. This result showed that the introduction of NMACOOH as the passivation layer of perovskite can effectively suppress the defect-assisted recombination at the interface of the device.

To evaluate the density of defect states within the devices, electron-only devices with the structure of ITO/SnO $ _2 $ /perovskite/[6,6]-phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PC $ _{61} $ BM)/Ag were fabricated and the space-charge limited current (SCLC) curves were tested (Figure 3c). The dark  $ J-V $  curves can be divided into three regions, e.g. ohmic, TFL (trap-filled limit), and Child region. The voltage at the intersection of the Ohmic and TFL region (V $ _{TFL} $ ) is proportional to the defect density ( $ N_t $ ) following the equation:

$$
N _ {\mathrm{t}} = 2 \varepsilon_ {0} \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{r}} V _ {\mathrm{TFL}} / q L ^ {2}
$$

(1)

where   $  \epsilon_{0}  $   is the vacuum permittivity, q is the elementary charge,   $  \epsilon_{r}  $   is the relative permittivity, and L is the thickness of the perovskite film. The   $  V_{TFL}  $   of the perovskite film was 0.066 V after NMACOOH treatment, which was smaller than that of the control film (0.145 V) and NMAI-treated device (0.103 V). The calculated   $  N_{t}  $   of the device reduced from   $  2.09 \times 10^{15}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $   for the control and   $  1.48 \times 10^{15}  $   for the NMAI-treated device to   $  9.51 \times 10^{14}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $   after the introduction of NMACOOH. This result demonstrated that defects in the perovskite films were effectively passivated after the introduction of the NMACOOH layer. We performed transient photovoltage (TPV) to further examine the carrier recombination dynamics of the devices. Figure 3d demonstrated that the charge recombination time (  $  \tau_{r}  $  ) of the NMACOOH-treated device is the longest (137, 145, and 225   $  \mu  $  s for the control, NMAI-treated, and NMACOOH-treated device, respectively), indicating the best recombination suppression effect of the NMACOOH treatment. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) data (Figure 3e) revealed that the NMACOOH-treated device had the highest recombination resistance, indicating less trap-mediated recombination.

The electroluminescence (EL) of the solar cells working as light-emitting diodes (LED) at forward voltages can more directly dictate the non-radiative recombination. As shown in Figure 3f and Figure S17 (Supporting Information), the NMACOOH-treated device obtained the highest EL EQE of  $ 5.9\% $  at an injection current density of  $ 25.51\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $ , which was significantly greater than that of the control device  $ (0.77\%) $  and the NMAI-treated device  $ (1.17\%) $ . The NMACOOH-treated device achieved a maximum EQE of  $ \approx 7\% $ . These results showed that the defect-assisted recombination inside the device was significantly reduced after NMACOOH treatment.

To evaluate the reproducibility of the effect of the NMACOOH on the performances, the statistics of the photovoltaic metric parameter were analyzed. The PCE,   $  V_{OC}  $  ,   $  J_{SC}  $  , and FF of 30 devices based on the control, NMAI-treated, and NMACOOH-treated perovskite films were shown in Figure 4a and Figure S18 (Supporting Information). The   $  V_{OC}  $   of the NMACOOH-treated device was increased by an average value of 60 mV and the PCE was increased by an average value of 1.45% compared with the control devices. The statistical data also showed that the device performance after NMACOOH treatment has good repeatability.

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, we designed and synthesized a novel bulky organic ammonium salt NMACOOH with a non-halide molecular anion for the surface modification of perovskite films. Compared with I  $  ^{-}  $  , owning to the stronger interaction between HCOO  $  ^{-}  $   and Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  , it inhibited the transformation of the NMACOOH salt to 2D perovskite under thermal stress. In other words, the surface reactivity of the alkyl salt was reduced, thus improving the defect passivation effect and thermal stability of the devices. The NMACOOH salt can interact with the residual PbI  $  _{2}  $   at the perovskite surface to form the PbI  $  _{2}  $  : NMACOOH adduct, hindering the formation of deep-level metallic Pb  $  ^{0}  $   trap states. The anion of the adduct can

also interact with the organic cation through hydrogen bonding, further improving the stability of the perovskite film. As a result, perovskite solar cells with a champion efficiency of 24.75% and a high   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.19 V were achieved. The NMACOOH-passivated devices also showed improved thermal-, operational, and environmental stability than the control and NMAI-passivated devices. This work provides a new idea for the development of multifunctional surface passivation molecules.
