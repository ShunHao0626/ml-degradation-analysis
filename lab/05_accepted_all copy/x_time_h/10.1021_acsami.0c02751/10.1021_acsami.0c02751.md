KEYWORDS: perovskite solar cells, hole-transporting materials, butadiene, charge transport, film morphology

ABSTRACT: Two three-dimensional symmetric tetraphenylbutadiene derivatives decorated with diphenylamine or triphenylamine fragments are first prepared for use as hole-transporting materials (HTMs) in perovskite solar cells (PSCs). The HTMs are acquired using straightforward synthetic methods and facile purification techniques. The thermal stability, photophysical properties, electrochemical behaviors, computational study, hole mobility, X-ray diffraction, hole transfer dynamics, hydrophobicity, surface morphology, and photovoltaic performances of the HTMs are discussed. The highest power conversion efficiency (PCE) of CJ-04-based cell is 13.75%, which is increased to 20.06% when CJ-03 is used as HTM, superior to the PCE of the cell based on 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-di-p-methoxyphenylamine)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (spiro-OMeTAD) (18.90%). The preparation cost of CJ-03 accounts for merely 23.1% of the price of commercial spiro-OMeTAD, while the concentration of CJ-03 solution used in the device fabrication (60.0 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) is lower compared with that of the spiro-OMeTAD solution (72.3 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ). These results corroborate that the screw-like HTMs with a highly distorted configuration are facilely available and promising candidates for PSCs. More importantly, a practical solution is proposed to achieve moderate charge mobility and good film-formation ability of the HTMs simultaneously.

# Introduction

During the past ten years, organic–inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted considerable attention due to their fast-growing power conversion efficiency (PCE), which was only 3.8% in 2009 and skyrocketed to 25.2% today.  $  ^{1,2}  $   The improvement has been ascribed principally to the exceptional virtue of perovskite, such as intense panchromatic absorption,  $  ^{3,4}  $   tunable band gaps,  $  ^{5,6}  $   decent ambipolar charge-transporting capability,  $  ^{7,8}  $   long carrier lifetimes and diffusion lengths,  $  ^{9,10}  $   unusual defect tolerance,  $  ^{11,12}  $   and low exciton binding energy.  $  ^{13}  $   In addition, the hole-transporting materials (HTMs) play a pivotal part in achieving the high performances of PSCs, as they ensure the efficient hole extraction and transportation, and the suppression of photo-generated carrier recombination.  $  ^{14}  $

Numerous HTMs including inorganic materials and organic compounds (small molecules and polymers) have been developed and utilized in PSCs.  $  ^{15-17}  $   HTMs based on organic small molecules hold many advantages over inorganic and polymeric rivals, for instance, structural versatility, definite chemical structures with accurate molecular weight, convenient synthesis and purification with good yield and high purity, good solubility, easy infiltration into the nanostructured materials, and excellent batch-to-batch reproducibility.  $  ^{18-20}  $   Representative HTMs based on small molecules are spiro-linked compounds,  $  ^{21}  $   3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene analogues,  $  ^{22,23}  $   phthalocyanine,  $  ^{24}  $   phenoxazine,  $  ^{25,26}  $   phenothiazine,  $  ^{27,28}  $   thiophene,  $  ^{29,30}  $   anthracene,  $  ^{31}  $   and carbazole.  $  ^{32,33}  $   In the HTMs library, 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-di-p-methoxyphenylamine)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (spiro-OMeTAD) has achieved a dominant position due to its outstanding performances in PSCs. Unfortunately, onerous synthetic procedures and complicate purification processes make it cost-ineffective, and significantly hamper its potential scale-up production in commercial photovoltaics.  $  ^{34}  $   Therefore, much effort has been devoted to

In general, the HTMs with planar structures favor strong   $  \pi-\pi  $   interactions, leading to high hole mobility.  $  ^{36}  $   However, these planar molecules tend to aggregate and thus form films with excessively large crystalline domains, resulting in coarse surface morphology.  $  ^{37}  $   Thus, it is very important to develop high-performance HTMs with high charge mobility and good film-forming ability.

Tetraphenylethylene-based compounds have been proven competitive HTMs by us and other researchers.  $  ^{38-40}  $   It is worth noting that tetraphenylethylene is a prototypical building block for the aggregation-induced emission (AIE) materials.  $  ^{41}  $   Generally, intramolecular charge delocalization is considered important for charge transport and can be improved by the enhancement of conjugation.  $  ^{42}  $   The conjugation of tetraphenylethylene is less effective because the olefinic bond and four phenyl rings are not coplanar. To enhance the conjugation, one strategy is to increase the molecular planarity by locking the phenyl rings.  $  ^{43,44}  $   Another one is to replace the olefinic bond by butadiene, whose four carbon atoms are coplanar. In addition, the four phenyl rings may tilt less against the plane of butadiene because of a reduced steric hindrance. Tetraphenylbutadiene is another structural entity prevailing among the AIE domain.  $  ^{45}  $   Despite the fact that the fluorescence behaviors of tetraphenylbutadiene derivatives are irrelevant to PSCs, their special molecular structure may assist in the charge transport. Through rotatable C–C single bonds, four phenyl rings are connected to the central butadiene stator of the tetraphenylbutadiene molecule. These screw-shaped molecules are prone to facilitate the establishment of a three-dimensional (3D) charge-transporting system. In addition to the charge transport properties, the intermolecular interactions of the tetraphenylbutadiene molecules are weak due to their distorted structures. Briefly, the chief building block [brominated tetraphenylbutadiene (4)] was synthesized in four steps starting with the olefination of 4,4'-dibromobenzophenone, followed by the bromomethylation of olefine (1), the preparation of Wittig reagent (3), and the subsequent Wittig–Horner reaction (Scheme S1, Supporting Information). # AIE attributes

In spite of the prevalence of tetraphenylbutadiene for constructing AIE molecules, the fluorescence behaviors and PSCs performances of CJ-03 and CJ-04 are unrelated. For the sake of completeness, their light-emitting features in the diluted solutions of water/THF mixtures were studied as well (Figures S17–S19, Table S4 and S5, Supporting Information). Since CJ-03 and CJ-04 are insoluble in water, they have to form aggregation upon the addition of large quantity of water. Due to the tetraphenylbutadiene unit, CJ-03 emits weak fluorescence in a diluted THF solution as anticipated (Figure S17a). Surprisingly, the emission of CJ-03 abruptly intensifies after the water fraction reaches 60%. The AIE phenomenon of CJ-03 can be explained by the restricted intramolecular rotations.  $  ^{45}  $   On the contrary, CJ-04 emits intense fluorescence in THF (Figure S17b). As water is added, the emission of CJ-04 in the water/THF mixtures decreases until 40% water fraction, and then increases back to the approximately initial intensity at 60% water fraction. Nevertheless, the intrinsic mechanism remains obscure.

# Thermal stability

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was used to evaluate the stability of CJ-03 and CJ-04 under heat (Figure S20, Supporting Information). The decomposition temperatures   $  (T_{\mathrm{d}})  $   (5% weight loss) are 417.5 and 423.3 °C for CJ-03 and CJ-04, respectively. The temperatures are comparable with that of spiro-OMeTAD (417.0 °C).  $  ^{46}  $   Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurements were adopted to examine the thermal behavior of CJ-03 and CJ-04 (Figure S21, Supporting Information). For CJ-03, the first heating exposes a melting peak   $  (T_{\mathrm{m}})  $   at 299.3 °C, and the cooling uncovers a crystallization peak   $  (T_{\mathrm{c}})  $   at 255.4 °C, whereas the second heating only discovers a melting peak at 299.1 °C. For CJ-04, the first heating unravels a melting peak at 171.8 °C, the cooling does not show any crystallization peak, and the second heating detects a glass transition   $  (T_{\mathrm{g}})  $   at 154.1 °C, higher than that of spiro-OMeTAD (126.0 °C).  $  ^{46}  $   Both HTMs are thermally stable enough for the general operation temperature of PSCs (  $  \sim80  $   °C).  $  ^{22}  $   The thermal properties are listed in Table 1.

The UV–vis absorption spectra display that all the HTMs possess two strong absorption bands between 260 and 500 nm (Figure 1a). Besides, CJ-03 shows a weak absorption band from 340 to 390 nm. The first bands (260–330 nm) come from the electron transition of triphenylamine groups. The second bands (330–500 nm) originate from the   $  \pi-\pi^{*}  $   transition of the whole molecules. CJ-03 shows a bathochromic absorption spectrum compared with that of CJ-04, resulting from the easier intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) interaction in CJ-03 compared to that in CJ-04.  $  ^{47}  $   The maximum emission wavelength (  $  \lambda_{em}  $  ) of CJ-03 is 518 nm, which increases to 540 nm for CJ-04 owing to the extended conjugated backbone (Figure 1a). The large Stokes shift enables a better pore-filling of the HTMs and improvement on the hole extraction efficiency of PSCs.  $  ^{48,49}  $   The PL spectra of CJ-03 and CJ-04 reveal their large Stokes shifts (68 and 102 nm, respectively), indicating the substantial structural variations in the excited state (Figures S22 and S23, Supporting Information). In contrast, the Stokes shift of spiro-OMeTAD is only 39 nm because of the rigid nature of the spiro-segment (Figure S24, Supporting Information).  $  ^{50}  $   Thus, it can be concluded that the stiff spiro-segment is not as conducive to large Stokes shifts as the screw-shaped tetraphenylbutadiene unit. The intersection wavelength of normalized absorption and emission spectra is used to determine the optical band gap (  $  E_{gap}  $  ) (  $  E_{gap} = 1240/\lambda_{intersection}  $  ). Figure S25 discloses that the intersection wavelength of CJ-03, CJ-04, and spiro-OMeTAD is 476, 470, and 406 nm, respectively, which can be converted into   $  E_{gap}  $  , i.e. 2.61, 2.64, and 3.05 eV, respectively. The UV–vis absorption spectra of   $  TiO_{2}/MAPbI_{3}  $   substrates without or with HTMs display a wide absorption spanning the region from 300 to 800 nm (Figure 1b). The absorption edge at ~780 nm corresponds to the   $  E_{gap}  $   of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   (~1.59 eV). After the spin-coating of HTMs, the intense absorption bands, arising from the superposed absorption of the chromophores, appear at 360–500 nm. Cyclic voltammograms of HTMs reflect their highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) levels and redox characteristics. As shown in Figure 2a, there are two groups of redox peaks for CJ-03 and CJ-04, but for CJ-04, the redox peaks at higher potentials are not obvious. Both CV curves are well reversible, implying that they are electrochemically stable. According to the redox peaks at the lowest potentials and using the formula:  $  E_{1/2} = (E_{\mathrm{ox}} + E_{\mathrm{red}})/2  $ , the  $  E_{1/2}  $  of the HTMs is obtained. Hysteresis frequently occurs in the J-V tests, in which the striking discrepancy of PSCs performances emerge when different scan directions, scan rates, and pre-conditioning are utilized.  $  ^{68}  $   As a result, it is hard to appraise the precise performances of the cells. The formula below describes the hysteresis index (HI), which is often employed to determine the hysteresis extent:

$$
\mathrm{HI} = \frac {J _ {\mathrm{RS}} (0 . 8 V _ {\mathrm{OC}}) - J _ {\mathrm{FS}} (0 . 8 V _ {\mathrm{OC}})}{J _ {\mathrm{RS}} (0 . 8 V _ {\mathrm{OC}})}
$$

Here,   $  J_{\mathrm{RS}}  $   (  $  0.8V_{OC}  $  ) and   $  J_{\mathrm{FS}}  $   (  $  0.8V_{OC}  $  ) denote the current density of the reverse and forward scan at 80% of the   $  V_{OC}  $ , respectively.  $  ^{69}  $   Apparently, HI = 0 signifies that no hysteresis exists in the device, and a larger HI is indicative of a greater hysteresis degree. The hysteresis phenomena of devices containing CJ-03, CJ-04, and spiro-OMeTAD were analyzed under reverse and forward scan (Figure S56 and Table S11, Supporting Information). The HI values of the cells with CJ-03, CJ-04, and spiro-OMeTAD are 0.0486, 0.2113, and 0.0941, respectively. # Conclusions

The synthesis of two 3D HTMs comprising a central tetraphenylbutadiene scaffold and terminal diphenylamine or triphenylamine branches, together with their application to PSCs, were reported for the first time. The preparation expense of CJ-03 is  $ \sim 34.43 $  USD/g, accounting for  $ 23.1\% $  of the price of spiro-OMeTAD from commercial source. The required amount of CJ-03 in the cell assembly is fewer than that of spiro-OMeTAD (60.0 versus  $ 72.3\mathrm{mg~mL}^{-1} $ ). The HTMs exhibit several appealing merits due to their unique molecular configuration: (1) the increased planarity of tetraphenylbutadiene by comparison with tetraphenylethylene enhances the conjugation and thus the intramolecular charge delocalization, which is advantageous to the
