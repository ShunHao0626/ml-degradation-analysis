# Indium doped   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   films for inorganic perovskite solar cells with efficiency exceeding 17%

# ABSTRACT

In recent years, all-inorganic perovskite materials have set off a research boom owing to features, such as good thermal stability, suitable bandgap, and fascinating optical properties. However, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) and the ambient stability of all-inorganic perovskite solar cells still remain a challenge. Herein, we investigate the effect of the addition of   $  InI_{3}  $   into   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film on the corresponding device.   $  InI_{3}  $   incorporation could retard the crystallization process and control the growth rate of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   polycrystalline films, yielding a high quality film with large grains and few voids. The increment in electrostatic potential and the reduction of carrier recombination enabled the open-circuit voltage of fabricated perovskite solar cell to be increased from 0.89 to 0.99 V. The champion device delivered a power conversion efficiency of 17.09%, which is higher than 14.36% for the reference device. And the   $  InI_{3}  $  -included solar cell without any encapsulation retained 77% of its original efficiency after 860 h aging at room temperature in   $  N_{2}  $   condition.

indium doping,  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_3 $ , crystallization retardation, stability

# 1 Introduction

Hybrid lead halide perovskite has become one of the most eye-catching materials in recent years due to its low cost, narrow band gap, easy processability, and high efficiency in both solar cells and light-emitting diodes [1, 2]. Particularly, power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 25.2% for photovoltaic devices has been achieved within a relatively short time of ten years [3–7]. However, the easy decomposition of the hybrid lead halide perovskite material at high temperature limits their potential applications [8–10]. Incidentally, inorganic perovskite materials have been presented as alternatives due to their good thermal stability, suitable band gap, and excellent photoelectric performance [11, 12]. They can be realized by substituting the inorganic cation   $  Cs^{+}  $  for the volatile organic cation   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}^{+}  $   (  $  MA^{+}  $  ) and   $  [(NH_{2})_{2}CH]^{+}  $   (  $  FA^{+}  $  ) to yield the all-inorganic perovskites   $  CsPbX_{3}  $   (  $  X = I^{-}, Cl^{-}  $   or   $  Br^{-}  $  ) [13–15]. As an example,   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   with a high band gap of 2.3 eV, which can limit the absorption of light, shows good stability [16, 17]. In order to change the bandgap, the component ratio of the   $  X^{-}  $  site can be adjusted accordingly. Wang et al. reported that a   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film possesses a bandgap of 1.95 eV [18]. Similarly,   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   has a bandgap of 2.05 eV. However, the wide bandgaps gave unsatisfactory PCEs.   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   shows a good visible light absorption by its suitable bandgap of 1.73 eV [19]. Recently, Wang et al. reported a PCE of 19.03% by utilizing phenyltrimethylammonium chlorine (PTACl) as the surface passivation [20]. However, the

perovskite is easily transformed from a black phase (  $  \alpha  $   phase) into a yellow phase (  $  \delta  $   phase) due to the defective Goldschmidt tolerance factor. To improve the stability, many approaches, such as interface engineering and doping strategy, have been explored. Wang et al. reported the enhanced phase stability of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film via phenyltrimethylammonium bromide (PTABr) treatment [11]. The cell was exposed to ultraviolet (UV) light for 500 h without impairing the device efficiency [21]. Zhang et al. showed that an addition of HI additive into   $  PbI_{2}  $   could facilitate the growth of   $  \beta  $  -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   and   $  \gamma  $  -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   films with improved phase stability [22]. Furthermore, Hu et al. demonstrated that a partial substituting of   $  Pb^{2+}  $   with   $  Bi^{+}  $  could retard the degradation of perovskite layer effectively [23]. Some other elements, such as   $  Eu^{2+}  $   [24, 25] and   $  Ca^{2+}  $   [26], could also partially replace   $  Pb^{2+}  $   to enhance the resistance of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   films from water and oxygen erosion by keeping their black phase. These findings give a clue that suitable substitution is an effective method to improve the device performance of inorganic perovskite solar cells.

Herein, we incorporate   $  In^{3+}  $   into   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film to investigate its effect on the electrical structure and the photoelectric property of the perovskite.   $  InI_{3}  $   is chosen by avoiding the intervention of other new elements.   $  InI_{3}  $  -involved   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   perovskite presented a high quality film with large grains and few voids owing to the retarding role of   $  InI_{3}  $   in controlling the perovskite crystallization. The developed strategy resulted in an increase in PCE from 14.36% to 17.09% because of an obvious increase of open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ) from 0.89 to 0.99 V. The

# 2 Experimental

# 2.1 Materials

PbI $ _2 $ -xDMAI (dimethylammonium iodine, Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp), cesium iodide (99.99% CsI, Alfa Aesar Ltd), N, N-Dimethylformamide (99.9% DMF, Alfa Aesar Ltd), and Spiro-OMeTAD (1-Material Ltd) were used in this work.

# 2.2 Device fabrication

A device structure of fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)/TiO $ _2 $ /CsPbI $ _3 $  (InI $ _3 $ :CsPbI $ _3 $ )/Spiro- OMeTAD/MoO $ _3 $ /Ag was adopted. The FTO glass substrate was ultrasonically cleaned with detergent, deionized water, acetone, and ethanol in succession. The FTO substrate was then immersed in an aqueous solution of titanium tetrachloride (TiCl $ _4 $ , 200 mL) and placed in an oven at 70 °C for 1 h to form a TiO $ _2 $  layer. 0.8 M CsPbI $ _3 $  solution was prepared by dissolving PbI $ _2 $ -xDMAI: CsI (1:1) in DMF. The solution was spin-coated on the TiO $ _2 $  layer at 3,000 rpm for 50 s to form the absorber layer. Holes transport material (HTM) was prepared by dissolving Sprio-OMeTAD (90 mg), a sufonyl imide (Li-TFSI, 22.5 μL) solution (520mg Li-TFSI in 1 mL acetonitrile), and tert-butylpyidine (tBP, 36 μL) in 1 mL of chlorbenzene (CB) solution, and stirring at 60 °C for 24 h. Then HTM was spin-coated in the absorbed layer at 5,000 rpm for 40 s. Finally, 10 nm MoO $ _3 $  and 100 nm silver electrode were slowly evaporated to complete the device.

# 2.3 Measurement and characterization

The current density–voltage   $  (J-V)  $   characteristics were measured by using a programmable Keithley 2,400 source meter under AM 1.5G illumination with a light intensity of   $  100\ mW/cm^{2}  $  . Atomic force microscopy (AFM) images were obtained on a Veeco Multimode V instrument. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses were done by using an ESCALAB250Xi (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Electrical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted on an IM6e Electrochemical Workstation (ZAHNER, Germany). Capacitance vs. frequency   $  (C-F)  $   was measured by Precision Impedance Analyzer (Agilent 4294A). The absorption spectra were obtained using ultraviolet and visible (UV–Vis) spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer Lambda 750). Surface morphologies were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Quanta 200 FEG, FEI Co.). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were achieved on PANalytica 80 equipment.

# 3 Results and discussion

$ \mathrm{InI}_3 $  was directly introduced into  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_3 $  perovskite precursor.  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_3:\mathrm{InI}_3 $  film was deposited by spin-coating the precursor solution onto  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  layer with an annealing at  $ 180^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 15\mathrm{min} $ . Figure 1(a) shows the core levels of the chemical composition of pure  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_3 $  and  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_3:\mathrm{InI}_3 $  films as obtained from XPS. The  $ \mathrm{Pb4f_{7 / 2}} $  core level in  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_3:\mathrm{InI}_3 $  films is upshifted by  $ 0.25\mathrm{eV} $  relative to the pure  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_3 $  film. This is originated from the partial substitution of  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2 + }} $  by  $ \mathrm{In}^{3 + } $ , proving that  $ \mathrm{In}^{3 + } $  can be successfully incorporated into the perovskite crystal lattice [27, 28]. As shown in Figs. 1(b) and 1(c), the binding energies of Cs 3d and I 3d are also upshifted by 0.10 and  $ 0.15\mathrm{eV} $ , respectively, indicating that the force of interaction between Pb-I and Cs-I are enhanced. This means that the structure of

$ \mathrm{InI}_3 $ -involved  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_3 $  is more stable and difficult to decompose. As shown in Fig. 1(d), a peak located at  $ 445.5\mathrm{eV} $ , associated with the In  $ 3\mathrm{d}_{5/2} $  core level is detected in  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_3:\mathrm{InI}_3 $  film, confirming the presence of  $ \mathrm{In}^{3+} $  in the perovskite film.

The formation of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   perovskite films requires an annealing after spin-coating. The light absorbing layer is slowly changed from yellow phase to black phase. However, a prolonged annealing time and higher temperature would result in a quick transformation into orthorhombic phase (  $  \delta  $   phase). In order to investigate the annealing behavior of the   $  CsPbI_{3}:InI_{3}  $   film, the effect of   $  InI_{3}  $   doping was first evaluated by XRD. The peaks of the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   and   $  CsPbI_{3}:InI_{3}  $   films located at 14.2° and 28.8° correspond to (110) and (220) crystal planes, respectively [29–31]. Obviously, the peak intensity of   $  CsPbI_{3}:InI_{3}  $   film was stronger than that of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film (see Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). Furthermore, the intensity was increased with   $  InI_{3}  $   concentration from 0 to 1 mg, and then followed by a reduction at the large doping ratio (see Fig. S2 in the ESM). In addition, the peak shift from 28.8° to 28.9° corresponding to the (220) plane was observed in the   $  InI_{3}  $  -doped   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film, indicating a successful incorporation of   $  In^{3+}  $   into the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   perovskite (see Fig. S3 in the ESM).

To further study the effect of   $  In^{3+}  $   doping on the crystallization of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film, top-view SEM images of the pristine and doped perovskite films were obtained. As shown in Figs. 2(a) and 2(b), larger grain size can be seen in the   $  CsPbI_{3}:InI_{3}  $   film. SEM image at micron level also confirmed the enhanced film quality of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   perovskites with enlarged grain size and reduced pin-holes by   $  InI_{3}  $   incorporation (see Fig. S4 in the ESM). Superior film quality facilitates interfacial charge transfer and reduces charge accumulation [32]. The statistics charts of grain sizes in the pristine and doped   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film (Figs. 2(c) and 2(d)) showed an increase in average grain size from 225 to 325 nm. The effectiveness of grain size optimization by InI, doping was further confirmed by AFM (see Fig. S5 in the ESM).

In-depth investigation of the effect of   $  InI_{3}  $   doping on perovskite crystallization was performed. The XRD patterns of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   and   $  CsPbI_{3}:InI_{3}  $   films were further recorded at different stages during the annealing process. As shown in Fig. 3(a), the XRD patterns of the pristine   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film showed an intermediate phase at 15.8°. This peak disappeared when the annealing time was prolonged. In contrast, no intermediate phase was observed firstly for   $  CsPbI_{3}:InI_{3}  $   film. However, the intermediate phase appeared after 30 s annealing (Fig. 3(b)). The difference

Figure 4(a) shows the UV-Vis absorption spectra and photoluminescence (PL) spectra of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   and   $  CsPbI_{3}:InI_{3}  $   films. Obviously, the absorption of   $  InI_{3}  $  -doped   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film is enhanced compared with the pristine   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film. The PL peak (726 nm) is red-shifted by 2 nm relative to the pristine film at 724 nm. This is consistent with the absorption spectra. The slight red-shift and the enhanced absorption are ascribed to the large grain size and good charge transfer capability [29, 33–35]. At the same time, the absorption spectra of the perovskite films casting from different concentrations were also measured (see Fig. S6 in the ESM). It is well known that charge transfer capability is related to charge transfer resistance, which was estimated by EIS as shown in Fig. 4(b). The equivalent circuit is placed in the inset of Fig. 4(b). The   $  R_{SH}  $   and   $  R_{CT}  $   are the sheet and charge transfer resistances, respectively [36–38]. As can be seen from Fig. 4(b), the high frequency area represents   $  R_{SH}  $ . Both devices possessed the values provided from where they

were fabricated under the same condition.   $  R_{CT}  $   is associated with the low frequency area, and has a value of 1.69 and   $  1.98 k\Omega\cdot cm^{2}  $   for doped and pristine device, respectively. Consequently, the doped device shows better charge transport.

In order to fully understand the carrier transport characteristics, we measured the dark J-V curves of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   and   $  CsPbI_{3}:InI_{3}  $   based hole-only and electron-only devices. Figure 4(c) shows the electron-only device with a structure of FTO/  $  TiO_{2}/CsPbI_{3}  $   (  $  CsPbI_{3}:InI_{3}  $  )/[6,6]-phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM)/Ag. The linear increase of current with voltage can be divided into three parts. Firstly, the current density increases slowly with voltage in the ohmic current region. Secondly, the trend of rapid current rise happens in the trap-fill limited (TFL) region. The coordinate value of the horizontal axis at the kink point between ohmic current region and TFL region is called trap-filled limit voltage (  $  V_{TFL}  $  ). The third part is space charge limited current. The trap state density (  $  n_{trap}  $  ) and carrier mobility   $  \mu  $   could be calculated by fitting the J-V curves [35, 39, 40]. We can calculate the carrier mobility using the Mott-Gurney equation:

$$
\mu = \frac {8 J L ^ {3}}{9 \varepsilon \varepsilon_ {0} V ^ {2}}
$$

(1)

where L is the thickness of inorganic perovskite film,   $  \varepsilon  $   represents the dielectric constant under vacuum condition, and   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   is the dielectric constant of the film. The electron mobility of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   and   $  CsPbI_{3}:InI_{3}  $   was extrapolated to be 0.51 and   $  1.30\ cm^{2}/(V\cdot S)  $  , respectively. Figure 4(d) shows the hole-only device with a structure of FTO/poly(3,4-ethylene dioxythiophene) and poly(styrene sulfonate)(PEDOT:PSS)/CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   (CsPbI  $  _{3}  $ :InI  $  _{3}  $ )/SpiroOMeTAD/Ag. Similarly, the hole mobility of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   and   $  CsPbI_{3}:InI_{3}  $   are determined to be 0.29 and   $  0.52\ cm^{2}/(V\cdot S)  $  , respectively. Obviously, electron and hole mobilities in the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film could be improved by InI  $  _{3}  $   incorporation. This is consistent with the conclusion of EIS. The enhanced carrier mobility is attributed to the InI  $  _{3}  $   doping, which retards the perovskite crystallization by giving enough time for the growth of large grains and the passivation of film defects.

Figure 4(e) shows the open-circuit photovoltage decay (OCVD). During the test, the devices were irradiated under standard sunlight and immediately blocked, resulting in an instantaneous decay for OCVD. The electron lifetime can be calculated from the curve according to Eq. (2) [41-44]:

$$
\tau_ {\mathrm{n}} = \frac {- T K _ {\mathrm{B}}}{e} \left(\frac {\mathrm{d} V _ {\mathrm{oc}}}{\mathrm{d} t}\right)
$$

(2)

where  $ e $  represents the elementary charge,  $ T $  is the temperature, and  $ K_{\mathrm{B}} $  is the Boltzmann's constant. The relationship between electron lifetime and voltage is shown in the inset of Fig. 4(e). It can be seen that the electron lifetime in the control device is longer than that in the reference device, indicating that the carrier recombination was suppressed due to  $ \mathrm{InI}_3 $  doping.

The effect of   $  InI_{3}  $   doping on the trap density of states (tDOS) is further evaluated by thermal admittance spectroscopy as shown in Fig. 4(f) [45–47]. It can be seen that the tDOS in the reference device is higher than that in the control device, indicating the passivation effect of   $  InI_{3}  $   on the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film. The time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectra are shown in Fig. 5(a). The PL lifetime was increased from 2.41 to 6.40 ns in fast decay process (see Table S1 in the ESM), demonstrating an effective reduction in recombination losses due to   $  InI_{3}  $   doping [48].

# 4 Conclusions

In summary, we have incorporated   $  InI_{3}  $   into   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   inorganic perovskite for photovoltaic application.   $  InI_{3}  $   doping could retard the crystallization and control the crystal growth rate of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film, yielding a perovskite film with large grain size and less voids. XRD measurements revealed that   $  InI_{3}  $   doping could hinder intermediate phase formation and stabilize the black phase during annealing. As for the red-shifted UV-Vis and PL peaks, smaller   $  R_{SH}  $   and larger carrier mobility of the   $  CsPbI_{3}:InI_{3}  $   enabled the solar cells present an increased   $  V_{oc}  $   from 0.89 to 0.99 V and deliver a largely improved PCE from 14.36% to 17.09%. The finding in this work gives a clue that   $  In^{3+}  $   doping is an effective method to increase the   $  V_{oc}  $   of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $ -based inorganic solar cells.
