# Graded Heterojunction Engineering for Hole-Conductor-Free Perovskite Solar Cells with High Hole Extraction Efficiency and Conductivity

Bo Li, Yanan Zhang, Luyuan Zhang, and Longwei Yin*

Despite great progress in the photovoltaic conversion efficiency (PCE) of inorganic–organic hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs), the large-scale application of PSCs still faces serious challenges due to the poor-stability and high-cost of the spiro-OMeTAD hole transport layer (HTL). It is of great fundamental importance to rationally address the issues of hole extraction and transfer arising from HTL-free PSCs. Herein, a brand-new PSC architecture is designed by introducing multigraded-heterojunction (GHJ) inorganic perovskite   $  CsPbBr_{x}I_{3-x}  $   layers as an efficient HTL. The grade adjustment can be achieved by precisely tuning the halide proportion and distribution in the   $  CsPbBr_{x}I_{3-x}  $   film to reach an optimal energy alignment of the valance and conduction band between   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   and   $  CsPbBr_{x}I_{3-x}  $  . The   $  CsPbBr_{x}I_{3-x}  $   GHJ as an efficient HTL can induce an electric field where a valance/conduction band edge is leveraged to bend at the heterojunction interface, boosting the interfacial electron–hole splitting and photoelectron extraction. The GHJ architecture enhances the hole extraction and conduction efficiency from the   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   to the counter electrode, decreases the recombination loss during the hole transfer, and benefits in increasing the open-circuit voltage. The optimized HTL-free PCS based on the GHJ architecture demonstrates an outstanding thermal stability and a significantly improved PCE of 11.33%, nearly 40% increase compared with 8.16% for pure HTL-free devices.

It has been proposed that energy structure grading can help charge transfer by extending the width of depleted layer in which a significant electrical field is provided for drift-based driving force.  $  ^{[22,23]}  $   Constructing controllable grade heterojunction with precise gradient distribution, convertible energy-level depth, and desired energy-level alignment remains a challenge for high-performance perovskite solar cells.

Recently, pure inorganic perovskite   $  CsPbX_{3}  $   nanocrystals (NCs) have drawn substantial attention for the high photoluminescence quantum yield, narrow line width, and high stability.  $  ^{[24,25]}  $   Especially, perovskite   $  CsPbX_{3}NCs  $   provide the experimentally convenient route to build an electron/hole funnel and achieve the energy-level alignment by tuning the proportion of halide (X).  $  ^{[26]}  $   Additionally, as the optical absorption layer used in perovskite solar cells, the cubic-  $  CsPbX_{3}NC  $   films exhibit an outstanding photoelectric and photophysical properties, which enhances the PCE to over 10% for the pure inorganic perovskite solar cells.  $  ^{[27]}  $   The charge carriers of these NCs are highly mobile (  $  \approx4500~cm^{2}~V^{-1}~s^{-1}  $  ) with long diffusion lengths (>9.2   $  \mu  $  m), which is orders of magnitude higher compared to hybrid halide perovskite films and single crystalline semiconductors.  $  ^{[28]}  $

Here, we design a brand-new PSC architecture with a  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3\cdot \mathrm{CsPbBr}_x\mathrm{I}_{3 - x} $  perovskite graded heterojunction (GHJ) by introducing  $ \mathrm{CsPbBr}_x\mathrm{I}_{3 - x}\mathrm{NCs} $  on the top of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ . The grade adjustment can be achieved by precisely tuning halide proportion and distribution in  $ \mathrm{CsPbBr}_x\mathrm{I}_{3 - x} $  film to reach an optimal energy alignment of valance and conduction band between  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  and  $ \mathrm{CsPbBr}_x\mathrm{I}_{3 - x} $ . We demonstrate that a rational graded heterojunction architecture can enhance the hole extraction and conduction efficiency from  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  to counter electrode, decreases the recombination loss during the hole transfer, and benefits in increasing the open-circuit voltage. The optimized HTL-free PCS based on GHJ architecture demonstrates an outstanding thermal-stability and a significantly improved PCE of  $ 11.33\% $ , nearly  $ 40\% $  increase compared with pure HTL-free devices ( $ 8.16\% $ ). This design of controllable gradient heterojunction offers a new way to solve the

electron or hole extraction obstacle not only in HTL-free PSCs, but also in other structural systems of PSCs.

With the benefit of wavelength tunability via composition and similar energy structure with organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite,   $  CsPbBr_{x}I_{3-x}  $   NCs can be the suitable candidate for fabricating heterojunction with   $  MAPbI_{3}  $  . For this purpose, the proportion of Br/I in   $  CsPbBr_{x}I_{3-x}  $   NCs are fine-tuned with the nanocrystal size fixed. As confirmed by X-ray diffraction of NCs (Figure 1a), the crystal structures of the   $  CsPbBrI_{2}  $  ,   $  CsPbBr_{0.7}I_{2.3}  $  ,   $  CsPbBr_{0.3}I_{2.7}  $  , and   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   NCs are all assigned as the cubic structure (JCPDS 54-0752). As increasing bromide incorporation, the characteristic peaks of   $  CsPbBr_{x}I_{3-x}  $   shift gradually to high-angle position, demonstrating the decrease of lattice constant. In Figure 1b, the visible-light absorption and emission spectra could be finely tuned via compensational modulation. A linear blueshift of photometric characteristics from 670 to 620 nm could be achieved by gradually increasing the proportion of bromide to 33% in the colloidal   $  CsPbBr_{x}I_{3-x}  $   NCs.

To examine the effect of heterojunction type (type I or type II) on the photovoltaic performance, the perovskite solar cells based on single heterojunction of   $  MAPbI_{3}-CsPbBr_{x}I_{3-x}(x=0-3)  $   are first fabricated and the corresponding photovoltaic data are displayed in Figure S9 and Table S1 (Supporting Information). The devices based on   $  CsPbBrI_{2}  $   and   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   which have deeper VB than   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   exhibit enhanced PCE and boosted hole extraction rate compared with the device in the absence of   $  CsPbBr_{x}I_{3-x}  $   NC film. Oppositely, the type I heterojunction ones for   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   and   $  CsPbBr_{2}I  $   with shallower VB present decline tendency for both PCE and hole extraction rate.

We then fabricate perovskite solar cells based on the graded heterojunction structure in which we deposit different components of   $  CsPbBr_{x}I_{3-x}  $   NC films orderly on the top of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   based on different halide concentration, keeping the total thickness fixed. Figure 3a shows the diagram of energy-level alignment for   $  CsPbI_{3}  $ ,   $  CsPbBr_{0.3}I_{2.7}  $ ,   $  CsPbBr_{0.7}I_{2.3}  $ , and   $  CsPbBrI_{2}  $ . For 1-grade, single   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   NC film is coupled with   $  MAPbI_{3}  $ , forming type II heterojunction. The 2-grade and 3-grade correspond to   $  CsPbI_{3}/CsPbBr_{0.3}I_{2.7}  $   and   $  CsPbI_{3}/CsPbBr_{0.3}I_{2.7}/CsPbBr_{0.7}I_{2.3}  $ , respectively. The 4-grade is 4 layers heterojunction consisting of   $  CsPbI_{3}/CsPbBr_{0.3}I_{2.7}/CsPbBr_{0.7}I_{2.3}/CsPbBrI_{2}  $ , just as depicted in energy level diagram (Figure 3a). Figure 3b depicts the variation of energy level sat the grade heterojunction

interface where Fermi levels balance at the same level due to the electron and hole flow in the opposite direction reaching to lowest CB and highest VB, respectively.  $  ^{[29]}  $   The grade heterojunction results in a reduction of the work function and a downward vacuum level shift at the interface. Moreover, the difference between the Fermi level and the valence band edge decreases with increasing Br substitution amount.

The energy level alignment and variation demonstrate the role of grade heterojunction as a hole extraction and conduction layer between the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  and counter electrode. Figure 3c,d,f show the schematic of the device structures studied in this work. In HTL-free device, excited electrons flow from  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  not only to electron extraction layer, but also to counter electrode, which is in the opposite direction for the photocurrent, and might cause serious interfacial electron-hole recombination (Figure 3c). By introducing  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_3 $  forming heterojunction (Figure 3d), an additional driving force for holes is generated at the heterojunction interface owing to the aid of built-in electric field of valance band offset. Moreover, the conduction band offset between the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  and  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_3 $  layers offers an energy barrier that restrains photogenerated electron from flowing to counter electrode (Figure 3d). For further increasing the grade to 2-, 3-, and 4-grade (Figure 3e), the multigrade type II heterojunction where both VB and CB edge array gradually creates a "hole channel," deriving hole conduction to counter electrode, efficiently, and provides more energy barriers that prevent the generation of reverse photocurrent inside the device.

In order to better understand the photovoltaic performance enhancement mechanism of PSCs including the   $  J_{sc}  $  ,   $  V_{oc}  $  , FF, and PCE, the corresponding issues are discussed as following. For the enhancement of the   $  J_{sc}  $  , two elucidations can be accounted. The improvement of hole collection efficiency, on the one hand, plays a key role in the enhancement of photocurrent density. By properly introducing grade   $  CsPbBr_{x}I_{3-x}  $   NC films with energy level (both VB and CB) alignment, holes can be efficiently extracted and transferred at the aid of built-in electric field, as a result of which, the photocurrent can be enhanced significantly. On the other hand, the potential barriers arising from type II heterojunction block the reverse transfer of excited electrons, which suppress the electron–hole recombination and decreases the loss of photocurrent. Moreover, the increase of grade of GHJ provides more carriers for recombination of reverse electrons with holes, and thus explains the reason for a gradual increase of   $  J_{sc}  $  .

For FF, the enhancement can be accounted for the suppression of interfacial electron–hole recombination owing to the multipotential barriers provided by grade heterojunction.  $  ^{[17]}  $   However, for the abnormal change in 1-grade and 4-grade in both   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF, we attribute this decrease to the relatively short minority carrier diffusion length (  $  W_{d}  $  ) of   $  CsPbBr_{x}I_{3-x}  $   NCs, which causes an un-depleted region as depicted in Figure 3d.  $  ^{[22]}  $   The photovoltaic performance measurement with respect to   $  CsPbBr_{x}I_{3-x}  $   NC film thickness gives more intuitive interpretation as shown in the Supporting Information. As a consequence,   $  J_{sc}  $  ,   $  V_{oc}  $  , and FF all are enhanced, resulting in a significant improvement in the PCE.

The thermal- and humidity-stability tests for the best-performance grade heterojunction PSCs are carried out in comparison with the devices based on conventional HTL (spiro-OMeTAD) and without HTL without encapsulation. For the thermal-stability measurements shown in Figure 5a,b, the effect of high temperature on the photovoltaic performance of different solar cells is revealed. With the temperature increasing (Figure 5a), the efficiency of PSCs based on spiro HTL shows a sharp decline to only 40% at 100 °C, while the efficiency of PCSs based on grade   $  CsPbX_{3}NC  $   film exhibits more stable to 75% at 100 °C high temperature. Similarly, at 50 °C working environment, the PSCs devices based on spiro HTL see the fast decrease to 20% just within 200 h. The samples in the absence of spiro HTL show a parallel decay rate. The PSCs based on grade   $  CsPbX_{3}NC  $   film possess the most stable photoelectric performance of 70% after 200 h at a temperature of 50 °C. In summary, high-performance and stable HTL-free perovskite solar cells based on  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3\text{-CsPbBr}_x\mathrm{I}_{3 - x} $  GHJ architecture is fabricated and characterized for the first time, which achieves a significantly enhanced PCE of  $ 11.33\% $  (nearly  $ 40\% $  increase compared with  $ 8.16\% $  for pure HTL-free devices). The improved photovoltaic performance originates from three advantages. The grade heterojunction presented demonstrates an enhancement in hole extraction and conduction from light absorption layer to the counter electrode, which is the first and foremost advantage for this new structure. The second advantage is that this GHJ architecture is effective to reduce the interfacial recombination loss by multigrade potential barriers. The third one can be assigned to the increase of open circuit voltage without modifying the light absorption layer. This work highlights the potential to improve the efficiency through a GHJ architecture strategy and offers a new way to solve the electron or hole extraction obstacle not only in HTL-free PSCs, but also in other structural systems of PSCs.

# Experimental Section

Material Synthesis: The colloidal   $  CsPbBr_{x}I_{3-x}  $   nanocrystals were synthesized as following.  $  ^{[23]}  $   Briefly, Cs-oleate solution was prepared at first by mixing   $  Cs_{2}CO_{3}  $   (0.8 g, Aladdin, 99.9%), 1-octadecene (ODE, 30 mL, Aladdin, 90%), and oleic acid (OA, 2.5 mL, Aladdin, 90%) into 100 mL 3-neck flask and heating to 150 °C to obtain a clear solution under   $  N_{2}  $  . Next, The   $  CsPbBr_{x}I_{3-x}  $   (taking   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   for example here) NC precursor was prepared by adding ODE (5 mL) and   $  PbI_{2}  $   (87 mg, Aladdin, 99.9%) into 25 mL 3-neck flask, drying under vacuum at 120 °C for 30 min, and then injecting 0.5 mL dried OA and 0.5 mL dried oleylamine (OLA, Aladdin, 80–90%) into this flask under the   $  N_{2}  $   at this temperature to dissolve the   $  PbI_{2}  $  . After the solution became clear, a suitable temperature (170 °C) was set and 0.4 mL prepared Cs-oleate solution was injected quickly. After 5 s, the obtained solution was put into ice bath to quench the reaction. The NCs were precipitated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 1 min and 12 000 rpm for 5 min with the moderate amount of MeOAc (Aladdin, 99.5%) to assist the precipitation. The acquired precipitate was redispersed in the octane (Aladdin, 99%) at a concentration of around 50 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  . For other   $  CsPbBr_{x}I_{3-x}  $   NCs, the addition amount of   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (Aladdin, 99.999%) and   $  PbI_{2}  $   depended on the stoichiometric ratio of Br/I in   $  CsPbBr_{x}I_{3-x}  $  .

Device Preparation: Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO, Opvtech) glass was etched first by Zn powder and diluted HCl (2 M) solution to obtain desirable pattern. Then, etched FTO glass was cleaned by acetone and ethanol, followed by oxygen plasma treatment for 20 min. Afterward, a thin TiO  $  _{2}  $  compact layer and mesoporous layer were prepared separately by spin-coating TiO  $  _{2}  $  precursor solution and TiO  $  _{2}  $  paste at 2500 rpm

for 30 s, followed by sintering at 500 °C for 30 min and 550 °C for 30 min, respectively. The MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  precursor solution was synthesized by dissolving methylammonium iodide (CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}  $  I) and lead iodide (PbI  $  _{2}  $  ) in N,N-dimethylformamide (Aladdin, 99%) at 1:1 molar ratio, and then the precursor solution was spin-coated on the TiO  $  _{2}  $  layer at 1000 rpm (10 s) and 5000 rpm (20 s) with dropping of toluene at the second spin-coating to control surface coverage, then annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. The preparation of CsPbBr  $  _{x}  $  I  $  _{3-x}  $  films consists of three stages as depicted in Figure S3 (Supporting Information). First, the CsPbBr  $  _{x}  $  I  $  _{3-x}  $  NC solution is spread over the surface of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  film, then spin-coating to a desirable speed to evaporate the solvent. Second, the ligand solution (Pb(OAc)  $  _{2}  $  (Aladdin, 99%) in anhydrous MeOAc is dropped on the NC film for several seconds followed by high-speed spin-coating. Third, the film is washed with neat MeOAc solvent by spin-coating. The procedure is repeated several times to build desirable thick films. Finally, an around 50 nm MoO  $  _{3}  $  (Aladdin, 99.9%) layer and a 50 nm Ag counter electrode were prepared by thermal evaporation.

Characterization: The composition and structure of obtained   $  CsPbX_{3}  $   NCs were characterized by X-ray diffractometer (Philips Rigaku D/Max-kA X-ray diffractometer) and high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM, Phillips Tecnai 20U-Twin). The morphology of   $  CsPbX_{3}  $   NC film and   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   film were characterized via field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, SU-70). The optical absorption and emission spectra were collected using a TU-1901 spectro-photometer and a Fluorescent transmitter (Hitachi, F4600), respectively. The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra (NEXUS 670) were used to characterize the surface functional group of NC film. The energy levels of   $  CsPbX_{3}  $   NC films were recorded by ultraviolet photoelectron spectrum (AXIS ULTRA DLD). The photoelectric data of current–voltage curves of solar devices were acquired via a solar simulator (Newport, Class 3A, 94023A) with a Keithley 2420 under simulated AM 1.5 G sunlight (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ). The solar cells measured were masked with a metal aperture to define the active area to be around 0.09 cm  $  ^{2}  $  . And a power source (Newport 300 W xenon lamp, 66920) equipped with a monochromator (Newport Cornerstone 260) and a multimeter (Keithley 2400) was used to record the IPCE. The time-resolved photoluminescence measurement were carried by FLS920 all functional fluorescence spectrometer (Edinburgh). The EIS was carried out under 1 sun light using a Princeton Parstate 2273A.

The stability measurements were performed based on the decay percentage of power conversion efficiency of perovskite solar cells for conversional HTL spiro-OMeTAD, HTL-free and 3-grade CsPbX $ _{3} $  NCs film, respectively. The experiments were lasted for 200 h with a test every 10 h. For the Normalized PCE-Temperature measurement, a hot plate was applied to regulate the temperature from 20 to 100 °C under the condition of isolation from atmosphere. For the measurement of thermal-stability as a function of times, the solar cells were kept in glovebox for excluding the effect of other conditions. The humidity-stability characteristics were conducted electric moister buster cabinet with a settled humidity of 40% at RT.
