# Synergistic Engineering of Natural Carnitine Molecules Allowing for Efficient and Stable Inverted Perovskite Solar Cells

Hsieh-Chih Chen,  $  ^{*}  $   Chieh-Ming Hung, and Chun-Hong Kuo

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Efficient control of the perovskite crystallization and passivation of the defects at the surface and grain boundaries of perovskite films have turned into the most important strategies to restrain charge recombination toward high-performance and long-term stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). In this paper, we employed a small amount of natural vitamin B (carnitine) with dual functional groups in the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  precursor solution to simultaneously passivate the positive- and negative-charged ionic defects, which would be beneficial for charge transport in the PSCs. In addition, such methodology can efficiently ameliorate crystallinity with texture, better film morphology, high surface

coverage, and longer charge carrier lifetime, as well as induce preferable energy level alignment. Benefiting from these advantages, the power conversion efficiency of PSCs significantly increases from 16.43 to 20.12% along with not only a higher open-circuit voltage of 1.12 V but also an outstanding fill factor of 82.78%.

KEYWORDS: MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  perovskites, carnitine doping, defects passivation, adjustable band gap, stability

# INTRODUCTION

Nowadays, methylammonium lead iodide (namely,   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   or   $  MAPbI_{3}  $  ) perovskites have drawn worthwhile attention as one of the most promising next-generation photovoltaics because of their excellent ambipolar charge mobilities, high absorption coefficient, small exciton binding energies, long charge carrier lifetimes and diffusion lengths, facile tuning of band gaps by varying compositions, as well as their cost-effective and easy solution manufacturing.  $  ^{1-5}  $   Perovskite solar cell (PSC) performance has significantly advanced through the incorporation of chemical additives,  $  ^{6-10}  $   methods for growing high-quality perovskite film,  $  ^{11-13}  $   charge extraction/transport material engineering,  $  ^{14-18}  $   and device architecture design.  $  ^{19,20}  $   Based on these tactics, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs has soared from 3.8 to 25.5% in the past decade, exhibiting a grand potential for commercial applications.  $  ^{21-23}  $   In spite of this, the poor stability of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $ , which consists of hydrophilic and volatile MA cation, related to the high sensitivity to moisture and heat is still a daunting challenge that is yet to be resolved for the outdoor applications.  $  ^{24-28}  $

PSCs can be divided into either regular (n–i–p) or inverted (p–i–n) architectures.  $  ^{19,20}  $   Inverted PSCs have gradually gained a growing interest because of their simple fabrication process and the exceptional steady-state output due to its negligible hysteresis and low temperature production, which demonstrates the potential for developing larger flexible solar cells.  $  ^{12,29,30}  $   Yet, the solar efficiency of inverted-type PSCs is

Additionally, the presence of a large number of defects, including amorphous regions, surface defects, vacancies, and grain boundaries of perovskite films, spontaneously produced by the solution process would dramatically impair the efficiency and stability of PSCs.  $  ^{31,32}  $   These defects are prototypical dangling bonds on the surface of perovskites, including undercoordinated ions from lead atoms or halide anions,  $  ^{33,34}  $   which could cause moisture and oxygen to penetrate into the perovskite and thereafter destroy the device stability.  $  ^{24-28}  $   Therefore, defect passivation has been widely investigated as an effective way to reduce the charge recombination rate and enhance the efficiency of PSCs.

Concretely, Lewis acids possess elegant passivation potentials for the defects with electron lone pairs, such as free iodide ions and lead iodine antiside defects,  $  ^{33,46}  $  whereas Lewis bases can be strongly bonded with the electron-deficient defects such as  $  Pb^{2+}  $  interstices.  $  ^{34,46}  $  Recently, Huang and co-workers reported that quaternary ammonium halides could simultaneously mend the positive- and negative-charged defects, resulting in high PCE and long-term stability.  $  ^{5}  $  Very recently, zwitterion molecules were demonstrated to have excellent passivation capabilities for positive- and negative-charged defects.  $  ^{10}  $  Other organic donor-  $  \pi  $  -acceptor molecules with different electron density distributions also displayed superior passivation effects coupled with enhanced  $  V_{OC}  $  of devices.  $  ^{47,48}  $  Looking into the passivation materials used for PSCs, we can infer that these passivation compounds typically consist of exceedingly active chemical substituents with electron lone pairs, including nitrogen, sulfur, oxygen, phosphorus, and halogen atoms as well as organic functional groups, in which the functional substituents interact with undercoordinated lead ions, halide ions, or organic MA cations in the perovskites to carry out the defect passivation. In addition to the passivation and optimization of the perovskites, interfacial engineering has been made to improve the charge extraction and enhance the chemical stability of the electron or hole transporting layer (ETL or HTL) toward boosting the device stability. Furthermore, as the topmost layer in the inverted PSC, ETL possesses a crucial influence on the device performance and stability because of the fact that ETL approaches to atmosphere. Typically, the defects in perovskite films are mainly situated on the film surfaces and grain boundaries,  $  ^{49-52}  $  and thus these defects are in close contact with ETL. In this regard, an ideal ETL for inverted PSCs should have good

solubility in common solvents, high thermal stability, high electron mobility, high hydrophobicity, and suitable cascade energy level for efficient electron extraction, effectively reduce surface traps at the perovskite/ETL interface, and also be processed at low temperatures without impairing underlying layers as well as be stable and dense to block metal, oxygen, and moisture interpenetration into the device.  $  ^{50}  $

Herein, we report, for the first time, that the incorporation of a small amount of natural vitamin B (carnitine) comprising carboxyl and quaternary ammonium functional groups into  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  PSCs can efficaciously control the perovskite with good crystallinity and texture, minimum pinholes, high surface coverage, augmented charge carrier lifetime, and preferable energy level alignment, and thus ameliorate both the device efficiency and stability. The results can be attributed to the effective passivation of undercoordinated lead ions and iodide anions in the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  film and forming better interfacial energy-level alignment in the PSCs to facilitate efficient charge transport and extraction, leading to an enhancement in  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  and FF of the carnitine-modified device. By optimizing the content of  $ 0.05\% $  carnitine, a high stabilized PCE of  $ 20.12\% $  was obtained with a  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  of  $ 1.12\mathrm{V} $ , a  $ J_{\mathrm{SC}} $  of  $ 21.70\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $ , and an FF of  $ 82.78\% $ . This method provides a green, low-cost, and widely applicable strategy to fulfil high-performance PSCs, which is suitable for future commercialization.

The influence of the carnitine dopants on the perovskite crystal was probed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurement (Figure 3d). Compared with the pristine film, the perovskite film with a small amount of carnitine (0.05%) additive possesses higher diffraction intensity, indicating that carnitine could improve the film crystallinity without disruption of the perovskite phase formation. The intensity ratio of the (110) plane at 13.9° to the (312) plane at 31.8° increased from 4.22 to 6.11 upon addition of 0.05% carnitine, suggesting favorable growth in the (110) direction. In other words, the interaction between carnitine and the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  perovskite precursor hindered the crystal growth and thus resulted in the preferred growth direction of the perovskite film. Furthermore, in order to gain an in-depth insight into the structure–function relationship on the morphological variety, we then performed synchrotron grazing incidence wide angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) measurements as shown in Figure 3e. The diffraction peaks observed from the GIWAXS profiles are in line with those from the XRD patterns. Pristine MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  film exhibits randomly oriented features with strongly scattered rings (Figure S7, Supporting Information). Comparatively, the

carnitine-addition  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  film demonstrates that the diffraction pattern of perovskite remains with brighter spots, suggesting its superior crystallinity without structural changes. Figure 3f illustrates the normalized azimuth angle plots on the (110) plane of the pristine and  $ 0.05\% $  carnitine-modified perovskites, which were integrated from the GIWAXS plots. In sharp contrast to the pristine film, the carnitine-modified perovskite manifests a very incisive peak at the azimuth angle of  $ 90^{\circ} $ . Moreover, the narrower full width at half maximum discloses that the introduction of carnitine molecules is beneficial for the crystal growth of perovskite in the out-of-plane direction, which would promote the charge transport in the PSCs.

To obtain deeper insights into the surface defects, we further delved into the influence of carnitine on the charge transport and electronic properties of the PSCs by conductive atomic force microscopy (c-AFM) with nanoscale resolution. The surface morphology and the corresponding current mapping images of the perovskite films under a DC bias voltage of 1.0 V applied to the tip are comparatively shown in Figure 4. The topographic and peak force error images of the pristine and carnitine-modified perovskite films are shown in panels a, d and b, e of Figure 4. The small decrease in the surface roughness of the carnitine-added film implies that carnitine indeed fills into the defects and thus diminishes the topographic difference between the surface and grain boundaries. Furthermore, the corresponding current mappings are shown in Figure 4c,f. The average current for carnitine-modified device increases from 52 to 88 fA after the incorporation of 0.05% carnitine. Accordingly, the incorporation of a small amount of carnitine into MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  perovskite can effectively ameliorate the device efficiency by promoting the strong interaction between electron lone pairs from the carnitine molecule and undercoordinated lead atom, forming conducting channels in perovskite film, which thereby benefits the charge transport.  $  ^{54}  $  These studies indicate that carnitine plays a vital role in charge transport behavior, which is in good agreement with the enhancement in  $  V_{OC}  $  and FF (vide infra).

Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved PL (TRPL) decay measurements of these perovskite films were performed to estimate the effect of dual functional groups of carnitine on the charge transport and defect passivation. The good passivation effect of carnitine was evidenced by the PL spectra, where the carnitine-modified film shows a large rise of about seven times in PL emission together with 8 nm blue shift as compared with the pristine counterpart (Figure 6a). The blue-shifted PL spectra are a result of the suppressed number of shallow trap density on account of the passivation effect of carnitine, which fills the defects on surfaces and grain boundaries.  $  ^{42,56}  $   This result is also in agreement with the SCLC and EIS measurements (vide infra). Consequently, both the   $  V_{OC}  $   and FF of the carnitine-modified PSCs are increased by virtue of the low energy loss, arising from the reduced nonradiative recombination and efficient charge transport. In addition, TRPL decay in this study can be expressed by a biexponential decay composed of a fast component   $  \tau_{1}  $   and a slow component   $  \tau_{2}  $  . Fast decay lifetime is related to the nonradiative recombination caused by charge-trapping defects, whereas slow decay lifetime is associated with free charge recombination in the perovskite film. As shown in Figure 6b, the pristine perovskite displays fast and slow decay lifetimes of   $  \tau_{1}=18  $   ns and   $  \tau_{2}=263  $   ns, respectively. By contrast, the carnitine film reveals decay lifetimes of fast   $  \tau_{1}=22  $   ns and slow   $  \tau_{2}=383  $   ns. The longer PL lifetime suggests obviously reduced density of defects and related charge recombination, which possibly contributes to the enhanced   $  V_{OC}  $ , as shown in the J-V measurements.  $  ^{57,58}  $

# CONCLUSIONS

In summary, we have successfully demonstrated that the introduction of dual-functionalized carnitine to the perovskite precursor for the fabrication of the perovskite film can simultaneously ameliorate the PCE and device stability. The carnitine-modified perovskite film possessed lower trap density, leading to an enhanced charge carrier lifetime and a more homogeneous conductivity across the perovskite film. The design strategy for carnitine-modified perovskite films is to provide a three-in-one functionality: fine-tuning of the band gap alignment to produce the intimate interface, effective defect passivation, and augmented the charge carrier lifetime, and it induced the (110) preferred crystal growth orientation, which proved to be a new and far-reaching strategy for acquiring highly efficient PSCs. Finally, the PCE of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -based inverted PSCs was augmented from 16.43 to  $ 20.12\% $  based on an optimized addition of  $ 0.05\% $  carnitine. This work presents an effective pathway for combining the morphology tailoring and defect passivation of perovskite films and opens up new opportunities for high-performing PSCs toward their great potential.
