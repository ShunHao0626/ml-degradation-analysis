Abstract The efficiency and stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) are hampered by the undesirable charge recombination on defective surface of polycrystalline perovskite grains and inferior charge transfer at relevant contact interfaces. Herein, we discovered that spontaneous anchoring of long-chain oleylamine (OA) ligands onto perovskite film surface can simultaneously improve the efficiency and stability of PSCs made by scalable blade-coating. The ultrathin insulating OA layer on perovskite film surface can achieve an ingenious surface/interface functionalization that plays synergistic roles in effective defect passivation and efficient charge tunneling. Blade-coated PSCs with OA functionalization acquire champion power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of  $ 21.5\% $ , accompanied by a surprisingly high fill factor up to  $ 86.3\% $ , which can be ascribed to the reduced series resistance, facilitated electron tunneling and suppressed interfacial charge recombination at relevant interface. The OA-functionalized perovskite film is much more resistant to moisture, and improves the device shelf lifetime to  $ >1000 $  hours, and operation stability to  $ >500 $  hours under continuous one sun illumination with  $ 96\% $  initial efficiency retained.

Keywords: additives; charge tunneling; defect passivation; perovskite solar cells; interfacial charge recombination

# Introduction

Organic-inorganic hybrid metal halide perovskite materials have attracted enormous research interest in a variety of optoelectronic applications, especially solar cells, owing to their advantages, such as high absorption coefficient, tunable bandgap, long carrier diffusion length and low-temperature solution processability.[1-3] To date, the power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been elevated to a certified value of  $ 25.2\% $ , which is already higher than other thin-film photovoltaic technologies, thus showcasing great potential for future commercialization.[4]

Over the past decade, continuous efforts have been made in terms of composition optimization, film processing technique developments and interface engineering, to improve the short-circuit current density ( $ J_{\text{sc}} $ ) and open-circuit voltage ( $ V_{\text{oc}} $ ) to their theoretical limits.[5-6] However, attempts to maximize the fill factor ( $ FF $ ) to an ideal value should not be overlooked, since almost all record efficiencies of reported PSCs exhibited  $ FFs $  greater than 81%. Thus,  $ FF $  has become imperative to further enhancing device efficiency that now approaches the Shockley–Queisser limit of 31%.[7] In general,  $ FF $  is strongly influenced by the charge transfer and recombination at the corresponding surface/interface between the perovskite layer and carrier transport layer. Through surface passivation, gradient heterojunction management or interfacial band alignment/contact engineering, high  $ FFs $  ranging 82%-85% have been achieved and reported for PSCs.[8-11] One of the most useful strategies to improve the  $ FF $  is to introduce an additional thin insulating layer, such as polystyrene (PS), polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) or Cytop (a fluorine containing insulating polymer, poly(perfluorobutenylvinylether)) on the surface of perovskite film to form the tunneling contact at the perovskite/ETL interface.[12-14] The concept of tunneling contact has been widely used in silicon solar cells, featuring a thin layer of insulating oxide or amorphous silicon on the surface of a heavily-doped silicon layer or indium tin oxide (ITO) glass.[15] Typically, the tunneling junction selectively allows one type of carrier transport to move from the photoactive layer to cathode, while prohibiting another type of carrier from passing through the interface. In PSCs, the tunneling contact could spatially separate photogenerated carriers, for instance, boosting electron extraction while blocking holes, thus reducing the interfacial non-radiative recombination loss. Though promising, the extra step of surface treatment is presently not compatible to the scalable fabrication of PSCs. In contrast, direct perovskite ink formulation is more desirable to tailor the surface/interface electronic properties of perovskite films via management of the heterogeneous additive distribution, and control of chemical interactions between additive molecules and perovskite materials.

Short-chain amines and their derivatives have proven to be useful ingredients in perovskite inks, benefitting the efficiency and stability of PSCs.[16] In general, the neutral   $  -NH_{2}  $   or positively charged   $  -NH_{3}^{+}  $  headgroups of amines and their derivatives are able to effectively passivate under-coordinated ions in perovskite films. However, the defect passivation often comes at a cost of hindered charge transport owing to the formation of quasi-2D or 2D/3D mixed perovskite, with insulating alkyl chains existing between neighboring perovskite crystals and/or within the spacing of the adjacent inorganic   $  [PbI_{6}]^{4-}  $   octahedral layers. Common strategies are to shorten the alkyl chains (< 8 carbons) or employ conjugated/fused benzene rings, which partially address this dilemma.[17-18] Compared with their short-chain counterparts, long-chain amines are more easily and spontaneously repelled to the top surface of the perovskite films to form a tunneling layer, owing to the strong repulsive force between long-chain hydrophobic alkyl units and the perovskite crystals. This provides an “out-of-the-box” solution to achieve effective defect passivation without sacrificing charge transport capability. One long-chain amine, e.g., oleylamine (OA), has been commonly used as a capping ligand to control the size and dimension of perovskite nanocrystals for perovskite-based light emitting diodes. However, the electroluminescence performance has been largely limited by the insulating properties of this long alkyl chain composed of 18 carbons. Hence, there is almost no expectation that OA can be utilized in high-efficiency PSCs.

Herein, we demonstrate, for the first time, that simply incorporating a trace amount of OA ligands into the perovskite ink can achieve ingenious surface/interface functionalization with synchronous defect passivation and charge tunneling for reduced non-radiative recombination loss. In particular, the spontaneous repelling behavior and surface-anchoring of the OA layer on top of the perovskite films resulted in robust passivation of deep-level surface defects via strong chemical interaction between   $  -NH_{2}  $   terminated groups of OA and undercoordinated ions of perovskite, while the insulating layer of long alkyl chains with epitaxial orientation and nanoscale thickness could serve as a tunneling layer for facilitated charge

# Results and Discussion

Solution-processed polycrystalline perovskite films inevitably contain a high density of crystallographic defects, such as uncoordinated ions and dangling bonds at surfaces and/or grain boundaries, which can result in charge recombination via non-radiative channels, thus impairing device performance.[21] For an inverted  $  p - i - n  $  PSC device, there are several non-radiative recombination channels close to the contact junction between the perovskite layer and electron transport layer (ETL) owing to the presence of numerous deep-level surface defects, which cause serious interfacial recombination loss. In general, there are four major non-radiative recombination channels within PSCs (Fig. 1a): photo-injected electrons in the ETL travel back to recombine with holes in the perovskite layer (channel 1); photo-injected holes in the hole transport layer (HTL) travel back to recombine with electrons in the perovskite layer (channel 2), and; surface traps and vacancies that detrimentally capture charge carriers during carrier generation and charge transport processes (channels 3 and 4), with the trapped charges recombining through nonradiative processes or being reemitted to their respective bands.[22] All of these non-radiative charge recombination pathways hamper device performance.[22-24] The key to suppressing non-radiative recombination in inverted PSCs is to simultaneously reduce the densities of surface defects via effective passivation while spatially separating the photo-generated electrons and holes at the perovskite/ETL interface, which beneficially reduces the interfacial recombination loss, thus enhancing the device

performance. Herein, we intentionally selected OA ligands featuring a very long hydrophobic alkyl chain core with up to 18 carbons and an  $ -\mathrm{NH}_2 $  group at the tail. A trace amount of 300 ppm OA was added into the methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI₃) perovskite ink for the fabrication of perovskite films (denoted as MAPbI₃-OA) via a blade-coating method (Fig. 1b). The OA molecules are spontaneously extruded and tend to anchor on the surface of the perovskite film with  $ -\mathrm{NH}_2 $  tails interacting with under-coordinated ions, consequently exposing the long alkyl chain. The amine additive normally functions as a Lewis base, which is able to form coordination bonds with perovskite by sharing the lone electron pair of the N atom with the empty 6p orbit of  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $ . This is also evidenced by the fact that adding some amount of OA into a lead(II) iodide ( $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ )/dimethylformamide (DMF) solution changes the solution appearance from transparent to cloudy while there is no change in methylammonium (MAI)/IPA solution with OA addition, indicating there must be strong molecular interaction between OA molecules and positively charged Pb clusters (Fig. S1). Considering OA is liquid at room temperature, we believed the anchoring of the OA ligands should be self-limited to an ultra-thin monolayer on the perovskite surface. This is because the interaction between the OA molecules is too weak to keep them in solid form. We calculated the length of the single OA molecule via simple modeling (Fig. S2), and inferred that the thickness of the OA monolayer was approximately 2.46 nm, which is thin but sufficiently condensed to serve as tunneling contact at the perovskite/ETL interface. We further examined the presence of OA in the perovskite films after thermal annealing. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra in Fig. S3 indicate the OA was still present within the bladed perovskite films with the observable characteristic spectral features (i.e., C-H stretching at 2780-3000 cm⁻¹) after high temperature annealing. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) study revealed that the Pb 4f signal peak shifted to a higher binding energy upon OA modification (Fig. 1c), owing to the interaction between OA and under-coordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $ , which marginally affected the electronic cloud density surrounding  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $ .[25] Additionally, the XPS measurement was only

We further measured the dark currents of hole-only devices featuring a structure of ITO/PTAA/MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   modified with or without OA/PTAA/Au (Fig. S4a). The OA-modified device had a lower hole current compared to the control device without any additive, again confirming the electron tunneling and hole blocking functions of the OA layer anchored on the perovskite film surface.

The introduction of a considerable amount of short-chain amines into a 3D perovskite precursor is associated with forming quasi-2D perovskite or 2D/3D mixed perovskite.[27] Interestingly, we found that despite the large amount of OA added (i.e. up to  $ 0.6\mathrm{wt}\% $ , relative to the weight of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ ), there were no XRD peaks below  $ 2\theta = 10^{\circ} $ , indicating that OA with its long alkyl chain and large molecular size does to some extent suppress the formation of 2D Ruddlesden-Popper phase perovskite (Fig. S6).

The OA molecules have been commonly used as surface ligands to control the sizes and dimensions of colloidal perovskite nanocrystals. Herein, particular attention was paid to studying the influence of OA on tuning the morphology of perovskite thin films. We propose a plausible growth mechanism for perovskite films with the assistance of OA ligands, involving the consecutive processes of molecular interaction, crystal growth and self-assembly of an OA layer (Fig. 2a). As the crystal growth of perovskite film is indeed a process of excluding the heterogeneous additive ingredients along with the grain-coarsening. In this case, the long-chain OA is easily to be expelled to the film surface owing to the large repulsive force induced by the adjacent perovskite grains anchored with long alkyl-chain ligands. Specifically, OA molecules firstly interact with perovskite species and then anchor on the surface of colloidal perovskite nuclei/particles. In this case, the OA molecules served as surface ligands suppressing the perovskite crystal growth, which resulted in the dramatic reduction of average grain size from up to  $ 1.3\mu \mathrm{m} $  for the control  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  film to 420 nm the for  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -OA film (Fig. 2b-2c). Subsequently, the OA ligands are spontaneously extruded from the perovskite crystals to the film surface during the thermal annealing process, which plays critical roles in surface/interface functionalization and passivation. During crystal growth, the OA-anchored perovskite

We fabricated the PSC device with an inverted  $  p-i-n  $  architecture of ITO/HTL/perovskite/ETL/Cu, as illustrated in Fig. 3a. The 500 nm thick blade-coated MAPbI $ _3 $  or MAPbI $ _3 $ -OA perovskite films were composed of columnar monolithic grains, sandwiched between top and bottom carrier-selective contacts (Fig. S7). Current density-voltage ( $  J-V  $ ) curves for the best-performing PSCs based on MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite films modified without or with OA are shown in Fig. 3b. A champion PCE of up to 21.5% was achieved for the MAPbI $ _3 $ -OA based PSC, remarkably outperforming the control MAPbI $ _3 $  based PSC at 18.6% (Table 1). The enhanced device performance with OA modification was attributed to the concurrent improvement of  $  J_{sc}  $ ,  $  V_{oc}  $  and  $  FF  $  parameters. Specifically,  $  V_{oc}  $  was enhanced from 1.08 V to 1.13 V, implying a very effective defect passivation induced by OA modification. The boosted  $  J_{sc}  $  and  $  FF  $  were attributed to the modified perovskite/ETL interface, with reduced series resistance ( $  R_s  $ ) from 3.6 Ω to 2.2 Ω. Specifically, the reduced  $  R_s  $  should be ascribed to the alteration of interfacial charge transfer properties induced by surface anchoring of OA monolayers at perovskite/ETL interface, which facilitated charge extraction from perovskite to C60 enabled by tunneling charge transfer and suppressed interfacial charge recombination benefited from effective passivation of surface defects.[19] Together these improved charge collection efficiency, as discussed below. Notably, a consistently high  $  FF  $  exceeding 82%, with a maximum value up to 86%, was achieved for a batch of 30 PSCs with OA modification (Fig. 3c). Several representative  $  J-V  $  curves for the MAPbI $ _3 $ -OA based PSCs with extremely high  $  FF  $  are displayed in Fig. S8, with corresponding photovoltaic parameters summarized in Table S1.

To better uncover the extraordinary functions of OA molecules, we carried out a series of optical, photoelectric and electrochemical measurements for the perovskite films and/or corresponding devices. Specifically, time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurement were performed to provide insight into charge recombination

In general, the relatively fast decay component  $ (\tau_{1}) $  was assigned to the charge trapping process, while the much slower decay component  $ (\tau_{2}) $  was related to the de-trapping process or carrier recombination process.[31] The TRPL decay in  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -OA film was largely dominated by the slow decay process, which showed a decreased ratio of  $ \tau_{1} $  from  $ 20.43\% $  for  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  to  $ 9.63\% $  for  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -OA, and exhibited a prolonged carrier recombination lifetime  $ (\tau_{2}) $ . We then used the thermal admittance spectroscopy (TAS), a well-established characterization technique, to quantify the trap density of states (tDOS) of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  and  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -OA devices. In general, the deeper trap region (0.40-0.55 eV) can be assigned to defects at the film surface, while the shallower trap region (0.30-0.40 eV) corresponds to traps at grain boundaries.[19] The OA passivation has significantly reduced deep level traps and/or defects on the film surface in the main (Fig. 4a), confirming that robust surface passivation originated from the anchored OA ligands. The space charge limited current (SCLC) characterization showed that the trap-filled voltage of the OA-modified device was smaller than the control device, again proving the beneficial role of OA passivation on healing surface traps and defects (Fig. S4b). The transient photovoltage (TPV) decay was characterized by irradiating the open-circuit device under one sun illumination and triggering a small transient photovoltaic signal by laser pulses (337 nm, 4 ns).[35] The carrier recombination lifetime increased from  $ 0.58~\mu \mathrm{s} $  to  $ 0.75~\mu \mathrm{s} $  upon OA passivation (Fig. 4b). The light intensity dependence of  $  V_{\mathrm{oc}}  $  was measured to understand the charge recombination process in the PSCs, from which can be deduced the ideal factor ( $  n  $ ) by using the equation  $  \boldsymbol{n} = \frac{kT}{q} \times \frac{\Delta V_{\mathrm{oc}}}{\Delta \ln J_L}  $  (where  $  k, T, q  $  and  $  J_{\mathrm{L}}  $  are the Boltzmann constant, temperature, elementary charge and photogenerated current density, respectively). This result indicates that the non-radiative (i.e., trap-assisted) recombination process was effectively suppressed by the OA passivation.[37]

Femtosecond (fs) transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy was used to characterize and understand the ultrafast carrier dynamics, i.e., charge extraction and collection at related interfaces within the PSCs. For the TA measurement, the perovskite films are sandwiched between PTAA HTL and   $  C_{60}  $   ETL contacts (Fig. 4d). As shown in Fig. 4e and 4f, contour plots of the TA spectrum for both MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   and MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  -OA exhibited distinct ground-state bleaching (GSB) peaks at approximately 750 nm. # Conclusion

A simple ligand-anchoring strategy has been demonstrated to simultaneously alter the surface and interface charge transfer properties of bladed perovskite films for efficient and stable solar cells. We provided an “out-of-the-box” idea of employing OA molecules with long alkyl chains, which could be spontaneously extruded out of, and self-assemble into an ultra-thin layer on the perovskite film surface. The OA participates in crystal growth modulation, robustly passivates surface defects, and facilitates charge extraction and collection, thus reducing the series resistance and interfacial recombination loss. Blade-coated PSCs with OA modification showed improved efficiency up to 21.5% along with a surprisingly high FF that exceeded 86%. The devices exhibited enhanced moisture tolerance and operational stability. We anticipate that the concept of surface/interface functionalization can be universally applied to other perovskites of arbitrary compositions for the fabrication of optoelectronic devices with superior performance and stability in a low-cost and scalable manner.
