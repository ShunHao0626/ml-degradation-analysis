# ARTICLEINFO

Defects can easily form at the surfaces and grain boundaries of solution-processed organic-inorganic halide perovskites films, leading to undesired non-radiative recombination and ion migration in the device. Consequently, this limits the performance of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Herein, a new organic ammonium halide salt with cyano (-CN) functional group, 4-cyanobenzenaminium iodide (CNBAI), was developed to passivate the defects in the bulk of perovskite films. It was found that the CNBAI can improve the film morphology of the perovskite layer and passivate the various types of defects in the perovskite. On the one hand, the -CN group could bind with the under-coordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  and form intermolecular halogen bonds with the iodide atom in perovskite, as illustrated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy measurements. On the other hand, CNBAI could fill the iodide vacancy with its own iodide ions. For the first time, we demonstrate experimentally that the -CN group can simultaneously interact with  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  and form CN-I halogen bond in the perovskite. In addition, the introduced CNBAI improved energy alignment between the perovskite and adjacent charge transport layers. Finally, the PSCs fabricated with CNBAI-based perovskite films achieved the best PCE of  $ 22.4\% $  with negligible hysteresis and displayed good long-term stability.

# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) employing organic-inorganic halide perovskites as the absorbing layer has become a shiny photovoltaic technology. Organic-inorganic halide perovskites own eminent optoelectronic performance, such as superior light absorption, high carrier mobility, long carrier diffusion lengths, large defect tolerance, etc. [1-4]. Moreover, with the development of fabrication process in recent years, such as solvent engineering [5-8], compositional engineering [9-11], and passivation engineering [12-21], PSCs has achieved power conversion efficiency (PCE) up to certified  $ 25.5\% $ , outperforming other third-generation solar cells. However, since the perovskite layer as a polycrystalline film is usually prepared through a low-temperature solution process, many defects tend to form at the surface and grain boundaries (GBs) of perovskites [22]. These defects, such as interstitials and vacancies, are the non-radiative recombination centers of

photo-generated carriers, which heavily influence the resulting energy conversion efficiency   $  [23,24]  $  . Moreover, the defect can cause ion migration and diffusion   $  [25–27]  $  , and accelerate moisture (oxygen) permeation into the perovskite films, which are detrimental to the stability of the devices   $  [28,29]  $  . Thus, it is imperative to eliminate or reduce such defects in the perovskite films   $  [30]  $  .

Passivation is an efficient method to eliminate or reduce defects in the perovskite films   $  [27,30]  $  . Many strategies have already been applied to passivate the defects in perovskite films, such as non-stoichiometric   $  PbI_{2}  $     $  [31,32]  $  , ionic liquids   $  [33,34]  $  , polymers   $  [35]  $  , organic molecules   $  [36–39]  $  , organic halide salts   $  [16,23]  $  , low-dimensional perovskites   $  [40–42]  $  , and fullerene derivatives   $  [43,44]  $  . Organic molecules with carboxyl, amino, carbonyl, phosphine oxides, and pyridine functional groups have been widely explored due to the contained O, N, and P atoms with electron lone pairs, which can interact with the under-coordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   defects. For example, they were added in the precursor solution as additives to form passivated low-dimensional perovskites in the bulk of perovskite films, such as butylammonium iodide (BAI) [40] and phenethylammonium iodide (PEAI) [45,46].

Meanwhile, some of them deposited on the surface of as-prepared perovskite films form an independent salts layer   $  [16,23]  $   or transform into low-dimensional perovskites to passivate the defects   $  [47–51]  $  . As far as the material itself is concerned, combining the organic ammonium halide salts with functional groups may provide multiple benefits. Recently, our group first adopted the fluorinated PEAI (F-PEAI) to passivate the surface defects of the perovskite film and get the best PCE of 23.8%   $  [41,52,53]  $  . Very recently, Min et al. treated the as-prepared 3D perovskite with OMe-PEAI to realize a certified PCE of 25.5%, which is the record value in the normal architecture devices   $  [17]  $  . Degani et al. incorporated F-PEAI at both the bottom and top interfaces of the perovskite active layer, obtaining a record PCE of 23.7% in inverted architecture devices   $  [54]  $  . However, most of the functional organic ammonium halide salts were deployed on the surface of perovskite films   $  [12,55]  $  , and their passivation effect in the bulk of perovskite films was less mentioned   $  [56–58]  $  .

Previous reports showed that cyano (-CN) groups with electron lone pairs in N could effectively passivate the defects of perovskite and enhance the performance of resulting devices   $  [59–61]  $  . Our previous report also showed that cyano groups introduced into the hole transport materials could effectively passivate the surface defects of perovskite and suppress the devices hysteresis   $  [21]  $  . Here, we developed a new cyano (-CN) group substituted organic ammonium halide salt, namely 4-cyanobenzenaminium iodide (CNBAI, shown in Fig. 1a), and introduced it into the sequentially deposited perovskite films together with the organic cation solution in isopropanol. The electrostatic surface potential (ESP) map is helpful to visualize the charge distributions and physiochemical features of the molecules   $  [21,62–64]  $  . The ESP map of CNBAI from density functional theory (DFT) calculations (Fig. 1b) showed that the cyano groups in CNBAI present high electron densities, which can act as Lewis base sites to interact with defects like under-coordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   cations in the perovskite. Moreover, CNBAI has a high dipole moment up to 14.43 D due to the strong electron-withdrawing cyano substituting at the para position of the

benzene ring, (Fig. 1c) which may favor the charge separation in the perovskite films [65–67].

The CNBAI showed multiple roles, such as improving the perovskite film morphology and passivating the immediate defects. For example, the -CN group could interact with the under-coordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   [61,68] and form supramolecular interactions [69] (e.g., halogen bonds) with iodide ions in perovskite. In addition, CNBAI could fill the iodide vacancy with its own iodide ions. These interactions were illustrated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) measurements. Significantly, the coexistence of   $  -CN:Pb^{2+}  $   interactions and   $  CN\cdots I  $   halogen bonds in the perovskite are demonstrated experimentally for the first time.

Furthermore, these passivated perovskite films exhibited enhanced radiative efficiencies due to reduced defect concentration as indicated by the steady-state photoluminescence (PL), time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL), and trap densities measurements. Moreover, the ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) measurements showed improved interfacial band alignment between the perovskite and charge transport layers. As a result, the best PCE of the planar n-i-p PSCs based on  $ (\mathrm{FAPbI}_3)_x(\mathrm{MAPbBr}_3)_y $  perovskite film was improved from  $ 21.26\% $  to  $ 22.4\% $  with reduced hysteresis and enhanced aging stability up to  $ 864\mathrm{h} $ .

# 2.2. The synthesis of CNBAI

A solution of 4-Aminobenzonitrile (40 mmol) (98 wt%, Nanjing Kangmanlin Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd.) in 50 mL dichloromethane was cooled to 0 °C in an ice bath. Next, 60 mmol hydroiodic acid (55.0–58.0 wt% in   $  H_{2}O  $  , Aladdin) was added slowly, and the reaction mixture was allowed to warm to room temperature over 2 h. During the reaction process, an appropriate amount of ethanol was added to dissolve the crude product. Afterward, remove all volatiles with a rotary evaporator. Then, the solids were rinsed with dichloromethane. Finally, repeated crystallization from boiling ethanol and rinsed with diethyl ether yielded the title compound as a slightly yellowish powder (27 mmol, 67.5% yield).   $  {}^{1}  $  H NMR (500 MHz, DMSOD6): δ 7.48 (m, 2H), 6.79 (m, 2H), 6.50 (s, 3H,   $  NH_{3}  $  ).   $  {}^{13}  $  C NMR (126 MHz,

# 2.3. Fabrication of the devices

The FTO substrate was sequentially washed with deionized water, acetone, and ethanol for 10 min, respectively. Then, after the dry clean substrates were treated by plasma for 10 min, a thin layer of   $  SnO_{2}  $   nanoparticle film (  $  SnO_{2}  $   colloid precursor diluted by deionized water, 1:3, weight ratio) were spin-coated on the FTO substrates at 3000 rpm for 30 s and annealed in ambient air at 150 °C for 30 min. The substrates were then treated by plasma for 10 min before transferring into the glovebox. A typical two-step sequential deposition method was deployed to fabricate perovskite films. # 2.4. Film and device characterization

The surface morphology was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Apreo S) and atomic force microscopy (AFM, Multimode-8 J, America). The cross-sectional view morphology of the perovskite films was observed using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM, Apreo S). The Ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption and reflection spectra of deposited films were measured by a spectrophotometer (Agilent Cary 5000). Photoluminescence was measured at room temperature on a fluorescence spectrophotometer (FLS980, Edinburgh Instruments). The steady-state PL emissions were measured under 460 nm light source excitation using a monochromatized Xe lamp, while the TRPL decays were carried out with a pulsed diode laser excitation source. The XPS was performed using an X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy system (Axis Supra, Shimadzu) with Al Kα X-ray radiation (1486.6 eV) as the X-ray source, and C-C (C1s) peak at 284.8 eV is used as a charge correction reference. UPS measurement was conducted on a photoelectron spectrometer (Axis Supra, Shimadzu) using a non-monochromated He I photon source ( $ h\nu = 21.22 $  eV). FTIR spectrum analysis was performed using an FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50) with an ATR accessory. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the films were obtained from an X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku Miniflex 600, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation. The photovoltaic performance of devices was measured with a Keithley 2401 source meter using a solar simulator (SS-F5–3A, Enlitech) at 100 mW cm $ ^{-2} $  illumination (AM 1.5 G) equipped with a calibrated Si reference cell (SRC-2020, Enlitech). The J-V curves of the PSCs were measured in reverse scan (from 1.2 to -0.1 V) or forward scan (from -0.1 to 1.2 V) modes with a Step 0.01 V. The active area of devices was defined by a metal shadow mask of 0.1 cm $ ^{2} $ . External quantum efficiency (EQE) was obtained on a computer-controlled quantum efficiency measuring instrument (QE-R, Enlitech). Stability test: The dark long-term stability assessment of the perovskite solar cells was carried out by repeating the J-V characterizations over various times. The unsealed devices were stored under an ambient atmosphere with 20 ± 10% relative humidity at room temperature. The electron-only devices (FTO/SnO $ _{2} $ /perovskite/PCBM/Ag) were fabricated for the trap densities measurements. The dark J-V characteristics of the devices were measured by a Keithley 2401 source meter in the range of 0–5 V. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were carried out under dark at applied voltages (-0.5 V) using an electrochemical workstation (Zennium Zahner, Germany) with an AC perturbation of 10 mV ranging from 100 mHz to 1 MHz, at room temperature with 40–50% humidity.

# 3. Calculations

DFT calculations were carried out with Gaussian 09 using the spin-unrestricted hybrid density functional B3LYP [70] and the 6–31G(d) basis set. The calculated molecular electrostatic surface potential (ESP) result was obtained with the Multiwfn 3.7 program [71]. The dipole moments of CNBAI were calculated by DFT.

# 4. Results and discussion

Fig. 1d depicts the process of perovskite film preparation as detailed in Section 2.3. CNBAI as an additive was deployed in organic ammonium halide precursor to fabricate the high-quality perovskite films. The described passivation mechanism of CNBAI on the perovskite is shown in Fig. 2a. The CNBAI molecules mainly distribute at the perovskite GBs and chemically anchor to adjacent perovskite grains. In these cases, the -CN groups are expected to interact with the under-coordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  and form supramolecular interactions (e.g., halogen bonds) with iodide atoms in perovskite. Meanwhile, the CNBAI molecules can also fill the iodide vacancies with their own iodide ions.

To confirm the interaction behavior between CNBAI and   $  PbI_{2}  $   during the intercalation step of sequential deposition, the XPS measurements on the CNBAI,   $  PbI_{2}  $ , and   $  PbI_{2}  $ -CNBAI (1:1 mixed   $  PbI_{2}  $   and CNBAI in mole ratio, DMF as the solvent) films were conducted Fig. 2.b shows the N 1 s XPS spectra of the films. For CNBAI film, there are two peaks at 399.5 eV and 400.93 eV in the spectra, attributing to the C≡N bond and C–N bond (associated with -Ar-  $  NH_{3}I  $ ), respectively. The   $  PbI_{2}  $ -CNBAI film displayed a higher binding energy peak at 401.91 eV, which indicates that the N atom (-Ar-  $  NH_{3}I  $ ) forms the hydrogen bonds (N–H  $  ^{-1}  $ ) with the I atom in   $  PbI_{2}  $ . This agrees with the energy shift of N 1 s in the conditions when methanaminium iodide (MAI) transformed into perovskite MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  [72]. Moreover, compared to the single peak corresponding to the C≡N bond in the neat CNBAI film, two peaks at binding energies of 399 eV and 399.67 eV were found in the PbI  $  _{2}  $ -CNBAI film, which are related to the interaction of -C≡N with Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  in PbI  $  _{2}  $  (at 399 eV) [73] and intermolecular halogen bond (N  $  ^{-1}  $ ) [69,74] between -C≡N and PbI  $  _{2}  $ . The splitting of the binding energy could be explained by the different nature of the two interactions: the interaction of -C≡N with Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  in PbI  $  _{2}  $  could decrease the binding energy of N 1 s [73], while the halogen bond (N  $  ^{-1}  $ ) between -C≡N and PbI  $  _{2}  $  will increase the binding energy of N 1 s since the N atom is an electron donor [74,75]. In the PbI  $  _{2}  $ -CNBAI film (Fig. 2c), the Pb 4f spectra shifted slightly to lower binding energy than pristine PbI  $  _{2}  $  film, which is ascribed to the coordination of negative I  $  ^{-}  $  (CNBAI) with Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  and partly canceled by the interaction between -C≡N and Pb  $  ^{2+}  $ . The I 3d spectra (Fig. S1) in the PbI  $  _{2}  $ -CNBAI film shifted around 0.38 eV toward lower binding energy compared with pristine PbI  $  _{2}  $  or CNBAI, which are the integrated effect of downshifting binding energy in PbI  $  _{2}  $  (including N–H  $  ^{-1}  $  hydrogen bonds and intermolecular N  $  ^{-1}  $  halogen bond) and the upshifting binding energy in CNBAI (form Pb  $  ^{-1}  $  bond). However, given the twofold numbers of I atoms in PbI  $  _{2}  $  than that in CNBAI, the detected change of I 3d binding energy is mainly determined by PbI  $  _{2}  $ , resulting in decreased binding energy. These results suggest that CNBAI can react with under-coordinated Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  and form the intermolecular halogen bonds with the iodide atom through the -C≡N group, and fill the iodide vacancy in the perovskite film by the counter charged iodide ions.

We further carried out FTIR characterization to get more experimental evidence of the interactions. The FTIR spectra of the CNBAI and CNBAI-PbI $ _2 $  films are shown in Fig. S2a. We could see apparent stretching vibration of C≡N at  $ \sim $ 2232.7 cm $ ^{-1} $  for both samples [76]. Nevertheless, in Fig. 2d, compared with CNBAI, a similar splitting phenomenon was found for the peak from C≡N stretching vibration in CNBAI-PbI $ _2 $ , where two new peaks at  $ \sim $ 2248.6 and  $ \sim $ 2209.5 cm $ ^{-1} $  appear. This result agrees perfectly with the splitting of N 1 s binding energy in the XPS. The peak at 2248.6 cm $ ^{-1} $  is linked to the effect of intermolecular -C≡N $ ^{···} $ I halogen bond and the peak at 2209.5 cm $ ^{-1} $  is due to the interaction between -C≡N and Pb $ ^{2+} $ . Meanwhile, in Fig. S2b, the peak at  $ \sim $ 538.5 cm $ ^{-1} $  of sample CNBAI, which is assigned to the out-of-plane bending vibration of C≡N, exhibited a substantial shift after mixing with PbI $ _2 $ , further verifying the interaction of CN. It is worth mentioning that this is the first experimental evidence of the possible interaction between CN groups with perovskite (forming -C≡N $ ^{···} $ Pb $ ^{2+} $  interaction and -C≡N $ ^{···} $ I halogen bond), corroborating previous theoretical studies [61]. Consequently, we investigated the stability of the resulting PSC devices. The long-term stability of unencapsulated PSCs with or without CNBAI passivation stored under the ambient environment with relative humidity  $ 20 \pm 10\% $  were tracked. As shown in Fig. 6d, the CNBAI-based device maintained  $ 98\% $  of the initial PCE after  $ 864\mathrm{h} $ , while the PCE of the control device decreased to  $ 86\% $  of the initial value. The improved stability is attributed to the reduced defect density of the CNBAI-passivated film. Moreover, the bulky CNBAI anchoring to perovskite grains via multiple interacting bonds could inhibit the ions migration

and prevent water penetration at the grain boundaries.

# 5. Conclusions

In summary, we first synthesized and introduced an organic ammonium halide salt with cyano functional groups (CNBAI) into the perovskite films with a two-step sequential deposition process. The results showed that CNBAI could enhance the electronic quality of the perovskite films due to the multiple bonding types. The XPS and FTIR measurements indicated that CNBAI could interact with   $  PbI_{2}  $   through the cyano functional groups by forming CN  $  \cdots  $  Pb interactions and the formation of CN  $  \cdots  $  I halogen bonds. It is the first time to demonstrate experimentally that the -CN group can simultaneously interact with   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and form CN  $  \cdots  $  I halogen bond in the perovskite. These interactions regulated the growth of perovskite and thereby improved the morphology. More importantly, due to these interactions, the CNBAI in the perovskite film could efficiently passivate under-coordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   defect, iodide vacancy and suppress ion migration. Besides, the introduced CNBAI could improve energy alignment between the perovskite and adjacent charge transport layers. Finally, the PSCs based on CNBAI perovskite films achieved a PCE of 22.4%, higher than the control devices (21.26%). The CNBAI-based devices also exhibited much less hysteresis and enhanced ambient stability. This work provides an efficient strategy for improving the performance of PSCs by introducing organic ammonium halide salts with functional groups into the bulk perovskite films.
