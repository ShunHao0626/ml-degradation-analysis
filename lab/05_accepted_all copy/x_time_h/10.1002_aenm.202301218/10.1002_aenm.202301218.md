# Incorporating Potassium Citrate to Improve the Performance of Tin-Lead Perovskite Solar Cells

Lei Chen, Chongwen Li, Yeming Xian, Sheng Fu, Abasi Abudulimu, Deng-Bing Li, Jared D. Friedl, You Li, Sabin Neupane, Marie Solange Tumusange, Nannan Sun, Xiaoming Wang, Randy J. Ellingson, Michael J. Heben, Nikolas J. Podraza, Zhaoning Song,* and Yanfa Yan*

Easy-to-form tin vacancies at the buried interface of tin-lead perovskites hinder the performance of low-bandgap perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Here, a synergistic strategy by incorporating potassium citrate (PC) into the poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrene sulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS) hole-transport layer to passivate the buried interface of Sn-Pb PSCs is reported. PC neutralizes the acidity of PEDOT:PSS and stabilizes the perovskite front surface, enhancing device stability. Citrate moieties coordinate with   $  Sn^{2+}  $   on the buried perovskite surface, preventing   $  Sn^{2+}  $   oxidation and suppressing defect formation. Additionally, potassium cations incorporate into Sn-Pb perovskites, enhancing crystallinity and passivating halide defects. The combined benefits enable efficient low-bandgap Sn-Pb PSCs with a power conversion efficiency of 22.7% and a high open-circuit voltage of 0.894 V. Using this method, 26.1% efficiency for all-perovskite tandem solar cells is demonstrated. These results emphasize the significance of buried interface passivation in developing efficient and stable Sn-Pb PSCs and all-perovskite tandem solar cells.

The most challenging issue for Sn-Pb PSCs is the easy oxidation of  $ \mathrm{Sn}^{2+} $  to  $ \mathrm{Sn}^{4+} $ . Many strategies have been reported to address this issue.  $ \mathrm{SnF}_2 $ , a commonly used additive in Sn-based PSCs, is included in the precursor solution to prevent the oxidation of  $ \mathrm{Sn}^{2+} $  to  $ \mathrm{Sn}^{4+} $ , significantly reducing background hole density and defects (e.g.,  $ \mathrm{Sn}^{4+} $  and Sn vacancies) and thereby suppressing

nonradiative recombination.  $  ^{[13,14]}  $   Lead halides (PbCl  $  _{2}  $   and PbBr  $  _{2}  $  ) with higher halogen electronegativity than iodide were introduced to the perovskite absorber composition to reduce bulk defect density and dark saturation current density of Sn-Pb PSCs.  $  ^{[15,16]}  $   The utilization of metallic Sn and Pb powders in galvanic displacement reactions with Sn  $  ^{4+}  $   in the precursor mitigates Sn vacancy formation in the resulting perovskite film.  $  ^{[17-19]}  $   Other reducing agents or antioxidants, such as hydrazine,  $  ^{[20]}  $   ascorbic acid,  $  ^{[21]}  $   and formamidine sulfinic acid,  $  ^{[22]}  $   have been employed to retard or prevent the oxidation of Sn  $  ^{2+}  $   and consequently reduce the formation of Sn  $  ^{4+}  $  -related defects. Despite the advances in passivation strategies, most of the research has focused on preventing the formation of Sn  $  ^{4+}  $   in bulk or at the top surface of perovskite films, and less attention has been given to suppressing the formation of Sn  $  ^{4+}  $   defects at the buried interface between the perovskite absorber layer and the underneath hole-transport layer (HTL).

In this work, we report a buried interface passivation strategy for Sn-Pb PSCs by incorporating potassium citrate (PC) into the PEDOT:PSS HTL. The PC modification strategy provides synergistic effects on addressing the buried interface issues of Sn-Pb PSCs and enhances the electronic quality of Sn-Pb perovskites. First, citrate coordination with  $ \mathrm{Sn}^{2+} $  on the surface of the perovskite film prevents the oxidation of  $ \mathrm{Sn}^{2+} $  at the buried surface, significantly suppressing the nonradiative recombination associated with the formation of  $ \mathrm{Sn}^{4+} $  defects. Additionally, PC neutralizes the acidity of PEDOT:PSS, suppressing halide and tin redox

reactions at the interface and enhancing the interface stability. Finally, the potassium   $  (K^{+})  $   incorporation into Sn-Pb perovskite enhances the perovskite crystallinity and improves its optoelectronic properties. Using this approach, we achieved a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 22.7% with a high open-circuit voltage   $  (V_{\mathrm{OC}})  $   of 0.894 V in single-junction Sn-Pb PSCs. Owing to the reduced   $  Sn^{4+}  $   defects at the buried surface and reduced PEDOT:PSS acidity, the device with the PC-modified buried interface also displayed enhanced light stability, retaining 80% of its initial efficiency after the continuous operation for more than 400 h (  $  T_{80}  $   lifetime). This passivation strategy also enabled the fabrication of all-perovskite tandem cells with a PCE of 26.1% and a   $  T_{80}  $   lifetime of   $  \approx700  $   h under 1 sun maximum power point (MPP) tracking conditions.

To further corroborate the defect properties at the buried interface, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to scrutinize the chemical valence state of the elements on the peeled surface of Sn-Pb perovskite films. To avoid oxidation of   $  Sn^{2+}  $  , the peeling-off process was carried out in a   $  N_{2}  $  -filled glovebox. Figure 1c shows the XPS spectrum of Sn   $  3d_{5/2}  $   and fitting curves for a peeled-off mixed Sn-Pb perovskite film, where the Sn 3d peak can be resolved into two individual peaks at 486.4 and 487.2 eV, which are associated with   $  Sn^{2+}  $   and   $  Sn^{4+}  $  , respectively. The 16.4%   $  Sn^{4+}/Sn^{2+}  $   ratio provides evidence of the existence of a significant amount of   $  Sn^{4+}  $   at the buried interface.

One possible route to the formation of Sn $  ^{4+}  $  defects is through the redox reactions with I $  _{0}  $  interstitials or I $  _{2}  $  molecules, $ ^{[37]} $  resulting from the oxidation of iodide species through photoelectrochemical reactions or partial decomposition at elevated temperatures. This degradation process can be accelerated by the acidic PEDOT:PSS. To reveal the impact of PEDOT:PSS on the surface chemistry of Sn-Pb perovskites, UV-visible (UV-vis) spectroscopy measurements of the Sn-Pb perovskite films were taken. Figure 1d shows the formation of I $  _{2}  $  and SnI $  _{4}  $  in a control Sn-Pb perovskite film deposited on PEDOT:PSS after heating the film in toluene at 85 °C for 72 h, whereas no I $  _{2}  $  or SnI $  _{4}  $  were found in the pristine film immersed in toluene (Figure S2, Supporting Information), confirming that Sn-Pb perovskite films deposited on untreated PEDOT:PSS deteriorate at an elevated temperature.

To address the defective buried interface issue, we introduced a multidentate chelating citrate ligand molecule (PC) to passivate the buried interfaces of Sn-Pb perovskites. Because PC molecule exhibits low solubility in nonaqueous solvents, including dimethyl formamide (DMF)/dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) mixed solvent, perovskite precursor solution, isopropanol, toluene, and chlorobenzene, and high solubility in water (Figure S3, Supporting Information), we introduce PC in the aqueous PEDOT:PSS solution and expect that PC molecules remain in the PEDOT:PSS film and on its top surface instead of being washed away by the DMF/DMSO mixed solvent. To evaluate the impact of PC incorporation on the properties of PEDOT:PSS, we conducted ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy measurements for the PEDOT:PSS layer with or without PC incorporation (Figure S4, Supporting Information). The work function of the PEDOT:PSS layer was reduced by 0.1 eV after PC incorporation, likely due to the removal of some PSS dopants from PEDOT:PSS.  $  ^{[38]}  $   This result confirms the interaction between PC and PEDOT:PSS.

Figure 1e shows the proposed working mechanism of PC-modified buried interface passivation. To gain insight into the working mechanism, we first employed density functional theory calculations (Figure S5, Supporting Information) to investigate the interaction of citrate with the  $ \mathrm{SnI}_2 $ -terminated surface of  $ \mathrm{FASnI}_3 $ . The citrate anion in PC is a strong chelating agent that can interact with  $ \mathrm{Sn}^{2+} $  to form strong bonding and prevent its oxidation. As shown in Figure S5a (Supporting Information), the citrate anion contains two types of carboxylate moieties (Type I and II) that can coordinate with  $ \mathrm{Sn}^{2+} $  on the surface. We calculated the corresponding binding energy of these two types of

carboxylate moieties (Figure S5b,c, Supporting Information), respectively. The results indicate that the Type II carboxylate group is more energetically favorable to bond with the SnI  $  _{2}  $  -terminated surface due to its larger binding energy (0.34 eV), similar to the citrate coordination to other transition metal surfaces.  $  ^{[39]}  $

To confirm the interaction between citrate anion and   $  Sn^{2+}  $  , Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy measurement was conducted on PC and   $  PC/SnI_{2}  $   blend powders. As shown in Figure S6 (Supporting Information), the carboxylate group asymmetric stretching vibration mode shifted from 1587 to 1582 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   after blending PC with   $  SnI_{2}  $  , indicating a strong interaction between the carboxylate group and   $  Sn^{2+}  $  . Owing to this strong chelating interaction, the pinholes resulting from   $  Sn^{4+}  $   defects disappeared at the buried interface after introducing PC, as shown in Figure 1f. Moreover, the grains at the buried interface of the target film are more densely packed and smooth.

We then calculated the formation energy of a Sn vacancy with or without coordination with a citrate molecule. It was found that the formation energy of the tin vacancy increased by 0.14 eV with citrate group binding with   $  Sn^{2+}  $   on the surface, suggesting that PC can passivate under-coordinated surface   $  Sn^{2+}  $   and prevent Sn vacancy formation. XPS measurements were also conducted on a peeled-off film with PC passivation to verify its effect on passivating   $  Sn^{4+}  $   defects. Figure S7 (Supporting Information) shows a shift in binding energy for Pb and Sn peaks toward a lower energy but no obvious changes in I peaks, confirming the coordinating interactions between PC and metal cations. Besides, the ratio of   $  Sn^{4+}/Sn^{2+}  $   decreased to 10.3% after PC passivation (Figure 1g), confirming that the interaction between PC and   $  Sn^{2+}  $   can suppress the oxidation of   $  Sn^{2+}  $   at the buried interface.

Additionally, PC, as a weak base, can neutralize the acidity of PEDOT:PSS (Figure S8, Supporting Information) and thus mitigate the oxidation of iodide at the buried interface. Interestingly, we found no   $  I_{2}  $   or   $  SnI_{4}  $   signal after heating a PC-modified Sn-Pb film in toluene solution at 85 °C for 72 h (Figure 1d), indicating decent thermal stability of Sn-Pb perovskite films with proper surface passivation. The result also reveals that the thermal instability of Sn-Pb PSCs is mainly caused by the unpassivated buried interface between the PEDOT:PSS HTL and the perovskite absorber layer.

To better understand the impact of PC surface passivation on the morphological and structural properties of Sn-Pb perovskites, SEM and X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were carried out. The surface SEM images of the perovskite films exhibit no apparent changes, regardless of the concentration of PC used (Figure S9, Supporting Information). Moreover, the cross-sectional SEM images of the perovskite films, without and with PC passivation, display similar column-like grains. This observation provides further confirmation that PC modification did not alter the morphology of Sn-Pb perovskites (Figure S10, Supporting Information). However, Figure 1h reveals that the XRD peak intensities of the target perovskite film are higher than that of the control film, indicating improved crystallinity of mixed Sn-Pb perovskite upon PC passivation. In addition, the (100) peak of the target film exhibits a slight shift toward a higher angle (inset of Figure 1h), and no shifting in the ITO peak is observed for both control and target films (Figure S11, Supporting Information), suggesting perovskite lattice compression after PC surface passivation. To gain more insights into the optoelectronic properties of the Sn-Pb perovskite films with or without PC buried interface passivation, we conducted UV-vis, steady-state photoluminescence (PL), PL quantum yield (PLQY) mapping, time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL), and photothermal deflection spectroscopy (PDS) measurements. The absorbance spectra and the Tauc plot in Figure S14 (Supporting Information) demonstrate minimal differences in absorbance between films with or without buried interface passivation. PL measurements were performed on samples with or without PC passivation from the glass and film sides (Figure 2a,b). The PL intensity detected from the glass side of the target film is significantly higher than that of the control film, indicating substantially decreased nonradiative recombination at the buried interface. In Figure 2c, the mean PLQY of the target film is five times higher than the control when measured from the glass side, displaying a similar trend as the PL results, further confirming that the defects at the buried interface are suppressed after PC passivation. Furthermore, the narrower distribution of PLQY of the target than that of the control shows enhanced homogeneity in recombination properties at the buried interface. The TRPL decay curves in Figure 2d were fitted with the biexponential decay formula, and detailed parameters are shown in Table S1 (Supporting Information). The effective lifetime of the target film (1.11 μs) is significantly longer than that of the control film (78.7 ns), indicating that the surface recombination induced by Sn $  ^{4+}  $  defects was substantially mitigated by PC passivation. The values of Urbach energy ( $  E_{u}  $ ) obtained from PDS measurements are presented in Figure 2e. A smaller value of  $  E_{u}  $  was observed for the target film, indicating superior electronic properties compared to the control film. $ ^{[42]} $  These enhanced electronic properties are attributed to the reduced defect density at the buried interface. Figure 2f–h compares PL, PLQY, and TRPL results measured from the film side. These results exhibit a similar trend as those obtained from the glass side, mainly due to the improved crystallinity and halide defect passivation resulting from K $  ^{+}  $  incorporation. However, this improvement is not substantial in comparison to the glass side, indicating that PC plays a more significant role at the buried interface than the top surface.

We fabricated Sn-Pb PSCs in the p-i-n structure involving a film stack of ITO/PEDOT:PSS/Sn-Pb perovskite/  $  C_{60}  $  /bathocuproine (BCP)/Ag, as illustrated in Figure 3a and Figure S15 (Supporting Information). To modify the buried interface, we screened different passivants as analogous to PC, including sodium citrate (SC), lithium citrate (LC),

and KI, and evaluated their impact on device performance. To ensure a fair comparison, the concentration of these salts was maintained at the same level as that of PC. The detailed photovoltaic (PV) parameters are presented in Figure S16 (Supporting Information). The results indicate that device performance was slightly enhanced when the buried interface was modified by KI, confirming the beneficial passivation ability of   $  K^{+}  $  . However, this passivation ability was found to be less effective than that of PC, suggesting that the citrate group is the primary contributor to the improved performance. The superior passivation ability of the citrate group is further supported by the study of devices passivated by SC and LC. Both devices showed a significant improvement in performance after buried interface passivation, but this improvement was still inferior to that of PC. This finding can be attributed to the slightly inferior passivation ability of   $  Li^{+}  $  and   $  Na^{+}  $  compared to   $  K^{+}  $  .[43]

Following the selection of PC, we conducted an optimization study on its concentration. The optimal concentration of 5 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  was determined from the survey of the effect of the concentration of PC on the PV parameters shown in Figure S17 (Supporting Information). The distribution of PV parameters is presented in Figure 3b. The control devices exhibit an average PCE of  $  \approx  $  19.5%, whereas the target devices exhibit an average PCE of  $  \approx  $  22%, confirming the enhancement of device performance with PC passivation. This improvement in performance was attributed to concurrent enhancements in  $  V_{oc}  $ ,  $  J_{sc}  $ , and FF. Figure 3c displays the J-V curves of champion devices, and their detailed parameters are listed in Table S2 (Supporting Information). The target device achieved a PCE of 22.67% (22.48%), with a  $  V_{oc}  $  of 0.894 V (0.89 V), a  $  J_{sc}  $  of 31.98 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  (31.97 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $ ), and an FF of 79.3% (79%) under reverse (forward) scans, while the control device exhibited inferior PCE of 20.37% (20.20%), with lower  $  V_{oc}  $  of 0.860 V (0.860 V),  $  J_{sc}  $  of 31.29 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  (31.23 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $ ), and FF of 75.7% (75.2%). Figure 3d presents the external quantum efficiency (EQE) curves of the best control and target devices. The integrated  $  J_{sc}  $  values of control and target devices are 30.73 and 31.39 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $ , respectively, consistent with the values obtained from the J-V scans. To evaluate the operational stability of the devices, we conducted MPP tracking under 1 sun illumination in a N  $  _{2}  $ -filled glovebox at  $  \approx  $  45 °C. As illustrated in Figure 3e, the control device exhibited inferior stability, retaining 80% of its initial PCE (  $  T_{80}  $  ) for  $  \approx  $  50 h. In contrast, the target device exhibited improved stability, achieving a  $  T_{80}  $  lifetime for  $  \approx  $  400 h, significantly superior to the control device.

As shown in Figure 4f, the TPV decay lifetime of the target device (1.18   $  \mu  $  s) is longer than that of the control device (0.89   $  \mu  $  s). The prolonged lifetime implies that PC passivation suppresses nonradiative recombination at the buried interface, contributing to the improved   $  V_{oc}  $   of the target device.

Motivated by the improved performance in Sn-Pb PSCs with PC buried interface passivation, we fabricated two-terminal all-perovskite tandem solar cells by combining low bandgap (LBG) bottom subcells with wide bandgap (WBG,   $  FA_{0.8}Cs_{0.2}Pb(I_{0.6}Br_{0.4})_{3}  $  , 1.79 eV) top subcells. Figure 5a presents the structure of a tandem cell, which has a configuration of glass/ITO/[2-(9H-carbazol-9-yl)ethyl]phosphonic acid (2PACz)/WBG perovskite/  $  C_{60}  $  /ALD-  $  SnO_{2}  $  /Au/PEDOT:PSS/LBG perovskite/  $  C_{60}  $  /BCP/Ag. As shown in Figure S18a (Supporting Information), the best WBG PSC exhibited a PCE of 16.67% (16.26%), with a   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.252 V (1.247 V), a   $  J_{sc}  $   of 17.27 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   (17.36 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ), and an FF of 77.1% (75.1%) under reverse (forward) scans. The integrated   $  J_{sc}  $   from the EQE spectrum (Figure S18b, Supporting Information) for the champion WBG device is 16.67 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , consistent with the J-V result. Figure 5b shows the J-V curves of champion tandem cells, and their corresponding PV parameters are presented in Table S4 in the Supporting Information. The target device achieved the highest PCE of 26.08% (25.65%), with a   $  V_{oc}  $   of 2.09 V (2.08 V), a   $  J_{sc}  $   of 15.50 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   (15.51 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ), and an FF of 80.5% (79.5%) under reverse (forward) scans, whereas the control device only exhibited a champion PCE of 24.68% (23.90%), with a   $  V_{oc}  $   of 2.04 V (2.03 V), a   $  J_{sc}  $   of 15.20 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   (15.27 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ), and an FF of 79.6% (77.1%). The EQE spectra of the two subcells in both control and target devices are presented in Figure 5c.

The EQE spectra of WBG subcells did not notably change in the two tandem devices. However, the EQE of the LBG subcell in the target device (15.45 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) was slightly higher than that of the LBG subcell in the control device (15.14 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ), consistent with the results shown in the LBG single-junction solar cells. Figure 5d shows that the steady-state efficiency outputs of the best-performing control and target tandem devices are 24.45% and 25.98%, respectively, which agree with the J-V results. In addition, the target devices were reproducible with an average PCE of   $  \approx  $  25.4% and a narrower distribution, while the control devices yielded an average PCE of   $  \approx  $  23.6% and a wider distribution (Figure 5e). We further investigated the light stability of tandem devices. Figure 5f shows the MPP tracking of tandem cells under 1 sun illumination in ambient air. The target device exhibited enhanced light stability, retaining 90% and 80% of its initial PCE for   $  \approx  $  300 and   $  \approx  $  700 h, respectively, significantly outperforming the control device.

# 3. Conclusion

In conclusion, we have demonstrated that Sn vacancies were prone to form at the buried interface in Sn-Pb PSCs, leading to inferior device performance and stability. To address this issue, we passivated the buried interface with a PC molecule, improving the performance and light stability of Sn-Pb PSCs. We found that the carboxylate group in PC strongly bonded with   $  Sn^{2+}  $  , preventing its oxidation to   $  Sn^{4+}  $   and reducing the nonradiative recombination at the buried interface. Additionally,   $  K^{+}  $  is incorporated into the perovskite layer, enhancing crystallinity and passivating halide defects. As a result, the champion device exhibited a higher PCE of 22.7% and improved light stability compared to the control device. Finally, using our PC buried interface passivated LBG subcell in combination with a WBG subcell, we demonstrated an all-perovskite tandem device with a PCE over 26.1% and   $  T_{80}  $   lifetime of 700 h under 1 sun MPP tracking conditions.
