# Effect of bidentate and tridentate additives on the photovoltaic performance and stability of perovskite solar cells†

Jiangzhao Chen, $ ^{a} $  Seul-Gi Kim, $ ^{a} $  Xiaodong Ren, $ ^{b} $  Hyun Suk Jung $ ^{b} $  and Nam-Gyu Park $ ^{*a} $

Fabrication of high-quality perovskite films with a large grain size and fewer defects is always crucial to achieve efficient and stable perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Here, we report a simple and effective method for crystal growth and defect passivation via additive engineering. It is found that bidentate ligand 2,2'-bipyridine (Bpy) and tridentate ligand 2,2':6',2''-terpyridine (Tpy) show a positive effect on the power conversion efficiency (PCE) while a negative effect is observed for monodentate pyridine (Py). Bpy or Tpy additives increase the grain size and carrier lifetimes, which indicates that the degree of interaction between Lewis acid species in the precursor solution and pyridine derivatives plays a critical role in modulating grain growth and passivating defects. Moreover, device stability is improved upon introduction of Bpy or Tpy additives, which is mainly ascribed to suppressed trap-assisted non-radiative recombination as a consequence of reduced defect density. This work highlights the importance of rational engineering of additives for the purpose of simultaneous realization of morphological improvement and defect passivation in efficient and stable PSCs.

# Introduction

Owing to their intriguing and unique optoelectronic properties, organic-inorganic hybrid metal halide perovskites have been extensively applied in several fields, such as perovskite solar cells (PSCs),  $  ^{1-9}  $  light-emitting diodes (LEDs),  $  ^{10,11}  $  photodetectors,  $  ^{12,13}  $  memristors,  $  ^{14,15}  $  etc. Since the pioneering report on solid-state PSCs with a PCE of 9.7% in 2012,  $  ^{2}  $  PSCs have been the focus of academia and industry accompanied by an unprecedented power conversion efficiency (PCE) improvement from 3.8% in 2009 (ref. 1) to a presently certified 23.7%,  $  ^{16}  $  and are widely recognized as promising and appealing next generation solar cells. The striking PCE enhancement in the past several years is mainly ascribed to the device structure engineering, development and optimization of charge transporting materials, interface engineering and optimization of perovskite film quality. Among them, perovskite film quality plays an important role in the achievement of efficient and stable PSCs. Although the currently certified PCE is up to 23.3%, it is still far from the theoretical Shockley–Queisser limit efficiency (30.5%) for the perovskite material with a bandgap of 1.6 eV,  $  ^{17}  $  which was reported to be mainly due to interface recombination

and the trap-assisted non-radiative (also called Shockley–Read-Hall, SRH) recombination in perovskite films.  $  ^{[17,18]}  $   The perovskite morphology has been known to affect charge transport and collection, where a low PCE correlates with a poor morphology.  $  ^{[19]}  $   Except for affecting the PCE, perovskite film quality is closely related to device stability, including light stability, moisture stability and thermal stability.  $  ^{[18,20,21]}  $   It was reported that decomposition of perovskite films preferentially occurred at grain boundaries (GBs) because a large number of defects especially deep energy level defects were demonstrated to be mainly located at GBs during uncontrollable crystallization.  $  ^{[18,20,22,23]}  $   Consequently, it is indispensable to maximize the PCE and stability through fabricating high-quality perovskite films with minimized trap states and thus SRH non-radiative recombination losses.

For the purpose of improving perovskite film quality, various strategies have been developed and attempted, such as composition engineering,  $  ^{7,24,25}  $   precursor solvent engineering,  $  ^{26-28}  $   anti-solvent engineering,  $  ^{29-31}  $   procedure development and optimization,  $  ^{5,32}  $   post-treatment,  $  ^{33}  $   and additive engineering.  $  ^{8,34-42}  $   Among them, additive engineering was found to be one of the effective ways for realization of high-quality perovskite films because of the structural and functional diversity of additives.  $  ^{18,43}  $   Various additive materials were investigated including polymers, fullerenes, organic halide salts, inorganic acids, metal halide salts, and nanoparticles.  $  ^{43}  $   Among the reported additives, some additives were developed to just improve film stability or charge transportation and collection.  $  ^{43}  $   However, the purpose of developing most of the additives in the past was to improve the

It is widely recognized that poor perovskite film quality with a small grain size and a large number of GBs and defects is mainly attributed to fast crystallization.  $  ^{44,45}  $   Therefore, it is required to appropriately control and modulate the crystallization rate through rationally tuning the strength of the chemical interaction between additives and perovskite precursor compositions. Most of the additives, such as MACl (MA = CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}^{+}  $  ),  $  ^{9,46}  $   MASCN,  $  ^{47}  $   NH  $  _{4}  $  SCN,  $  ^{48}  $   and inorganic acids like HI,  $  ^{49}  $   HBr,  $  ^{50}  $   and hypophosphorous acid,  $  ^{51}  $   were demonstrated to be effective in controlling crystal growth and, however, were less effective for defect passivation. Some of the additives with Lewis base or acid properties, such as PVP (poly(4-vinylpyridine)),  $  ^{38}  $   organic dye (AQ310),  $  ^{52}  $   PCBM and ITIC,  $  ^{53}  $   and the p-type π-conjugated polymer PBDB-T,  $  ^{54}  $   were used to only passivate defects in perovskite films without improving crystallization.

In order to simultaneously achieve an improved morphology and reduced defects, incorporation of multiple additives with different functions into perovskite precursor solutions may be one of the best ways. However, to the best of our knowledge, few studies on simultaneous achievement of morphological improvement and defect passivation through introduction of different additives have been reported. Since two additives were reported to synergistically improve the perovskite film morphology,  $  ^{37,55}  $  it seems interesting to develop additive compositions (or formulations) to achieve simultaneous effects. Another way is to integrate multiple functions into the same additive. Simultaneous improvement of the perovskite morphology and passivation of its defects was reported with a single additive such as N-doped graphene,  $  ^{45}  $  side-chain liquid crystalline polymer (SCLCP),  $  ^{56}  $  and carbonized bamboo-derived carbon nanodots (CNDs).  $  ^{57}  $  Thus, additive engineering has been proved to be an effective approach to obtain high quality perovskite films.

Herein, we report a simple and effective additive engineering strategy through introducing the bidentate ligand 2,2'-bipyridine (abbreviated as Bpy) or tridentate ligand 2,2':6',2''-terpyridine (abbreviated as Tpy) into the perovskite precursor solution. The effects of the monodentate ligand pyridine (Py), Bpy and Tpy on device performances are compared. Bpy and Tpy additives are found to have a positive effect on the PCE as compared to that of the control device without additives, while a negative effect is observed for Py. The degree of interaction between   $  PbI_{2}  $   and additives is considered to be involved in crystallization. Additionally, Bpy or Tpy with strong Lewis base properties can effectively passivate defects due to iodide vacancies. The increased grain size and effective defect passivation are responsible for the substantial increase in the carrier lifetime and reduction in defect density, which eventually leads to an improved PCE and device stability.

# Results and discussion

In order to improve the perovskite film quality, we try to incorporate Lewis base additives with different coordination abilities into the perovskite precursor solution, aimed at

improving perovskite film crystallization and morphology and passivating the defects in films. The molecular structures of the additives used in this work are shown in Fig. 1a, and Fig. 1b schematically illustrates the device configuration employed in this study, which is composed of FTO/bl-TiO $ _2 $ /mp-TiO $ _2 $ /perovskite/spiro-MeOTAD/Au (bl and mp represent blocking and mesoporous, respectively). The perovskite composition for this study is FA $ _{0.88} $ Cs $ _{0.12} $ PbI $ _3 $ , in which we used Cl $ ^- $  ions to control crystal growth. It is well known that improved crystallization, morphology and thus carrier lifetimes can be achieved by incorporation of Cl into the precursor solution. $ ^{58-60} $  Considering that there is still no clear conclusion about whether or not Cl $ ^- $  ions can be incorporated into the lattice of MAPbI $ _3 $  or FAPbI $ _3 $ , $ ^{18} $  the formula FA $ _{0.88} $ Cs $ _{0.12} $ PbI $ _3 $  is adopted in the present work. Besides, hydroiodic acid (HI) solution was also added to the precursor solution to improve the morphology of perovskite films according to its effect reported elsewhere. $ ^{61} $  The additives studied here were directly added to the above as-prepared perovskite precursor solution.

First of all, we investigate the effect of the concentration of different additives (Py, Bpy and Tpy) on the photovoltaic performance. The ratio of additive to   $  Pb^{2+}  $   is varied and denoted as Py-x%, Bpy-x%, and Tpy-x%, where x represents the content of additives. The device without additives is defined as the control device. Fig. S1–3† present the photographs of perovskite precursor solutions with different additives. As shown in Fig. S1 and S3,† no precipitates are observed after introduction of Py or Tpy after aging for 12 h even if the content is up to 4% for Py and 2% for Tpy. In fact, it was reported that   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   can be obtained at room temperature using the   $  PbI_{2}\cdot(Py)_{2}  $   complex.  $  ^{62}  $   This suggests that the interaction between Py and   $  PbI_{2}  $   is very weak. However, the stable Lewis acid–base adduct   $  PbI_{2}\cdot DMSO  $   can be obtained at room temperature,  $  ^{26,27}  $   which indicates that Py exhibits a weaker interaction with   $  PbI_{2}  $   as compared to DMSO. In contrast, a large amount of precipitate is observed for both fresh and 12 h-aged precursors with Bpy when the Bpy content exceeds 2%, although no precipitate is observed when adding less than 1% Bpy as illustrated in Fig. S2.† However, the precipitate is dissolved after addition of HI solution, which means that the solubility of the formed Bpy-  $  PbI_{2}  $   complex is increased upon decreasing the pH of the solution. As illustrated in Fig. S4,† a large number of solids are formed at a higher concentration (10%) of Bpy or Tpy. Moreover, the solids exhibit different colors probably due to complexation between   $  PbI_{2}  $   and additives with different chelation ability. This indicates that both Bpy and Tpy can form complexes with   $  PbI_{2}  $   and the formed complexes exhibit poor solubility in DMF/DMSO mixed solvents. We also check the solubility of Bpy or Tpy additives in pure DMF or DMSO as shown in Fig. S5.† It is clearly found that both Bpy and Tpy exhibit excellent solubility in pure DMF or DMSO solvents even in the case where the concentration is increased up to 100 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  . This demonstrates that the generated precipitates in perovskite precursor solutions are from complexes between additives and   $  PbI_{2}  $   instead of bare additives. To investigate the effect of additives on the light harvesting efficiency, UV-vis absorption spectra of control, Bpy-1%, Bpy-4%, Tpy-0.2%, and Tpy-2% perovskite films spin-coated on mp-TiO₂/bl-TiO₂/FTO/glass substrates are measured and shown in Fig. S11a and c.† As illustrated in Fig. S11a,† UV-vis absorbance is slightly increased for Bpy-1% and Bpy-4% films relative to the control film, which is attributed to the improved crystallinity and increased grain size as confirmed by XRD and SEM results (Fig. 3c and 4). The increased IPCE and  $  J_{\mathrm{sc}}  $  could be partially ascribed to the slightly increased UV-vis absorbance. In contrast, almost similar absorbance is observed for Tpy-0.2% and Tpy-2% films as compared to the control film (Fig. S11c†). In addition, the bandgap ( $  E_g  $ ) was estimated by fitting the ( $  \alpha h\nu  $ )² plot as a function of photon energy based on the Kubelka-Munk equation (Fig. S11b and d†).⁶⁷ The  $  E_gS  $  of Bpy-1% and Bpy-4% films are calculated to be 1.55 eV, which are similar to that of the control film (Fig. S11b†). Thus, the band gap of the bulk perovskite is hardly affected by additives. Steady-state photoluminescence (SSPL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectra are collected to study the effect of additives on carrier lifetimes and the interfacial charge extraction efficiency. Fig. 5 shows SSPL and TRPL spectra of perovskite films (control, Bpy-1%, Bpy-4%, Tpy-0.2% and Tpy-2%) deposited on non-conductive glass substrates, and the fit results are summarized in Table S5.† The biexponential decay equation of  $  I(t) = I_0 + A_1 \exp(-t/\tau_1) + A_2 \exp(-t/\tau_2)  $  was used to fit TRPL curves, where  $  A_1  $  and  $  A_2  $  are the relative amplitude fraction for each decay component,  $  I_0  $  is a constant, and  $  \tau_1  $  and  $  \tau_2  $  represent fast and slow decay time constants, respectively.⁶⁶,⁶⁸ The average PL lifetime is calculated using the equation of  $  \tau_{avg} = (A_1\tau_1^2 + A_2\tau_2^2)/(A_1\tau_1 + A_2\tau_2)  $ .⁶⁹ As illustrated in Fig. 5a and c, Bpy-1% and Tpy-0.2% films show much improved PL intensity as compared to the control film, which is consistent with a significant enhancement in the average PL lifetime ( $  \tau_{avg}  $ ) from 124.14 ns for the control film to 253.58 ns for the Bpy-1% film and 223.68 ns for the Tpy-0.2% film (Fig. 5b and d). Except for the improved crystallinity and increased grain size, defect passivation is probably one of the important reasons for the increased carrier lifetimes when considering that Bpy and Tpy can interact with PbI₂ in the perovskite composition because of their strong Lewis base properties. Significantly improved lifetimes at the optimal ratio should be responsible for the increased  $  V_{oc}  $  and FF because of suppressed SRH non-radiative recombination.¹⁸ However, the  $  \tau_{avg}  $  is reduced from 124.14 ns for the control film to 103.14 ns for the Bpy-4% film and 79.36 ns for Tpy-2%, respectively. This manifests that the amount of additive used for defect passivation is limited. For the Tpy-2% film, the reduced grain size and thereby increased grain boundaries could be responsible for the reduced lifetimes to some extent. Consequently, the overall performance is highly

sensitive to the additive concentration. As shown in Fig. S12 and Table S5,  $  ^{\dagger}  $  slightly improved electron or hole extraction is achieved for Bpy-1% and Tpy-0.2%, which is indicative of better interfaces formed by additive addition. Therefore, the improved charge extraction and carrier lifetime can lead to reduced bulk and interface non-radiative recombination, which eventually results in improved photovoltaic parameters as evidenced in Fig. 2a–d. However, slightly worse charge extraction is observed when the content of additives is much larger than the optimal ratio.

In order to gain further insight into the origin of the improved   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF along with increased   $  J_{sc}  $ , we quantitatively evaluate the defect density of perovskite films with Bpy and Tpy additives using a sandwiched FTO/perovskite/Au device through the space-charge-limited current (SCLC) technique. As shown in the typical dark J-V characteristics in Fig. 6, three regions are observed: (I) the ohmic region at low bias exhibiting a linear relationship between the current and electric field, (II) the trap-filled limit region at intermediate bias showing a steep increase in current, and (III) a trap-free space-charge limited-current (SCLC) region at high bias potential. In the trap-filled limit region, the trap density levels are continuously filled as the bias increases and all the traps are filled until trap-filled limit voltage (  $  V_{TFL}  $  ). The trap density is calculated according to the equation of   $  n_{\mathrm{t}} = (2\varepsilon \varepsilon_{0} V_{\mathrm{TFL}})/(eL^{2})  $ , where   $  \varepsilon  $ ,   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $ , L and e are the dielectric constants of the perovskite, the vacuum permittivity, the thickness of the perovskite film, and the elementary charge, respectively.  $  ^{66,70}  $     $  V_{TFL}  $   values are obtained by fitting the dark J-V curves. The relationship of   $  \varepsilon = (C_{\mathrm{g}} L)/( \varepsilon_{0} A)  $   is utilized to calculate the dielectric constant of the perovskites, where   $  C_{g}  $   is the geometrical capacitance of the perovskite layer, the permittivity of free space   $  \varepsilon_{0} = 8.85 \times 10^{-14}  $   F cm  $  ^{-1}  $ , and A is the active areas of the FTO/perovskite/Au devices.  $  ^{66,70,71}  $     $  C_{g}  $   is obtained by fitting the impedance spectra of the FTO/perovskite/Au device. Table S6† illustrates the corresponding parameters for calculation and calculation results. The trap density is reduced from   $  1.47 \times 10^{16}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $   for the control film to   $  0.81 \times 10^{16}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $   for the Bpy-1% film and to   $  0.95 \times 10^{16}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $   for Tpy-0.2%, which is in good agreement with the increased carrier lifetime. For a higher content of the additives (Bpy-4% and Tpy-2%), trap density is even higher than that of the control (Fig. 7f), which leads to lower   $  V_{oc}  $ .

In Fig. 7, the crystallization process of perovskite films is schematically illustrated, where three states might exist: (I) the wet film state before dripping the antisolvent, where the perovskite composition, solvents and additives are dispersed relatively randomly, (II) the small grain size state after dripping the antisolvent, where DMSO or additives interact with the perovskite according to a specific ratio, and (III) the large grain size state after annealing, where DMSO will evaporate from the final film but additives still exist and strongly interact with the perovskite. In case of stronger interactions with bidentate or tridentate than DMSO, which is evidenced by FTIR and XPS, the crystal growth rate can slow down. # Conclusions

In conclusion, we demonstrated a simple and effective grain growth modulating and defect passivation engineering through rationally tuning the coordination ability of additives from monodentate to tridentate ligands. After incorporation of Bpy and Tpy additives, the photovoltaic performance and stability were much improved. The grain size and crystallinity of perovskite films were increased and defect density was decreased in the presence of additives, which should be mainly responsible for the improved PCE and device operational stability. This work can provide important insight into simultaneous achievement of grain growth modulation and defect passivation through additive engineering in PSCs.

# Experimental section

# Materials

Formamidinium iodide (FAI; FA:   $  NH=CHNH_{3}  $  ) was synthesized according to our previous reports.  $  ^{66,68}  $   All the other materials were purchased from commercial sources and used as received without further purification.

# Device fabrication

FTO-coated glass substrates (Pilkington, TEC-8,  $ 8\Omega \mathrm{sq}^{-1} $ ) were etched with a laser and then ultrasonically cleaned for  $ 15\mathrm{min} $  with detergent, deionized water, and ethanol, respectively. After the substrates were further treated with UV ozone for  $ 20\mathrm{min} $ , a  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  blocking layer  $ (\mathrm{bl - TiO_2}) $  was fabricated by repeatedly

spin coating a solution of titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetyla-cetonate) (Sigma-Aldrich, 75 wt% in isopropanol) in 1-butanol (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.8%) on the FTO-coated glass at 2000 rpm for 20 s and annealed at 125 °C for 5 min, which was repeated three times. A mesoporous TiO₂ (mp-TiO₂) layer was prepared by spin coating a diluted TiO₂ paste (50 nm-sized TiO₂ nanoparticles, terpineol, ethylcellulose and lauric acid with a nominal composition of 1.25 : 6 : 0.9 : 0.1 in wt%) in anhydrous 1-butanol (0.1 g mL⁻¹) on top of the bl-TiO₂ layer at 2000 rpm for 20 s, which was annealed at 125 °C for 5 min and then annealed at 550 °C for 1 h. After cooling down to room temperature, the mp-TiO₂ film was treated with UVO for 20 min and then treated with 0.02 M aqueous TiCl₄ (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9%) solution at 90 °C for 10 min. The TiCl₄-treated film was repeatedly cleaned with deionized water and annealed at 500 °C for 30 min. The mp-TiO₂ film was exposed again to UVO for 20 min before depositing the perovskite film. 1.5 M FA₀.₈₈Cs₀.₁₂PbI₃ perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving FAI (0.2269 g), CsI (0.0468 g, Alpha Aesar, 99.99%), PbI₂ (0.6639 g, Alpha Aesar, 99.9985%), and PbCl₂ (0.0167 g, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.999%) in DMSO (0.106 mL, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9%) and DMF (0.894 mL, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.8%), where the concentration of the precursor solution was calculated according to the content of Pb²⁺, and the molar ratio of DMSO/Pb²⁺ is 1 : 1. For additive-containing perovskite precursor solutions, different molar ratios of additives were added to the above prepared 1.5 M FA₀.₈₈Cs₀.₁₂PbI₃ perovskite precursor solution: 0.5%, 1%, 2% and 4% for pyridine (Py) (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.8%), 0.5%, 1%, 2%, 3% and 4% for 2,2'-bipyridine (Bpy) (Alpha Aesar, 98%), and 0.1%, 0.2%, 0.5%, 1% and 2% for 2,2':6',2''-terpyridine (Tpy) (Sigma-Aldrich, 98%), respectively. The ratio of additives is calculated according to the molar ratio of additive to perovskite. Before depositing perovskite films, HI solution (57 wt% in water, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.99%) was added to the perovskite precursor solution, where volume (precursor): volume (HI) = 50 : 1. Perovskite films were prepared by spin coating the as-prepared precursor solution at 4000 rpm for 25 s where 500 μL diethyl ether was poured onto films at 10 s after spinning and then the films were annealed at 150 °C for 30 min. After cooling down to room temperature, 20 μL of 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-di-p-methoxyphenylamine)-9,9-spirobifluorene (spiro-MeOTAD) solution was spin coated on the as-prepared perovskite layer at 3000 rpm for 30 s. The spiro-MeOTAD solution was prepared by mixing 72.3 mg spiro-MeOTAD (Merck), 28.8 μL of 4-tert-butyl pyridine (t-BP) and 17.5 μL of lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (Li-TFSI) stock solution (520 mg Li-TFSI in 1 mL acetonitrile (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.8%)) in 1 mL chlorobenzene. Finally, an Au electrode was thermally evaporated on the spiro-MeOTAD-coated perovskite film at an evaporation rate of 0.3 Å s⁻¹.
