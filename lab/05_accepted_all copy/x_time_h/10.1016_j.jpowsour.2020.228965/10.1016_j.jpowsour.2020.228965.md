# GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

# ABSTRACT

Low-temperature fabrication is of vital importance for solar cells in reducing energy consumption and developing diversified device structures. Here, we present an efficient low-temperature fabrication of   $  \alpha  $  -phase   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   via introducing a low toxicity and high volatility solvent, acetone, into the precursor to assist the nucleation and growth of perovskite. The addition of acetone significantly improves the wettability of perovskite precursor on compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   substrate and induces strong interaction between lead iodide and dimethyl sulfoxide solvent. This in turn decreases the crystallization active energy of perovskite, resulting in a fast formation of   $  \alpha  $  -phase   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite at 40 °C. As a result, the acetone-assisted   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   device gives a superior power conversion efficiency of 16.03% along with good long-term and operational stability, approaching the highest reported efficiency for   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   solar cells. The present study demonstrates that the rational precursor engineering is an effective strategy for low-temperature fabrication of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   solar cells.

# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic hybrid lead halide perovskites are regarded as promising next-generation photovoltaic (PV) materials [1-3], because of their excellent optoelectronic properties, such as good charge-carrier mobility, long carrier diffusion length, and high tolerance of chemical defects [4]. The power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) based on organic-inorganic hybrid lead halide perovskites have recently been boosted from  $ 3.8\% $  to over  $ 25\% $  [5]. However, the PSCs based on organic-inorganic components, such as methylammonium (MA) and formamidinium (FA), suffer from not satisfactory thermal stability [6-10], which makes it challenging for future commercialization [6,11]. Thus, the removal of organic components from the perovskite layer is a potentially effective strategy for solving the stability issue of perovskite materials.

Cs-based all-inorganic perovskite materials,   $  CsPbX_{3}  $   (X = Cl, Br, I, or mixed), have demonstrated high thermal stability and ever-increasing device performance [12–20]. Among them, mixed-halide inorganic perovskite   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   is very attractive because of its high phase stability and suitable bandgap for use as top cells in tandem devices [15,16,21–26]. As is well known, high-quality polycrystalline all-inorganic perovskite films with good morphology and large grain size are desirable for realizing high-performance devices. Numerous interfacial engineering, ion substitution, and doping methods have thus been carried out to tailor the crystallization process, reduce the open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) deficit, and improve the device efficiency of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   PSCs [16,21–24,27–29]. For example, Han et al. [27] partially replaced the   $  PbI_{2}  $   with   $  ZnI_{2}  $   to manipulate the crystal growth; the   $  CsPb_{0.9}Zn_{0.1}I_{2}Br  $   device gave a PCE of 13.6%. However, the processing temperature as high as 350 °C was needed to improve the crystalline quality. Liu et al. [24] designed a graded bandgap structure-based solar cell, comprising a   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   bottom cell and a   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   quantum dot top cell, and a PCE of over 14% was obtained upon annealing at 300 °C. Afterward, the processing temperature was reduced to ~200 °C by using a lead-DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide) adducts in the precursor [29]. Based on which, a record efficiency of 16.79% for   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   solar cell has recently been achieved along with adding   $  CaCl_{2}  $   into the precursor to simultaneously passivate trap-states and tune the Fermi level of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   [30]. It's noticeable that low-temperature treatment is conducive to the development of flexible optoelectronic devices and reduction of energy consumption. However, the processing temperature for high-quality photoactive   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite films was traditionally higher than 180 °C or even higher than 300 °C [16,21–24,27–31], which is not beneficial for the diversified development of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   solar cell. Moreover, current film fabrication of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite is mostly carried out in toxic solvents, such as N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) [15,16,21,23,24,27–30] and anti-solvent chlorobenzene (CB) [22,29], hindering the future practical application of the perovskite device [32,33]. Therefore, it is highly desirable to develop a method features both low-temperature and low-toxicity to prepare high-crystal-quality   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film for high-performance all-inorganic PSCs.

Acetone is a low-toxic, high-volatile Lewis base chemical and can be easily removed subject to low-temperature treatment. In this work, we have developed an acetone-assisted low-temperature crystallization strategy for   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite film. The experimental results reveal that the wettability of the perovskite precursor and the strength of the lead-Lewis base interaction both improved with adding acetone into DMSO solvent. The increased lead-Lewis base interaction in turn led to the direct crystallization of the desired   $  \alpha  $  -phase   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   via skipping sequential crystallization from   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   to   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $ . As a result, a high-quality   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film with larger crystal grains and a preferable (100) crystal facet was obtained at 40 °C in the presence of acetone. Excellent optoelectronic properties of the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film were translated to a high open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) of 1.27 V and PCE of 16.03% in solar cells, which is among the highest efficiencies for   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   solar cells fabricated at low temperature, and is close to the record efficiency of 16.79%.

[30]. Moreover, the unsealed device exhibited good long-term storage, thermal and operational stability. Our work demonstrates the effectiveness of the precursor tailoring for efficient all-inorganic PSCs fabricated at low temperature.

# 2. Results and discussion

The as-formed perovskite film properties and the overall device performance are significantly dependent on the precursor solution chemistry. Good wettability and suitable viscosity of the precursor solution will improve the interfacial contact between the wet film and the substrate. In this study, the perovskite precursor solutions (0.9 M) were prepared via dissolving   $  CsI:PbI_{2}:PbBr_{2}  $   (molar ratio = 2:1:1) in neat DMSO or DMSO:Acetone (v/v = 7:3) solvents. Fig. 1a and b show the perovskite precursor solution contact angle (CA) on the compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   substrate. A CA of 71.3° is obtained for the control DMSO sample, whereas the CA is reduced to 23.9° for the DMSO-Acetone sample. The clearly decreased CA indicates that the wettability of the acetone containing precursor solution on   $  TiO_{2}  $   substrate improved significantly, which is of vital importance to interface contact and crystal growth. The reduced CA is partially related to the viscosity of the solution. The viscosity (Fig. 1c) was measured to be 7.9 and 2.3 mPa s for the control DMSO and DMSO-Acetone solutions, respectively. The lower viscosity and CA are expected to be beneficial to the interfacial direct contact between the   $  TiO_{2}  $   and perovskite precursor, which, in turn, favors the heterogeneous nucleation and crystallization of perovskite by reducing the nucleus spacing [34].

We investigated the Lewis acid–base interaction between   $  PbI_{2}  $   and DMSO solvent to probe the difference between the control DMSO and the DMSO-Acetone cases. The optical properties were studied by using the UV–vis absorption spectra (Fig. 1d and Fig. S1). A red-shift of   $  PbI_{2}  $   absorption was observed in the DMSO-Acetone solvent, which indicates an enhanced interaction between   $  PbI_{2}  $   and Lewis bases in contrast to the control DMSO case. Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was then conducted to investigate the interaction between   $  PbI_{2}  $   and the active functional group in Lewis bases. Fig. S2 presents the full spectra for the pure Lewis bases,   $  PbI_{2}  $ , and perovskite precursor solutions (the Lewis base was the solvent). The characteristic stretching vibration peak of S=O (  $  \nu(\mathrm{S}=\mathrm{O})  $   around   $  1040\ cm^{-1}  $  ) in DMSO can be identified for all samples. The partially enlarged FT-IR spectra of the   $  PbI_{2}  $ -Lewis base solution are shown in Fig. 1e. The   $  \nu(\mathrm{S}=\mathrm{O})  $   appearing at approximately   $  1043\ cm^{-1}  $   belongs to the pure DMSO, which was shifted to   $  1030\ cm^{-1}  $   for the DMSO-  $  PbI_{2}  $   solution, indicating the formation of DMSO-  $  PbI_{2}  $   complexes [35,36]. The value was further shifted to   $  1024\ cm^{-1}  $   with the addition of acetone, which should be ascribed to the increased synergistic coordination effect between   $  PbI_{2}  $   and the mixed Lewis bases (DMSO-Acetone) [37]. Similar phenomena can be observed for the case of the perovskite precursor in the Lewis base solvents (Fig. 1f), implying that the coordination interaction mainly occurs between   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and Lewis bases. Liquid-state   $  {}^{1}H  $   nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were collected to further evaluate the interaction between Lewis bases and the   $  PbI_{2}  $   solution. Fig. 1g shows a shift of proton signal in the   $  PbI_{2}  $ -DMSO-Acetone solution in contrast to   $  PbI_{2}  $ -DMSO. This indicates that a much stronger coordinating ability between   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and DMSO formed with the addition of acetone. The same conclusion can be reached for the proton signal in the perovskite precursor-Lewis base solutions (Fig. S3). These results are in good agreement with the FT-IR results that the acetone can increase the Lewis acid–base interaction between   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and DMSO.

It is necessary to evaluate how the enhanced Lewis-acid–base interaction between   $  PbI_{2}  $   and DMSO influences crystallization kinetics, so that we carried out quasi-in-situ X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis during the crystallization process. Therefore, the addition of acetone in the precursor solution leads to a direct and fast crystallization of CsPbI $ _2 $ Br perovskite at temperature of 40  $ ^{\circ} $ C. Moreover, it skipped the sequential crystallization from CsPbBr $ _3 $  to CsPbI $ _2 $ Br in the presence of acetone.

The crystallization-kinetics-dependent film quality was evaluated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As shown in Fig. 3a and b, all optimized perovskite films are fully covered without pinholes. The average crystal size (Fig. S5) of the film prepared from DMSO is around  $ 177~\mathrm{nm} $ , which was improved to  $ \sim 375~\mathrm{nm} $  (the largest one is on the micrometer scale) for the one fabricated from DMSO-Acetone. The crystal structures of the as-prepared perovskite films were then investigated via using XRD patterns. Fig. 3c clearly shows that all perovskite films exhibit the characteristic peaks of cubic  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  with preferable crystal growth along the (100) facet. Notably, the intensity of the main diffraction peaks for the acetone-based perovskite films is almost four times stronger than that of the control one, indicative of the improved crystallinity, which agrees well with the SEM images. Fig. 3d shows the optical properties of the perovskite films. The intensity of the acetone-based perovskite film is much stronger than that of the control one. To accurately evaluate the improvement of the optical properties, absorption coefficient has been extracted (inset of Fig. 3d). The acetone-treated film show significantly increased absorption coefficient compared to the control one, verifying the higher quality of the acetone-based perovskite film. The two films exhibit the same bandgap of  $ \sim 1.91\mathrm{eV} $  (Fig. S6), indicating that the addition of acetone into the precursor does not change the intrinsic optical properties of  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  perovskite. To gain insight into the distribution of elements in the perovskite film, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed for the control and acetone-based samples. As shown in Fig. S7, all the elements are uniformly distributed in the perovskite crystals. The acetone-based film exhibits a stronger intensity and a slight shift to higher binding energy, likely due to the enhanced chemical bonding [28]. Photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence spectra (TRPL) were further applied to evaluate the quality of the perovskite films deposited on the glass substrate. The acetone-based sample has a clearly stronger PL intensity than the control sample (Fig. 3e), indicating that the film quality improved, consistent with the XRD results. The average PL lifetime ( $ \tau_{\mathrm{ave}} $ ) for the control perovskite film was calculated to be  $ 13~\mathrm{ns} $  from Fig. 3f,

which further improved to 37 ns for the acetone-based perovskite film (the detailed fitting data are shown in Table S1). Although the values are lower than those of the organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite films [41, 42], the PL lifetime is one of the highest values reported for all-inorganic perovskite films [14,16]. These results indicate that the film quality is significantly improved with reduced non-radiative recombination, which is beneficial for achieving high  $  V_{\mathrm{OC}}  $ .

To evaluate the influence of film quality on device performance, we then fabricated perovskite devices comprising FTO/c-TiO $ _2 $ /perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD/Au (where FTO is fluorine-doped tin oxide, spiro-OMeTAD is 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis (N,N-dipmethoxyphenylamine)-9,9-spi-robifluorene). The PCE from the reverse scan for the control device was 12.31% (Fig. S8), which increased to 15.52% and 15.74% when 20% and 30% acetone was introduced, respectively. However, the further increase in the concentration of acetone to 40% resulted in a PCE degradation (14.96%), which mainly reflects in the  $ V_{OC} $  and FF, and should be related to the defects within perovskite films. To deeply understand the reason for the degradation of the device with high acetone concentration, the cross-sectional SEM images for PSCs with different acetone concentrations were firstly performed. As shown in Fig. S9, with the increase of acetone concentration, the thickness of perovskite films gradually increased from  $ \sim $ 200 nm to  $ \sim $ 370 nm,  $ \sim $ 400 nm, and  $ \sim $ 420 nm for the control and 20%, 30% and 40% acetone based devices, respectively, which presents the same trend with the  $ J_{SC} $  values for the corresponding devices. However, the increase in  $ J_{SC} $  is negligible compared to the reduction of  $ V_{OC} $  and FF for the 40% acetone-based device, which in turn decreased the device performance. It can also be found (from the photographs of the perovskite precursor solutions) (Fig. S10) that for the sample with acetone concentration of 40%, a cloudy yellow solution can be clearly observed due to the low solubility of CsI in acetone. The decreased solubility of CsI in the mixed solvent will results in formation of non-stoichiometric perovskite films, leading to increased trap state. The space-charge-limited current (SCLC) method was thus employed to examine the trap-states in the perovskite films.

$$

$$

(1)

where  $ e $  is the elementary charge,  $ n_t $  is the trap density,  $ L $  is the thickness of the crystal between two Au electrodes,  $ \varepsilon $  is the relative dielectric constant of CsPb $ _2 $ Br ( $ \varepsilon = 8.5 $ ) [27], and  $ \varepsilon_0 $  is the dielectric constant of vacuum. Obviously, the  $ 30\% $  acetone-treated perovskite shows the minimum electron trap-state compared to all other samples, confirming that the optimal concentration of acetone is  $ 30\% $  and further increasing the concentration to  $ 40\% $  will enhance the electron traps within perovskite. Therefore, the decreased solubility and increased electron traps should be the main reasons for the decreased device performance

with increasing the acetone concentration to 40%.

To understand the large improvement in   $  V_{OC}  $  , the J-V curves for the control (champion cell) and acetone-based best device under different light intensities were tested. The acetone treatment also improved device stability, including ambient storage stability, operational stability, and thermal stability. Fig. 6a–d and Table S2 show the main parameters for the unencapsulated control and acetone-treated devices. The acetone-treated device retained 88% of its initial efficiency after 1000 h in an   $  N_{2}  $  -filled glove box. However, 50% degradation of its initial efficiency was observed for the control device. We also measured the operational stability of the unsealed device under 1 sun illumination around the maximum power point voltage (Fig. 6e). For the control device, the output efficiency decayed by 22% after 70 h. However, more than 92% of its original output efficiency could still be maintained after 70 h of steady output, demonstrating excellent operational stability. Moreover, the thermal stability of both devices were also evaluated via replacing the widely used spiro-OMeTAD with a thermally stable hole transporting material – PTAA (poly[bis(4-phenyl)(2,4,6-trimethylphenyl)amine]) [50,55]. As shown in Fig. 6f, more than 90% of its initial efficiency could be retained after 500 h of thermal aging at 85 °C in an   $  N_{2}  $  -filled glovebox, while only around 65% of its original efficiency remained for the control device. The improved stability of the present   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   all-inorganic PSCs could be attributed to the significantly improved crystal quality.

To further explore the potential of ultra-low temperature fabrication of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   solar cells, the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film obtained without the second annealing step (i. e. only annealed at 40 °C for 5 min) was directly used to assemble the solar cell. The device gives a PCE of 14.05% with   $  V_{OC} = 1.213  $   V,   $  J_{SC} = 14.54  $   mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  , and FF = 79.67% (Fig. S16a). The unsatisfactory device performance is mainly coming from the lower   $  J_{SC}  $   in comparison with the champion device, which should be attributed to the low crystallinity and the existence of residual solvent for the film annealed only at 40 °C. The SCLC result confirmed its higher electron traps (  $  4.40 \times 10^{15}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  ) compared to the champion device (Fig. S16b). Moreover, there is a few of pin-hole and not well crystallized large grains (over 500 nm) composed of small grains with ambiguous grain boundaries (Fig. S16c). Therefore, the low crystallinity and high electron traps may be the reason for the unsatisfactory device performance. Nevertheless, the PCE is among the highest efficiencies for solar cells fabricated at quasi-room temperature. These results pave a novel avenue for room-temperature processed solar cells.

# 3. Conclusions

In summary, we have presented an effective precursor engineering strategy to improve both the efficiency and stability of all-inorganic PSCs by introducing a low-toxicity, high-volatility Lewis base (acetone) into the precursor solution. The wettability of the perovskite precursor and the strength of lead-Lewis base interactions have been well tuned with the addition of acetone. The decrease in the crystallization active energy of perovskite resulted in the direct formation of   $  \alpha  $  -phase   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   at quasi-room-temperature (40 °C) via skipping the sequential crystallization from   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   to   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $  . The optimized acetone-based film exhibited enlarged crystal grains with improved PL lifetime and reduced non-radiation recombination. As a result, a PCE of 16.03% was achieved for the acetone-based device, which is among the highest values for   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $  -based all-inorganic PSCs fabricated at low temperature. Additionally, the unencapsulated PSC device exhibited good long-term storage, thermal and operational stability. Our findings demonstrate that rational precursor tailoring may be an effective method for achieving efficient all-inorganic PSCs.
