# Enhanced Performance of Perovskite Solar Cells by luminescent Small Molecule DBP: Perovskite Absorption Spectrum Modification and Interface Engineering

Abstract: The interfacial defect states between active layer and charge transport layer in perovskite solar cells (PSCs) are one of main channels of energy-loss, which results in carrier recombination loss and then limits efficiency and stability of device. In this work, a luminescent organic small molecule material tetraphenyldibenzoperiflanthene (DBP) is inserted between  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  perovskite and electron transport layer PCBM as an interface modification layer. As a result, DBP-based PSCs attained an average power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 15.61\% $ , obviously higher than  $ 14.26\% $  of the reference device. The enhanced PCE is attributed to the multifunctional synergistic effect of DBP. DBP not only behaves an efficient interface modification layer to passivate perovskite defect and improve energy level alignment and thus reduce carrier recombination loss at the interface, but also play a  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  absorption spectrum modification layer by FÖster energy transfer and thus enhance the light absorption of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  layer in near-infrared region. In the meantime, the hydrophobic and passivation effect of DBP improve the air-, thermal- and photo-stability of PSCs. This work demonstrated that the multifunctional organic luminescent molecules as an

# Introduction

Over the past several years, organic-inorganic halide hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs) were extensively investigated, and so far its power conversion efficiency (PCE) has exceeded 23%  $  ^{1}  $  . The high efficiency is related to the intrinsic nature of the perovskite materials, including high absorption coefficient, low exciton binding energy, high charge carrier mobility, bipolar carrier transport and tunability bandgap.  $  ^{2}  $   The initial device structures of the PSCs depend on a mesoporous layer that requires high-temperature preparation, which limit the practical application of PSCs. After that, the planar heterojunction PSCs prepared by low temperature solution method were developed. Their fabrication process is not only relatively simple and low-cost, but also suitable to the preparation of large-area flexible devices. Therefore, this type of structures has been recognized as a promising alternative for efficient photovoltaics.  $  ^{3-5}  $

It is recognized that crystalline quality of perovskite active layer is critical for achieving high PCE. Despite the prominent advantages of current solution processing method for the perovskite preparation, it easily begets some defect states at the surface and grain boundaries of perovskite layer, acting as carrier recombination centers and thus greatly limiting the open-circuit voltage (Voc) and the short-circuit current density  $ (J_{SC}) $  of PSCs. Meanwhile, the weak grain boundaries easily lead to the infiltration of

It had been proved that introducing polymer or organic small materials into the contact interface of perovskite is an effective strategy to reduce the defect states at the surface and grain boundaries of perovskite to realize high-performance PSC.  $  ^{9, 10}  $   For example, Wang et al. demonstrated that inserting a tunneled polystyrene (PS) layer between perovskite and electron transport layer (ETL) can significantly improve device performance by suppressing carrier recombination. The thin PS layer also protects the perovskite layer from water and moisture damage.  $  ^{11}  $   Zhang et al. reported that the insertion of a thin insulating poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) between the perovskite and p-doped hole transport layer (HTL) also significantly improve the photovoltaic performance of PSCs. The steady-state PCE of 20.3% is achieved accompanied with the negligible hysteresis by passivating trap-assisted interfacial recombination and suppressing current leakage at the perovskite layer/HTL interface.  $  ^{12}  $   Huang et al. suggested that the passivation of ionic defects using quaternary ammonium halides is a general approach to remarkably improve the efficiency and stability of PSCs by passivating both types of charged ionic defect.  $  ^{13}  $   Han et al. proposed another strategy for defect passivation in perovskite films using an organic dye (AQ310) and achieved an enhanced average efficiency of 19.43%, demonstrating that organic dyes with -COOH group is commonly suitable for the use as passivator in the perovskites.  $  ^{14}  $   Li et

Based on the existing investigations,  $  ^{9-16}  $  it is found that the interface engineering play three main roles: one is to passivate the defect states of perovskite and then reduce the recombination loss of charge carriers; the second is to adjust the energy level alignment between the perovskite layer and the charge transport layer for better carriers transport; the third is to protect the active layer from the unfavorable environment damage to improve the stability of PSCs. In this work, we incorporate a luminescent organic small molecule tetraphenyldibenzoperiflanthene (DBP) ultra-thin layer into the perovskite/ETL interface in inverted planar heterojunction PSCs. DBP had been well investigated as a donor material in organic solar cells  $  ^{17-20}  $  and as a red fluorescent dopant in organic light-emitting diodes.  $  ^{21}  $  Our researches found that DBP also is an efficient interface modification layer to passivate the perovskite defect and improve the energy level alignment and thus reduce the carrier recombination at the interface. More interestingly, DBP can behave a   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   perovskite absorption spectrum modification layer.   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   layer usually has weak absorption in long wavelength region of more than 550nm. Benefiting from the multifunctional DBP,   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $  -based PSCs attained an average PCE of 15.61%, obviously higher than 14.26% of the reference device, and exhibited better air-, thermal- and photo-stability. This work provides a novel and effective strategy to fabricate PSCs with high efficiency and stability simultaneously.

Figure 3a shows the external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra of the control and DBP devices, which reflects the incident photon-to-electron conversion efficiency, being related with optical absorption ability and electrical properties of devices. It is found that the integrated current densities from the measured EQE spectra are 18.41mA/cm² (none DBP), 19.93mA/cm² (THF-DBP) and 20.12mA/cm² (CB-DBP), respectively, very close to the corresponding  $  J_{SC}  $  values of 19.59 mA/cm², 21.23 mA/cm², and 21.32 mA/cm² extracted from the  $  J-V  $  curves. To clarify the DBP effect, the differences of EQEs between the control and DBP devices are shown in Figure 3b. From Figure 3b, it can be seen that the EQEs of DBP devices are superior to that of the control device over the entire spectrum region. Particularly, it is noted that the EQE spectra of devices with DBP are more obviously enhanced in the near-infrared region. To explore the cause of EQE improvement, the perovskite films with and without DBP deposition are evaluated by UV–vis absorption spectra and photoluminescence (PL) spectra. As shown in Figure 4a, the light absorption of perovskite films with DBP are also enhanced over the entire spectrum region. To gain a more intuitive understanding, the absorption and emission spectra of DBP film, the absorption difference between perovskite films with and without DBP are shown in Figure 4b. It can be found that the absorption band of DBP is located at 500-650 nm, while its emission band is located at 650-830 nm and has an emission peak at 700 nm, which is well overlapping with the absorption spectrum of perovskite. Therefore, DBP can absorb the remaining visible light across perovskite and then is excited. Next, the positive effects of DBP are further confirmed by saturation photocurrent of solar cells. Figure 4e shows the photocurrent densities (  $  J_{ph}  $  ) versus the effective voltage (  $  V_{eff}  $  ) for the control and DBP PSCs.   $  J_{ph}  $   is determined by   $  J_{ph} = J_{light} - J_{dark}  $  , where   $  J_{light}  $   and   $  J_{dark}  $   are the current densities measured under illumination and in dark, respectively.   $  V_{eff}  $   is determined by   $  V_{eff} = V_{0} - V_{a}  $  , where   $  V_{0}  $   is the voltage at   $  J_{ph} = 0  $   and   $  V_{a}  $   is the applied bias voltage. One can see that   $  J_{ph}  $   increases linearly in the low   $  V_{eff}  $   range and then saturates at the high value of   $  V_{eff}  $  . DBP devices have higher saturation photocurrent (  $  J_{sat}  $  ) (20.57 mA/cm² for the THF-DBP device and 20.68 mA/cm² for the CB-DBP device) than that of the control device (19.19 mA/cm²). In general,   $  J_{sat}  $   is associated with maximum generation rate (  $  G_{max}  $  ) of electron-hole pairs,  $  ^{13,26,27}  $   which is given as   $  J_{ph} = qG_{max}L  $  , where q is the electronic charge and L is the thickness of perovskite film (650 nm). The values of   $  G_{max}  $   calculated by   $  J_{ph}  $   are   $  2.01 \times 10^{27}  $   m⁻³·s⁻¹ and   $  2.02 \times 10^{27}  $   m⁻³·s⁻¹ for devices with THF-DBP and CB-DBP modifications, respectively, higher than that of the control device (  $  1.87 \times 10^{27}  $   m⁻³·s⁻¹), which well correspond to the UV-vis absorption and PL measurements. For the DBP modification perovskite, the stronger light absorption and lower defect states exhibit larger generation rate of free electrons and holes.

# Conclusion

In summary, we have demonstrated that the insertion of an organic luminescence small molecule material DBP between the perovskite layer and the electron transport layer significantly improve the PCE of PSCs. On the one hand, the addition of DBP increase the light absorption efficiency of the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  active layer at near-infrared region by Föster energy transfer from DBP to perovskite and makes up for the absorption shortage of perovskite at near-infrared region. On the other hand, the addition of DBP effectively passivate the defect states of active layer and improve the interface energy level alignment, thereby reducing the carrier recombination loss at the interface. As a result,  $ 8.5\% $  and  $ 9.5\% $  gains in average PCEs were achieved by the addition of DBP in different solvents of THF and CB, respectively. At the same time, the introduction of DBP block the erosion of perovskite by water and oxygen and ion/molecule diffusion. Due to the hydrophobicity of DBP and its the passivation effect, the air, illumination and thermal stability of DPB-based PSCs are improved obviously. These results indicate that the multifunctional luminescent organic molecules as

# Experimental Section

Materials:  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{I} $  was synthesized by methods reported in the literature. $ ^{18} $   $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  (99.99%), Poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene)/poly(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS), [6,6]-phenylC61-butyric acid methyl ester ( $ \mathrm{PC}_{61}\mathrm{BM} $ ) and bathophenanthroline (Bphen) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp., N,N-dimethyl formamide (DMF) and Dimethyl sulfoxide(DMSO), tetrahydrofuran(THF), chlorobenzene (CB), ethanol, and isopropanol were purchased from Sigma. Tetraphenyldibenzoperiflanthene(DBP) was bought from Taiwan Nichem Co., LTD.

Device Fabrication: The DBP precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 10 mg DBP in 2ml THF or CB. After filtration, it was diluted to 1 mg/ml, 2 mg/ml, 3 mg/ml and 4 mg/ml, respectively. The perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 0.3482 g of MAI and 0.922g PbI  $  _{2}  $  (MAI: PbI2 = 1.095: 1) into 0.9 ml of DMF and 0.1 ml of DMSO mixed solvent. PCBM precursor solution was formulated to 20 mg/ml in CB and the formula of Bphen precursor solution was 0.7 mg/ml in ethyl alcohol.

At the first, the patterned indium tin oxide (ITO) transparent conductive glass was first ultrasonically and subsequently cleaned with deionized water, acetone and isopropanol for 15 minutes, respectively. Then the ITO glass was dried with nitrogen and placed in a plasma cleaner for 5 minutes before using. After that,  $ 15~\mu \mathrm{l} $  of PEDOT:PSS was spin-coated on the cleaned ITO at  $ 6000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 30~s $ . Then, the films

Subsequently sent the sample to the glove box and spin-coated the perovskite precursor solution on PEDOT:PSS/ITO substrate at 6000 rpm for 30 s. During the spin-coating process, a sec-butanol (250 μl) as anti-solvent was dropped on wet CH₃NH₃PbI₃ precursor film at the 8-10 th seconds after spin-coating starting. Then the resulting film was annealed at 100 °C for 30 s. The above spin coating process was carried out in a glove box under a nitrogen atmosphere with a real-time humidity of about 1 ppm. Finally, the perovskite film was transferred to a hot plate, first annealed in ambient air (at 100 °C for 30 minutes, real-time humidity was 30-50%) and then annealed in a DMSO atmosphere at the same temperature and time.
