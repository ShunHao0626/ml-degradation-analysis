Abstract: Additive engineering is an effective method to manipulate formation of methyl ammonium metal halide perovskite film for high performance perovskite solar cells (PSCs). In this work, carbon quantum dots (CQDs) prepared from two different carbon sources named as A-CQDs and CA-CQDs respectively are applied as additives into  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  precursor solution

Journal Pre-proof

in one-step processing. Film morphology and charge transport dynamics of perovskite films with different CQDs additives are investigated. It is found that the A-CQDs incorporated perovskite film has large grain size, excellent crystallinity, enhanced carrier transport ability and high hydrophobic surface. Carbon-based perovskite solar cell using A-CQDs additive shows increased cell performance with the maximum power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 13.28\% $ . While, PCE of the PSCs modified by the CA-CQDs additive and without additive is  $ 7.85\% $  and  $ 10.50\% $  respectively. The A-CQDs additive modified PSC also presents the good long-term stability and maintains more than  $ 90\% $  of its initial PCE after  $ 200\mathrm{h} $ .

Keywords: Perovskite solar cell; additive; carbon quantum dots (CQDs); functional group; stability.

# 1. Introduction

Energy conversion and storage are promising research areas to provide solutions for potential energy crisis in human society   $  [1-5]  $  . With great advantage of using abundant and clean solar energy, photovoltaic technology has gained steadily development for the last several decades. Organic-inorganic lead halide perovskite material has emerged as rising star for light harvesting, attributing to its superior light absorption property, high carrier mobility, and long charge diffusion length   $  [6]  $  . To date, Perovskite solar cell (PSC) PSC has achieved 25.2% power conversion efficiency (PCE) since it was initially reported in 2009   $  [7-11]  $  . Extensive researches have been conducted on manipulating morphology of perovskite films for high performance PSCs   $  [12-15]  $  . The main direction to improve the quality of perovskite film is to enhance film surface coverage on the substrates, gain large gain size, and decrease the amounts of grain boundaries etc   $  [16]  $  . Large crystal size of perovskite is benefit for higher charge carrier

2

Journal Pre-proof

transformation efficiency due to fewer amounts of grain boundaries, where the non-radiative loss and trap-assisted non-radiative recombination are most likely taken place. Therefore, it is essentially important to enlarge grain size of perovskite via purposely adjusting crystallization of the perovskite films. Researchers have developed various of strategies such as thermal annealing, post-processing technology and additive engineering etc. to produce good perovskite films with large grain size [17-24].

Carbon nanomaterials such as graphene, carbon nanodots and graphdiyne are efficient additives to modify the formation of perovskite. Owing to excellent optical and electrical properties of carbon nanomaterials, they have been applied into electron transporting layers (ETLs), active layer or interfacial layer etc. in PSCs [21, 25-32]. Hadadian et al. introduced the nitrogen-doped graphene (N-RGO) into the perovskite layer to increase solar cell performance [33]. The N-RGO slowed down the crystallization process of perovskite and increased the grain size of perovskite, thus the perovskite surface was passivated and the charge recombination was minimized. Zhang et al. used novel  $ \gamma $ -graphdiyne quantum dots (GD QDs) as surface modifiers or dopants to modify functional layers of  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ ,  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  and Spiro-OMeTAD respectively [34]. The synergy advantage from three modified components attributed to higher PCE of  $ 19.89\% $  for the as-fabricated PSC than that without using GD QDs. Specifically, the perovskite active film blended with the GD QDs additive formed more efficient carrier transport channel to improve the performance of PSC than that without GD QDs additives. Hsu et al. adopted carbonized bamboo-derived carbon nanodots (CND) and urea as additives to modify the perovskite film, increasing PCE up to  $ 16.47\% $  [35]. The carboxylic acid and hydroxyl-rich CNDs were effectively bonded to the perovskite to passivate the defects at the grain boundaries. Ma et al. introduced carbon quantum dots (CQDs) into the perovskite precursor solution to passivate

3

Journal Pre-proof

uncoordinated lead ions at the grain boundaries [25]. They confirmed that the addition of CQDs can reduce non-radiative recombination by grain boundary passivation. Although researchers reported that additives of carbon nanomaterials can effectively improve the performance of PSCs, reports rarely studied the effect of functional groups of carbon additives on the perovskite formation. Different functional groups of additives play crucial roles in the formation of perovskite films. The functional groups can passivate the charged defects of perovskite during the crystallization process [36, 37]. While, how the functional groups of CQDs additives affect the formation of perovskite films has not been revealed to the best of our knowledge.

Herein, two different CQDs named as A-CQDs and CA-CQDs were prepared from two carbon sources of acetone and anhydrous citric acid respectively. The different CQDs with and without nitrogen functionalities were synthesized and used as additives for the first time in perovskite solar cells. The photophysical properties of two CQDs were characterized by UV-visible absorption spectroscopy and fluorescence spectroscopy. The chemical bonding and functional groups of CQDs were identified by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. The morphologies of perovskite films modified by the two respective CQDs additives were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atom force microscopy (AFM). The as-fabricated hole transport layer (HTM)-free carbon-based PSCs using the A-CQDs additives presented better power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 13.28\% $  than that of the AC-CQDs of  $ 7.85\% $ . While the PSCs fabricated by the pristine perovskite film showed  $ 10.50\% $  PCE. It is found that the A-CQDs additive interacted with the perovskite  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $ , retarded the growth rate of crystal grains, thus the grain size of perovskite film was enlarged. The A-CQDs modified perovskite film had facilitated carrier transport and enhanced photovoltaic performance. The long-term stability of the A-CQDs

4

Journal Pre-proof

incorporated PSCs was also greatly improved. Our work demonstrated that A-CQD with favorable functional groups is a promising additive to improve the performance of perovskite solar cell.

# 2. Results and Discussion

Figure 1a&b show the synthesis schemes of two carbon quantum dots (CQDs) prepared from two different carbon sources of acetone (A) and anhydrous citric acid (CA) respectively. The CQDs were named as A-CQDs and CA-CQDs according to their respective precursors. The A-CQD was obtained by simply treating acetone with NaOH for several days. It was explained that Aldols reaction was taken place under strong base condition, and then oligomer chains was polymerized, and further crimped and entangled to form CQDs as illustrated in Fig. 1a.[38] The CA-CQD was synthesized by reacting anhydrous citric acid with ethylenediamine, followed by carbonization to form the CQDs.[39, 40] The reaction process is shown in Fig. 1b. The as-synthesized A-CQD can be well dispersed in ethanol and N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF), but it has poor solubility in water. CA-CQD has good solubility in water and DMSO, but insoluble in ethanol. The different solubility of A-CQDs and AC-CQDs indicates the distinct nature of the surface ligands. TEM images of A-CQD and CA-CQD were shown in Fig. 1c&d respectively. Two CQDs all present uniformly distributed dot shape, and the average diameters of A-CQDs and CA-CQDs are  $ 4\mathrm{nm} $  and  $ 2\mathrm{nm} $  respectively as shown in the inserted figure. It confirms that both A-CQDs and CA-CQDs were successfully prepared. Optical properties of two CQDs were characterized by UV-visible absorption and fluorescence spectroscopy as shown in Figure S1. The absorption peak of A-CQDs located at  $ 293\mathrm{nm} $  and two typical absorption bands of CA-CQDs at  $ 240\mathrm{nm} $  and  $ 345\mathrm{nm} $  can be observed respectively. Film morphologies of perovskites with addition of A-CQDs and CA-CQDs were characterized to further identify the effect of the CQDs additives. Fig. 5a-c show the scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of the pristine perovskite film, the perovskite film with  $ 0.1\mathrm{wt}\% $  A-CQD additive, and the perovskite film with  $ 0.05\mathrm{wt}\% $  CA-CQD additive, respectively. The average grain size of above three films are  $ 170~\mathrm{nm} $ ,  $ 245~\mathrm{nm} $  and  $ 160~\mathrm{nm} $ , respectively. The perovskite film with  $ 0.1\mathrm{wt}\% $  A-CQDs showed the largest grain size among all three samples. The grain size of the perovskite film with  $ 0.05\mathrm{wt}\% $  of CA-CQDs were slightly reduced compared with the pristine perovskite film. The grain size distribution of three perovskite films was shown in Fig. S1. It can be observed that the grain size of the perovskite/0.1 wt% A-CQDs film is in the range of  $ 180 - 300~\mathrm{nm} $ . Increased grain size of perovskites can reduce the total number of grain boundaries in the perovskite film, promoting carrier transport and reducing carrier recombination [36]. The change in the grain sizes of the perovskite can be attributed to the interaction between  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and CQDs. The incorporation of the CQDs additive can inhibit the crystallization rate of the perovskite, thus affect the grain sizes. It was found that the two CQDs presented significantly different phenomenon on the perovskite crystal growth. When A-CQDs were used as additives,  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  can strongly interact with the  $ \mathrm{C = O} $  in the A-CQDs to delay the rapid crystallization of perovskites, increasing the grain sizes. However, the interaction of the CA-CQD additive with the  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  might be relatively weak, resulting in smaller average crystal grain. Figure 6b shows the XRD patterns of the pristine perovskite film, the perovskite/0.1 wt% A-CQDs film, and the perovskite/0.05 wt% CA-CQDs film. XRD peaks located on 14.1°, 28.5°, 31.9°, 40.7° and 43.2° were respectively corresponded to the crystal planes of (110), (220), (114), (224) and (314) of the MAPbI₃ orthogonal lattice [35]. No diffraction peak of PbI₂ can be detected at 12.5° for all three films, confirming the completed conversion of the PbI₂ to the MAPbI₃ for all three conditions. The peak intensity at (110) and (220) for the perovskite/0.1 wt% A-CQDs film was increased. In order to quantify crystallinity of the as-prepared perovskite films, the full-width half-maxima (FWHM) of the (110) plane was measured and evaluated by using the Scherrer equation. The pristine perovskite film and the perovskite/0.05 wt% CA-CQD film showed almost identical value of the FWHM. The FWHM of perovskite film with the 0.1 wt% A-CQDs reduced significantly, indicating that the perovskite film with 0.1 wt% A-CQD additive promoted better crystal crystallinity and higher average grain size [44]. The XRD results are in good agreement with the film morphology images in the Fig. 4. The XRD results confirmed that the A-CQDs can be used as effective additive to obtain large grain sized perovskite films.

# 3. Conclusions

Two carbon quantum dots (CQDs) named as A-CQDs and CA-CQDs were synthesized from two different carbon sources of acetone and citric acid, and they were used as additives to manipulate perovskite  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  formation respectively. The two CQDs showed different effect on the perovskite film formation. The A-CQDs incorporated perovskite film has the largest grain size, the highest crystallinity and the best carrier transport property, least non-radiative recombination losses among the all three perovskite films. Alkyl groups in A-CQDs improved the hydrophobic property of the perovskite film. While, the CA-CQDs showing an obviously negative effect on carrier recombination in the perovskite/CA-CQDs films. The carbon-based perovskite solar cells (PSC) with  $ 0.1\mathrm{wt}\% $  A-CQD additive achieved  $ 13.28\% $  PCE with the comparison of the pristine PSC of  $ 10.50\% $  and the  $ 0.05\mathrm{wt}\% $  CA-CQD blended PSC of  $ 7.85\% $ . The long-term stability of PSCs blended with A-CQDs has been greatly improved. This work reveals the difference of CQD additives on the formation of perovskite films. It provides meaningful understanding about how the CQDs additive would affect the perovskite film formation and the performance of the responding perovskite solar cells.

# 4. Material and methods

# 4.1 Synthesis of carbon quantum dots (CQDs)

23

Journal Pre-proof

Two CQDs of A-CQD and CA-CQD were synthesized from different carbon sources. A-CQD was obtained from Aldol reaction of acetone according to the literature method [38]. In detail, 8 g NaOH and 40 mL acetone were mixed under vigorously magnetic stirring for 1 hour. The mixture was kept under ambient conditions for 120 hours. After the reaction, HCl solution (1 M) was added to neutralize the reactant. The product was precipitated by centrifuge, and then washed by deionized water for several times. The as-obtained A-CQD solid was freeze-dried for 48 hours. The CA-CQD was synthesized via a modified method in our previous report [40]. Briefly, 4.204 g anhydrous citric acid (  $  \geq99.5\%  $  ), 1.34 mL ethylenediamine (  $  \geq99.0\%  $  ) and 40 mL of deionized water were mixed by stirring. The mixture was transferred into a Teflon-lined autoclave and kept at 200 °C for 5 h. A brownish black solution was obtained after naturally cooling to room temperature. The purification process was done by using dialysis bag to remove the impurities. The final CA-CQD power was obtained by freeze-drying.

# 4.2 Preparation of perovskite precursor solution and CQDs solution

The perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 159 mg MAI (>99.5%, Xi'an p-OLED Corp.) and 461 mg PbI $ _2 $  (>99.99%, Xi'an p-OLED Corp.) in mixed solvents of 78 mg N, N-dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (99.7%, Macklin) and 600 mg N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) (99.8%, Aladdin). The perovskite precursor solution was stirred in N $ _2 $  atmosphere overnight. Stock solution of CQDs (20 mg/mL) was prepared by dissolving CQDs in DMF solution. To form CQD doped perovskite solution, the diluted CQD solution was added into the original perovskite precursor solution and stirred for 20 min to form homogeneous mixture. The weight percentage of the CQD in the doped perovskite solution was considered by taking the relative weight ratio of CQD to the mass of MAI.

# 4.3 Fabrication of perovskite solar cells

Journal Pre-proof

Conductive FTO glass substrates were ultrasonically cleaned in bath of detergent, deionized water, acetone, isopropanol and anhydrous ethanol for 15 min sequentially. Multiple layers of nanocrystalline  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  films were coated on the cleaned FTO glass to form the electron transport layer. In detail,  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  compact layer (c- $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ ) was prepared by spin-coating  $ 0.15\mathrm{M} $  titanium diisopropoxide dis (acetylacetonate) (Sigma-Aldrich,  $ 75\mathrm{wt}\% $  in isopropanol) in 1-butanol (Sigma-Aldrich,  $ 99.8\% $ ) on the FTO substrate at  $ 2000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 30~s $  and annealed at  $ 125\square $  for 10 min. Suspension of diluted mesoporous  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  (dyesol, 18NR-T) by ethanol (1: 3.5, wt:wt) was spin-coated on top of the c- $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  layer at  $ 5000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 30~s $  and gradually annealed up to  $ 500\square $  and kept for  $ 30~\mathrm{min} $ . Perovskite precursor solution was spin-coated on top of the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  films at  $ 4000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 20~s $  after filtration. During the spin-coating process, antisolvent diethyl ether was dripped quickly onto the perovskite film. Then the perovskite film was annealed at  $ 95\square $  for 10 min. A commercial carbon paste (Guangzhou Seaside Technology Development Co., Ltd.) was doctor-bladed onto the perovskite film, followed by heating at  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 15\mathrm{min} $  to finish the device fabrication.

# Highlights

- Two different carbon quantum dots from different carbon sources are prepared and introduced as additives in perovskite solar cells.

- Large grain size, thick perovskite films and enhanced carrier transport abilities are obtained by the A-CQDs incorporated perovskite film.

- 0.1 wt% A-CQDs additives modified perovskite solar cells show increased PCE of 13.28%.
