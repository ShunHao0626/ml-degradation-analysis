# Poly(N,N'-bis-4-butylphenyl-N,N'-bisphenyl)benzidine-Based Interfacial Passivation Strategy Promoting Efficiency and Operational Stability of Perovskite Solar Cells in Regular Architecture

Erdi Akman and Seckin Akin*

The failure of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) to maintain their maximum efficiency over a prolonged time is due to the deterioration of the light harvesting material under environmental factors such as humidity, heat, and light. Systematically elucidating and eliminating such degradation pathways are critical to imminent commercial use of this technology. Here, a straightforward approach is introduced to reduce the level of defect-states present at the perovskite and hole transporting layer interface by treating the various perovskite surfaces with poly(N,N'-bis-4-butylphenyl-N,N'-bisphenyl)benzidine (polyTPD) molecules. This strategy significantly suppresses the defect-mediated non-radiative recombination in the ensuing devices and prevents the penetration of degrading agents into the inner layers by passivating the perovskite surface and grain boundaries. Suppressed non-radiative recombination and improved interfacial hole extraction result in PSCs with stabilized efficiency exceeding 21% with negligible hysteresis (  $  \approx  $  19.1% for control device). Moreover, ultra-hydrophobic polyTPD passivant considerably alleviates moisture penetration, showing   $  \approx  $  91% retention of initial efficiencies after 300 h storage at high relative humidity of 80%. Similarly, passivated device retains 94% of its initial efficiency after 800 h under operational conditions (maximum power point tracking under continuous illumination at 60 °C). In addition to interfacial passivation function, hole-selective role of dopant-free polyTPD is also evaluated and discussed in this study.

As a matter of fact, many research efforts have already been dedicated to improve the long-term stability of perovskite materials and ensuing devices.  $  ^{[5]}  $   Among the various promising approaches,

such as, encapsulation,  $  ^{[6]}  $   composition engineering,  $  ^{[2a,7]}  $   and dimensional control,  $  ^{[2b,8]}  $   the interfacial engineering has gained a tremendous attention for not only to protect the perovskite from the ambient moisture but also to passivate the defect states on the surface/grain boundaries of perovskite layer, which provide charge accumulation sites, as well as, infiltration pathway of degradation agents.  $  ^{[9]}  $   In other words, a well-regulated interlayer could improve the photovoltaic performance and device stability by shielding perovskite layer from extrinsic factors, while suppressing charge recombination and promoting charge transport.

As is known, there are two external factors leading to the long-term stability problem of PSCs: i) Prolonged humidity and ii) thermal stress. These degradation paths can be mainly associated with high volatility of hydrophilic organic cations (e.g.,   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}^{+}  $  ) and hygroscopic nature of perovskite materials and lead to the formation of defect states by triggering internal factors like dominoes over time.  $  ^{[3]}  $   Such defects accelerate the non-radiative recombination reducing the charge-carrier lifetime and form pathways for the penetration of moisture and migration of ions. Therefore, it is substantially important to heal the defects at the surface and grain boundaries to further improve photovoltaic performance and enhance stability of these devices.  $  ^{[4]}  $

Arguably, spiro-OMeTAD is one of the most commonly used HTMs in high-efficiency devices.  $  ^{[11]}  $   However, in addition to its severe morphological deformation under thermal stress, the hygroscopicity of very mobile lithium dopant (Li-TFSI) usually provokes substantial moisture instability of such devices.  $  ^{[12]}  $

Based on this assessment, high hydrophobicity, high glass transition temperature (  $  T_{g}  $  ), easy applicability, and superior optoelectronic properties are some of the factors to be considered in the selection of interlayer materials. All these desirable properties led us to triphenylamine-based materials exhibiting high cell performance and long-term stability in different positions.  $  ^{[13]}  $   As a well-known triphenylamine derivative, poly(bis(2,4,6-trimethylphenethyl)amine) (PTAA) is a milestone in the history of PSCs owing to its striking performance in electron-blocking and hole-transportation due to electron-rich components. PTAA offers great advantages owing to its tunable chemical/electronic and outstanding thermal properties not only as HTM but also as passivation material in PSCs. However, despite the strong chemical interaction of PTAA with perovskite, not only its high-price but also high molecular weight limits the employment in large-scale applications.

Similar to PTAA, recently reported poly(N,N'-bis-4-butylphenyl-N,N'-bisphenyl)benzidine (polyTPD), is one of the family members of triphenylamine, exhibited an admissible performance in PSCs and perovskite light-emitting diodes (PLEDs) due to its well-matched energy level with perovskite, high   $  T_{g}  $  , and hydrophobic nature.  $  ^{[14]}  $   PolyTPD is a fluorescent   $  \pi  $  -conjugated polymer with a wide bandgap where the   $  \pi  $  -conjugated segments are oriented in plane which makes it potentially a promising passivation material in light-harnessing applications.  $  ^{[15]}  $   Although polyTPD has been reported in countless PLED studies,  $  ^{[16]}  $   it has only employed in several inverted type perovskite cells.  $  ^{[14a,17]}  $

In one of the pioneering studies, large perovskite grains in high crystal quality was obtained on polyTPD layer, yielding a PCE of 15.3% which was threefold higher than PEDOT:PSS HTM-based control cell.  $  ^{[17a]}  $   In another inverted architecture, well-matched energy level alignment and hydrophobic property of polyTPD have been introduced with champion PCE of 18.19%, along with improved long-term stability as compared to the PEDOT:PSS counterparts.  $  ^{[14a]}  $   Particularly, the PCE of PEDOT:PSS HTM employing devices rapidly decreased to nearly 0% after 16 days under ambient atmosphere with a relative humidity (RH) of around 70%, while that of polyTPD counterparts retained over 85% of their initial performance.

To the best of our knowledge, no studies have been reported based on polyTPD HTM employed in regular architecture. Thanks to its substantial properties, it would be possible to apply polyTPD in regular-type perovskite cells. In this study, we reported two different functions of highly selective polyTPD donors in regular-type planar PSCs. In one, dopant-free polyTPD molecules have been employed as an HTM to eliminate the drawbacks of state-of-the-art spiro-OMeTAD. Despite the moderate PCE of 15.6%, a considerable stability has been achieved under operational conditions as compared to benchmark spiro-OMeTAD. In the other part, we reported

an interfacial heterojunction engineering strategy for defect healing at the perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD interface and grain boundaries of perovskites, which is known as the most vulnerable sites for degradation. The interfacial trap states have been well passivated by polyTPD interfacial layer along with the substantially minimized non-radiative recombination, which is favorable for effective charge transportation/collection. This is confirmed by enhanced open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) and fill factor (FF) of ensuing devices and is substantiated by various characterization techniques. As a result, the passivated cells yielded a   $  V_{OC}  $   as high as 1.18 V and a stabilized PCE of 21.4% with negligible hysteresis. More importantly, such a thin polyTPD layer showed an excellent long-term stability under harsh conditions such as high relative humidity of 80% or thermal stress at 60 °C, showing the effectiveness of defect healing to obtain high efficiency and operationally stable perovskite cells under harsh conditions. We believe that dual-functional polyTPD provides an insight in the design of high-efficiency and stable PSCs in regular architecture.

In this study, we investigated the dual-functional role of polyTPD molecule as i) HTM and ii) interfacial passivation material in the regular configuration of planar-type PSCs. The devices were fabricated in the architecture of FTO/c-SnO $ _2 $ /FA $ _{0.85} $ MA $ _{0.15} $ Pb(I $ _{0.85} $ Br $ _{0.15} $ ) $ _3 $ /polyTPD/Au and FTO/c-SnO $ _2 $ /FA $ _{0.85} $ MA $ _{0.15} $ Pb(I $ _{0.85} $ Br $ _{0.15} $ ) $ _3 $ /polyTPD/spiro-OMeTAD/Au. The molecular structure of polyTPD and schematic illustration of the fabricated devices are shown in Figure 1a,b, respectively. PolyTPD includes two triphenylamine units around the biphenyl core which is formed by covalently bonded benzene rings. As have been reported so far, triphenylamine-based molecules have an outstanding effect on the charge transport properties and carrier dynamics of the perovskite devices. $ ^{[18]} $  As a triphenylamine derivative, polyTPD also exhibits fascinating properties such as beneficial energy levels indicated by UV photoelectron spectroscopy measurements (Figure 1c–f and Figure S1, Supporting Information), whereby its highest occupied molecular orbital level of  $ -5.34 $  eV perfectly matches the analog values of perovskite ( $ -5.53 $  eV) and spiro-OMeTAD ( $ -5.20 $  eV), leading to a good band alignment of hole injection at the interface (Figure 1g). $ ^{[19]} $  Moreover, polyTPD acts somewhat as an electron blocking layer on the basis of relatively high-lying lowest unoccupied molecular orbital level of  $ -2.23 $  eV above the singlet ground state. Hereby, interfacial charge recombination can be severely alleviated. $ ^{[20]} $

In addition to the band alignment, mobility (  $  \mu  $  ) of employed hole transporting layers (HTLs)/interfacial layers is responsible for the effective charge carrier lifetimes. Hole mobility values of the corresponding materials were estimated from the Poole-Frenkel field-dependent mobility model by using the equation of   $  \mu=\mu_{0}\exp(a\sqrt{E})  $  , where,   $  \mu_{0}  $   is mobility at zero field, E is electric field strength, and   $  \alpha  $   is Poole-Frenkel parameter) (Figure 2a). In comparison, polyTPD exhibits a hole mobility as high as   $  \approx4\times10^{-4}~cm^{2}~V^{-1}s^{-1}  $   at an applied field strength of   $  1.6\times10^{5}~V~cm^{-1}  $   which is remarkably higher than that of dopant-free spiro-OMeTAD (  $  \approx2\times10^{-5}~cm^{2}~V^{-1}~s^{-1}  $  ) and most of other dopant-free HTMs reported in the literature.  $  ^{[21]}  $   This leads to the light-generated holes to move large enough distances to be extracted as current, instead of losing their energy as heat within the cell. It has been shown previously that HTM layer has a vital role on the degradation rate of PSCs especially in regular architecture. Therefore, the photostability of unencapsulated devices was evaluated under AM 1.5 G conditions at 55 °C. As shown in Figure 3c, polyTPD HTM-based device exhibited a remarkable stability by maintaining >90% of its initial efficiency after 500 h at 55 °C while around 70% of the initial efficiency has been lost for spiro-OMeTAD HTM-based device. This outstanding difference in photostability can be ascribed to ultra-hydrophobic property of polyTPD and the absence of hydrophilic additives.  $  ^{[14a]}  $   Moreover, as revealed by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurement (Figure S6, Supporting Information), relatively high glass transition temperature (  $  T_{g}  $  ) of polyTPD (228 °C) is also highly responsible for the prolonged stability under thermal stress. Despite the relatively low efficiency of polyTPD in regular type PSCs, its striking photostability can pave the way towards large-scale commercial deployment.

In the second section of this study, we tested these fascinating properties of polyTPD as a passivation layer between perovskite and spiro-OMeTAD HTM to alleviate interfacial recombination pathways and trigger hole transportation at the

corresponding interface. A strong coordination between central nitrogen atom of the triphenylamine group in polyTPD can be formed with Lewis acid sites, such as uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   defects via Lewis acid–base interaction, thereby suppresses the formation of   $  Pb^{0}  $   defects. This phenomenon is confirmed by investigating the binding energy changes of uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy analysis (Figure S7, Supporting Information). A shift toward a lower binding energy of about 0.3 eV was occurred at the Pb   $  4f_{7/2}  $   and Pb   $  4f_{5/2}  $   peaks after passivation of perovskite surface, corroborating the interaction between polyTPD and uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $  . Moreover,   $  Pb^{0}  $   species also disappeared after passivation. The chemical interaction between polyTPD and perovskite was further revealed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis. The FTIR measurements were conducted in attenuated total reflection mode in the range of 4000–1000 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  . As depicted in Figure S8, Supporting Information, the red-shift of C–N stretch signifies that the polyTPD molecules mainly coordinate to Pb ions in perovskite.  $  ^{[2a,25]}  $

As far as we know, controllable deposition of passivation layers is critical to achieve high device performance. To this end, the effect of polyTPD passivation layer on the morphological properties of double-cation   $  FA_{0.85}MA_{0.15}Pb(I_{0.85}Br_{0.15})_{3}  $   perovskite film was investigated on the basis of different concentrations (ranging from 1 to 10 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ). As clearly seen in Figure 4a, SEM image of pristine perovskite film demonstrates deep slits extending along the grain boundaries while well-passivated surface without obvious defects/pin-holes is achieved in the presence of 4 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   of polyTPD layer (Figure 4b). A uniform coverage can be clearly seen as compared to 1 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   of polyTPD layer (Figure S9, Supporting Information). The thicknesses of the polyTPD layers were estimated to be 7 ± 1.6 nm by using a profilometer over the polyTPD film prepared in 4 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   concentration. To get a deeper insight into the effect of polyTPD layer on the surface profile of perovskite films, we carried out atomic force microscopy measurements for varying polyTPD concentrations (Figure S10, Supporting Information). All the passivated perovskite films (12.2–17.4 nm) demonstrated lower surface root-mean-square roughness compared to pristine film (18.5 nm). It is therefore obvious that the polyTPD passivation approach favors more for the formation of high quality perovskite film, which can be ascribed to the suppression of PbI  $  _{2}  $   over crystallization and/or passivation of deep grain boundaries.

These results demonstrated that both   $  n_{t,e}  $   and   $  n_{t,h}  $   values in pristine perovskite films are reduced after polyTPD modification, revealing the impact of passivation strategy on the trap states.

We next evaluated the effects of passivation on the interfacial charge transport dynamics by performing steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) characterizations. A notable quenching in PL spectra was observed when polyTPD was employed as a passivant at the interface of perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD (Figure 6d). This phenomenon indicates that the surface passivation accelerates carrier extraction from perovskite to spiro-OMeTAD, contributing to the improvement of  $  J_{SC}  $  and FF parameters.[30] Similar results were further provided by the TRPL spectroscopy measurements showing biexponential decay with fast and slow components (Figure 6e and Table S3, Supporting Information). The fast decay component ( $  \tau_1  $ ) mainly represents the non-radiative recombination induced by trap states at grain boundaries and surfaces while the slow decay component ( $  \tau_2  $ ) is assigned to the free carrier recombination (radiative recombination).[9b,31] The decrease in  $  \tau_1  $  component suggests that polyTPD passivated perovskite (3.5 ns) injects photogenerated holes into spiro-OMeTAD faster than the pristine perovskite (6.8 ns), implying an enhancement of the charge extraction process at the corresponding interface. This should be ascribed to the minimized interfacial trap state and hole transfer ability of the passivant.[32] As far as we know, reduced PL decay time is beneficial for suppressing the charge recombination at the interface and for improving the photovoltaic performance. We then performed the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy measurement with a frequency ranging from 1 Hz to 500 kHz to study the charge transfer behaviors of these devices. Figure 6f shows the Nyquist plots of devices in the dark under a reverse potential of 1.0 V (near the open-circuit potential). As is known, the arc at high frequencies represents the carrier transport features while the incomplete semicircle in low frequency is ascribed to the carrier recombination process at interfaces. The values of recombination resistance ( $  R_{\text{rec}}  $ ) and charge transfer resistance ( $  R_{\text{ct}}  $ ) were estimated by fitting the curves according to the equivalent circuit. The passivated cell exhibited a lower  $  R_{\text{ct}}  $  (308 vs 630 Ω) and higher  $  R_{\text{rec}}  $  (3912 vs 2175 Ω) compared with the control cell, indicating a faster charge-carrier transfer and lower recombination rate, in good agreement with the previous results.

As aforementioned, filling the grain boundaries and covering the surface defects using polyTPD passivation not only improve the efficiency but also enhance the long-term stability. To elucidate this, the fabricated cells were subjected to stability testing under different conditions. We first monitored the shelf-stability of devices under dry-air conditions by measuring the J-V scans periodically to assess the device performance. Both unencapsulated devices exhibited an admissible shelf-life during 60 days under dark. The efficiency evaluation plots versus storage time (Figure S16, Supporting Information) demonstrate a slight increase in efficiency for control and passivated devices at the beginning of storage, which can be ascribed to the oxidation behavior of spiro-OMeTAD. After reaching the maximum efficiency, both devices began to lose efficiency steadily and maintained over 85% (control cell) and 98% (passivated cell) of their initial efficiencies after having been stored for 60 days. As can be also seen here, shelf-stability cannot accurately reflect the real degradation progress in PSCs. Therefore, we have also conducted aging tests of under harsh conditions, such as high temperature, high humidity, and light soaking.

The operational stability of fabricated cells was monitored by MPPT under continuous illumination system equipped with a white LED lamp at 60 °C. As shown in Figure 7a, the passivated device showed only less than 5% efficiency drop after 800 h. By contrast, the control device showed severely deteriorated operational stability after merely 400 h. As compared the dry-air conditions, both light soaking and thermal stress exhibited a strong degradation effect on the control device which can be attributed to highly-mobile Li-doped nature and amorphous state of spiro-OMeTAD.  $  ^{[33]}  $   These drawbacks were severely suppressed by polyTPD barrier with high   $  T_{g}  $   as revealed by DSC measurement (Figure S6, Supporting Information). As is well known, the rapid degradation mechanism under MPP conditions is activated by different extrinsic (moisture, thermal stress, oxygen-rich conditions) and intrinsic (perovskite stoichiometry, ion diffusion, Au migration) factors.  $  ^{[34]}  $   Under these undesired circumstances, perovskite devices would undergo a rapid degradation. The considerable improvement in operational stability of perovskite devices can be attributed to multi-functions of polyTPD material revealing thermal robustness, ultra-hydrophobic nature, and efficient hole extraction ability as discussed above. Moreover, the presence of defects (especially grain boundaries) may provide a favorable pathway for the ion/metal migration causing degradation. In this respect, polyTPD can also play a key role in suppressing possible ion/metal migration as a result of the formation of smooth and compact perovskite surface with reduced grain boundaries where the volatile ions can readily escape.  $  ^{[2a]}  $   Such thoroughly passivated grain boundaries might be also responsible for the mitigation of lithium (Li  $  ^{+}  $  ) ion diffusion, though further studies are required to verify this. Although several studies reported that Li  $  ^{+}  $   ion diffusion can improve the perovskite charge transport  $  ^{[35]}  $   and device characteristics, degradation effect of very mobile Li  $  ^{+}  $   diffusion over time under operational conditions has a dominant impact on the poor device stability.  $  ^{[36]}  $

In conclusion, we investigated the dual-functional role of polyTPD as an HTM and a passivation material in regular type PSCs. As an HTM, polyTPD exhibited a moderate efficiency with considerable photostability owing to its ultra-hydrophobic character and hydrophilic additives-free nature. In the lateral form, polyTPD could not only protect perovskites from degradation agents, but also promote hole transportation and reduce charge-carrier recombination by modulating the interfacial trap states by forming Lewis acid–base interaction, resulting in a high efficiency and excellent operational stability. As a result, we achieved perovskite devices over 21% stabilized efficiency with ≈95% retention after 800 h under operational conditions. The

universality of polyTPD passivation strategy was confirmed by testing different perovskite compositions. We anticipate that the defect healing by outstanding materials will be a crucial strategy to boost the efficiency of PSCs to the theoretical Shockley–Queisser limit, as well as, to stabilize perovskite materials.
