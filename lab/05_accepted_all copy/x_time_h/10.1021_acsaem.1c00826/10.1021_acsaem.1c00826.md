# Multi-Scalable Grain Growth via Phenyl- $ C_{60} $ -Butyric Acid Methyl Ester Molecular Aggregation in Perovskite Solar Cells

Su Ryong Ha,  $  ^{1}  $   Saemon Yoon,  $  ^{1}  $   Sangwon Eom, Woo Hyeon Jeong, Jeseob Woo, Jonghee Yang Daekyung Sung, Jae Taek Oh, Hong In Jeong, Won Il Choi, Youngjong Kang, Bo Ram Lee,  $  ^{*}  $   Dong-Won Kang,  $  ^{*}  $   and Hyosung Choi  $  ^{*}  $

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: The light-harvesting properties of perovskite solar cells (PeSCs) are determined by a handful of properties, including the grain size of the perovskite crystallites. Here, we report a successful strategy for controlling the grain sizes of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   perovskite films via inducing solubility-dependent aggregation of the phenyl-  $  C_{60}  $  -butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) molecules in the solution. The solubility of PCBM in toluene (Tol) was modulated by varying the solution temperature from 75 to 25 °C, which provides a mean for tuning the grain size over an order of magnitude from 0.8–1.4 to 18.1–23.2 μm. Using this tunability of grain size, we demonstrate the correlation between the grain size of perovskites and the electrical property via transient charge

dynamics and impedance spectroscopy in solar cells. Devices prepared by this method exhibit much shorter extraction times   $  (1.46-0.88\ \mu s)  $   and improved recombination resistances   $  (20.7-93.3\ k\Omega)  $  , compared to the control devices. Optimization of the PCBM solution temperature results in PeSCs with a higher power conversion efficiency of 15.95% and current density   $  (J_{sc})  $   of 22.93 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , compared to those using pure Tol treatment (11.42% and 16.51 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ). Our approach therefore provides a facile and promising method for improving the performance and stability of PeSCs by controlling the grain size of perovskites.

KEYWORDS: perovskites, perovskite solar cells, grain size, molecular aggregation, PCBM

# 1. INTRODUCTION

Solar energy is a promising candidate to replace the fossil fuels due to its renewable, sustainable, and eco-friendly properties. In particular, silicon-based solar cells make up a significant portion of the solar cell industry and have been studied in depth over the past decades. However, the disadvantages of silicon-based solar cells such as high cost, low efficiency, large space requirements, and inflexibility have recently kindled the research into next-generation solar cells using new materials such as conjugated polymers, quantum dots, and organic-inorganic hybrid perovskites. Among these, hybrid perovskite-based solar cells have received extensive attention because of their excellent advantages such as low cost, high efficiency, lightweight, and device flexibility.  $  ^{1-6}  $   Moreover, extensive efforts have been made to enhance the power conversion efficiency (PCE) and the device stability via control of the grain size, morphology, and structure of perovskites, as well as the adjustment of the precursor ratio and further improvements in the charge transport layer.  $  ^{7-11}  $

Control over the perovskite crystallites to yield low defects and large grain sizes has been one of the most successful strategies for improving the device performances in perovskite solar cells (PeSCs). Perovskites with larger grain size can

Molecular aggregation in solution is largely influenced by the solubility, which in turn is generally determined by a number of physical and chemical factors such as the polarity of the solvent and solution temperature. In our previous study, the PCBM molecular aggregation was controlled by the use of different solvents with varying degrees of PCBM solubility such as chlorobenzene (CB), chloroform (CF), and toluene (Tol); the influence of this aggregation to the surface morphology and charge transport pathway in perovskite films was shown. However, this previous study was mainly focused on PCBM penetration and effects of intermixing layers of perovskites and PCBM on device performances, rather than being a study on the change of grain size properties of perovskites.  $  ^{18}  $

Thus, there is considerable room for a systematic investigation on the potential of this solubility modulated control over the perovskite grain sizes. To this end, we successfully demonstrated the grain size modulation of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   perovskite films by applying solubility-dependent aggregation behavior of the PCBM molecules in Tol at various temperatures. The solubility of PCBM in Tol (PCBM–Tol) was modulated by varying the solution temperatures from 75 to 25 °C, which provides a mean for tuning the grain size over an order of magnitude from 0.8–1.4 to 18.1–23.2 μm as the solution temperature is decreased. In particular, a PeSC with the largest grain size exhibits the highest PCE of 15.95% with a short-circuit current density (  $  J_{sc}  $  ) of 22.93 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   and improved long-term stability, compared to the control device using pure Tol treatment (11.42% and 16.51 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ). systematic correlation between the grain sizes and the electrical properties of the perovskites is supported by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and transient charge dynamics analyses.

# 2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

2.1. Device Fabrication. The patterned ITO glass substrates (  $  R_{sheet}  $  : 30   $  \Omega  $     $  sq^{-1}  $  ) were cleaned via sequential sonication with deionized water, acetone, and 2-propanol for 30 min. After drying, the substrates were subjected to UV-  $  O_{3}  $   treatment for 15 min. Subsequently, the PEDOT:PSS (Clevios VP AI 4083) solution was spin-coated at 3000 rpm for 30 s and then stored in a vacuum chamber for 20 min. The precursor solution of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   (45 wt %.) consisting of methylammonium iodide (Greatcell Solar) and PbI  $  _{2}  $   [lead(II) iodide, 99.999%, Sigma-Aldrich (a molar ratio of 1.1:1) dissolved in mixed solvents of dimethyl sulfoxide/dimethylformamide (1:10 v/v) was spin-coated on the PEDOT:PSS layer. The precursor solution was spin-coated at 1000 rpm for 5 s and then at 5000 rpm for 50 s. For both reference devices and PCBM-Tol devices, an anti-solvent process was performed after 10 s at the time of spin-coating. The anti-solvent treatment involved a Tol-based solution containing 32 mg PCBM (purity > 99.5%) in 1 mL of Tol. After the spin-coating process, the film was thermally annealed at 110 °C for 10 min. For the reference device fabrication, we spin-coated PCBM dissolved in CB with a concentration of 30 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   at 900 rpm for 30 s. The recoating method proceeds in two steps. First, a washing process is performed using a CB solvent as the process of removing the uneven PCBM covering the perovskite grains. Second, the PCBM dissolved in CB to be used for the uniform ETL is spin-coated through the recoating method. The process involves spin-coating of PCBM dissolved in CB with a concentration of 20 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   at 1500 rpm for 30 s. Subsequently, a 100 nm aluminum metal electrode was deposited on top of the PCBM layer under high vacuum (<10  $  ^{-6}  $   Torr) by thermal evaporation. The active area of the Al electrode was found to be 0.13 cm  $  ^{2}  $  .

2.2. Characterization. The current density–voltage (J-V) parameters of the PeSCs were measured by an LCS-100 series small-area solar simulator. External quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra were measured using a power source (Abet Technologies 150 W xenon lamp, 13014) with a monochromator (DongWoo Optron, MonoRa500i) and a CompactStat potentiostat (Ivium Technologies) to detect the response of the spectral wavelengths. EIS measurements were carried out with a potentiostat (Ivium Technologies, Compactstat) by sweeping AC voltage with the frequency from 0.01 to 500,000 Hz under dark conditions. X-ray diffraction (XRD) data were measured using a Rigaku model SmartLab at Hanyang LINC + Analytical Equipment Center (Seoul). Transient photovoltage (TPV) and transient photocurrent (TPC) measurements were performed by a nanosecond-pulsed Nd:YAG laser source (OPOTEK, Vibrant 355 LD) and a continuous light illumination from a xenon lamp for   $  V_{oc}  $   generation. A laser pulse of 532 nm wavelength was applied to the devices, and the fluorescence of the pulse was adjusted by using a series of neutral density filters to generate small perturbation of transient signals (within 5% of applied steady-state   $  V_{oc}s  $  ). The repetition rate and time width of the laser were 10 Hz and 5 ns, respectively. The transient signals were collected by using an oscilloscope (Agilent, DSO3202A) with an input terminal of 1 MΩ and an external terminal of 50 Ω for TPV and TPC measurements, respectively. During the measurements, the applied photovoltages and photocurrents of the devices were monitored by using a Keithley 2400 source meter. The resulting transients fitted to the triexponential decay model and the average time constants, calculated by an

equation  $ \Sigma A_{i}\tau_{i}^{2}/\Sigma A_{i}\tau_{j} $  were defined as the carrier lifetime and transit time for TPV and TPC measurements, respectively. The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra were measured using a pulsed xenon lamp (PerkinElmer, LS 55). Optical and polarized optical microscopic images were recorded on the Olympus BX51 TR-N33MU instrument. The atomic force microscopy (AFM) images were obtained using an atomic force microscope (Park Systems, XE-100) with a non-contact detection mode. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained using a field emission SEM (Carl Zeiss, SIGMA) instrument. The aggregate size distribution was obtained using dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis with the Zetasizer (Otsuka Electronic, ELSZ-2000) instrument.

It is well known that large PCBM aggregate effectively inhibits solvent evaporation of the perovskite precursor, thereby allowing the perovskite crystals to grow slowly into large grains during the annealing process and vice versa (Figure 2a);  $  ^{18,27}  $  therefore, large grains are expected for films processed with low-temperature PCBM solution. To investigate this grain size variation of perovskites as influenced by the PCBM–Tol solution temperature, we measured SEM and AFM of resulting perovskite films, as shown in Figure 2. Figure 2b,f provides the SEM and AFM images for the reference perovskite films, respectively. Figure 2c–e shows the sizes of perovskite grain depending on the different PCBM solution temperature, which were approximately 0.8–1.4  $  \mu  $  m (75 °C PCBM–Tol), 4.8–6.4  $  \mu  $  m (50 °C PCBM–Tol), and 18.1–23.2  $  \mu  $  m (25 °C PCBM–Tol). This trend in grain sizes is also supported by optical micrographs (Figure S1) and visual inspection of the perovskite precursor films (i.e., before the annealing step): photographs of the precursor films treated with pure Tol is dark brown, whereas the films treated with PCBM–Tol solutions are light brown with increasing opacity as the temperature is increased (Figure S2).  $  ^{28}  $  This difference in color arises from the difference in the rate of crystallization, where the color becomes much brown colored as the crystallization progresses. Thus, the Tol-treated film undergoes fast crystallization to show brown color, whereas the PCBM–Tol-treated film crystallizes slowly as the solution temperature is decreased (i.e., the solvent confinement effect effectively

traps the film between the amorphous precursor and crystalline product states).

In addition, the EDS data are shown in Figure S3, PCBM exists at the perovskite grain boundary, and the size distribution of the PCBM aggregates present at the interface affects the solvent confinement effect during the growth of the perovskite crystal, and the crystal size changes.

These combined DLS and micrograph measurements thus provide us with a correlation between the temperature-dependent PCBM aggregation and the perovskite grain sizes, which is shown in Figure S4. To confirm the crystallinity of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   perovskites depending on the conditions, XRD was measured and is shown in Figure S5. XRD samples of each perovskite were prepared after the PCBM was washed away. All perovskite films with different conditions show the same reflection peaks at 14.1, 28.4, 31.9, 40.4, and 43.2°, corresponding to the (110), (220), (310), (224), and (314) planes,  $  ^{18,29-32}  $   respectively. This result exhibits successful conversion into the tetragonal   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   phase after PCBM aggregates treatment.

The crystallinity of grains could be determined from the XRD peak intensities and line widths of the perovskite films. In general, more crystalline films exhibit increased reflection intensities together with narrow line widths;  $  ^{18,33-37}  $  therefore, the higher intensities and reduced line widths of the PCBM–Tol-treated films against the Tol-treated reference clearly show the more crystalline nature of the former samples, especially as the PCBM–Tol solution temperature is reduced (Table S1). Taken together, this increased crystallinity and large grain sizes suppress the grain boundary effects, such as trapping of charge carriers and initiation of perovskite decomposition, resulting in a significantly enhanced device performances (vide infra).

While the low-temperature PCBM–Tol solution treatment results in large perovskite grains with improved crystallinity, this approach also has a drawback: the rough grain surface and non-uniform height distribution of the perovskite grains lead to large leakage current and poor device performance. This is clearly illustrated in Figure 2f–i, which shows the AFM topographic images of perovskite films prepared from pure Tol and PCBM–Tol solutions at different temperatures. The morphologies of perovskites films with PCBM–Tol treatment were significantly altered, compared to that without PCBM–Tol treatment. The root-mean-square (rms) roughness values of perovskites films with PCBM–Tol treatment at 75, 50, and 25 °C are 31.81, 55.83, and 83.46 nm, respectively. In particular, the rms roughness of perovskites films with PCBM–Tol treatment near the grain boundary (selected area: red quadrangle) dramatically increased as the PCBM solution temperature decreases (Table S2). This roughness and non-uniform morphology are detrimental in terms of device performances, due to the leakage currents arising from incomplete contact between the ETL and perovskite layers; therefore, an ETL that can completely fill the pinholes of perovskites and form excellent contacts at the interface is required for reducing the leakage current.

To mitigate this problem, a novel PCBM re-coating process was introduced in the device fabrication step. In this process, the coated PCBM aggregates (used to induce the solvent confinement effect) are first removed using CB, and a fresh PCBM layer is subsequently reintroduced as the ETL. The whole process is schematically illustrated in Figure 3a. The perovskite films before the washing (Figure 3b) show non-uniform morphological roughness, whereas the PCBM layer, re-coating the final film, exhibits a uniform and smooth surface (Figure 3c). In Figure S6, when looking at the surface of the perovskite film after being washed with the CB solvent, there was no physical damage or deformation on the perovskite surface, showing that only the PCBM on the surface was removed by the CB washing process. In addition, the cross-sectional SEM images (Figure S7) provide the evidence for the complete removal and subsequent reintroduction of the PCBM layers before and after the final PCBM coating.

To investigate the photovoltaic performances depending on the different grain sizes of perovskites by PCBM–Tol treatments, we fabricated the PeSCs with and without PCBM–Tol solution treatments (50 devices for each condition). The energy-level diagram of our device is shown in Figure S8.  $  ^{11}  $   As shown in Figures 4a, S9, and Table 1, the device performances tend to decrease significantly despite the crystal size increase due to non-uniform morphological roughness and large roughness, as discussed above. It was observed that the 25 °C PCBM–Tol film (i.e., perovskite films with large grains) exhibited higher PL intensity compared to the reference perovskite film produced by the conventional method, indicating that the resulting larger grains of perovskites provide reduced defect sites mainly located at surface and grain boundaries, which promote nonradiative charge recombination. Given that the PCBMs deposited via the conventional approach exclusively cover the surface of the perovskite film with negligible penetration into the grain boundaries,  $  ^{38}  $  the enhanced PL quenching in re-coating the perovskite film strongly suggests that the surface and the crevices (i.e., regions between the grain boundaries) would be uniformly covered by PCBM, leading to an enhanced perovskite/PCBM contact which provides an efficient charge extraction. To confirm the correlation between the defect sites and the grain size of the perovskites, the Shockley–Read–Hall (SRH) trap-assisted recombination characteristics in the PeSCs were investigated from the light intensity-dependent  $  V_{oc}  $  measurements (Figure 5b). The diode ideality factor n, a parameter roughly describing the type of the recombination process,  $  ^{39,40}  $  was defined as  $  n = q/kT\cdot dV_{oc}/d \ln \phi  $ , where k is the Boltzmann constant, T is the temperature, q is the elementary charge, and  $  \phi  $  is the light intensity.  $  ^{41}  $  Under the conditions  $  J_{sc} \gg J_{0}  $  and  $  J_{sc} \propto  $  light intensity, the tangent of  $  V_{oc}  $  versus  $  \ln(\phi)  $  equals  $  nkT/q  $ , from which n could be extracted. It is generally regarded that the n of a device approaches 2 as the SRH trap-assisted recombination becomes dominant;  $  ^{42}  $  the measured ideality factors of 1.23 and 1.38 for the re-coating and reference devices indicate that the SRH trap-assisted recombination is effectively suppressed due to the reduced recombination sites in the enlarged perovskite grain size upon the 25 °C PCBM–Tol treatment. Meanwhile, the light intensity-dependent  $  J_{sc}  $  characteristics in Figure 5c show a power law relationship given by  $  J_{sc} \propto \phi^{\alpha}  $ . The power factor  $  \alpha  $  generally reflects charge extraction and the bimolecular charge recombination process in the device under short-circuit conditions; as the value gets closer to unity, the charges can be efficiently extracted from the device with suppressed recombination.  $  ^{40,43}  $  Given that the PeSC with a low  $  \alpha  $  of  $  \sim0.75  $  implies the presence of carrier imbalance or an interfacial barrier in the device, the estimated  $  \alpha  $  values of 0.907 and 0.895 for re-coating and reference devices indicate that both PeSCs are relatively free from these effects.  $  ^{40,44}  $  Thus, the result suggests that improved perovskite/PCBM interfacial contact could be obtained by the re-coating process, providing better charge extraction as speculated in enhanced  $  J_{sc}  $ .

To gain more insights into the charge transport characteristics in the devices, EIS analysis was performed on the reference and PCBM–Tol-treated devices. Figure 5d shows the Nyquist plots of the devices, which were measured by applying zero bias under dark conditions. An equivalent circuit as included in the inset of the plot, consisting of a series resistance   $  (R_{\mathrm{s}})  $  , charge-transfer resistance   $  (R_{\mathrm{CT}})  $  , and recombination resistance   $  (R_{\mathrm{rec}})  $   with their corresponding capacitance elements, was adopted for the fitting. The fitted results of the EIS data, as depicted with solid lines in the plot, provided in-depth information regarding the charge transport processes in the devices, which are summarized in Table S4. First, the recoating device exhibited a lower   $  R_{s}  $   of 17   $  \Omega  $   compared to the reference device (32   $  \Omega  $  ). By covering PCBM into the grain boundary region, enhanced interfacial contact between the perovskite and PCBM could be established, thereby reducing the   $  R_{s}  $  . Also, the significant reductions of   $  R_{CT}  $   (from 25.49 to 9.00 k  $  \Omega  $  ) and the capacitance (153 to 22.5 nF) values of the re-coating device imply that carrier accumulation at the grain boundaries and the interfaces would be diminished, which result in the improved charge extraction and consequently,

enhancement in   $  J_{sc}  $   of the devices.  $  ^{11,36,45}  $   Meanwhile, the   $  R_{ReO}  $   an indicative of difficulty for charge recombination, was significantly increased in the optimized device (25 °C PCBM–Tol with re-coating PCBM treatment), compared to the reference device (93.3 and 20.7 kΩ, respectively), demonstrating the impact of enlarged perovskite grains on reducing the number of defect sites and grain boundary sites. This is consistent with the light intensity-dependent   $  V_{oc}  $   results and the improvement of   $  V_{oc}  $   in the re-coating device, as a consequence of the suppressed charge recombination process.  $  ^{46,47}  $   From EIS analysis, it is speculated that the performance enhancement of the re-coating device with large perovskite crystallites is attributed to the improved interfacial contact, enhanced charge transport, and suppressed charge recombination. The suppressed charge recombination and charge extraction in the optimized device could also be directly observed from TPV and TPC results. For TPV measurements, decay of the photovoltage, as a consequence of small perturbation by a laser pulse, could be described by the following approximate relationship,   $  d\Delta V/dt \propto dn(t)/dt = -\Delta n/\tau  $  , where the n and   $  \tau  $   indicate charge density and the charge lifetime, respectively.  $  ^{48}  $   This indicates that the decay of photovoltage as well as the decay of charge carrier density, mainly attributed to charge recombination, takes place with an exponential decay model,   $  \Delta V \propto \exp(-t/\tau)  $  . From the TPV decay curve, as shown in Figure 5e, the average charge lifetime, estimated from the triexponential fitting of the decay curve, became longer for the optimized device (93.9   $  \mu  $  s) compared to that for the reference device (77.2   $  \mu  $  s), in line with the larger   $  R_{rec}  $   of the device from EIS analysis by suppressed charge recombination. It is interesting to note that the absence of negative TPV overshoot during the measurements implies that interfacial recombination associated with the mobile ions was negligible, such that the measured recombination processes exclusively take place within the device.  $  ^{49}  $   Furthermore, the transit time of photocurrent extraction in TPC measurements, which was also obtained by using triexponential fitting of the decay curve, of the optimized device became faster (0.88   $  \mu  $  s) compared to that of the reference device (1.46   $  \mu  $  s), as shown in Figure 5f. This indicates that the generated charges were extracted faster from the optimized device, which is also consistent with the reduced   $  R_{CT}  $   of the devices. The TPV and TPC results, combining with EIS results, collectively suggest that suppressed charge recombination and faster charge extraction, stemming from larger perovskite grain and improved interfacial contact, indeed led to the   $  V_{oc}  $   and   $  J_{sc}  $   enhancements.

# 4. CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, we have demonstrated a successful strategy for controlling the grain sizes of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   perovskite films via inducing solubility-dependent aggregation of PCBM. Optimized PeSCs with the enlarged grain size of perovskites show a higher PCE of 15.95% and   $  J_{sc}  $   of 22.93 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , compared to the reference PeSCs (11.42% and 16.51 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ). In addition, this work shows the correlation between the grain size of perovskites and the performances of PeSCs via various analyses including the steady-state PL, light intensity-dependent   $  V_{oc}  $   and   $  J_{sc}  $   measurements, EIS, TPV, and TPC. Furthermore, our approach therefore provides a facile and promising method for improving the performance and stability of PeSCs by controlling the grain size of perovskites.
