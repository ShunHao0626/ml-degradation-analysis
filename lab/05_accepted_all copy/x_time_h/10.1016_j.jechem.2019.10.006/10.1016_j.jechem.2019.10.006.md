# Graphical Abstract

High efficiency and stability (19.58%) of perovskite solar cells could be obtained by the post-treatment of three-dimensional perovskite with 1,4-butanediamine iodide.

# Abstract

Organic-inorganic perovskites solar cells (PSCs) have attracted great attention due to their rapid progress in power conversion efficiency (PCE). However, there is still an enormous challenge to achieve both high efficiency and stability devices as the decomposition of perovskite materials under humid and light conditions. Herein, we demonstrate that high efficiency and stability of PSCs can be obtained by the reaction of three-dimensional (3D) perovskite with 1,4-butanediamine iodide  $ \mathrm{(BEAI_2)} $  vapor. The incorporation of  $ \mathrm{BEAI}_2 $  intensively promotes the crystallization of perovskite film with large grain size ( $ \sim 500~\mathrm{nm} $ ). Further characterization reveals that the post-treatment perovskite film delivered low interface trap density with long carrier lifetime ( $ >200~\mathrm{ns} $ ), long carrier diffusion length ( $ >600~\mathrm{nm} $ ) and large carrier mobility ( $ >1.5~\mathrm{cm}^2~\mathrm{V}^{-1}~\mathrm{S}^{-1} $ ). Solar cells employing such post-treatment films demonstrated  $ 19.58\% $  PCE without hysteresis. Moreover, the post-treatment devices can retain over  $ 90\% $  original efficiencies stored under ambient atmospheric conditions and exhibit better stability under  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  and continuous illumination as a two-dimensional (2D) perovskite thin layer is formed on the surface/or at the grain boundaries of 3D perovskite. This study offers an effective way to obtain PSCs with high efficiency and stability.

# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic metal halide perovskite (ABX  $  _{3}  $  , where A is methylamine (MA), formamidine (FA) or caesium (Cs); B is lead (Pb) or tin (Sn); X is a halide ion) is one of the most promising next-generation photovoltaic materials due to strong light absorption, long carrier diffusion lengths, balanced mobilities, and tunable bandgaps, giving rise to efficient light-harvesting devices based on these materials   $  [1-9]  $  . Certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has already reached 25.2%, matching mainstream multi-crystalline silicon   $  [10]  $  . In spite of this achievement, long-term stability suitable to deliver industrial application still remains complex issues   $  [11-14]  $  . Promoting the stability of perovskite active layers, concurrently having high PCEs is still a priority area for further research and development.

Three-dimensional (3D) perovskite materials sustain intrinsic instability and are prone to degradation after encountering moisture, heat, oxygen and light   $  [15–19]  $  . To enhance stability, many efforts have been devoted. For example, material instability can be controlled by compositional engineering to a certain extent, which partial substitution of MA with FA and Cs cations to form a thermally and structurally stable perovskite   $  [20,21]  $  . The main reason is that the A site is replaced by more stable ligands, forming a stable 3D perovskite phase. In addition, high stability of perovskite materials can be achieved by tuning the bond strength of the additives with the perovskites. Organic cations or halide anion vacancies are inhibited by these bonds, obstructing decomposition of perovskite   $  [22–24]  $  . Furthermore, the long-term stability of devices also has been resisted owing to low-energy barrier for the ions migration within perovskite whose crystal lattice can be easily decomposed. Many approaches have been successfully introduced to improve the stability of PSCs, including composition engineering with mixed anions or cations, interface engineering and packaging engineering, etc.   $  [25–28]  $  . Despite the tremendous improvement in the stability of 3D PSCs, the instability of the intrinsic structure has not yet been thoroughly solved. Significantly, introducing some long organic cations into the

perovskite crystal to terminate the lattice, function as moisture and oxygen passivation layer and ion migration blocking layer, producing two-dimensional (2D) or quasi 2D perovskite [29–33]. Compared with 3D perovskites, 2D perovskites possess much larger formation energy, superior moisture stability and ultralow self-doping effect [34–37]. Therefore, solar cells based on the 2D perovskites had been proven to improve device stability. Despite the significantly improved stability, the PCE of 2D PSCs is suppressed by their quantum confinement, low carrier mobility, high dielectric constant, and narrower absorption windows [38–40]. Therefore, a tradeoff between the PCE and stability has to be made.

The 2D/3D mixtures seemed to be able to inherit the good stability from 2D perovskite and the excellent optoelectronic properties from 3D perovskite [41-43]. Herein, by introducing organic ligand (1,4-butanediamine iodide,  $ \mathrm{BEAI}_2 $ ) into 3D perovskite ( $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ ) surface by vapor-assisted interface optimization, we have obtained highly efficient and stable 2D/3D perovskite solar cells. As organic components (such as MAI or FAI) easily evaporate from the surface during the thermal annealing process, a low-dimensional perovskite thin layer is formed on the surface/ or at the grain boundaries of 3D perovskites, which can effectively suppress electrons and holes at grain boundaries and the ion migration [31]. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns also prove the formation of 2D perovskite. In addition, the low-dimensional perovskite thin layer has defect passivation and defect healing effects, which prolongs the carrier lifetime beyond 300 ns, enhance carrier diffusion length over 600 nm and carrier mobility beyond  $ 1.50~\mathrm{cm}^2\mathrm{V}^{-1}\mathrm{S}^{-1} $ . Planar n-i-p solar cells made on the 2D/3D perovskite yield an increased PCE of  $ 19.58\% $  compare with the control one ( $ 17.93\% $ ). Furthermore, the low-dimensional perovskite at the surface of 3D perovskite can prevent water and oxygen ingress, which can greatly improve the stability of the PSCs. The 2D/3D devices can retain over  $ 85\% $  initial PCE stored under ambient atmospheric conditions after 1000 h. Moreover, The 2D/3D devices can retain over  $ 75\% $  initial PCE under continuous illumination beyond 150 h and have no significant degradation under constant  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  over 100 h. This work provides an effective way to simultaneously obtain high efficiency and stability of perovskite solar

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1 Materials

MAI, PbI $ _2 $ , PCBM, spiro-MeOTAD 4-tert-butylpyridine (t-BP), lithium-bis(tri-fluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (Li-TFSI) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. The SnO $ _2 $  colloidal dispersion (tin (IV) oxide, 15% in H $ _2 $ O colloidal dispersion), and all anhydrous solvents were purchased from Alfa-Aesar. BEAI $ _2 $  was synthesized by reacting BEA with hydroiodic acid in an ice bath for 2 h, following the relational expressions: BEA + 2HI → BEAI $ _2 $ . Hydroiodic acid was added slowly to stirred BEA solution with a 1:2 molar ratio in a flask. After the solution was cooled, diethyl ether was added while stirring the solution and then white powder product appeared at the bottom of the flask. The powder was filtered and washed three times with ethanol and then vacuum-dried for overnight.

# 2.2 Fabrication of solar cells

FTO were cleaned by sequentially sonication in de-ionized water, acetone, ethanol, and isopropanol for 10 min. Then the clean FTO glasses were treated under UV-ozone for 15 min. The as purchased SnO $ _2 $  colloidal dispersion was diluted by water (1:6, V:V), and then spin-coated onto the clean FTO/glass substrates at 3000 rpm for 30s. Then the films substrates were transferred to hot-plate and annealed at 150 °C for 30 min. The preparation of perovskite used anti-solvent method as previous report [31]. For BEAI $ _2 $  treatment, the perovskite films were transferred to the hot-plat in a nitrogen-filled glove box, and then the BEAI $ _2 $  power was spread out around the perovskite coated substrates with a petridish covering on the top and heated at 110°C for 1 h. After cooling down, the remaining powder was purged with a nitrogen gun and the as-prepared substrates was washed with isopropanol, dried and annealed for 10 min. Then the spiro-OMeTAD solution was spin-coated on the perovskite layers at 2,000 rpm for 30 s in a glove box as a hole transporting layer. The spin-coating formulation was prepared by dissolving 72.3 mg spiro-MeOTAD, 28.8 mL t-BP, and 17.5 mL of a stock solution of 520 mg mL $ ^{-1} $  Li-TFSI in acetonitrile in 1mL chlorobenzene. Finally, 80 nm Au was deposited on spiro-OMeTAD layer by

thermal evaporation.

# 2.3 Characterization

SEM were measured via a field emission scanning electron microscope (JEM-7500F). XRD measurements were characterized by using a D8 Advance diffractometer from Bruker at  $ 40\mathrm{kV} $  and  $ 40\mathrm{mA} $  with a Cu  $ K $ -alpha (1.54 A) X-ray source. Absorbance spectra measurements were carried out by UV-vis spectroscopy with an integrating sphere (SHIMADZU, UV-2600). AFM images were measured using a Dimension-Icon (Bruker). The current  $ J-V $  characteristics of the PSCs were measured by reverse (forward bias  $ (1.3\mathrm{V})\rightarrow $  short circuit  $ (0\mathrm{V})) $  or forward (short circuit  $ (0\mathrm{V}) $   $ \rightarrow $  forward bias  $ (1.3\mathrm{V})) $  scan with different scan rate under AM 1.5G solar irradiation at  $ 100\mathrm{mW/cm^2} $  (Newport, Class 3A solar simulator). IPCE spectra were measured in air using PV measurement system (Newport). EIS measurement was carried out by using an Electrochemical Workstation (AUTOLAB) with alternating current amplitude of  $ 10\mathrm{mV} $  at the frequency range from  $ 1\mathrm{Hz} $  to  $ 1.0\mathrm{MHz} $ .

# 3. Results and discussions

The schematic illustration of the key step is shown in Fig. 1(a). The one-step spin-coating method (detail in experiment section) is employed to form polycrystalline  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  thin films. Then the  $ \mathrm{BEAI}_2 $  powder is evenly laid on the surface of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ , followed by annealing at  $ 110^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  in  $ \mathbf{N}_2 $  atmosphere for  $ 1\mathrm{h} $ . As  $ \mathrm{BEAI}_2 $  can easily react with  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  to form 2D perovskite  $ \mathrm{BEAPbI}_4 $ , and thin layer of 2D perovskites is generated on top of 3D perovskite polycrystalline films, forming the 2D/3D stacking structures [44]. Peaks below  $ 2\theta = 10^{\circ} $  corresponding to the  $ \mathrm{BEAPbI}_4 $  phase was found in the post-treatment sample (Fig. 1d), confirming 2D/3D stacking structures was achieved.

The film quality of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite treated by  $ \mathrm{BEAI}_2 $  was observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM). As shown in Fig. 1(b), the morphology of pristine  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  thin film exhibits small grains with higher roughness (RMS (root mean square)  $ \approx 34.3\mathrm{nm} $ , Fig. S1b). After  $ \mathrm{BEAI}_2 $  treatment, the grains reach as large as  $ 500~\mathrm{nm} $  (Fig. 1c) and the surface became smoother ( $ \mathrm{RMS} \approx 13.2\mathrm{nm} $ , Fig. S1a). This is attributed to the effect of  $ \mathrm{BEAI}_2 $  treatment where the surfaces of perovskite films were dissolved by  $ \mathrm{BEAI}_2 $  vapor and re-formed after the amine evaporated. In addition, the post-treatment improves the crystallinity of perovskite. Fig. 1(d) shows the corresponding XRD of the fabricated  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  films with or without post-treatment on  $ \mathrm{FTO / SnO_2} $  substrate. A set of stronger peaks at  $ 14.08^{\circ} $ ,  $ 28.41^{\circ} $ , and  $ 43.19^{\circ} $ , assigned to (110), (220), and (330) of the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  crystal [45], indicating an orthorhombic crystal structure of post-treatment perovskite with higher crystallinity compare with the pristine one. Traditionally, 2D perovskite has larger exciton binding energy, which leads to a narrower optical absorption window compared with 3D perovskite. The optical absorption of pristine and post-treatment film was investigated to clarify the effect of 2D perovskite. As shown in Fig. 1(e), negligible differences are observed, indicating the thin 2D perovskite layer does not sacrifice the absorption ability of 3D perovskite. Furthermore, the charge transfer is not affected by thin layer 2D perovskite due to the

electron tunneling effect as previous reports [29-31].

We further carried out steady-state photoluminescence (PL) to study the defect passivation effects of BEAI $ _2 $  treatment on MAPbI $ _3 $  film. As shown in Fig. 2(a), the steady-state PL spectra of perovskite films on quartz exhibited an emission peak at  $ \approx $  770 nm, which is typical of MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite. The intensity of the peak on pristine is substantially less than that of the peak on post-treatment sample, which confirmed non-radiative recombination is effectively suppressed in post-treatment perovskite film, meaning the defects are largely passivated after BEAI $ _2 $  treatment. To confirm whether the post-treatment benefit for carrier transport, time-resolved PL (TRPL) were used to study the carrier lifetimes, diffusion lengths, and mobility, respectively. TRPL measurements were carried out with perovskite films and heterojunctions with [6,6]-phenyl-C61-butyric acid methylester (PCBM) electron and 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N di-p-methoxyphenylamino)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD) hole extraction materials, which were analyzed by charge-carrier extraction model [6]. Note that the effective lifetime is regarded as carrier lifetime to calculate carrier diffusion length and mobility. As shown in Fig. 2(b), the carrier lifetime of post-treatment film (218 ns) is much longer than that of MAPbI $ _3 $  (66 ns). With the relation of  $ \tau_{ET} = (\tau_{Heterojunction} \cdot \tau_{Perovskite}) / (\tau_{Perovskite} - \tau_{Heterojunction}) $ , the electron extraction times ( $ \tau_{ET} $ ) are estimated to be 3.5 and 3.3 ns for the MAPbI $ _3 $ /PCBM and MAPbI $ _3 $ /BEAI $ _2 $ /PCBM heterojunctions (Fig. 2c), respectively. These results are consistent with the values of SnO $ _2 $ /MAPbI $ _3 $  and SnO $ _2 $ /MAPbI $ _3 $ /BEAI $ _2 $ /heterojunctions (Fig. S2). Then the corresponding electron extraction efficiencies are calculated to be 96% and 87%. Similarly, the hole extraction times ( $ \tau_{HT} $ ) are estimated to be 28.6 and 18.4 ns for hole transport layer samples (Fig. 2d), respectively. Then the corresponding hole extraction efficiencies are calculated to be 91% and 72%. The we used the equation of  $ L_D = \sqrt{D \cdot \tau} $  and D = MkT/q to calculat carrier diffusion lengths and mobility, where  $ L_D $  is carrier diffusion and M is the mobility. To investigate whether the photovoltaic performance based on this post-treatment method could be improved, we fabricated n-i-p planar PSCs with architecture of fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)/SnO $ _2 $ /perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD/Au (Fig. 3a). In Fig. 3b, we show current density voltage ( $ J $ - $ V $ ) characteristics of champion devices based on MAPbI $ _3 $  with and without BEAI $ _2 $  treatment, measured under simulated sunlight, and give the full performance parameters in Fig. 3(b) (inset table). Clearly, the BEAI $ _2 $  treatment device exhibits a short-circuit current ( $ J_{\text{SC}} $ ) of 23.32 mA cm $ ^{-2} $ , an open-circuit voltage ( $ V_{\text{OC}} $ ) of 1.15 V, and a fill factor (FF) of 0.73, yielding a PCE of 19.58%. However, the control device exhibits inferior performance with a  $ J_{\text{SC}} $  of 22.96 mA cm $ ^{-2} $ , a  $ V_{\text{OC}} $  of 1.10 V, a FF of 0.71, and a PCE of 17.39%. In addition, the post-treatment device demonstrated considerable reproducibility with an average efficiency of 18.56% ± 1.02% for 30 devices with small distribution. However, the control devices only exhibited an average efficiency of 16.02% ± 1.17%, as illustrated through the statistical PCE distribution presented in Fig. 3(c). The other parameters of the device are also counted and presented in Fig. S3. The significant improvement is mainly attributed to the enhancement crystallinity and carrier performance of post-treatment perovskite solar cells. Furthermore, the incident photon-to-electron conversion efficiencies (IPCEs) and corresponding integrated J $ _{\text{SC}} $  for both devices are illustrated in Fig. 3(d). The  $ J_{\text{SC}} $  values extracted from the integration of IPCEs are 23.16 and 22.63 mA cm $ ^{-2} $ , which are close to that from the  $ J $ - $ V $  curves, confirming the validity of the  $ J $ - $ V $  scans. We further studied the maximum power point (MPP) stability of PSCs based on the two devices. As shown in Fig. 3(e), the stable output at MPP gives 18.96% PCE for the post-treatment device at 0.893 V and 17.11% for the control device at 0.872 V. Moreover, the post-treatment device exhibits negligible hysteresis with 19.03% PCE for reverse scan and 18.77% PCE for forward scan (Fig. 3f). Comparatively, the control device exhibits significant  $ J $ - $ V $  hysteresis with 17.23% PCE for reverse scan and 15.04% PCE for forward scan (Fig. S4).

# 4. Conclusions

In conclusion, we have demonstrated organic ligand 1, 4-butanediamine iodide (BEAI $ _2 $ ) vapor treated 3D perovskite to formed 2D/3D stacking structures, resulting in high performance and good stability devices. In details, the incorporation of BEAI $ _2 $  can significantly improve the crystallinity of 3D perovskite with improvement grains over  $ 500~\mathrm{nm} $ . Furthermore, the defects of 3D perovskite are effectively passivated with enhanced carrier lifetime over  $ 200~\mathrm{ns} $ , diffusion length beyond  $ 600~\mathrm{nm} $  and mobility exceeds  $ 1.5~\mathrm{cm}^2~\mathrm{V}^{-1}~\mathrm{S}^{-1} $ . Employed post-treatmen perovskite as the active layer, we achieved champion device efficiency of  $ 19.58\% $ , which is much higher than that of pristine ( $ 17.93\% $ ). More importantly, the post-treatment devices show much more stable against humidity, heating and light soaks. The work provides an effective way to obtain efficient and stable perovskite solar cells.
