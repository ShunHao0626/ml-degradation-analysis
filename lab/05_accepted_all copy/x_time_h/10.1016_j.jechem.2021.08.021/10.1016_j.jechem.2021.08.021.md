# ABSTRACT

Inorganic  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have a tremendous development in last few years due to the trade-off between the excellent optoelectronic properties and the relatively outstanding stability. Herein, we demonstrated a strategy of secondary crystallization (SC) for  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  film in a facile planar n-i-p structure (ITO/ZnO-SnO $ _2 $ /CsPbI $ _2 $ Br/Spiro-OMeTAD/Ag) at low-temperature (150 °C). It is achieved through the method of post-treatment with guanidinium bromine (GABr) atop annealed  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  film. It was found that the secondary crystallization by GABr can not only regulate the crystal growth and passivate defects, but also serve as a charge collection center to effectively collect photogenerated carriers. In addition, due to the excess Br ions in GABr, the formation of the Br-rich region at the  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $

Journal Pre-proofs

perovskite surface can further lower the Fermi level, leading to more beneficial band alignment between the perovskite and the hole transport layer (HTL), while the phase stability was also improved. As a result, the champion cell shows a superb open-circuit voltage  $ (V_{\mathrm{oc}}) $  of  $ 1.31\mathrm{V} $ , a satisfactory power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 16.97\% $  and outstanding stabilities. As far as we know, this should be one of the highest PCEs reported among all-inorganic  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  based PSCs.

Keywords:   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $  , Low temperature, Secondary crystallization, High performance

# 1. Introduction

In last ten years, organic-inorganic hybrid halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have developed rapidly to an incredible certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 25.5% from the first PCE of 3.8% [1-6]. In addition, the organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite has shown broad applications in LEDs, photodetectors, transistors and electronic devices [7-11]. Nevertheless, the operational stability of PSCs is a major obstacle to commercialization. The unavoidable organic groups containing perovskites, such as formamidinium (FA) and methylammonium (MA) cations, always exhibit poor durability because of the inherent nature of volatility and hygroscopicity. At the standard operating temperature of 85 °C or higher temperature, the traditional perovskite is easily decomposed into  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and MAI/FAI, negatively affecting the performance of perovskite solar cells and hindering the commercial application [12-14].

The all-inorganic perovskite   $  CsPbX_{3}  $   (X=I, Br) greatly improves the thermal compositional stability by replacing organic molecules with Cs atoms and inorganic PSCs have made great progress in the past few years [15–17,18]. Very recently, all-inorganic PSCs based on   $  \beta-CsPbI_{3}  $   which has a favorable bandgap from 1.68 to 1.70 eV have successfully achieved the PCE of 18.4% and 20.37% by Wang and

Journal Pre-proofs

Yoon et al, respectively [19,20]. However, the Cs atom (1.81 Å) is smaller than MA (2.70 Å), which will cause the lattice distortion of perovskite [21], while the Goldschmidt tolerance factor of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   will reduce to 0.81 [22], easily resulting in the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   perovskite phase transition from photosensitive phase (  $  \alpha  $  ,   $  \beta  $  ,   $  \gamma  $  ) to non-photosensitive phase (  $  \delta  $  ) at room temperature with trace moisture [23–25]. On the contrary,   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   possesses better phase stability, while the photocurrent is limited by its large bandgap (  $  \sim  $  2.3 eV), leading to the low cell efficiency. In order to balance the efficiency and stability, the doping strategy of halogen-Br doped most stable phase   $  \alpha  $  -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   was proposed. The Br dopant cannot only compensate the lattice distortion caused by the small size of Cs and improve phase stability, but also accelerate the nucleation process and crystal growth rate, and reduce the phase transition temperature [26]. Among them, PSCs based on   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   which has a   $  \sim  $  1.92 eV bandgap have obtained a high PCE (17%) with good stability and the   $  V_{oc}  $   also broke through 1.4 V with low   $  E_{loss}  $   (  $  E_{loss}=E_{g}-eV_{oc}, <0.5~eV  $  ), making it become a good candidate for the top cell of tandem cells [27].

Despite the rapid development of perovskite solar cells, their efficiency is still limited by the existence of significant non-radiative recombination [28]. In addition to the perovskite itself and grain boundary defects which have been studied extensively, the interface of ETL/perovskite or perovskite/HTL is another important source of non-radiative recombination. Interface engineering is a common way to passivate the defects of perovskite and induce a band alignment [18,29]. Zhao Huan et. al introduced an interface passivation for  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  PSCs with DPP-DTT. Due to the DPP-DTT passivation, the perovskite film quality is improved, the Fermi level is lowered, and both the performance and stability are enhanced [30]. Guo Xing et al. recently reported that FDC-2-5Cl molecular could cause p-doping effect on the

Journal Pre-proofs

perovskite surface, induce the band bending and promote the extraction of the hole to Spiro-OMeTAD, suppressing the charge recombination between perovskite/hole transport layer [31]. In general, the passivated molecular cannot only change the surface energy of perovskite but also play a synergistic role in the perovskite surface defect passivation. Therefore, we proposed a strategy of secondary crystallization (SC) for  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  film with molecular guanidinium bromide (GABr), forming the Br-rich region, reducing the defects and accelerating the efficiency of hole extraction and collection. Besides, Br-region could also regulate the Fermi level of perovskite film to achieve better band matching with HTL. Consequently, the champion device based on  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  perovskite exhibits a PCE of  $ 16.97\% $  with a high  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  of  $ 1.31\mathrm{V} $  and better stability.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1.Materials

Lead iodide (PbI $ _2 $ , 99.999%), lead bromide (PbBr $ _2 $ , 99.999%) Spiro-OMeTAD (99.5%), tris[2-(1H-pyrazol-1-yl)-4-tertbutylpyridine] cobalt (III) tris [bis-(turfluoromethylsulfonyl)imide] (FK209), bis-(trifluoromethance) solfonimide lithium salt (LiTFSI) and guanidinium bromine (GABr) all were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. Other materials are the same as previously reported [29,32]. All materials were used as received without further purification.

# 2.2. Sample preparation

The indium tin oxide (ITO) glass substrates were cleaned with the same four-step surface treatment as before [29,32]. After that, the ITO substrates were further cleaned by UV-ozone for  $ 15\mathrm{min} $  to remove organic residues. The preparation process of sol-gel  $ \mathrm{ZnO} $  is that zinc acetate and ethanolamine were dissolved to get 0.046 mol/L sol-gel  $ \mathrm{ZnO} $  solution rigorously stirred for  $ 12\mathrm{h} $  at room temperature.  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $

Journal Pre-proofs

colloid precursor was diluted to 5% with deionized water for use. The 1.2 M   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   (  $  PbI_{2}: PbBr_{2}: CsI  $   (1: 1: 2) precursor in DMSO were filtered with filter (0.22   $  \mu  $  m) for use. The different concentrations of GABr were dissolved in IPA solution. Firstly, a thin layer ZnO film was spin-coated on ITO substrate at 3000 rpm for 30 s with 30 min annealing process at 150 °C. After cooling down to the room temperature, the   $  SnO_{2}  $   nanoparticle layer was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 30s with 30 min annealing process at 150 °C. After cooling down to the room temperature, the ITO substrates were transferred to glove box immediately. The   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   precursor solution was spin-coated on the   $  ZnO/SnO_{2}  $   ITO substrate at 500 rpm for 5 s and 3000 rpm for 30 s. Then the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film annealed at 50 °C for 3 min and 150 °C for 10 min. After cooling down to the room temperature, the prepared GABr solutions were spin-coated on the surface of perovskite film and annealed at 150 °C for 3 min. The next steps are the same as before [29,32]. The device effective area is 7.5 mm  $  ^{2}  $  .

# 2.3. Materials and device characterization

The materials and device characterization are similar as previously reported [29,32]. All the devices were measured via using Keithley 2400 under standard solar simulator with an intensity of  $ 100\mathrm{mW/cm^2} $ . All the measurements of the solar cells were performed under ambient atmosphere at room temperature without encapsulation. IPCE measurements were carried out on the SCS10-X150 systems (Zolix instrument. Co. Ltd). XRD measurements were performed by Bruker D8 Advance XRD. PL and TR-PL spectra were recorded via using the Pico Quant Fluotime 300 with a  $ 510~\mathrm{nm} $  picosecond pulsed laser. The morphology characterization of the films was measured by SEM (JSM-7800F). XPS and UPS measurements were performed by the Escalab 250Xi with a source of monochromatic Al-Ka (1486.6 eV). Transient photocurrent (TPC) measurement was performed with a

Journal Pre-proofs

system excited by a 532 nm (1000 Hz, 3.2 ns) pulse laser. Transient photovoltage (TPV) measurement was performed with the same system excited by a 405 nm (50 Hz, 20 ms) pulse laser. A digital oscilloscope (Tektronix, D4105) was used to record the photocurrent or photovoltage decay process with a sampling resistor of 50 Ω or 1 MΩ, respectively.

To deeply explore the crystallization quality of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite films, the X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurement was implemented (Fig. S1a). It is obviously that they all exhibit clear typical peaks (100) and (200) crystal orientation of the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   cubic perovskite structure at   $  14.6^{\circ}  $   and   $  29.5^{\circ}  $  , respectively. Compared to the pristine film, GABr precursors hardly influence the intensities of typical peaks of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite films at low concentrations (1, 2 mg/mL), while the intensities of typical peaks would reduce at high concentrations (5, 10 mg/mL). The decrease of XRD typical peak intensities indicates the degradation of crystal quality and the appearance of lattice defects, which is consistent with SEM results. Moreover, it could be observed clearly from Fig. 2a and b that the (100) and (200) XRD typical peaks firstly shift to lower angles for the SC-  $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite films with 1 and 2 mg/mL GABr, and then shift toward higher angles for films treated with 5 and 10 mg/mL GABr, which corresponds to the lattice expansion and contraction, respectively. This phenomenon is a general reflection of the interaction between GA or Br and   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite. To further explain the internal mechanism of this phenomenon, the absorption spectra of the pristine film and SC-  $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   films were necessarily characterized by ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectroscopy and the bandgaps of all samples have been calculated. It can be observed from Fig. 2c that the

Journal Pre-proofs

films without or with GABr treatment exhibit a similar absorption intensity but different absorption onsets. Compared to the bandgap of pristine film (1.881 eV), the bandgaps of SC-CsPbI $ _2 $ Br films increase to 1.887, 1.894, 1.903 and 1.910 eV corresponding to 1, 2, 5, 10 mg/mL GABr solutions, respectively, as shown in Fig. S2, indicated that Br-ions were incorporated into the bulk by the halide exchange reaction [33]. This could explain the part of lattice contraction in XRD results. By the way, the crystallization state of the films can be demonstrated by the absorption edge steepness [12,34,35]. Quantificationally, the step band edges could be characterized by Urbach energy ( $ E_0 $ ), which could be calculated by the Urbach rule:

$$

where  $ \alpha_0 $  and  $ E $  are material-dependent constants [36]. Fig. 2d shows the relationship of  $ \ln \alpha $  versus  $ E_0 $ . Consequently, based on the reciprocal of slope near the band edge, the Urbach energy values of films obtained are 47.96, 44.24, 40.86, 43.45 and 48.92 eV for the pristine film and SC-CsPbI $ _2 $ Br films with 1, 2, 5, 10 mg/mL concentrations, respectively. The minimum value of Urbach energy indicates that the SC-CsPbI $ _2 $ Br film with 2 mg/mL concentration possesses a best crystalline microstructure and a lower defect density, which contributes to the improvement of  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  and FF.

To confirm existence of GA atom and understand the interaction between GA and other components, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were implemented (Fig. 2e and Fig. S3). As expected, a clear N 1s signal in GA  $ (\mathrm{CH}_6\mathrm{N}_3\mathrm{Br}) $  is observed in the SC-CsPbI\$_2\$Br film, but not in pristine film. In addition, the spin-orbital doublet peaks of their core-levels, Pb 4f, Br 3d, I 3d and Cs 3d shown in Fig. S3a-d all shift slightly to the higher binding energy compared to those of the pristine film, ascribing to the formed hydrogen bonds and molecular interactions between GA and halide anions, as reported before [37,38]. The chemical environment of Cs is also

affected by the surrounding Br after GABr treatment due to larger electronegativity compared to I. In general, the crystal lattice expansion is caused by larger molecules entering the lattice to form the low-dimensional perovskite, like PEA in the organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite [39]. Considering that only surface composition of films could be detected by the XPS measurement and to understand the blue-shift behavior of absorption edge, we performed the XPS depth-profile analysis for the SC-CsPbI $ _2 $ Br film with 2 mg/mL GABr to explore the depth of GA $ ^+ $  penetration and the thickness of Br $ ^- $  ion halogen exchange reaction, as shown in Fig. S4. However, the disappearance of N 1s peak at the first etching observed clearly from Fig. S4a, indicating that GA $ ^+ $  fails to penetrate into the bulk CsPbI $ _2 $ Br perovskite. Meantime, the analyzed results of Fig. S4b-d are summarized in Fig. 2f, which illustrates that when the etching time approaches to 90 s, the [Br/Pb] atomic molar ratio reduces gradually to a stable value with the corresponding increase of the value for [I/Pb] atomic molar ratio. In order to facilitate the understanding, the Br-rich region schematic diagram is presented in Fig. S5. These results and analysis manifest that the Br $ ^- $  ion diffuses to a certain depth of perovskite bulk through ion exchange reaction to form Br-rich region, which is consistent with the increased bandgap of SC-CsPbI $ _2 $ Br film.

Furthermore, we also performed the photoluminescence (PL) spectra measurements of the perovskite films deposited on bilayer ETL to study the carrier recombination behaviors, and the time-resolved PL (TR-PL) decay from the perovskite side has been characterized to investigate the charge carrier dynamics, as shown in Fig. S6a and b. Unexpectedly, the PL intensity of SC-CsPbI $ _2 $ Br film with 2 mg/mL GABr has a slight quenching compared to pristine film, while TR-PL results show a similar tendency that the lifetime decreases from 2.11 to 1.25 ns, which is in contract to normal passivation effect. Thus, we further performed PL mapping to confirm this phenomenon with the same conditions. As shown in Fig. 3a-d, the results of PL mapping manifest that the steady PL intensity and TR-PL lifetime of SC-CsPbI $ _2 $ Br film both decrease again compared to the pristine film. Especially, the TR-PL lifetime reduces to 3.4 from 5.7 ns. In order to thoroughly investigate the causes of this phenomenon, considering the limited depth of laser penetration, we have performed the PL measurements of the pristine film and the SC-CsPbI $ _2 $ Br films with different concentrations from ITO side and perovskite side, respectively. Consequently, except for the expected blue-shift behaviors, it is clearly observed from Fig. 3e and f that compared to the pristine film, the PL intensities of SC-CsPbI $ _2 $ Br films with different concentrations measured from ITO side show a negligible change, while the PL intensities measured from perovskite side decrease gradually with the increase of GABr solution concentration. Moreover, it is noticed that a new PL peak appears at 556 nm for SC-CsPbI $ _2 $ Br film with 5, 10 mg/mL concentrations in Fig. 3g, corresponding to the new absorption onset occurred in Fig. 2c, and it should belong to the low-dimensional perovskite. In addition, with the concentration of GABr increasing, the peak width of PL curves begins to widen and phase separation occurs.

This is because other perovskite components appear due to halide exchange, like  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_{(2 - x)}\mathrm{Br}_{(1 + x)} $  ( $ 0 < x < 0.5 $ ), similar to the blue-shift behavior observed in the absorption curves. These results demonstrate that the secondary crystallization of perovskite after GABr treatment forms a Br-rich region with a certain thickness and affects the lifetime of photogenerated charge carriers. However, the energy level matching of the perovskite interface has a great influence on the charge transfer, leading to an obvious quenching phenomenon [40,41]. Therefore, ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) was used to characterize the surface energy of the pristine film and SC-CsPbI $ _2 $ Br film with  $ 2\mathrm{mg / mL} $  GABr solution, and the secondary electron cutoff edge with valence band is illustrated in Fig. S7. The result shows that GABr treatment actually changes the surface energy of CsPbI $ _2 $ Br perovskite and makes band bending at the surface, contributing to the photogenerated carrier transfer from the bulk to Br-rich region, resulting in the PL quenching behaviors after GABr treatment.

The device photostability was also monitored under the continuous light illumination of AM 1.5 at room temperature in ambient condition for  $ 400\mathrm{min} $  (Fig. S9). After  $ 400\mathrm{min} $ ,  $ 80\% $  of initial PCE was remained for the SC-CsPbI $ _2 $ Br device, while  $ 71\% $  of the PCE was remained for the control device. To deeply investigate the internal mechanism about the charge transfer and recombination process of the control and SC-CsPbI $ _2 $ Br device, transient photovoltage (TPV) and transient photocurrent (TPC) have been measured, and the results are showed at Fig. 4b-c. For the SC-CsPbI $ _2 $ Br device, the charge-recombination lifetime  $ (102.67~{\mu s}) $  and the charge-transfer lifetime  $ (1.10~{\mu s}) $  becomes longer and shorter, respectively, compared to those of the control device  $ (84.84~{\mu s} $  and  $ 1.61~{\mu s} $ , respectively). The result indicates that the efficiency of photogenerated charge collection and transfer is enhanced, and the interface charge recombination is suppressed when the perovskite film is optimized through secondary growth by GABr, resulting in the improved FF and  $ V_{oc} $ .

# 4. Conclusions

In summary, we have demonstrated that the reduction of defects at grain boundaries and optimization of the band alignment between   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite and HTL were successfully realized by the secondary crystallization strategy with GABr, accelerating hole collection and transfer, and suppressing the interface carrier recombination. Meanwhile, a better band alignment at the interface of perovskite/HTL was obtained. As a result, the SC-  $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   device delivers a high   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.31 V, an excellent efficiency of 16.97% and outstanding stability. This investigation indicates

that the secondary crystallization strategy is an effective way to improve the performance and stability of all-inorganic PSCs.
