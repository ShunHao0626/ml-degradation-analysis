# A Chain Entanglement Gelled   $  SnO_{2}  $   Electron Transport Layer for Enhanced Perovskite Solar Cell Performance and Effective Lead Capture

Yuchen Zhou, Zhengyan He, Qilin Wei, Anni Sun, Zilong Wu, Dan Huang, Shufang Zhang,  $  ^{*}  $   and William W. Yu  $  ^{*}  $

$ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  is a widely used electron transport layer (ETL) material in perovskite solar cells (PSCs), and its design and optimization are essential for achieving efficient and stable PSCs. In this study, the in situ formation of a chain entanglement gel polymer electrolyte is reported in an aqueous phase, integrated with  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  as the ETL. Based on the self-polymerization of 3-[[2-(methacryloyloxy)ethyl]dimethylammonium]propane-1-sulfonic acid (DAES) in an aqueous environment, combining the catalytic effect of LiCl (as a Lewis acid) with the salting-out effect, and the introduction of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) as the other polymer chain, a chain entanglement gelled  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  ( $ \mathrm{G - SnO_2} $ ) structure is successfully constructed with a wide range of functions. The PDEAS-PVP chain entanglement gel achieves passivation and  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2 + }} $  capture through chemical chelation mechanisms is explored. The results demonstrated that the all-in-air prepared PSC based on  $ \mathrm{G - SnO_2} $  exhibited an excellent power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 24.77\% $  and retained  $ 83.3\% $  of their initial efficiency after  $ 2100\mathrm{h} $  of air exposure. Additionally, the PDEAS-PVP exposes more  $ \mathrm{C = O} $  and  $ \mathrm{S = O} $  active sites, significantly enhanced the lead absorption capability of the PSCs.

Traditional n-i-p type PSCs typically consist of a perovskite light-absorbing layer and carrier transport layers on both the top and bottom. # 1. Introduction

Lead-based perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have rapidly emerged as one of the most promising thin-film solar technologies in the photovoltaic field, achieving an impressive certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 26.7%.  $  ^{[1]}  $   However, challenges such as poor structural stability, significant ion migration, and

the potential risks of lead contamination have been hindering their commercialization.  $  ^{[2-4]}  $   Also, lead leakage not only poses a substantial environmental threat but also endangers human health.  $  ^{[5,6]}  $   Consequently, effectively addressing lead contamination while maintaining high performance has become a critical challenge in advancing PSC technology toward practical application.

They do not fundamentally solve the structural defects of   $  SnO_{2}  $   or provide effective lead protection at the interface layer.

Among the methods used to prevent lead leakage, the incorporation of lead-absorbing materials in the polycrystalline photosensitive layer is effective.  $  ^{[19-22]}  $   This approach, especially when using in situ polymerized polymers, can reduce lead leakage.  $  ^{[23,24]}  $   However, overuse of these materials leads to reducing device efficiency and stability, while underuse may not provide effective protection against lead ions.

Due to their easily customizable network structure, controllable pore size, and ease of fabrication, hydrogels have attracted great interest in a variety of fields including controlled drug release, tissue engineering, environmental governance, and biosensing.  $  ^{[15-18]}  $   However, studies on the application of hydrogels in   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL are limited. Therefore, the development of a hydrogel network that can both physically encapsulate the transport layer and chemically chelate the interfacial layer of the PSC is critical to improving its performance and environmental sustainability.

In this study, we report for the first time the self-polymerization of 3-[[2-(methacryloyloxy)ethyl]dimethylammonium]propane-1-sulfonic acid (DAES) in an aqueous phase, then combining the catalytic effect of LiCl (as a Lewis acid) with the salting-out effect, and the introduction of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) as the other polymer chain of a chain entanglement structure. We have succeeded in constructing a chain entanglement gelled  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  ( $ \mathrm{G - SnO_2} $ ). It is noteworthy that the strong multidentate coordination effects between  $ \mathrm{G - SnO_2} $  and  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2 + }} $  not only successfully regulated the crystallization process of the encapsulated films, but also minimized the Pb leakage. In the meantime, the self-polymerization in an aqueous environment also avoided organic solvents. The  $ \mathrm{G - SnO_2} $ -based PSC prepared in the air achieved a PCE of  $ 24.77\% $  and demonstrated excellent environmental and thermal stability, including a lead storage efficiency of over  $ 95\% $  under extreme weather conditions. This environmentally friendly  $ \mathrm{G - SnO_2} $  ETL offers a new approach to enhance both the efficiency and sustainability of perovskite photovoltaics in ambient air environments.

# 2. Results and Discussion

In order to achieve a stronger lead adsorption effect, we plan to prepare a chain entanglement structure with a higher density of lead adsorption sites than traditional materials. This structure may be achieved based on the self-polymerization phenomenon of DAES, combined with the catalytic and salt precipitation effects of LiCl (as a Lewis acid). Based on the above point of view, we evaluated the influence of LiCl, PVP, and   $  SnO_{2}  $   nanoparticles on the gel process by observing the change of material state during DEAS polymerization, and compared the solubility difference of monomer in methanol before and after polymerization, which is a lateral evidence of the occurrence of polymerization. Briefly, a certain amount of DAES, DAES/PVP/LiCl, and DAES/PVP/LiCl/  $  SnO_{2}  $   were placed in sample vials, respectively, and a certain amount of   $  H_{2}O  $   was added, and the samples were

left to stand for some time to observe the changes. As illustrated in Figure 1a, when DAES was used alone, only a small amount of gel formed, and the gelation proceeded slowly, primarily at the bottom of the bottle. In contrast, the inclusion of LiCl and PVP significantly accelerated gelation and increased the gel yield, resulting in a fully formed gel (Figure 1b). Further addition of  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  did not notably affect the gel yield or gelation rate (Figure 1c), indicating that LiCl facilitated the gelation process, while  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  did not influence it. To confirm the occurrence of polymerization, we tested the solubility of the components before and after the reaction using methanol. As shown in Figure 1d, the DAES monomer dissolved rapidly in methanol, whereas the freeze-dried gel retained its original state after being soaked for  $ 24\mathrm{h} $  (Figure 1e). This observation confirms that DAES underwent polymerization.

To further investigate the structural changes of DAES before and after polymerization, we employed solid-state carbon-13 nuclear magnetic resonance (  $  ^{13}  $  C NMR) spectroscopy (Figure 1f). Before the reaction, there were peaks at 125.58 and 137.57 ppm from the C=C bonds (Figure 1f top). After polymerization, these two peaks disappeared and new peaks emerged in the 20–35 ppm range, indicating the occurrence of polymerization reaction. Additionally, the leftward shift of the carbonyl carbon signal and the presence of symmetrical rotation sidebands relative to peak A in the polymer spectrum provided further evidence supporting this conclusion.

Based on the gelation process and carbon spectrum analysis, the gel formation mechanism can be explained as follows (Figure 1g). In the presence of a cationic initiator, monomer molecules are activated into positively charged reactive ions. These reactive ions then undergo chain polymerization with monomers to form polymers. Specifically, LiCl initially forms  $ \mathrm{H^{+}[LiClOH]^{-}} $  in the presence of water.  $ \mathrm{H^{+}[LiClOH]^{-}} $  then induces  $ \mathbf{C}^+ $  as the active center to activate DAES into  $ \mathrm{RC^{+}[LiCl_3]^-} $ . Finally, these reactive ions react with the remaining DAES in a chain polymerization reaction to produce a polymer (PDAES). Based on the mechanism of cation-catalyzed polymerization, we speculate that DAES can be polymerized in pure water because the ionized  $ \mathrm{H^{+}} $  cations in water generate positively charged reactive ions with DAES, and these ions will interact with the monomers to initiate chain polymerization reactions. However, due to the low concentration of  $ \mathrm{H^{+}} $  in pure water, the polymerization rate is much slower, which also explains the phenomenon observed in Figure 1a. It is worth mentioning that, compared to traditional free radical polymerizations, there are two main advantages in this system. i) It can polymerize in an aqueous environment with oxygen; ii) it reacts with mild polymerization conditions (can be carried out at room temperature) and no need for light or heat to initiate.

In order to deeply explore the interaction between PDAES and perovskite, the electrostatic potential (ESP) of the DAES monomer was analyzed using density functional theory (DFT), as shown in Figure 2a. The analysis revealed that the carbonyl group of DAES, highlighted in red in the ESP, exhibits a strong electron-withdrawing ability. This indicates that the oxygen atom in the carbonyl group carries a significant negative charge, while the carbon atom holds a partial positive charge. This allows the carbonyl group to effectively interact with positively charged vacancies or uncoordinated metal cations in the perovskite, thereby further enhancing the stability of the perovskite layer and reducing surface defects.

Next, the energy levels of the DAES monomer's molecular orbitals were analyzed (Figure 2c,d). The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels were found to be -5.34 and -2.08 eV, respectively. Notably, a distinct spatial separation between the HOMO and LUMO orbitals was observed. The spatial distribution of these orbitals corresponds well with the electron acceptor and donor regions indicated in the ESP. This characteristic of orbital separation suggests that DAES possesses strong charge separation and transfer capabilities, which are crucial for enhancing the overall performance of PSCs.[27] Additionally, the charge distribution of each atom in the DAES molecule was assessed using the atomic dipole moment corrected Hirshfeld (ADCH) charge analysis method, as shown in Figure 2e. The sulfonic acid group exhibited a negative charge of -0.78, and the carboxyl group carried a negative charge of -0.28, both reflecting a notable aggregation of electrons. In contrast, the alkenyl and methylamino groups showed positive charges of 0.09 and 0.44, respectively, suggesting more positively charged sections in the molecule. The significantly higher negative charge of sulfonic acid group and carboxyl group suggest their potential importance in facilitating electron transfer during intermolecular interactions.[28] This implies that the sulfonic acid group can strongly interact with positive charged defects in the perovskite, effectively passivating these defects. This finding aligns with the ESP analysis results, further validating the passivation effect of DAES in PSCs.

The gelled   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL we designed incorporates both PDAES and PVP, rather than relying on a single material. To better understand the weak interactions within the entangled network formed by PDAES and PVP, we employed the reduced density gradient (RDG) method for analysis.  $  ^{[29,30]}  $   As shown in Figure 2f, the RDG analysis indicates that the weak interaction between PDAES and PVP is predominantly governed by van der Waals forces, which supports the theoretical basis for the formation of the entangled network. This network not only contributes to the stability of the system but may also enhance the photovoltaic efficiency of PSCs. In addition to this theoretical analysis, we simulated the optimal adsorption configuration between the perovskite structure (  $  FAPbI_{3}  $  ) and PDAES using Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) calculations. Figure 2g illustrates the differential charge density map, revealing significant charge transfer between the DAES monomer unit and the perovskite interface. This results in a delocalized electron cloud distribution. The observed charge transfer not only strengthens the interaction between DAES and perovskite but also demonstrates that DAES can effectively enhance charge separation and transport performance through interface optimization.

Further calculations of adsorption energy revealed that the interaction between DAES and perovskite had an adsorption energy of -1.27 eV, indicating a strong interaction that contributes

to reducing the defect density at the interface. The electronic density of states (DOS) diagrams for the pure   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   structure and the   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   structure incorporating DAES are shown in Figure 2h. The introduction of DAES notably decreases the interstitial state density in   $  FAPbI_{3}  $ , demonstrating that DAES effectively suppresses the formation of defect states in the perovskite structure.  $  ^{[31,32]}  $   This reduction in defect states potentially enhances both the stability and performance of PSCs.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) provided further confirmation of the reaction mechanism for DAES. As illustrated in Figure 2i, DAES exhibited a pronounced stretching vibration absorption peak for the C=C bond at   $  \approx1702.9~cm^{-1}  $  . In contrast, the absorption peak corresponding to this bond in the polymer obtained after the reaction (referred to as PDAES) was nearly absent. This disappearance indicates that C=C bonds were consumed during the polymerization reaction.  $  ^{[25]}  $   Even when the monomer was placed in   $  SnO_{2}  $  , we obtain the same result (Figure 2j).

The theoretical calculations suggested a potential interaction between PDAES-PVP and perovskite. To validate this conclusion, we conducted experimental analyses to systematically investigate the actual interaction. FTIR spectroscopy provided preliminary evidence for this interaction. As shown in Figure S1 (Supporting Information), the C=O stretching vibration peak of pure PDAES-PVP appears at   $  1727.0 \, cm^{-1}  $  , [33] while the S=O stretching vibration peak is observed at   $  1192.6 \, cm^{-1}  $  . [34] When PDAES-PVP was combined with perovskite, the characteristic peaks exhibited a slight red shift to 1723.4 and   $  1180.0 \, cm^{-1}  $  , respectively. These shifts indicate that an intermolecular interaction between PDAES-PVP and the perovskite material did occur, potentially due to the coordination of the carbonyl and sulfonic acid groups in PDAES with the uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   in the perovskite.

Next, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to investigate the composition and chemical interactions of the G-SnO $ _2 $  composite. The SnO $ _2 $  sample in the control group exhibited characteristic peaks for Sn 3d $ _{5/2} $  and Sn 3d $ _{3/2} $  orbitals at 485 and 495 eV, respectively (Figure 2k). In contrast, the gelled ETL composed of G-SnO $ _2 $  showed these peaks shifted  $ \approx $ 0.5 eV toward higher binding energies. This increase in binding energy is primarily attributed to the alteration in the electron cloud density around Sn induced by the PDAES-PVP structure. This change will cause the inner shell of Sn atoms to lose electrons, resulting in higher binding energies observed by XPS. Specifically, the functional groups in the PDAES-PVP network, such as carboxylic and sulfonic acid groups, can interact with Sn atoms on the surface of SnO $ _2 $  to form chemical bonds or coordination complexes. This interaction alters the local electronic environment of the Sn atoms and may lead to a redistribution of electron density, which in turn changes the binding energy of electrons. As a result, the electrons in the Sn atoms can more easily transition to the conduction band, thereby enhancing the mobility and fluidity of charge carriers. $ ^{[26]} $  Additionally, the XPS results for Li 1s and S 2p in the gelled SnO $ _2 $  (Figure S2a,b, Supporting Information) confirmed the presence of the PDAES-PVP polymer network on the surface of the gelled SnO $ _2 $  film. XPS analysis further revealed the elemental chemical states at the interface between PDAES-PVP and perovskite. Figure 2m illustrates the entangled polymer structure formed by PDAES and PVP. Compared to single-chain polymers, the dual network exposes more active sites within the same volume, which greatly enhances the chelation efficiency of uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   in the film. In addition, during the preparation of the electron transport layer, the polymer skeleton structure retained due to the water loss of the gel provides more channels for electron transport. Different from traditional dense materials, its pores allow the carrier to move more freely, because the existence of pores effectively reduces the internal resistance of ETL and promotes the charge transfer between the perovskite layer and the electrode.  $  ^{[36]}  $   This design has the potential to further improve the overall performance of PSCs. In summary, DFT calculations, FTIR, and XPS characterization have confirmed the strong chelation between PDAES-PVP and the perovskite. This mechanism provides a solid foundation for future characterization and device preparation of PSCs.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was used to compare the surface quality of the ETL layers composed of pure   $  SnO_{2}  $   and PDAES-PVP. The results indicate that the   $  G-SnO_{2}  $   surface exhibited superior smoothness. As illustrated in Figure 3a,b, the root mean square roughness (Rq) of the   $  SnO_{2}  $   film was 2.15 nm, whereas the Rq of the   $  G-SnO_{2}  $   film incorporating PDAES-PVP decreased to 2.06 nm. This smoother surface promotes a more uniform deposition of the perovskite film, thereby enhancing the contact quality at the film interface.

To better assess the conductivity of the ETL during practical operation, we measured the induced current of the   $  SnO_{2}  $   layer using current atomic force microscopy (C-AFM) at a 1 V bias. As shown in Figure 3c,d, the G-  $  SnO_{2}  $   structure generated a higher induced current under the same bias voltage, indicating that the PDAES-PVP gel network (Gel) can provide more charge transfer channels, significantly enhancing electron migration within the ETL.  $  ^{[37]}  $   This phenomenon suggests that the formation of the Gel allows   $  SnO_{2}  $   nanoparticles to more effectively fill the network's pores, thereby improving both conductivity and electron transport efficiency. Additionally, the Gel structure may contribute to trap passivation, mitigating the negative effects of interface defect states on electron transport.

The subsequent deposition of perovskite films confirmed the advantages of using  $ \mathrm{G - SnO_2} $ . As illustrated in Figure 3e,f, perovskite films deposited on  $ \mathrm{G - SnO_2} $  exhibit more uniform grain sizes and reduced surface roughness. These improvements in the perovskite crystal quality were further validated by in situ Kelvin

probe force microscopy (KPFM) measurements (Figure 3g.h). According to the KPFM results, the average contact potential difference (CPD) of the perovskite film deposited on G-SnO  $  _{2}  $  was -1.28 V, compared to only -0.94 V for the control. This finding indicates that G-SnO  $  _{2}  $  significantly alters the electron affinity and work function of the perovskite material's surface, enhancing its tendency toward n-type semiconductor behavior.  $  ^{[38]}  $  This regulation of electronic properties will be further discussed in the subsequent band structure analysis.

The impact of PDAES-PVP on the morphology of perovskite films was thoroughly investigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As shown in Figure 3i,j, the perovskite film deposited on a conventional SnO $ _2 $  layer exhibits more pronounced grain stacking. In contrast, the perovskite film deposited on the G-SnO $ _2 $  layer shows vertically oriented grain growth with minimal stacking between grains. This vertical grain alignment reduces the formation of grain boundary defects on a macro scale, which contributes to enhanced performance in PSCs. Additionally, the SEM top views in Figure 3k,l further confirm that the G-SnO $ _2 $  layer effectively regulates the crystal growth process in perovskite films, resulting in larger and more uniform grain sizes. When the two samples have the same film thickness, the enhancement of the XRD characteristic peaks of the G-SnO $ _2 $ -based perovskite film indicates that it has higher crystallinity (Figure S3, Supporting Information). Additionally, in situ analysis of the photoluminescence (PL) spectrum during the extended spin-coating process revealed that the luminescence intensity of the G-SnO $ _2 $ -based perovskite film was significantly lower than that of the SnO $ _2 $ -based perovskite film during the first 50 s (Figure S4, Supporting Information). Simultaneously, in situ, XRD analysis during the annealing stage of the spin-coating process showed that the characteristic peak intensity of the G-SnO $ _2 $ -based perovskite film was barely detectable within the first 3 min, and the intensity during the first 5 min remained significantly lower than that of the SnO $ _2 $ -based perovskite film (Figure S5, Supporting Information). This suggests that the crystallization rate of the G-SnO $ _2 $ -based perovskite film is considerably reduced. We speculate that this phenomenon is primarily due to the strong coordination between the functional groups in G-SnO $ _2 $  and the Pb $ ^{2+} $  ions in the perovskite, which effectively regulates the crystallization process, slows the crystallization rate, and ultimately leads to the formation of a high-quality perovskite film. $ ^{[39]} $

To further investigate the effect of the  $ \mathrm{G - SnO_2} $  on perovskite crystal growth, we conducted high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) analysis on the region marked in Figure 3j (labeled 1). As shown in Figure  $ 3\mathrm{m} $  and Figure S6 (Supporting Information), the dark area (labeled 2) that may represent the grain boundary shows a clear stripe orientation, while no similar phenomenon was observed in the control. We hypothesize that this phenomenon may result from the gel infiltrating the grain boundaries during the perovskite annealing process, where it could serve as a template for crystal growth at certain boundaries. sponding perovskite films (Figure 4l; Figures S9–S13, Supporting Information). This suggests that DAES molecules may interact with   $  SnO_{2}  $  , thereby altering its electronic structure. Experimental data showed that the maximum energy of the valence band (  $  E_{VBM}  $  ) and the minimum energy of the conduction band (  $  E_{CBM}  $  ) of   $  SnO_{2}  $   shifted from -4.10 and -7.89 eV, respectively, to -4.35 and -8.11 eV in G-  $  SnO_{2}  $   (Figures S10a and S11 and S14, Supporting Information). The enhancement of electron injection and extraction efficiency still requires further validation through the examination of energy level changes in   $  SnO_{2}  $   and   $  G-SnO_{2}  $  -based perovskites. Initially, we determined the energy level distribution (Figures S12 and S13, Supporting Information) and energy values of the   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   perovskite structure via theoretical calculations. It is important to note that the concentration of DAES in the experiment exceeds that of PVP. To simplify the calculations and focus on the primary role of DAES in energy level modulation, DAES was selected as the main modifier for the model. According to the calculation results, the band gap (  $  E_{g}  $  ),   $  E_{VBM}  $  , and   $  E_{CBM}  $   of the unmodified perovskite were found to be 1.61, 5.03, and 3.42 eV, respectively. After introducing DAES, these values shifted to 1.58, 5.14, and 3.56 eV, showing a downward trend. This change indicates that the introduction of DAES can effectively modulate the energy level structure of perovskite, thus providing more favorable conditions for electron injection and extraction. In conjunction with the experimentally measured energy level diagram (Figure 4l; Figure S10b and S14, Supporting Information), the shift in the perovskite energy levels aligns with the trend predicted by the theoretical calculations. The   $  E_{CBM}  $   of   $  SnO_{2}  $   and the perovskite deposited on it differs by only 0.02 eV. This larger energy level difference facilitates the electron extraction process from perovskite to   $  SnO_{2}  $  , thereby significantly improving electron transfer efficiency. Additionally, the detection of   $  S^{+}  $  in   $  G-SnO_{2}  $  -based devices indicates that the sulfonic acid groups in PDAES-PVP can penetrate the perovskite's internal structure, further enhancing the prevention of lead leakage. # 3. Conclusion

In summary, we have successfully constructed a dual network gelled   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL. This was achieved based on the self-polymerization phenomenon of DAES in the aqueous phase, combined with the catalytic action of LiCl (as a Lewis acid) with the salting-out effect. This system combines the excellent conductivity of LiCl with the multi-carrier transport channel of PDAES-PVP, resulting in a high PCE of 24.77% for PSC prepared in air. In addition, the salting-out effect of LiCl during the gel formation led to the chain entanglement formed between the two polymer chains (PDAES, PVP), and this entanglement exposes more carbonyl (  $  —C=O  $  ) and sulfonic groups (  $  —SO_{3}^{-}  $  ) to the active sites, which enhances the adsorption capacity of PSCs for lead. Notably, this study represents the first application of an in situ formed gel polymer electrolyte combined with   $  SnO_{2}  $   as an electron transport layer in PSCs. This work not only introduces a new method for passivating the electron transport layer, but also provides a promising material for the development of high-performance and environmentally friendly PSCs.

# 4. Experimental Section

This section outlines the key experimental methods and theoretical calculations employed in this study. Detailed information can be found in the Experimental Section of the Supporting Information.

Preparation of   $  SnO_{2}  $   Films: ITO substrates were cleaned in an ultrasonic bath using ultrapure water, acetone, and ethanol, followed by UV-Ozone treatment. A chemical bath deposition (CBD) solution was prepared by dissolving urea, HCl, and   $  SnCl_{2}\cdot2H_{2}O  $   in deionized water. The substrates were immersed in this solution and heated at 70 °C for 6 h. After rinsing, the films were annealed at 170 °C for 60 min. For the preparation of double-network gelled   $  SnO_{2}  $   films, DAES, PVP (at different ratios), and LiCl were added to the reaction solution, and the same deposition procedure was applied.

PSC Fabrication: The perovskite film was fabricated using a two-step sequential deposition method. PbI $ _2 $  was spin-coated onto the SnO $ _2 $  film and annealed, followed by spin-coating of an organic salt solution containing FAI, MAI, and MACl. The film was then annealed at 150 °C. The hole transport layer (HTL) was deposited from a solution of Spiro-OMeTAD, TBP, chlorobenzene, and LiTFSI. Finally, Ag electrodes were deposited by thermal evaporation. During the fabrication of the perovskite and HTL layers, the temperature and relative humidity (RH) were maintained at 20±5 °C and 35±5% RH, and at 20 °C and 30% RH, respectively.

Theoretical Calculations: DFT calculations were performed using Gaussian 16 (B3LYP/6-311G(d) level) for geometry optimization. Weak interactions were analyzed using Multiwfn 3.8 with the interaction region index (IRI), and molecular structures were visualized in VMD. For VASP-based DFT calculations, the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was employed, along with the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method. Ultra-soft pseudopotentials were applied for all elements, including organic molecules, Pb, and I.
