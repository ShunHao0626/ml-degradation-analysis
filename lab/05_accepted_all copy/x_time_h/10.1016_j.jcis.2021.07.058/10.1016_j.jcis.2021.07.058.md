# 1. Introduction

# ABSTRACT

The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) is already higher than those of other thin-film photovoltaic technologies, but the stability issue limits their applications. The introduction of sulfur-based compounds in PSCs could contribute to their stability. Herein, sulfur-based compounds have been embedded into each functional layer to stabilize carbon-based PSCs (C-PSCs). Results showed that the simultaneous introduction of sulfur-based compounds could decrease the trap states of perovskite film, enlarge the grain size of perovskite, and accelerate the charge transfer and extraction, leading to an improved performance. Comparing with the device without sulfide (10.77%), all sulfide C-PSCs obtained a PCE of 15.38%. The stability test showed much better resistance to humidity and thermal stress for all sulfide C-PSCs. They could retain 80% of initial PCE after aging about 700 h at relative humidity (RH) 45% ± 10% and 80 °C.

The certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been geared up 25.5%   $  [5]  $  . However, the instability issue, degradation from charge transport layers and PVK materials, greatly limits the commercial applications of PSCs   $  [6]  $  . Reports have shown that the introduction of sulfur-based compounds in PSCs could contribute to their stability   $  [7,8]  $  . Therefore, several strategies were introduced sulfur-based compounds into PSCs to enhance its stability, such as developing stable electron transport layers (ETLs), component engineering of PVK, utilization as additives, interfacial engineering, and exploring sulfur-based hole transport layers (HTLs).

For developing stable ETLs, sulfur-based compounds have been utilized as ETLs directly or modified other ETLs.   $  SnS_{2}  $   [9],   $  In_{2}S_{3}  $   [10],   $  TiS_{2}  $   [11],   $  TaS_{2}  $   [12],   $  Bi_{2}S_{3}  $   [13], and   $  WS_{2}  $   [14] have been used as ETLs directly in PSCs, where they could overcome the shortcomings of high temperature sintering and low charge mobility. For modified other ETLs, the sulfur-based compounds could establish new charge transport channels and anchor the PVK materials by the interaction of S and Pb, such as ZnS modified ZnO [15], ZnS modified   $  TiO_{2}  $   [16], CdS modified   $  TiO_{2}  $   [17], CdS modified   $  SnO_{2}  $   [18],   $  MoS_{2}  $   modified   $  TiO_{2}  $   [19], and   $  TiS_{2}  $   modified   $  SnO_{2}  $   [20]. For the component engineering of PVK, the sulfur-based compounds could improve humidity stability, enhance light harvesting, increase crystal orientation, extend charge carrier lifetime, and strengthen the interlayer molecular interaction [21–23]. When utilizing as additives in PVK precursors, the sulfur-based compounds, such as MASCN [7],   $  NH_{4}-SCN  $   [21], thioacetamide [22], thiourea [24] and thiophene derivatives [25], could stabilize precursors, improve film quality, control crystallization kinetics, and boost phase stability. For interfacial engineering, sulfur-based compounds could passivate the defects of the perovskite surface, optimize the energy level alignment, and suppress ion migration. Comparing with the commonly used HTLs (SpiroOMeTAD), sulfur-based HTLs have some advantages, such as simple processing technology, high glass transition temperature, adjustable energy band, high intrinsic hole mobility, and outstanding hydrophobic properties. Sulfur-based compounds HTLs included organic small molecule sulfur-based compounds (thiophene [26], pyrrole [27], porphyrin [28], carbazole [29], etc.), organic polymer sulfur-based compounds (polythiophene [30] and poly(3-hexylthiophen-2,5-diyl [31], etc.), and inorganic sulfur-based compounds (MnS [32], NiS [33], and CuSCN [34], etc.).

In our preliminary work, we found that the S in   $  SnS_{2}/SnS  $   could interact with the Pb in the PVK, anchoring part of the Pb to improve the stability of the devices [35]. And we found that the previous researches were limited to the introduction of S in a certain functional layer to improve the performance of devices [36]. The introduction of sulfur-based compounds into each functional layer to stabilize PSCs has not been reported yet. In this work, we simultaneously introduced sulfur-based compounds into each layer of carbon-based PSCs (C-PSCs). Results showed that the simultaneous introduction of sulfur-based compounds could greatly improve both performance and stability of C-PSCs. All sulfide C-PSCs, introduced sulfur-based compounds into each layer of C-PSCs, obtained a PCE of 15.38%, while the device without sulfide was only 10.77%. The stability test showed much better resistance to humidity and thermal stress for all sulfide C-PSCs. They could retain 80% of initial PCE after aging about 700 h at relative humidity (RH) 45% ± 10% and 80 °C.

O, 98.0%), Chlorobenzene (99.8%), N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.9%, Superdry), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, ≥99.7%), CuSCN, and ethyl sulfide were purchased from Aladdin biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. The carbon paste (carbon ink, CH-8(MOD2)) was purchased from Jujo Printing Supplies & Technology Co., Ltd. The dithioxamide (DTA) was purchased from Acros.

# 2.2. Device fabrication

The   $  SnS_{2}/SnS  $   were synthesized by a solvothermal process. CsI (0.07 M), MABr (0.21 M),   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (0.21 M), FAI (1.19 M), and   $  PbI_{2}  $   (1.31 M) were dissolved in anhydrous dimethylformamide (DMF): dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) = 4:1 (v:v). The different concentrations of DTA dissolved in DMF were added in the PVK precursor. Then, the precursor was spin-coated on   $  SnS_{2}/SnS  $   substrates in a two-step procedure (1000 rpm/10 s and 5000 rpm/30 s). During the second step, 150   $  \mu  $  L chlorobenzene was poured at 15 s as an anti-solvent. The substrates were annealed at 100 °C for 75 min. For CuSCN, 40 mg was dissolved in ethyl sulfide. 40   $  \mu  $  L CuSCN precursor was spin-coated in a procedure at 5000 rpm/30 s. After spin-coating, the substrates were annealed at 50 °C for 10 min. Then, the carbon paste was directly doctor-bladed on the substrates and dried at 85 °C for 2 h.

# 2.3. Characterization

The scanning electron microscope (SEM), transmission electron microscope (TEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), UV–vis absorption spectra, incident photon to current conversion efficiency (IPCE), photoluminescence (PL) spectra, time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectra and the Nyquist plot were implemented as our previous report [35]. The current-voltage (J-V) characteristics, current-voltage (I-V) curves, short-current current densities (  $  J_{sc}  $  ), open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ) versus light intensity (IMPS and IMVS), transient photocurrent measurements (TPC), transient photovoltage measurements (TPV) and space charge limited current (SCLC) were measured by the electrochemical workstation (Zen-nium Zahner, Germany).

# 3. Results and discussions

To explore the reason of the improved performance introduced sulfur to HTLs, the charge transfer dynamics were carried out, as shown in Fig. S3 and Table S1. After the introduction of CuSCN, faster carriers transport and extraction resulted in an enhanced PCE. After changing  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  ETLs by  $ \mathrm{SnS}_2 / \mathrm{SnS} $ , the  $ I - V $  curves demonstrated that the conductivity of  $ \mathrm{SnS}_2 / \mathrm{SnS} $  was higher than that of  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $ , which resulted in an enhanced  $ J_{sc} $ , as shown in Fig. S4. The TPC was employed to investigate the carrier recombination time ( $ \tau_{\mathrm{r}} $ ). And the results showed the device based on  $ \mathrm{SnS}_2 / \mathrm{SnS} $  had a much smaller  $ \tau_{\mathrm{r}} $  (9.6 ns) than that of  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  (60.8 ns), indicating more efficient charge extraction and the suppressed charge recombination [37].

We used DTA as an additive in the PVK precursor and optimized its condition, as shown in Fig. S5. Comparing with pristine PVK film, the grain size of the PVK film with DTA additive grew much bigger, as SEM shown in Fig. 2 a and b. This was because   $  PbI_{2}  $   could interact with DTA during the growth of the PVK. The Lewis alkali of DTA could interact with   $  PbI_{2}  $ , which required a longer process to release   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and formed the final perovskite structure, leading to a slower crystallization rate and bigger crystal size [25]. The larger crystal size resulted in slightly enhanced absorption and   $  J_{sc}  $   than that of additive-free PVK, as UV-visible absorption spectrum in Fig. S6. XRD in Fig. 2c suggested that DTA additives did not fill into the PVK lattice.

SCLC measurements were carried out to evaluate the trap state density of PVK films, as shown in Fig. 2d. There were three different regions identified by the values of the exponent  $  n  $  ( $  J \propto V^n  $ ). The curves located in ohmic region for  $  n = 1  $  under a low bias applied; With the bias raised, the trap-filled limited (TFL) region for  $  n > 3  $  was occurred; Finally, the curves reached the child region for  $  n = 2  $  [38]. The voltage of the crossover point between the ohmic and TFL regions was referred as trap-filled limit voltage ( $  V_{\mathrm{TFL}}  $ ), which was used to relate the trap state density ( $  N_{\mathrm{defects}}  $ ) by the following equation:  $  N_{\mathrm{defects}} = 2\varepsilon \varepsilon_0 V_{\mathrm{TFL}} / qL^2  $ , where  $  \varepsilon  $  is the relative

dielectric constant,   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   is the vacuum permittivity,   $  V_{TFL}  $   is trap-filled limit voltage, L is the thickness of the PVK films, and q is the elementary charge [39]. The   $  V_{TFL}  $   of the PVK with and without DTA additives were 0.13 V and 0.19 V, respectively. Therefore,   $  N_{defects}  $   of the PVK with and without DTA were calculated at   $  8.6 \times 10^{14} \, cm^{-3}  $   and   $  1.25 \times 10^{15} \, cm^{-3}  $  , respectively. The introduction of DTA significantly decreased 31%   $  N_{defects}  $   of PVK film. PL spectra of PVK with and without DTA deposited on glass were displayed in Fig. S7. PVK with DTA additive performed approximately three times PL intensity than its pristine PVK film, which further proved that the trap state density was reduced [40].

The photophysical process was employed to investigate the carrier dynamic of C-PSCs based on PVK with and without DTA additive, as shown in Fig. 3. The shorter carrier lifetime in TRPL spectra, as shown in Fig. 3a and Table S2, indicated that C-PSCs based on PVK with DTA additive showed suppressed recombination and accelerated carrier extraction, which were related to the improved quality of PVK film [41]. IMVS of C-PSCs based on PVK with and without DTA additive were depicted in Fig. 3b. The slope  $ \alpha (kT / q) $  reflected the trap-assistant recombination in IMVS spectrum, when fitted with the equation  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} \propto \alpha (kT / q)\ln (I) $  [42]. The recombination was severer when the bigger value of  $ \alpha $  was. The devices based on PVK without DTA additive held a slope of 1.42 kT/q, while devices based on PVK with DTA additive were 1.28 kT/ q. It indicated DTA additive could contribute to weaker trap-assistant recombination [43]. TPV measurements in Fig. 3c provided insight into carrier recombination rates in device [44]. It suggested the suppressed charge recombination in C-PSCs based on PVK with DTA additive. Finally, the Nyquist plot in Fig. 3d and Table S2, revealed the same results with TRPL, IMVS, and TPV. All of the carrier dynamic investigations have proven that the sulfur-based additive DTA played a significant role to accelerate charge extraction and transfer in C-PSCs.

# 4. Conclusion

We have introduced sulfur-based compounds in each functional layer of C-PSCs. Results showed that the sulfide ETLs had excellent conductivity, efficient charge extraction and the suppressed charge recombination. The introduction of sulfur-based compounds into PVK could enlarge the grain size of PVK, decrease the trap state density, and enhance light absorption of PVK. After introduction of sulfur-based compounds in each functional layer of C-PSCs, a PCE of 15.38% has been achieved. Stability tests demonstrated that the interaction of S, from SnS₂/SnS ETLs, DTA additive and CuSCN, with Pb could stabilize the PVK film. All sulfide C-PSCs showed much better resistance to humidity and thermal stress than that of devices without sulfide. They could retain 80% of initial PCE after aging about 700 h at relative humidity (RH) 45% ± 10% and 80 °C.
