# Highlights

- Perovskite solar cells with CuI/PEDOT:PSS double HTLs achieved a PCE of 14.3%.

- The inorganic CuI buffer layer significantly reduced the average exciton lifetime and enhanced the hole extraction.

- The device stability greatly enhanced by CuI buffer layer.

# Abstract

The hole transport layer (HTL) plays an important role in perovskite solar cells (PSCs). We demonstrate the copper iodide (CuI)/poly (3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) poly (styrenesulphonate) (PEDOT:PSS) double layers as the anode buffer layer for efficient hole transport in PSCs. An obvious enhancement of open-circuit voltage and power conversion efficiency (PCE) is observed, and a final PCE of  $ 14.3\% $  is achieved. While the single PEDOT:PSS based device's PCE is  $ 12.9\% $ . The inorganic CuI buffer layer significantly enhanced the hole extraction from the perovskite film, as revealed from the photoluminescence spectra. The average exciton lifetime is reduced to 2.7 ns. Moreover, the devices with double HTLs structure exhibit better long time stability. The PCE remains  $ 88\% $  of the initial value after 720 hours' storage.

Keywords: Perovskite solar cell; Hole transport layer; Cupper iodide; Hole extraction; Power conversion efficiency; Stability

# 1. Introduction

2

ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

Organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cell (PSCs) is originally invented by Miyasaka and Kojima in 2009 as an alternative to traditional dye-sensitized solar cells.[1] The highest power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs is vigorously increased from  $ 3.8\% $  to  $ 22.1\% $  [2] within a few years. This remarkable progress is basically due to the excellent opto-electrical properties of perovskite materials, such as high absorption coefficient, long carrier diffusion length, and bipolar carrier transport characteristic with decent mobility.[3-6] Usually, PSCs are structured with either formal n-i-p or inverted p-i-n configurations.[6-8] In the former case, inorganic semiconductors, such as  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ ,  $ \mathrm{ZnO} $ ,  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ , are employed as n-type layer and  $ 2,2',7,7' $ -tetrakis-(N,N-di- $ p $ -methoxyphenamine)- $ 9,9' $ -spiro-bifluorene (spiro-MeOTAD) is employed as the p-type layer. PSCs based on this structure always suffer from large hysteresis that may originate from insufficient interfacial interaction between these inorganic semiconductors and perovskite materials.[9] In contrast, PSCs with inverted structure usually employ water soluble organic polymer poly (3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) poly (styrenesulphonate) (PEDOT:PSS) as hole transport layer (HTL) and fullerene derivative [6,6]-phenyl- $ \mathrm{C_{61}} $ -butyric acid methyl ester ( $ \mathrm{PC}_{61}\mathrm{BM} $ ) as electron transport layer (ETL).[8, 10] Although PEDOT:PSS is most commonly used as HTL in inverted PSCs, it has shown drawback towards long-term stability resulting from its hygroscopicity and acidity, based on the organic photovoltaic research.[11, 12] Therefore, some p-type inorganic semiconductors such as CuI, CuO, and NiO were studied as alternatives to PEDOT:PSS owing to their high conductivity and good chemical stability.[13]-19 However, the PCEs of reported PSCs with these inorganic semiconductors were insufficient, owing to the poor interface between these inorganic semiconductors and perovskite absorbers.[13-16]

3

Among these inorganic semiconductors, CuI is a very promising HTL in PSCs, because it has many advantages such as broad band gap (3.1 eV), high hole mobility, hydrophobicity, low cost, environmental stability, and solution processibility.[13] Devices employ CuI as the HTL, resulting in higher stability and less hysteresis but lower PCE compared to spiro-OMeTAD based control devices.[16, 17] Recently, CuI was employed as HTL in inverted PSCs with an excellent PCE of  $ 16.8\% $ , and the device exhibited better stability compared to PEDOT:PSS based devices.[18]

However, we have found that perovskite solar cells with a CuI HTL exhibit a low PCE (that may due to the interface trap state between CuI and perovskite layer[16]). Hou et al. and lee et al. found similar phenomenon and they reported  $ \mathrm{MoO_3} $ /PEDOT:PSS and Graphene Oxide/PEDOT:PSS bilayer HTLs for perovskite solar cell with better PCE and stability.[19, 20]

In this work, we employ CuI/PEDOT:PSS double HTLs in PSCs and record the device current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics and external quantum efficiency (EQE). The insertion of an CuI layer between ITO and PEDOT:PSS not only increased the hole extraction from perovskite to the ITO electrode but also completely eliminates direct contact between ITO and PEDOT:PSS. These two effects result in an increase of PCE and stability, respectively. Along the aim of studying how the CuI buffer layer affect the photovoltaic performance, EQE spectra, transmission spectra, scanning electron microscope (SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns, steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence spectra (TRPL) of the corresponding HTL/perovskite layers are carried out.

# 2. Experimental methods

# 2.1 Materials

The lead iodide, PEDOT:PSS,  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{Br} $  (MABr,  $ >99.5\% $  ) and  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{I} $  (MAI,  $ >99.5\% $  ) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. The CuI ( $ >99.998\% $  metal basis) was purchased from Alfa. The Lead acetate trihydrate  $ (\mathrm{PbAc}_2\cdot 3\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}, $  CAS no. 6080-56-4) was purchased from Aldrich and dehydrated before use. For the ultradry solvents,  $ N,N $ -dimethylformamide (DMF,  $ >99.9\% $  ) was obtained from J&K, chlorobenzene and isopropanol ( $ >99.9\% $  ) were obtained from Acros. ZnO nanoparticles were synthesized as reported[21],  $ \mathrm{PC}_{61}\mathrm{BM} $  was purchased from Hangzhou Perovs Optoelectronic Technology Co., Ltd. All the chemicals and solvents were stored in a  $ \mathbf{N}_2 $  filled glovebox and used as received.

# 2.2 Solar cell fabrication

The pre-patterned ITO glass substrates were cleaned by ultrasound in detergent, deionized water, chloroform, acetone and 2-propanol sequentially. Before spin-coating, the substrates were treated by plasma cleaner for 5 min. CuI was dissolved in acetonitrile and the concentration was 20 mg/ml. Then, the CuI solution was spin-coated on the ITO substrate at 4000 rpm for 30 s. After drying for few minutes, the PEDOT:PSS solution was filtered with a PES 0.22 μm filter and spin-coated on ITO/CuI substrates at 3000 rpm for 30 s and followed by annealing at 120 °C for 20 min. After that, the perovskite precursor (PbAc₂:MAI 1:3 mol ratio, 1 M in DMF, with 1.5% M MABr) was spin coated on the substrate at 3500 rpm for 30 s followed by annealing at 100 °C for 10 min. Then, the PC₆₁BM chlorobenzene solution (20 mg/ml) was spin-coated at 1000 rpm for 30 s. After dried for few minutes, the ZnO nanoparticles dispersed in isobutanol were then spin-coated at 1000 rpm for 30 s and followed by annealing at 60 °C for 1 h. Finally, a 80 nm thick Al electrode was thermal evaporated on top of ZnO to produce the complete device. The device active area was 0.1 cm².

# 2.3 Characterization

The  $ J-V $  characteristics were test by a source meter (Keithley 2400) measurement system with an AM1.5G filter at a calibrated illumination intensity of  $ 100\mathrm{mW}\cdot \mathrm{cm}^{-2} $ , as calibrated by a standard silicon reference cell (91150 V Oriel Instruments). Film thicknesses were measured by a Veeco Dektak 150 surface profilometer. EQE spectra were measured in air by a commercial setup (7-SCSpec IV) from Sofn instrument co., LTD, which was equipped with a  $ 100\mathrm{W} $  Xe arc lamp, filter wheel, and monochromator. The transmission spectra were measured by a Shimadzu UV-2550 spectrophotometer. A field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi SU8020) was used to acquire SEM images. The XRD patterns were recorded on an X-ray diffractometer (Hao Yuan Instrument, DX-2700B) with CuKα radiation ( $ \lambda = 1.5418\AA $ ) at room temperature. The data were collected with a  $ 0.02^{\circ} $  step size ( $ 2\theta $ ). PL and TRPL spectra were obtained from samples excited with a picosecond pulsed diode laser (EPL-375) at  $ 400\mathrm{nm} $  using a PL spectrometer (Edinburgh Instruments, FLS 920). A Hamamatsu C5680-04 streak camera was used for TRPL.

# 3. Results and discussion

Figure 1 shows the device structure and the energy alignment of the CuI modified inverted PSCs. The CuI has a similar valence-band maximum with PEDOT:PSS, indicating its good hole transport capability.[22] It also has a higher bottom of conduction band than PEDOT:PSS, indicating CuI has a better electron blocking capability from the perovskite layer. Here, we investigated CuI/PEDOT:PSS double-layer thin film as HTL in comparison with either CuI or PEDOT:PSS single-layer based HTL. We employed a previously reported “acetate route” process to fabricate the perovskite film, i.e., replacing the lead source by  $ \mathrm{PbAc_2} $ , thus preventing  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  fast

precipitate crystal during spinning process to get a smooth film.[23] The optimized thickness of each film was  $ 20\mathrm{nm} $  for CuI,  $ 40\mathrm{nm} $  for PEDOT,  $ 300\mathrm{nm} $  for perovskite,  $ 50\mathrm{nm} $  for  $ \mathrm{PC}_{61}\mathrm{BM} $  and  $ 40\mathrm{nm} $  for  $ \mathrm{ZnO} $ .

J-V curves of the PSCs with different HTLs are shown in Figure 2. The corresponding photovoltaic parameters are summarized in Table 1. The CuI/PEDOT:PSS based devices achieved the highest PCE of 14.3%, with an open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) of 0.92 V, a short-circuit current density (  $  J_{SC}  $  ) of 20.5 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $   and a fill factor (FF) of 76% while the single PEDOT:PSS based devices got a PCE of 12.9%, a   $  V_{OC}  $   of 0.86 V, a FF of 73% and a   $  J_{SC}  $   of 20.5 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  . These results showed that the CuI buffer film significantly affected the photovoltaic performance. For comparison, the PEDOT:PSS/CuI structure based devices only reached a PCE of 10.8%, a   $  V_{OC}  $   of 0.78 V, a FF of 68% and a   $  J_{SC}  $   of 19.4 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  . In addition, the single CuI based devices showed a poor photovoltaic performance with a PCE of only 1.7%, a   $  V_{OC}  $   of 0.78 V, a FF of 30% and a   $  J_{SC}  $   of 7.4 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  .

Figure 3 gives the quantum efficiency spectra of the devices based on PEDOT and CuI-modified HTL. From about  $ 360~\mathrm{nm} $  to  $ 750~\mathrm{nm} $ , they showed good photovoltaic response of above  $ 60\% $  with the maximum EQE of about  $ 80\% $ . The EQE of CuI-modified one is a bit lower at the wave band near  $ 405~\mathrm{nm} $  but higher at long-wave band. Meanwhile, Figure 4 shows the optical transmission spectra of different HTLs on ITO substrate. An absorption peak of CuI at  $ 405~\mathrm{nm} $  is also observed, corresponding to the band gap of CuI (3.1eV). This is well matched to the EQE spectrum. From the transmission spectra, the inserted CuI layer reduced the transmittance, especially in the region near  $ 405~\mathrm{nm} $ , then less light went into

perovskite film, indicating that the devices based on CuI-modified HTL must have better electrical properties to offset optical loss.

In Figure 5, top-view SEM images of perovskite on PEDOT and on CuI-PEDOT HTL showed the similar morphology and coverage, the average grain size is about  $ 100\mathrm{nm} $  from the SEM image. The CuI-PEDOT HTL based perovskite film seems to be more compact and more uniform, which may give rise to a higher Voc due to less leakage current. The XRD patterns of different HTLs and perovskite deposited on different HTLs are shown in Figure 6. The ITO/PEDOT:PSS film showed the same spectra as ITO substrate, owing to the amorphous nature of the PEDOT:PSS film. CuI film showed a diffraction peak at  $ 25.5^{\circ} $ , attributed to the (111) reflection of  $ \gamma $ -CuI with a zincblende face centered cubic structure (Figure 6a). After perovskite was deposited, the diffraction peaks of the CuI were covered up (Figure 6b). The perovskite deposited on different HTLs showed the same crystal configuration. The XRD peaks of the tetragonal phase  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  at  $ 14.0^{\circ} $ ,  $ 24.4^{\circ} $ ,  $ 28.4^{\circ} $ ,  $ 31.8^{\circ} $  and  $ 50.3^{\circ} $  were indexed to (110), (202), (220), (310) and (404) reflections, respectively.

To further explore the effect of CuI inserted layer, steady-state PL and TRPL spectra of perovskite coated on various HTLs (CuI, PEDOT, PEDOT-CuI, CuI-PEDOT) and ITO substrate were measured, as displayed in Figure 7. CuI-introduced HTL showed more efficient PL quenching than the PEDOT:PSS film from the steady-state PL, especially in the case of CuI-PEDOT HTL. The smaller PL intensity means less radiative recombination. We fitted the PL lifetime  $ (\tau) $  of the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3/\mathrm{HTL}/\mathrm{ITO} $  films with a bi-exponential decay model. As a result, the larger time constant  $ \tau_{2} $  fitted from the curve was used to plot the life time of the photo carriers, showing the values of 23, 17, 13 and 2.7 ns for CuI, PEDOT, PEDOT-CuI and CuI-PEDOT HTL, respectively. The longer life time of CuI based perovskite

indicates the interface between CuI and perovskite is not very good. The life time is just 2.7 ns for CuI-PEDOT double-layer based perovskite, which is much shorter than the one just based on PEDOT. This indicates the CuI inserted buffer layer much enhanced the hole extraction ability and reduced radiative recombination between perovskite and HTLs, which lead to a higher  $  V_{OC}  $  and FF.

The long term stability of solar cells is a very important parameter for the practical applications. The widely used HTL PEDOT:PSS shows the nature of acidity and hydrophily, which is not good for the device stability. While the CuI is ambient stable and hydrophobic, which would be beneficial to the device stability. Figure 8 gives the devices' long term stability without encapsulation under glovebox. The CuI-PEDOT HTL based devices showed better stability than PEDOT:PSS based devices. After 720 hours' storage, the PCE of PEDOT:PSS-based devices decreased by  $ 28\% $  of the initial efficiency, while the PCE of CuI-PEDOT HTL based devices only decreased by  $ 12\% $ . The efficiency of PEDOT:PSS based devices was decreased due to the decreasing of fill factor and current density. This may be owing to the corrosive effect of PEDOT:PSS to the ITO substrate. While the enhanced stability of CuI-PEDOT based device can be interpreted by that the inserted CuI buffer layer defenses ITO substrate from the sour corrosion of PEDOT:PSS.[24]

# 4. Conclusions

As a conclusion, perovskite solar cells with CuI-PEDOT:PSS double HTLs structure were fabricated. The CuI buffer layer enhanced the hole extraction ability of the hole transport material thus reduced the interface hole recombination, lead to higher  $  V_{oc}  $  and FF. Moreover, the CuI layer prevented the corrosion of acid PEDOT:PSS on the ITO electrode and thus improved the long time stability of

CuI/PEDOT:PSS double HTLs PSCs. Compared with traditional single HTL (PEDOT:PSS) devices showing a PCE of 12.9%, these double HTLs devices showed a higher efficiency of 14.3%. The PCE stability was improved from 72% to 88% of its initial value after storage of 720 hours. This work demonstrates a double HTLs architecture for high performance and stability inverted perovskite solar cells.
