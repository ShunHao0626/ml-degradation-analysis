# A universal tactic of using Lewis-base polymer-CNTs composites as additives for high performance cm  $  ^{2}  $  -sized and flexible perovskite solar cells

Chang Liu  $  ^{1}  $  , Jingsong Sun  $  ^{2}  $  , Xiao-Fang Jiang  $  ^{3}  $  , Like Huang  $  ^{1}  $  , Qiang Lou  $  ^{1}  $  , Yi-Bing Cheng  $  ^{2,4}  $  , Shulin Song  $  ^{1*}  $   & Ziyi Ge  $  ^{1*}  $

$  ^{3}  $  School of Physics and Telecommunication Engineering, South China Normal University, Guangzhou 510006, China

Lewis-base polymers have been widely utilized as additives to act as a template for the perovskite nucleation/crystal growth and passivate the under-coordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  sites. However, it is uncovered in this work that the polymer on the perovskite grain boundaries would significantly hinder the charge transport due to its low conductivity, which brings about free carrier recombination and photocurrent losses. To circumvent this issue while fully exploiting the benefits of polymers in passivating the trap states in perovskite, we incorporate highly conductive multiwall carbon nanotubes (CNTs) with Lewis-base polymers as coadditives in the perovskite film. Functionalizing the CNTs with -COOH group enables a selective hole-extraction and charge transport from perovskite to the hole transporting materials (HTM). By studying the charge transporting and recombination dynamics, we revealed the individual role of the polymer and CNTs in passivating the trap states and facilitating the charge transport, respectively. As a result, the perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with polymer-CNTs composites exhibit an impressive PCE of  $ 21.7\% $  for a small-area device  $ (0.16~\mathrm{cm}^2) $  and  $ 20.7\% $  for a large-area device  $ (1.0~\mathrm{cm}^2) $ . Moreover, due to the superior mechanical flexibility of both polymer and CNTs, the polymer-CNTs composites incorporation in the perovskite film encourages the fabrication of flexible PSCs (f-PSCs) with an impressive PCE of  $ 18.3\% $ , and a strong mechanical durability by retaining  $ 80\% $  of the initial PCE after 1,000 times bending. In addition, we proved that the selection criteria of the polymers can be extended to other long-chain Lewis-base polymers, which opens new possibilities in design and synthesis of inexpensive material for this tactic towards the fabrication of high performance large-area PSCs and f-PSCs.

# 1 Introduction

Organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite materials have revolutionized the next-generation photovoltaics due to their

exceptional optical and electronic characteristics [1-4]. Thanks to the tremendous efforts devoted to the research area of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) [5-12], power conversion efficiency (PCE) of the state-of-the-art device has surged from  $ 3.8\% $  to  $ 25.2\% $  within just a decade [13]. However, this value still far lags the theoretical (Shockely-Queisser) PCE

limit for the perovskite materials with a bandgap   $  (E_{\mathrm{g}})  $   of   $  \sim1.6~eV  $   [14]. Non-radiative recombination through the under-coordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $  , which acts as trap states at the grain boundaries, has been identified as the primary origin for the efficiency losses [15–17]. Therefore, to bridge the efficiency gap between the experimental record and theoretical limit, suppressing the trap-assisted recombination at the perovskite grain boundaries is of critical importance.

Lewis-base polymers containing S, N and O atoms with lone pair electrons, such as poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA), polyacrylic acid (PAA), poly(4-vinylpyridine) (PVP), benzodithiophene-based polymer (PBDB-T) have recently been demonstrated as effective passivating agents   $  [18–21]  $  . Through delocalizing the lone pair electrons from S, N or O atoms to the 6p empty orbitals of   $  Pb^{2+}  $  , nonradiative recombination losses in PSCs can be successfully reduced with correspondingly improved open circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ). Moreover, the hydrophobic polymers at grain boundaries could protect the perovskite from moisture ingression, which improves the moisture stability of PSCs. However, it is argued that polymers, possessing much lower conductivity than that of perovskite, would inevitably block charge transport across the perovskite grains, which induce photocurrent losses and compromise the PCE of PSCs. Worse still, charge accumulations, arisen from the insufficient charge extractions, would generate J-V hysteresis as well as instability of PSCs under working conditions   $  [22,23]  $  . Therefore, on top of passivating the non-radiative recombination traps, it is paramount to increase the charge extraction/transport rate across the perovskite grains, which could compensate the poor conductivity and photo-in-stability of the device caused by the polymers incorporation.

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) with excellent charge mobility, chemical stability, mechanical flexibility and solution processability, are generally regarded as an ideal carrier conductor in solar cell devices   $  [24–31]  $  . Whereas, the incorporation of CNTs in the perovskite active layer is rarely reported, possibly due to the great challenges of dispersing the CNTs into perovskite matrixes, the aggregates of CNTs would induce fatal short circuit of PSCs. Furthermore, without charge selectivity, small amount of metallic CNTs in perovskite film could act as undesirable recombination pathways, thus generating charge recombination losses.

Herein, to circumvent the above issues while taking full advantage of the high conductivity of CNTs, we mix the multi-wall CNTs into a series of Lewis-base polymer/chlorobenzene (CB) solution, forming polymer-CNT composites. The long-chain polymer wrapping the CNTs could assist the dispersion of CNTs in CB solvent [32]. Through applying the composites into the perovskite film during “antisolvent” step, we obtain well-dispersed CNTs in the perovskite film without aggregation. Additionally, we functionalize the CNTs by carboxylic acid group (-COOH),

tuning the work function (WF) of CNTs from -4.5 to -5.3 eV [33]. The down-shifted WF lies closer to the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of perovskite (-5.4 eV), selectively promoting the hole extraction and transport. In the meantime, the Lewis-base material could act as passivating agents as aforementioned [19,34,35]. The combined functions of Lewis-base polymer and CNTs simultaneously decrease the trap-assisted recombination losses and enhance the charge extraction rate in PSCs, which lead to an outstanding PCE of 21.7% for small-area devices (0.16 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ) and 20.7% for large-area devices (1.0 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ). The PSCs also show an excellent long-term moisture stability by retaining 93% of the initial PCE after 1,200 h aging under 40%–60% relative humidity (RH), and good light stability by preserving 90% of the original PCE after continuous 1 sun illumination for 700 h. More importantly, due to the superior mechanical flexibility of both polymer and CNTs, the polymer-CNTs composites incorporation in the perovskite film encourages the fabrication of flexible PSCs (f-PSCs) with an impressive PCE of 18.3%, and a strong mechanical durability by retaining 80% of the initial PCE after 1,000 times bending. These are among the highest reported performances from the f-PSCs to date.

# 2 Experimental

# 2.1 Materials

Formamidinium iodide (FAI), methylammonium bromide (MABr) and butylammonium iodide (BAI) were purchased from Greatcell;   $  PbI_{2}  $   and   $  PbBr_{2}  $   were purchased from Alfa Aesar; PMMA (molecular weight of   $  \sim120,000  $  ), CsI, RbI, KI, dimethyl sulphoxide (DMSO), isopropanol (IPA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All of chemicals were used as received without any further purification. The pristine multi-wall CNTs (diameter 7–15 nm, length 0.5–10   $  \mu  $  m) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. We functionalized it with –COOH through the following procedures: the pristine CNTs were suspended in a mixture of concentrated   $  H_{2}SO_{4}  $   and   $  HNO_{3}  $   (3:1, v/v), followed by sonicating for 3 h. The suspension was then refluxed with vigorous stirring at 90 °C for 12 h. The mixture was then filtered with 0.2   $  \mu  $  m porous Teflon filter after cooling to room temperature. The powder was then washed with DI water until reaching neutral pH, then drying the powder in vacuum oven at 70 °C overnight.

# 2.2 Device fabrication

PMMA-CNTs/CB solution preparation: weighing CNTs and PMMA with different weight ratios, dissolving the PMMA with CB solvent, followed by sonicating the solution to fully disperse CNTs. Then centrifuge the solution at 10,000 r/min for 10 min, and use the supernatant for device fabrication.

FTO-coated glasses or ITO-coated PET substrates are cleaned by ultra-sonication in 1% Helmanex surfactant aqueous solution, water and ethanol in sequence (15 min for each). For rigid PSCs fabrication, a compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   (c-  $  TiO_{2}  $  , thickness ca. 10–20 nm) blocking layer was deposited onto FTO by spray pyrolysis of 1 mL bis(isopropoxide)-bis(acetylacetonate)titanium (IV) dissolved in 19 mL anhydrous isopropanol (IPA) at 500 °C. After cooling down to room temperature, a mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer (m-  $  TiO_{2}  $  , thickness ca. 100–150 nm) was deposited by spin-coating at 4,000 r/min for 20 s from 30 nm particle paste (Dyesol 30 NR-D) in ethanol (weight ratio 1:6). The film was drying at 100 °C for 10 min followed by sintering at 500 °C for 30 min. For f-PSCs fabrication, the diluted   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloidal dispersion by de-ionized water at 1:4 volume ratio was spin-coated onto the PET/ITO substrate, followed by thermal annealing at 140 °C for 30 min.

The perovskite thin film was formed by spin-coating the perovskite precursor solution onto the FTO/c-TiO $ _2 $ /m-TiO $ _2 $  or PET/ITO/SnO $ _2 $  substrate using a two-step program: 1,000 r/min (ramp 1,000 r min $ ^{-1} $  s $ ^{-1} $ ) for 10 s and then 6,000 r/min (ramp 6,000 r min $ ^{-1} $  s $ ^{-1} $ ) for 20 s. 200  $ \mu $ M chlorobenzene (CB), PMMA/CB solution or PMMA-CNTs/CB solution was poured onto the substrate for 5 s prior to the end of the second step. The substrate was then annealing at 100  $ ^{\circ} $ C for 1 h. For the hole-transporting layer deposition, 60 mM spiro-OMeTAD with 198 mM 4- $ tert $ -butylpyridine (tBP), 30 mM bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) and 1.8 mM tris(2-(1H-pyrazol-1-yl)-4- $ tert $ -butylpyridine) cobalt (III) tris(bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide) (FK209) in CB was spin-casting onto the perovskite film at 3,000 r/min for 30 s. Finally, a gold electrode with a thickness of 80 nm was thermally deposited onto the substrate. The active area of the device was 0.16 cm $ ^{2} $ .

# 2.3 Characterizations

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of perovskite films on  $ \mathrm{FTO / c - TiO_2 / m - TiO_2} $  were measured by Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer (Bragg-Brentano geometry, Germany) equipped with a Cu Kα X-ray tune operated at  $ 40\mathrm{kV} $  and  $ 40\mathrm{mA} $ .

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging for both top view and cross-sectional modes were recorded on a FEI Nova NanoSEM 450 FEG microscope using a 5 kV acceleration voltage (USA).

Conductive atomic force microscopy (c-AFM) measurements were performed on a Dimension Icon (Veeco) in air.

The measurements were performed under constant external bias of  $ -1.0\mathrm{V} $ , and platinum-coated conductive probes were used for the measurements.

X-ray/Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS/UPS) analysis was performed using an AXIS Ultra DLD spectrometer (Kratos Analytical Inc., UK) with a monochromated Al Kα X-ray source (1,486.6 eV) and a He discharge lamp (He I radiation at 21.22 eV), respectively. The XPS survey spectra were acquired with the hemispherical analyzer set to a pass energy of 160 eV, while the high resolution narrow scans were made using a 40 eV pass energy.

Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) measurements were performed using a Fluoromax4 Spectro fluorometer. Samples were illuminated using a 465.8 nm excitation source. Slit widths were set to 1.2 nm.

Time-resolved PL measurements were performed using an FLSP920 time correlated single photon counting spectrometer equipped with a 465.8 nm pulsed diode laser excitation source (EPL-475, Edinburgh Instruments Ltd., UK) with ca. 100 ps pulse width and a peak laser excitation density of ca. 0.2 nJ/cm  $  ^{2}  $  . Luminescence was collected using a grating monochromator.

Photoelectron spectroscopy in air (PESA) measurements were performed using an AC-2 Riken Keiki spectrometer model, which allows measurements in the 4.5–6.2 eV range. To avoid contamination from the surface after deposition, the films were kept under inert atmosphere. The equipment allows for the measuring of the density of states (DOS) around the HOMO level (semiconductor) and work function (metallic materials). When a surface is bombarded with a slowly increasing amount of ultraviolet energy, photoelectrons start to emit at a certain energy level. When the photoelectron output is plotted on an X/Y axis, with horizontal axis as the UV energy applied, the vertical axis as the standardized photoelectron yield ratio (Y), the result is a line with a specific slope of degree (Y/eV). The intersect of the line with X axis thus defines the WF of the CNTs and HOMO of the perovskite.

$ J-V $  curve characteristics of completed solar cell devices were recorded using a Keithley 2400 Source Meter. The devices were illuminated using a Xenon lamp source  $ (100\mathrm{mW/cm}^2) $  provided by an Oriel solar simulator with an AM1.5 G filter. A non-reflective mask with an aperture of  $ 0.16\mathrm{cm}^2 $  was used to keep the irradiation area constant.

Incident photon to current conversion efficiency (IPCE) spectra were recorded using a Keithley 2400 Source Meter under 300 W Xenon lamp irradiation with an Oriel Cornerstone 260  $  ^{1/4}  $  m monochromator. The monochromatic photon flux was quantified through a calibrated silicon cell.

# 3 Results and discussion

We use mixed triple cation perovskite   $  Cs_{0.05}FA_{0.79}MA_{0.16}-PbI_{2.49}Br_{0.51}(FA^{+}=CH(NH_{2})_{2}^{+}, MA^{+}=CH_{3}NH_{3}^{+})  $   in this work. Firstly, PMMA is adopted individually and in conjunction with CNTs as an example to study the effects of the Lewis-base polymer and polymer-CNTs composites on the charge transfer/recombination kinetics in the perovskite films. Other than specified, all the CNTs applied in perovskite films are functionalized with -COOH. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of the CNTs and PMMA-CNTs composites (in CB solvent) are illustrated in Figure S1(a, b) (Supporting Information online), which proves that polymer wrapping CNTs could effectively assist the dispersion of CNTs. The selection of polymers will be then extended to other insulating or semiconducting Lewis-base polymers to attest the universality of this strategy, which will be detailly discussed in the later context. PMMA and PMMA-CNTs composites dissolved in CB solvent are applied onto the perovskite film during the “anti-solvent” step, and the experimental procedures are provided in the Supporting Information. Morphologies of the perovskite films are firstly inspected by top-view SEM images. Figure 1(a–c) show the

morphology of perovskite intermediates before annealing, which reveal a transition from randomly distributed to closely packed nanoparticles with the addition of PMMA. Upon thermal annealing, the intermediates transform to uniform perovskite films with the elimination of pinholes, as illustrated in Figure 1(d, e). Further incorporation of CNTs in the PMMA perovskite uniformly distributed isolated CNTs in the perovskite film without aggregation, as shown in Figure 1(f). To have a clearer image of the CNTs distribution in the perovskite film, we measured more SEM images on different areas in the PMMA-CNTs film, as shown in Figure S2(a, b), which provided a solid evidence that the CNTs are distributed in the perovskite film and connecting the perovskite grains. The CNTs could offer carrier transporting pathways across the perovskite grains, which expects to improve the charge extraction efficiency and suppresses the free carrier recombination losses. In addition, comparing Figure 1(e, f), the incorporation of CNTs enlarges the perovskite grain sizes. It could be explained that the -COOH functional groups on CNTs form intermediates with  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $ , which may decrease the supersaturation degree of the perovskite precursor and retard the nucleation rate [36].

To characterize the electrical conductivity of CNTs in perovskite film, conductive atomic force microscope (c-AFM) measurements are performed on PMMA-CNTs perovskite film. Height AFM image in Figure 1(g) clearly shows isolated CNTs on the perovskite grains. Correspondingly, c-AFM image in Figure 1(h) illustrates significantly higher current across the CNTs, which indicates the CNTs provide more efficient charge transporting pathways in the perovskite film. As a comparison, the height and conductive AFM images of the pristine perovskite are also provided, as shown in Figure S3(a, b). The result thus predicts concurrently suppressed photocurrent losses and charge recombination losses with the incorporation of PMMA-CNTs composites. While, we need to note that, as the c-AFM measurements were conducted under both humidity and external biases, analysis of the current would be complicated as several factors need to be simultaneously taken into considerations other than the intrinsic conductivity of the perovskite film. Therefore, parallel comparison between different films would not be very accurate. So in this work, we mainly focus on the current contrast between the perovskite grains and CNTs on the same film, which could be used for a solid comparison.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements are also conducted to inspect the oxidation states of the elements on the film surface. Figure S4(b) illustrates the Pb 4f spectra. The charge extraction and recombination kinetics are subsequently scrutinized by steady-state and time-resolved PL (TRPL) measurements. Steady-state PL spectra of the films are shown in Figure 1(i). It is found that with the addition of PMMA, the PL intensity increases, indicating the reduced non-radiative recombination sites upon the passivation of PMMA. Further incorporation of CNTs induces PL quenching, which can be explained by the charge conducting properties of the CNTs acting as hole transporting material. TRPL spectra of the films illustrated in Figure 1(j) are fitted by the bi-exponential decay of  $  I(t) = I_0 + A_1\exp (-t / \tau_1) + A_2\exp (-t / \tau_2)  $ , ( $  \tau_{1}  $  and  $  \tau_{2}  $  give the fast and slow decay time constant, where  $  A_{1}  $  and  $  A_{2}  $  are fractions of the two decay processes, and the average lifetime  $  \langle \tau \rangle = \alpha_{1}\tau_{1} + \alpha_{2}\tau_{2}  $ ,  $  \alpha_{i} = A_{i}\tau_{i} / \sum A_{i}\tau_{i}  $ ) [38]. The fitting parameters and the corresponding PL lifetimes are summarized

in Table S1 (Supporting Information online). The pristine perovskite film shows an average lifetime  $ \tau $  of 43.4 ns, which elongated to 117.2 ns with the PMMA passivation, indicating reduced trap-assisted recombination sites. In PMMA-CNTs perovskite film,  $ \tau $  decreases to 30.8 ns, which attests the role of CNTs as hole conductor in the film.

The structural crystallinity of perovskite films are examined by XRD patterns. Figure 2(a) shows that the three perovskite films possess the same tetragonal structure with dominant (111) lattice reflection from  $ 2\theta = 14.1^{\circ} $ , suggesting the incorporation of PMMA and PMMA-CNTs composites impose negligible influence on the perovskite crystalline structure. Stronger reflections from the (111) plane are observed from the PMMA perovskite and PMMA-CNTs perovskite films, which is indicative of a higher ordering of perovskite crystals, possibly due to the formation of intermediate adduct between carbonyl group in PMMA and  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ . Another important merit of incorporating PMMA and PMMA-CNTs composites into the perovskite film is that the hydrophobic polymer and CNTs prevent the moisture invasion into the perovskite grains. The contact angle measurements were conducted to test the moisture resistance of the perovskite films. To illustrate the hole conducting role of CNTs in the perovskite film, Fermi level (EF) and HOMO of the perovskite are measured by the ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS), and WF of CNTs are measured by the photoelectron spectroscopy in air (PESA). From Figure S6(a, b), EF and HOMO of the pristine perovskite are determined to be -4.3 and -5.4 eV, respectively. From Figure S6(c, d), the CNTs without functional groups demonstrate a WF of -4.5 eV, which shifts downward to -5.3 eV upon -COOH functionalization. The energy level diagram has been shown in Figure S6(e). Upon the contact between the perovskite CNTs, an up-shifted band-bending would be induced along with the Fermi level pinning between perovskite and CNTs, and a Schottky barrier would thus be formed (Figure S6(f)). This energy barrier (highlighted by the red rectangle) would prevents the electron transfer from perovskite to CNTs while facilitate the hole migration, thus selectively conducting holes in the perovskite. Due to this effect, EF of the PMMA-CNTs perovskite is downshifted to -4.6 eV (as determined from Figure S6(b)), indicating the perovskite transiting from n-type to more “intrinsic” semiconductor. From the energy level diagram illustrated in Figure 3(a), the matched WF of CNTs and HOMO level of spiro-OMeTAD subsequently guarantees a rapid hole transferring to the HTM and Au electrode, which would considerably suppress the free carrier recombination and photocurrent losses in the device.

Efficiencies of the PSCs are tested under simulated 1 sun illumination (intensity  $ 100\mathrm{mW/cm^2} $ , AM 1.5 spectrum). The performance of PSCs has been optimized by tuning the concentration of PMMA in CB and the weight ratio of CNTs: PMMA. From the results shown in Figure S7, the optimal concentration of PMMA in CB is determined to be

0.6 mg/mL, and the optimal CNTs:PMMA weight ratio is 1:20. In the following context, the characterizations will be performed under these optimized conditions other than specified. Figure 3(b) illustrates the J-V characteristics of the PSCs based on pristine perovskite, PMMA perovskite and PMMA-CNTs perovskite films, respectively, under reverse scan from 1.2 to -0.5 V at a scan rate of 0.1 V/s. The forward scan J-V characteristics of the PSCs are shown in Figure S8. Table 1 summarizes the photovoltaic performance parameters of the PSCs. PSCs based on pristine perovskite film show a PCE of 18.4%, with an   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.10 V,   $  J_{SC}  $   of 22.3 mA/cm² and fill factor (FF) of 75.0%. Upon the addition of PMMA,   $  J_{SC}  $   of the PSCs slightly decreases to 22.1 mA/cm², which is ascribed to the fact that the insulating PMMA on the grain boundaries inhibits the charge transport. However, owing to the passivating effects of PMMA on the under-coordinated Pb²⁺, non-radiative recombination losses are significantly reduced, which contributes to the enhanced   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.13 V and FF of 77.7%, compensating the photocurrent losses. As a result, an overall increased PCE of 19.4% is obtained from PSCs based on PMMA perovskite. For PSCs based on PMMA-CNTs perovskite, CNTs connecting the perovskite grains efficiently facilitate charge transport across the grain boundaries and charge extraction to the HTM, which results in both reduced charge recombination and photocurrent losses. Consequently, a significantly increased   $  J_{SC}  $   of 23.3 mA/cm², FF of 79.8%,   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.16 V, and an outstanding PCE of 21.7% has been obtained. Steady-state power output (SPO) tracking (conducted by applying a constant potential under continuous AM 1.5 G for 100 s) of PSCs is then performed to testify the J-V characteristics. In Figure S9(a), PSCs based on the pristine perovskite shows a drop in PCE from 17.8% to 16.1% after 100 s, while PSCs based on PMMA-CNTs perovskite exhibit more robust behavior with an almost constant PCE of 21.2%, which indicates the incorporation of PMMA-CNTs composites in the perovskite film improves the operational stability of PSCs. External quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra of the PSCs are recorded to testify the validity of   $  J_{SC}  $   from J-V characteristics. The integration of EQE spectra affords   $  J_{SC}  $   of 21.2, 21.0 and 22.3 mA/cm² for the pristine, PMMA and PMMA-CNTs PSCs, respectively, which agrees well with the   $  J_{SC}  $   from J-V characterizations. This tactic of using PMMA-CNTs as additives has also been extended to the fabrication of large-area (1.0 cm²) PSCs. The large devices based on the PMMA-CNTs perovskite achieve a PCE of 20.7%, with a   $  J_{SC}  $   of 22.9 mA/cm²,   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.15 V, FF of 78.1% and stabilized at 20.2% (Figure S9(b)), which is much higher than that of the pristine and PMMA PSCs. The promising photovoltaic performance of the large-area PSCs suggests the capability of PMMA-CNTs additives for homogeneously up-scaling the perovskite films. We then perform the TCD and TVD measurements to elucidate the effects of PMMA and PMMA-CNTs on the charge transporting/recombination kinetics in PSCs. Figure S10(a) illustrates the TVD spectra of PSCs under different   $  V_{OC}  $  . Bi-exponentially fitting the spectra reveals two recombination processes: the fast recombination lifetime (  $  \tau_{fast}  $  ) is dominated by the recombination at the surface trap states, and the slow recombination lifetime (  $  \tau_{slow}  $  ) is governed by the free carrier recombination in the bulk [43]. In Figure 5(a), both PSCs based on PMMA perovskite and PMMA-CNTs perovskite exhibit longer   $  \tau_{fast}  $  , suggesting reduced non-radiative recombination at the surface traps by forming the PMMA-PbI  $  _{2}  $   adducts. In terms of the slow recombination process, the shorter   $  \tau_{slow}  $   in PSCs based on PMMA perovskite indicates that PMMA incorporation impedes the charge transport, which induces larger amount of free carrier recombination in the bulk. With improving the conductivity by CNTs, free carrier recombination is suppressed, resulting in elongated   $  \tau_{slow}  $   in PMMA-CNTs PSCs. Fitting TCD spectra of PSCs under a variety of illumination intensities from 0.1–1 sun (Figure S10(b)) renders the transit time (  $  t_{tr}  $  ) of PSCs as a function of light intensities, as shown in Figure 5(b). According to the equation   $  \mu = \frac{d^{2}}{U \times t_{tr}}  $  , where   $  \mu  $   is the mobility, d

is the interelectrode distance and U is the applied voltage,   $  \mu  $   of the perovskite films (on average) can be determined to be   $  6.3\times10^{-3}  $  ,   $  3.4\times10^{-4}  $   and   $  1.3\times10^{-2}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $  /(V s), respectively. Notably, the charge mobility is decreased by five folds with the addition of PMMA, in comparison to that of the pristine PSCs, which should be ascribed to the low conductive PMMA retarding the charge transport across the perovskite grains. The incorporation of highly conductive CNTs, on the other hand, ameliorates this issue by substantially enhancing the charge mobility to the order of   $  10^{-2}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $  /(V s).

We continue to uncover the charge transport and recombination kinetics at the interface under illumination by implementing the capacitance-frequency measurements. As it is generally acknowledged that the capacitance in the intermediate frequency region is due to the electronic polarization in relation to the dipole polarization or chemical capacitance in the perovskite, while the capacitance in low frequency is suggested as the electrode polarization caused by interfacial phenomena such as electric or ionic charge accumulation at the interface of perovskite [44,45]. In our case shown by Figure 5(c), the remarkably lower capacitance of PSCs based on PMMA-CNTs perovskite at low frequency region implies a less ionic charge accumulation, which might be ascribed to the improved charge mobility of PMMA-CNTs perovskite film.

# 4 Conclusions

In conclusion, we uncovered that, although Lewis-base polymer could act as defect-passivators for suppressing the trap-assisted recombination in the perovskite film, the low conductivity of polymer could severely hinder the charge transport and charge extraction, which limited  $  J_{\mathrm{SC}}  $  and FF of the photovoltaic devices. Herein, a universal tactic of using Lewis-base polymer-CNTs composites as additives in perovskite film has been put forward in this work towards the fabrication of large-area PSCs and f-PSCs. CNTs effectively transport holes across the perovskite grains as well as from the perovskite to HTM, which compensates the photocurrent losses and charge recombination caused by the low conductivity of Lewis-base polymer. By this simple design, we achieve an outstanding PCE of  $  21.7\%  $  from a small-area device  $  (0.16~\mathrm{cm}^2)  $  and  $  20.7\%  $  from a large-area device  $  (1.0~\mathrm{cm}^2)  $ . More importantly, this strategy enables the fabrication of f-PSCs with strong mechanical durability. The f-PSCs based on the PMMA-CNTs composites in this work demonstrate an impressive PCE of  $  18.3\%  $  and good bending durability ( $  80\%  $  of the initial PCE can be retained upon 1,000 times bending), which is one of the highest reported performances of the f-PSCs to date.
