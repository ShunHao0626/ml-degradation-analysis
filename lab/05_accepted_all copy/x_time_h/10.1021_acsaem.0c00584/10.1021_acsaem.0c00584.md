ABSTRACT: All-inorganic   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   perovskite quantum dots (PQDs) are promising photovoltaic materials due to their exceptional electronic properties, including tunable bandgap, high photoluminescence (PL) quantum yields and enhanced electronic properties due to tunable surface chemistry. Charge transport in solid state films can be further improved by manipulating surface chemistry which enhances better coupling of neighboring QDs. Here, we have fabricated an all inorganic   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   QD solar cell (QDSC) in an inverted (p-i-n) configuration using inorganic selective contact layers for charge transport. Additionally, we have improved the solid-state ligand exchange process using a layer by layer (LBL) deposition method for ligand replacement in the QD films. Specifically, we have removed native ligands such as anionic oleate and cationic oleylammonium ligands using methyl acetate and formamidinium. This mixture enables the efficient removal of ligands and provides strong inter-QD coupling during each LBL deposition step. The ligand replacement helps in improving the film morphology by curing voids originating from volume loss through LBL assembly in addition to improving strong coupling while preserving quantum confinement. As a result, the inverted   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   QD solar cell exhibits power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 13.1 % with 5 photoactive layers and 12.1 % with only 3 layers, both with negligible hysteresis. Most importantly, we achieve improved efficiency in an efficient solution-processed device with fewer fabrication steps and superior transparency versus most n-i-p devices.

KEYWORDS:  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_3 $  QD solar cell, layer-by-layer treatment, perovskite solar cell, p-i-n inverted solar cell, nanocrystal transport layers

# INTRODUCTION

Colloidal quantum dots (QDs) have recently attracted significant attention in optoelectronic devices such as light-emitting diodes, photovoltaics (PVs), photodetectors and many other applications.  $  ^{1-5}  $   Colloidal QDs offers unique features such as ease of synthesis, size and composition tunability of the bandgap, and confined excitons. High quality films can be produced by using layer by layer (LBL) methods, a single step solution deposition and various other deposition techniques.  $  ^{6-7}  $   Once in the thin film, the energy levels of the QDs can be modified by changing their surface chemistry via thin film ligand-exchange techniques, a process which is considered essential to building efficient optoelectronic devices.  $  ^{8-13}  $   Solution-processed lead chalcogenides such as PbS QD solar cells have achieved improvements in PV performance in every year of the past decade, with best power conversion efficiency (PCE) of about 12.2 %.  $  ^{14-17}  $   Lately, hybrid organic-inorganic halide perovskite QDs have emerged in PV applications due to their high PCEs, which are beginning to approach those of many thin film designs.  $  ^{18-20}  $   In particular, progress in perovskite QD solar cells improved rapidly when fully inorganic cesium lead iodide (CsPbI  $  _{3}  $  ) QDSCs achieved an impressive PCE of 10.77%.  $  ^{21}  $   Thereafter, a range of material improvements have occurred ranging from fully inorganic CsPbX  $  _{3}  $   (X = Cl, Br and I) QDs,  $  ^{22-32}  $   to organic-inorganic perovskites, such formamidinium lead iodide (FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ),  $  ^{33-34}  $   and mixed A-group compositions of Cs  $  _{1-x}  $  FA  $  _{x}  $  PbI  $  _{3}  $  .  $  ^{10,35-36}  $

However,   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   QDs have been realized mainly in “regular” structured (n-i-p) devices using organic hole transport layers (HTL), such as spiro-OMeTAD or pTAAD. These organic transport materials need high purity for superior performance, and can be costly, possibly hindering future industrialization of the n-i-p device type and almost certainly affecting the lifetimes of these devices.  $  ^{22, 29}  $   In contrast, inverted (p-i-n) structures provide a large selection of cheap solution processable inorganic charge transport layers (CTLs) with better long-term stability and have been observed to display less hysteresis.  $  ^{37-38}  $   Additionally, inorganic CTLs possess high optical

The current state of art perovskite QD technology still suffers from fabrication issues such as poor morphology, insufficient light absorption, surface defects and importantly unoptimized ligand exchange procedures which are critical to the device performance. $ ^{47-48} $  By using sequential treatments with the FAI solution after each step, in contrast to a normal single-step, post-deposition FAI treatment, the PV performance is shown to be significantly enhanced. Colloidal   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   QDs were synthesized via a hot-injection method using oleate and oleylammonium ligands adapted from previous reports.  $  ^{[23, 47]}  $   Figure 1A illustrates the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   QDSC device architecture with an inset showing a transmission electron microscope (TEM) image of the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   QDs used in this study. Figure 1B presents a cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image with false color imaging to illustrate each layer in the inverted device. The synthesized QDs are monodisperse (Figure S1) with peak emission at 678 nm, a full width at half-maximum (FWHM) of 34.5 nm and a photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) of 80 % (Figure 1C). A high PLQY is necessary to achieve superior performance in devices, since reducing nonradiative losses leads to increased   $  V_{oc}  $  .  $  ^{[21]}  $   Figure 1D shows suitable energy level positions for each layer in the device architecture with minimal differences in band energies between the CTLs and photoactive layers. Band levels for the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   QD films were determined via UV photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) to be at 5.60 eV and 3.84 eV for the valence and conduction bands, respectively (Figure S3). The energy levels of   $  NiO_{x}  $ ,   $  C_{60}  $   and ZnO were adapted from the literature.  $  ^{[49-50]}  $

To fabricate devices,   $  NiO_{x}  $   solution was first deposited via spin-casting process onto a patterned FTO substrate as the HTL. Then a layer of QD solution was spin cast onto the substrate several times. As-synthesized QDs have insulating native ligands which must be removed after each LBL step to improve device performance. Figure 2(a) illustrates a typical ligand exchange protocol for   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   QD films as the “control QD”.  $  ^{21-22}  $   During each LBL step, oleate ligands are chemically replaced using saturated salts of lead (II) nitrate   $  (\mathrm{Pb}(\mathrm{NO}_{3})_{2})  $   in methyl acetate (MeOAc) solution, followed by post-treatment with AX salts   $  [A = formamidinium (FA^{+}), methylammonium (MA^{+}), cesium (Cs^{+}), and X = I^{-}, Br^{-}]  $  . There is some uncertainty in the literature as to the function of   $  \mathrm{Pb}(\mathrm{NO}_{3})_{2}  $   in the MeOAc solution; in some reports a pure MeOAc solution was used instead.  $  ^{26}  $

Figure 2(c) compares the FTIR spectrum of the as-cast neat   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   QDs with the control QD and FAI QD films. High intensity IR signals were seen at the wavenumbers 2853, 2924 and 2955 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   and are mainly attributed to aliphatic hydrocarbons. Peaks in the neat films were also observed at 1410, 1560 and 1710 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   and are attributed to carboxylate groups and which mainly originate from oleate and oleylammonium ligands. The control QD films were treated with   $  \mathrm{Pb(NO_{3})_{2}}  $   in MeOAc solution in each layer and finally with FAI in an EtOAc solution. In this case, most of the oleylammonium ligands were not exposed to the short ligand FAI so the exchange was limited. Thus the intensity of the IR signals (  $  v(N-H_{3}^{+})  $  ,   $  v(C-Hx)  $  ,   $  v(C=C-H)  $   were still observable in this case, but at a reduced intensity indicating the oleylammonium ligands are not removed completely  $  ^{51-52}  $  . In contrast, for the FAI-QD films, almost all of the C–H modes near 2900 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   or below ~2000 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   belonging to oleylammonium, oleate, or octadecene disappear, indicating the effective removal of all native ligands. As such, the LBL FAI treatments appear to have removed a larger amount of these carbon moieties while building up a thick layer and resulting in better films. The emergence of an IR signal at around 1710 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   can be seen for the FAI QDs which indicates the presence a   $  v(C=N)  $   stretch in   $  FA^{+}  $  , similar to that observed by Sanehira et al., suggesting successful replacement of the native ligands at the surface.  $  ^{22}  $   The control QD films, treated with a single post-treatment of FAI, also shows   $  v(C=N)  $   modes but at significantly less intensity. Overall, the FTIR spectra suggest that the FAI treatment shows better removal of the native ligands and hence FAI treatment could be useful to boost device performance.

The PL peak for the FAI QDs shifted from 684 to 688 nm, as was observed in previous reports.  $  ^{[22,25]}  $   We attribute the red-shift to a decrease in the inter-QD distance owing to the removal of oleate and oleylammonium ligands, which results in better coupling in QDs arrays.  $  ^{[47]}  $   The lack of significant change in the absorption (Figure S2) and PL peaks confirms the FAI treatment does not facilitate alloy formation (  $  Cs_{x}FA_{1-x}PbI_{3}  $  ). Time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements were carried out to observe the effect of the FAI treatment. Figure 2 (e) shows the TRPL decay kinetics of control and LBL FAI QDs in which bi-exponential decay model was used for fitting. The fast decay component (  $  \tau_{1} \sim 3  $  ns) was observed in both control and FAI QDs which is assigned to trap-assisted recombination in QDs while a long component (  $  \tau_{2}  $  ) was assigned to radiative recombination.  $  ^{52-53}  $  In Figure 2(e), the long component (  $  \tau_{2}  $  ) increased from 30 ns to 45.7 ns for the FAI QDs, which indicates a reduction in surface defect density resulting in enhanced charge carrier lifetimes. UPS measurements (Figure S3) were made to compare the energy levels of control and FAI QDs. These band levels can change due to surface manipulation and ligand treatments,  $  ^{12-13}  $  but here we see no significant difference, probably because UPS is highly surface sensitive. For the same reason X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed but shows little difference (Figure S4) indicating that FAI treatment is effective in the final step. In conjunction with the FTIR spectra, these indicate that the penetrative depth of a single post-treatment with FAI is limited. Thus, the ability to perform multiple QD treatment steps without damaging the underlying CTLs is a necessary feature in high performance QDSCs and easier to accomplish in the inverted structure.

The ligand exchange process can also affect the morphology of the QD films, as can the surface roughness of the HTL underneath. LBL fabrication can induce cracks or pinholes due to the use of additional rinsing solvents, as is often observed with organic CTLs underneath. Large grains can be formed by the addition of excess FAI to the surface. In that case, large grain formation would result in bulk thin film morphologies very different from a close-packed QD film and could account for differences in performance. To test this, we investigated the QD film morphology using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) and found that both films look similar (SEM and AFM, Figure S5) with crack-free surfaces devoid of obvious pinholes. Furthermore, no large grain formation can be seen in either AFM or SEM for either film.

We have fabricated a series of solar cells using two different ligand exchange methods. In addition to this, we used two different thicknesses of QDs to fabricate our films. The most often reported optimal concentration of QDs is 70 to 80 mg/ml repeated for 4 to 5 layers to achieve a thickness of   $  \sim  $  300 nm. To be consistent between QD films, we use a 75 mg/ml solution with 5 layers and 145 mg/ml with 3 layers to achieve a thickness   $  \sim  $  350 nm in each case. These films were characterized by spectroscopic ellipsometry to determine the thickness (Table S1) and optical constants (Figure S7). Devices were finished by spin-casting the ETL layers,   $  C_{60}  $   and zinc oxide (ZnO) nanocrystals (NCs), from solution. ZnO NCs have several favorable properties such as monodisperse samples, solution processability, good crystallinity, high electron mobility and high transmittance in the visible spectrum.  $  ^{54}  $   The nearly transparent ZnO NCs exhibit good dispersity in solvents and a high degree of crystallinity (Figure S6). A silver electrode was evaporated onto the device using a shadow mask as the final fabrication step.

# ■ CONCLUSION

In conclusion, we have demonstrated solution-processed inverted (p-i-n) all-inorganic   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   QD solar cells with an improved ligand exchange technique enabled by the robust inorganic HTL. Compared with the control QD device cells (11.5 % PCE), the FAI QD showed improved photovoltaic performance with a PCE of up to 13.1 % owing to the efficient removal of oleate ligands, as well as enhanced charge extraction and longer charge carrier lifetimes. We believe the inverted device structure aids in development of all-inorganic, all-solution processed QDSCs, with a wider range of useful materials for more transparent, more robust, with high PCE and cheaper, faster methods of fabrication.
