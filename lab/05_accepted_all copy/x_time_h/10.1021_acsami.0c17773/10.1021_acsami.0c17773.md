# Direct Surface Passivation of Perovskite Film by 4-Fluorophenethylammonium lodide toward Stable and Efficient Perovskite Solar Cells

Xiongzhuo Jiang, $ ^{\perp} $  Shi Chen, $ ^{\perp} $  Yang Li, Lihua Zhang, Nan Shen, Guoge Zhang, Jun Du, Nianqing Fu, $ ^{*} $  and Baomin Xu $ ^{*} $

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Passivating the defective surface of perovskite films is becoming a particularly effective approach to further boost the efficiency and stability of their solar cells. Organic ammonium halide salts are extensively utilized as passivation agents in the form of their corresponding 2D perovskites to construct the 2D/3D perovskite bilayer architecture for superior device performance; however, this bilayer device partly suffers from the postannealing-induced destructiveness to the 3D perovskite bulk and charge transport barrier induced by the quantum confinement existing in the 2D perovskite. Hence, developing direct passivation of the perovskite layer by organic ammonium halides for high-performance devices can well address the above-mentioned issues, which has rarely been explored. Herein, an effective passivation strategy is proposed to directly modify the perovskite surface with an organic halide salt 4-fluorophenethylammonium iodide (F-PEAI) without further postannealing. The F-PEAI passivation largely inhibits the formation of the iodine vacancies and thus dramatically reduces the film defects, resulting in a much slower

charge trapping process. Consequently, the F-PEAI-modified device achieves a much higher champion efficiency (21%) than that (19.5%) of the control device, which dominantly results from more efficient suppression of interfacial nonradiative recombination and the subsequent decreased recombination losses. Additionally, the F-PEAI-treated device maintains 90% of its initial efficiency after 720 h of humidity aging owing to the enhanced hydrophobicity and decreased trap states, highlighting good ambient stability. These results provide an effective passivation strategy toward efficient and stable perovskite solar cells.

KEYWORDS: 4-fluorophenethylammonium iodide, surface passivation, decreased defects, nonradiative recombination, high efficiency, perovskite solar cells

# 1. INTRODUCTION

Organic–inorganic perovskite solar cells have attracted great research attention due to their low-cost manufacture and rapidly increasing power conversion efficiency (PCE) exceeding 25%.  $  ^{1}  $   Currently, the state-of-the-art PCEs of perovskite devices still lag far behind their Shockley-Queisser limit of 31–33%.  $  ^{2-4}  $   To further improve perovskite device efficiency, several research strategies including fine-tuning perovskite components,  $  ^{5}  $   antisolvent treatment for film crystallization,  $  ^{6,7}  $   interface engineering,  $  ^{8,9}  $   and perovskite surface passivation  $  ^{10}  $   are extensively proposed and investigated. Among these techniques, perovskite surface passivation is treated as a particularly effective approach to boost device efficiency, given the fact passivating detrimental defects at the perovskite surface and/or grain boundaries can dramatically reduce the formation of undesirable recombination centers capturing photogenerated charge carriers and can effectively inhibit the ion migration responsible for current–voltage hysteresis.  $  ^{11,12}  $

Besides, this bilayer perovskite device performance highly depends on the orientation of the 2D perovskite atop the 3D perovskite layer due to a strong impact of the existing quantum confinement on charge transport across the 2D perovskite.  $  ^{24,25}  $   Therefore, it is highly desirable to directly passivate the perovskite layer through the deposition of organic ammonium halides atop without further postannealing, which has great potential to achieve prominent device performance without incurring destructiveness to the bulk of the 3D perovskite and charge transport barrier induced by 2D perovskites. Recently, You and co-workers  $  ^{26}  $   directly adopted an organic halide salt (i.e., phenethylammonium iodide) rather than the corresponding 2D perovskite for surface passivation of mixed perovskite films, and the corresponding device yielded an ultrahigh certificated efficiency of 23.3% due to remarkably decreased film defects. Inspired by this striking result, it is highly imperative and urgent to develop novel organic halide salts to passivate the perovskite film for high-performance solar cells.

Fluorinated organic ammonium halides are generally hydrophobic and scarcely wetted by water owning to retarded dispersion forces induced by the high electronegativity of fluorine (F), and thus they have been utilized as a passivation layer in the form of their 2D perovskites to realize stable and efficient perovskite solar cells.  $  ^{[27,28]}  $   Grancini and co-workers  $  ^{[29]}  $   designed and synthesized a new fluorous organic cation to modify the perovskite surface with its low-dimensional

perovskite form to obtain waterproof 20% efficient solar cells. Moreover, a 2D perovskite layer based on pentafluorophenylethylammonium was inserted on top of the absorber layer to yield an ultrahigh device efficiency exceeding 22% with good operational stability.  $  ^{30}  $   Despite these significant advances in the fluorinated 2D perovskite passivation agents, current related studies merely concentrate on the perovskite surface passivation by fluorinated 2D perovskites rather than their corresponding fluorinated organic halide salts. As discussed above, the direct adoption of these fluorinated ammonium halides as passivation materials have obvious advantages in charge transport and perovskite bulk nondestructiveness. As a consequence, the interfacial passivation of the perovskite film by fluorinated organic halide salts needs to be urgently explored.

Given these considerations, an effective passivation strategy is developed to directly treat the perovskite surface with a fluorinated organic halide salt (F-PEAI) without further postannealing, and we further exploit its influences on the surface state, film trap states, charge recombination, and device performance. After F-PEAI passivation, the effective filling of the iodine vacancies existing on the surface causes a significant reduction of film trap states, resulting in a much slower charge trapping process. Consequently, the F-PEAI-modified device yields a peak PCE of 21% and a high open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ) of 1.13 V, much better than that (19.5% and 1.07 V) of the control device. The superior device performance can be primarily explained by more efficient suppression of undesirable nonradiative recombination and the subsequent declining recombination losses. # 2. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

In this work, we employ the mixed perovskite (FAP-bI $ _3 $ ) $ _{1-x} $ (MAPbBr $ _3 $ ) $ _x $  as the light-absorber layer, and the corresponding perovskite film is fabricated using a two-step spin coating method as previously reported. $ ^{31} $  Once the active layer is prepared, a facile passivation strategy is introduced, where an F-PEAI salt solution dissolved in isopropanol is spin-coated onto the perovskite surface without postannealing. To investigate the photovoltaic performance of as-fabricated perovskite films without and with F-PEAI modification, planar perovskite solar cells are constructed with an architecture of indium tin oxide (ITO)/SnO $ _2 $ /perovskite/F-PEAI/SpiroOMeTAD/Au (Figure 1a), and the corresponding cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image is presented in Figure 1b. At first, the dependence of the device performance on the F-PEAI concentration is explored in Figure 1c and Table S1, where the PCE experiences a significant increase with F-PEAI treatment and the optimal performance is obtained at the concentration of 2.5 mg mL $ ^{-1} $ . At this optimized condition, the as-prepared F-PEAI film should be discontinuous or thin enough for charge transport. A further rise of F-PEAI concentration results in a decline of the device performance, which is probably caused by the excessive accumulation of the highly insulating F-PEAI (discussed in the following sections).

Figure 1d exhibits the current density–voltage (J-V) curves of the champion devices treated with F-PEAI (2.5 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) and without F-PEAI measured under the standard AM 1.5G illumination condition, and the corresponding photovoltaic parameters are listed in Table 1. The above PCE enhancement in the F-PEAI-treated device principally results from a higher   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF, and these superior photovoltaic parameters can be largely ascribed to the decrease of surface defects by F-PEAI passivation, contributing to more efficient suppression of interfacial charge recombination.

Obviously, the perovskite surface treatment by F-PEAI gives better device efficiency. To uncover the origin of superior device performance, a series of spectroscopic and morphological characterizations are implemented. At first, we analyze the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Figure 2a) of perovskite films without and with F-PEAI modification to clarify the states of F-PEAI on the surface. All film samples exhibit a strong main diffraction peak at   $  12.7^{\circ}  $  ,   $  {}^{26,31,32}  $   indicative of the existence of the excess   $  PbI_{2}  $   in the perovskite layer, consistent with previous findings. However, in this sample another three dominant diffraction peaks at   $  14.1^{\circ}  $  ,   $  24.5^{\circ}  $  , and   $  28.4^{\circ}  $   are also detected for the bare and F-PEAI-treated films, confirming the formation of the perovskite phase.  $  ^{34}  $   In the magnified XRD region (Figure S2), we observe a weak emerging diffraction peak located at   $  5.6^{\circ}  $   in the F-PEAI-treated films, in agreement with the main peak location as probed for the F-PEAI powder. Furthermore, considering the deposition of F-PEAI solution without further postannealing, we conclude that the surface passivation layer mainly consists of the F-PEAI crystal rather than the corresponding 2D perovskite F-PEA  $  _{2}  $  PbI  $  _{4}  $  .

The absorbance spectra of perovskite films without and with F-PEAI passivation are depicted in Figure 2b, where the surface treatment has a negligible influence on the absorption intensity. These film samples show almost the same absorption onset at 805 nm, and the corresponding optical bandgap is determined to be 1.54 eV according to the Tauc plots shown in Figure S3. Moreover, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements are performed to analyze the effect of F-PEAI modification on the surface composition, as displayed in Figure 2c. In the F-PEAI-treated film, the emergence of a typical F 1s peak at 687.1 eV along with a representative peak at 292.2 eV corresponding to the   $  \pi-\pi  $   bonding from the phenyl functional group of the F-PEA  $  ^{+}  $   cation confirms the existence of F-PEAI on the film surface. In addition to the detected C—C bond (284.8 eV) in the control film, we notice an obvious C=O peak (288.2 eV), which is probably caused by the decomposition of the perovskite film when exposed to the oxygen/moisture condition.  $  ^{28}  $   According to the XPS peak fitting results shown in Figure S4, the peak area ratio of C=O/C—C shows a remarkable decline from 0.169 to 0.096 after F-PEAI post-treatment, corresponding to a 43.2% decline. This result implies that the degradation of the perovskite film can be effectively retarded by the F-PEAI passivation layer. To quantitatively compare the trap states of perovskite films without and with F-PEAI treatment, the trap density of these films is evaluated using the space charge-limited model.  $  ^{34,35}  $   In this case, the trap density (  $  N_{t}  $  ) can be estimated according to the equation   $  N_{t} = 2\varepsilon\varepsilon_{0}V_{\mathrm{TFL}}/(qL^{2})  $  , where   $  V_{TFL}  $   stands for the onset voltage of the trap-filled limited region, L is the thickness of the perovskite film, q denotes the elementary charge, and   $  \varepsilon  $   and   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   represent the dielectric constant of the perovskite and the vacuum, respectively. The electron-only devices are fabricated with an architecture of ITO/  $  SnO_{2}  $  /perovskite/PCBM/Ag, and the corresponding dark current–voltage characteristics are recorded in Figure 2d. The trap density for the control film is calculated to be   $  6.59 \times 10^{15} \, cm^{-3}  $  . After F-PEAI treatment, the trap density immediately drops to   $  4.16 \times 10^{15} \, cm^{-3}  $   at   $  1.0 \, mg \, mL^{-1}  $   and then undergoes a further decline to the lowest value of   $  2.88 \times 10^{15} \, cm^{-3}  $   at   $  2.5 \, mg \, mL^{-1}  $  , followed by a slight rise to   $  3.49 \times 10^{15} \, cm^{-3}  $   at   $  5.0 \, mg \, mL^{-1}  $  . Such a reduction of defects enables the efficient inhibition of interfacial nonradiative recombination, which is beneficial for better device performance. This comparative analysis also manifests that the defects on the perovskite surface can be effectively passivated by the P-PEAI treatment.

To better clarify the relationship between the film imperfection and the charge recombination behavior, both steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved PL measurements of the perovskite films deposited on glass substrates without and with F-PEAI modification are conducted. As illustrated in Figure 2e, all film samples show a dominant emission peak situated at 787 nm, however much stronger PL intensities are observed for the F-PEAI-treated films as compared with the control film. This result indicates the F-PEAI passivation can greatly depress the surface nonradiative decay, consistent with the declining defects as observed in the F-PEAI-modified samples.  $  ^{36}  $   Furthermore, the time-resolved PL results (Figure 2f) can gain a deep insight into charge recombination via nonradiative pathways related to the surface defects. A biexponential fit to the decay signal gives the corresponding PL lifetimes (Table S3) where the fast decay component (  $  \tau_{1}  $  ) corresponds to the charge trapping process and the slow decay phase (  $  \tau_{2}  $  ) belongs to the bimolecular recombination of long-lived free charges.  $  ^{37}  $   The control film exhibits a PL lifetime (  $  \tau_{1}  $  ) of 66.3 ns, whereas much longer PL lifetimes (89.5–119.5 ns) are obtained for the film samples with F-PEAI treatment, indicative of a slower charge trapping phase because of the passivation-induced lower concentration of available charge defects. This further verifies the effective suppression of nonradiative recombination in the F-PEAI-modified films, which definitely contributes to a higher device   $  V_{oc}  $  . It should be pointed out that the F-PEAI (2.5 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) treated film presents the strongest PL signal and the longest PL lifetime (119.5 ns) among all samples, in good accordance with the lowest trap density observed above.

To further survey the effects of F-PEAI passivation on the device charge recombination kinetics, several comprehensive characterization techniques including light intensity-dependent   $  V_{oc}  $   and   $  J_{sc}  $ , transient photovoltage, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) are used for analysis. A power-law relationship between   $  J_{sc}  $   and light intensity (  $  J_{sc} \propto I^{r}  $  ) is illustrated in Figure 4a, where the index   $  \alpha  $   close to unity means that a solar cell will not suffer from space charge limit effects.  $  ^{38}  $   The devices without and with F-PEAI treatment deliver similar   $  \alpha  $ -values of 0.950 and 0.961, respectively, suggesting that both devices are operating with negligible space charge limit effects. Furthermore, the slope of   $  V_{oc}  $   versus the natural logarithm of light intensity experiences a decline from 1.25 to 1.05 kT/q after the introduction of an F-PEAI passivation layer (Figure 4b). The dominant occurrence of the bimolecular recombination corresponds to an ideal slope of kT/q, whereas a slope larger than kT/q implies the existence of the additional trap-assisted recombination.  $  ^{39}  $   Therefore, the smaller slope manifests that this F-PEAI treatment can more efficiently suppress the monomolecular recombination through a trap state, which in good agreement with the decreased trap states in the F-PEAI-modified samples, favorable for the improvements of   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF. Additionally, the charge recombination lifetime can be estimated on the basis of the transient photovoltage decay curves without and with F-PEAI modification, as displayed in Figure 4c. Finally, we compare the stability of the devices without and with F-PEAI passivation stored in nitrogen or in the ambient air with   $  \sim40\%  $   relative humidity. After storing in nitrogen for 1440 h, the control and F-PEAI-treated devices maintain 93.3% and 98.9% of their initial efficiencies (Figure 4e), respectively, highlighting good intrinsic stability. On this basis, the ambient stability of both devices without encapsulation is further monitored. As shown in Figure 4f, the PCE of the F-PEAI-modified device only degrades by 10% after 720 h, whereas the control device expresses a much faster PCE degradation of 23% under the same aging conditions. This comparative result reflects that the control device without F-PEAI passivation is more sensitive to moisture and oxygen, consistent with XPS results as discussed above. The digital images showing the humidity stability of bare, PEAI-coated and F-PEAI-coated perovskite films stored in the air atmosphere with relative humidity   $  60\pm5\%  $   are shown in Figure S7. It is clear from Figure S7 that the humidity stability of F-PEAI-modified perovskite film is significant enhanced when compared with the bare and PEAI-modified perovskite film. After 5 and 10 days of storage, visible decomposition can be clearly observed for the bare- and PEAI-coated perovskite films, respectively, whereas the F-PEAI-modified perovskite film remains as the black perovskite phase on the 10th day. The better intrinsic and moisture stabilities of the F-PEAI-treated device primarily originate from the decreased trap states and enhanced hydrophobicity of the perovskite layer induced by F-PEAI passivation. Now, it can be concluded here that the F-PEAI is as efficient as PEAI for defect passivation but outperforms the latter for enhancing the humidity stability of devices.

The thermal stability of devices is compared in Figure S8. The spiro-OMeTAD was replaced by a more thermally stable hole transport layer of poly[bis(4-phenyl) (2,4,6-trimethylphenyl)amine] (PTAA) in thermal stability tests. It can be seen that both of the devices with and without F-PEAI passivation remain around 84% of their initial PCE after 60 h of storage at 85 °C, indicating that the F-PEAI will not introduce any source of thermal instability for long-term operation.

# 3. CONCLUSION

In summary, we demonstrate an effective passivation strategy to modify the perovskite surface with a thin layer of F-PEAI to obtain efficient and stable solar cells and further investigate its effects on the surface state, film defects, charge recombination, and device performance. The incorporation of the F-PEAI passivation layer atop the perovskite film largely suppresses the formation of the iodine vacancies, and thereby the existing film defects are substantially decreased, leading to a much slower

charge trapping process. As a result, the F-PEAI-treated device achieves an optimal PCE of 21% along with a high   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.13 V, which greatly outperforms that (19.5% and 1.07 V) of the control device. This significantly enhanced efficiency can be mainly attributed to the stronger inhibition of interfacial nonradiative recombination and the resulting reduced charge recombination losses. In addition, a lower level of film defects and the enhanced hydrophobicity jointly result in much better ambient stability of the F-PEAI-modified device, retaining 90% of its initial efficiency after 720 h of moisture aging. These findings afford a promising passivation strategy toward high-performance perovskite solar cells.
