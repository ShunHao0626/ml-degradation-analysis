# Small Molecule Modulator at the Interface for Efficient Perovskite Solar Cells with High Short-Circuit Current Density and Hysteresis Free

Ming Wang, Wei Li, Huaxin Wang, Ke Yang, Xiaofei Hu, Kuan Sun, Shirong Lu, and Zhigang Zang*

Interface engineering is widely applied in the one-step antisolvent deposition for elevating efficiency of the perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Herein, an alcohol-soluble small molecule, namely 2-mercaptoimidazole (MI), is inserted between the hole transport layer and perovskite layer to form a cross-linking bridge, which is built through: i) Coulomb interaction between the negatively charged MI and positively charged poly (3,4-ethylene dioxythiophene); ii) electrostatic interaction between imidazolium cation and iodide anion in perovskite. The cross-linking bridge can accelerate the hole transmission and inhibit interfacial recombination. Hence, by the optimum design of MI concentration, hysteresis-free devices with a high power conversion efficiency of  $ 20.68\% $  are obtained. The overall efficiency increase is due to significantly enhanced short-circuit current density from 21.28 to  $ 23.45\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $  and slightly raised open-circuit voltage from 1.06 to  $ 1.08\mathrm{V} $ , suggesting that the MI modified layer can effectively improve the performance of the devices. Moreover, unencapsulated devices with MI interfacial modification show better environmental stability compared to the reference devices. This facile interfacial approach is promising for future commercialization of inverted PSCs.

In planar PSCs, the electron transfer layer (ETL) and hole transport layer (HTL) have crucial influences on the crystallization process of perovskite films. Meanwhile, the interfacial contacts between the ETL (or HTL) and the perovskite films affect the photogenerated carriers dynamic and thus change the overall device efficiency, stability and hysteresis.  $  ^{[34-38]}  $   It is well known that the enlarged grains of perovskite films and decreased interface defects boost the performance of PSCs. Accordingly, the interface modification at perovskite/HTL and perovskite/ETL interfaces have been investigated and optimized. For instance, several effective electronic transport materials (i.e.,   $  TiO_{2}  $  , ZnO,   $  SnO_{2}  $  ) have been modified by inserting a passivation layer to aggrandize grain size, reduce interface recombination and increase interface carrier transmission in normal planar PSCs.  $  ^{[39-43]}  $   For the inverted PSCs, several organic and inorganic materials formed on HTL by interface modification for improving PCE, such as polymers poly(4-vinyl pyridine) (PVP), poly(N-90-heptadecanyl-2,7-carbazole-alt-5,5-(40,70-di-2-thienyl-20,10,30-benzothiadiazole))PCDTBT),

These splendid works on the ETL/perovskite interface for normal PSCs inspire us to investigate the effective interface modulator for the HTL/perovskite interface of inverted PSCs.

Generally, organic molecules dissolved in toxic solvent such as toluene or chlorobenzene are employed to passivate the interface or the perovskite film, which is unfriendly to the environment. Therefore, it is urgent for the interface engineering to use eco-friendly molecules, such as the alcohol-soluble 2-Mercaptoimidazole (MI) with multifunctional units. It is reported that the PEDOT:PSS is formed by the integration of the positively charged PEDOT and the negatively charged PSS via Coulomb interaction.  $  ^{[50]}  $   Meanwhile, the MI containing S–H units are easily deprotonated and thus have a negative charge.  $  ^{[51,52]}  $   Hence if the MI is spun on above the PEDOT:PSS film, the negatively charged S units of MI and positively charged PEDOT could be compositely integrated via Coulomb interaction. Moreover, the N units of the imidazole group in MI have been reported to be able to passivate defects of the perovskite layer via interaction with Pb–I units in perovskite film.  $  ^{[53]}  $

In this work, alcohol-soluble small molecule MI has been used as the passivation material inserted between HTL and perovskite film. The MI acts as a bridge to cross-link the perovskite layer and HTL, accelerating carrier transmission, and thus improving the PSCs performance. As a result, the best PSCs with 3 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  MI showed an improved PCE from 18.22% to 20.68%, with a significantly increased  $  J_{sc}  $  from 21.28 to 23.45 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  and a moderate growth of  $  V_{oc}  $  from 1.06 to 1.08 V. These results confirmed that the MI layer strengthens the quality of perovskite films and boosts the overall performance of PSCs.

# 2. Results and Discussion

To investigate the MI passivation effect on the photovoltaic performance of devices, we fabricated p–i–n planar PSCs (device structure: ITO/PEDOT: PSS/Perovskite/PCBM/Ag), as shown in Figure 1a, where the MI modified layer was inserted between the HTL and the perovskite layer. Figure S1 (Supporting Information) shows the clear layered structure with sharp interface which could be observed in the cross-section scanning electron microscope (SEM) image of the PSCs. The MI layer is used as a bridging agent at HTL/perovskite interface (Figure 1b), where the molecular structure is depicted in Figure S2a (Supporting Information). Essentially, PEDOT: PSS is made up of negatively charged PSS and positively charged PEDOT via Coulomb interaction, where the chemical structure is depicted in Figure S2b (Supporting Information).[40] So the negatively charged of S units in MI and positively charged PEDOT in PEDOT: PSS was integrated via Coulomb interaction as shown in Figure S2c (Supporting Information). Simultaneously, the pyridinium cation could effectively passivate the Pb–I antisite defects or cation vacancy to reduce interfacial recombination and accelerate carrier extraction,[55,56] which accordingly improves the PSCs performance.

Here, we investigate the photovoltaic performance by inserting different concentrations of MI (0, 1, 3, and 6 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ). Figure S3 (Supporting Information) show the Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) for the MI film and the mixed solvents rinsed MI film, where it is laterally confirmed that MI is between perovskite film and PEDOT: PSS film. The statistic photovoltaic data of the PSCs depending on MI concentration are collected in Figure S4 and Table S1 (Supporting Information). When the concentration of MI is 3 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  , we observed the highest PCE. Hereafter, all the data for comparison are based on the PEDOT: PSS as HTL with and without 3 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   MI insert layer. Figure 1c shows the current density–voltage (J–V) characteristics of the champion devices based on the PEDOT: PSS as HTL with (red) and without (black) MI layer and the inserted table (Figure 1c) summarizes the photovoltaic parameters of the best devices. Among them, the best PCE of the control devices is 18.22%, with a   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.06 V,   $  J_{sc}  $   of 21.28 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and FF of 80.66% in the forward scan (FS) direction at a scan rate of 20 mV s  $  ^{-1}  $   under simulated AM 1.5G one sun illumination of 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  . As a contrast, the MI passivated devices show a champion PCE of 20.68%, with a higher   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.08 V, a higher   $  J_{sc}  $   of 23.45 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and FF of 80.91% under the same testing conditions. From the overall devices performance, the increase in PCE is due to a significant increase in   $  J_{sc}  $   and the slight raising of the   $  V_{oc}  $  . Figure 1d shows the external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra of the PSCs with and without MI modification. The integrated photocurrents density (21.28 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   for the reference devices and 23.45 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   for MI modified devices) calculated from EQE data are well consistent with those   $  J_{sc}  $   from J–V curves as displayed in Figure 1c. Meanwhile, the J–V curves measured in reverse scan (RS) and FS indicate that MI modification can effectively reduce the hysteresis compared with that of the control devices, as shown in Figure 1e and Table S2 (Supporting Information). Furthermore, the stabilized power output in air environment (the steady-state output photocurrent and PCE) of the devices was tested at the maximum power point (  $  V_{max} = 0.88  $   V for the control device and   $  V_{max} = 0.91  $   V for MI modified device) to evaluate the reliability of the devices performance, as shown in Figure 1f. For the reference devices and MI modified devices, the stabilized PCE of 19.14% and 17.02% were observed after 180 s in the air environment (temperature: ≈25 °C humidity: 50%), respectively, which is in consistent with the J–V curves in Figure 1c,e.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that the small molecule MI inserted between HTL and perovskite layer cloud improve the photovoltaic performance of PSCs. The inserted MI layer could strengthen interfacial contact between PEDOT:PSS and perovskite films which suppressed the interfacial recombination and accelerated hole transfer. As a result, by optimizing

the concentration of MI, the short-circuit current density of devices was significantly increased from 21.28 to 23.45 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  . The champion devices with a PCE of 20.68% and hysteresis free were obtained, which had a 13.5% improvement compared with that of the reference devices. More importantly, the unencapsulated PSCs devices with MI modification also showed improved stability under the ambient condition (25°C at 50% humidity) due to the enhanced crystallization of perovskite films. This study demonstrates MI modulator at the interface are helpful to further improve the performance and the stability of PSCs.

Devices Fabrication: ITO glass substrates (sheet resistance of 14 Ω [−1]) were ultrasonically cleaned for 30 min with detergent, deionized (DI) water, ethyl alcohol, acetone, sequentially, and isopropanol (IPA). After the ITO substrates were dried with nitrogen flow, the substrates were treated by UV ozone (UVO) for 30 min. Then PEDOT:PSS layer was spin-coated on the ITO substrates through a 0.45 micrometer Teflon filter by spin-coating at 5000 rpm for 40 s in ambient atmosphere, and annealed in ambient air at 120 °C for 30 min. After the ITO substrates were cooled down to room temperature, the substrates were moved into a nitrogen-filled glove box with dried nitrogen (<1.0 ppm of O² and H₂O) for the deposition of perovskite films. 80 μL different concentrations of a 2-Mercaptoimidazole solution (0, 1, 3, and 6 mg mL⁻¹ isopropanol) were spin-casted on the top of PEDOT: PSS film at 5000 rpm for 40 s and annealed on a hot plate at 70°C for 1 min. After the films were cooled down to room temperature, the FA₀.₃MA₀.₈PbI₃₋ₓClₓ photoactive layer was then deposited by a simple one-step deposition method. The perovskite precursor solution (50 μL) was spin-coated on the PEDOT:PSS films and 2-Mercaptoimidazole-modified films at a rotation speed of 1000 rpm for 20 s and 3500 rpm for another 40 s, and before the end of the spin coating process, 1 mL chlorobenzene was poured into the spinning film in a few seconds. Subsequently, the obtained perovskite films were put on the heating table at 95 °C for 5 min form FA₀.₃MA₀.₈PbI₃₋ₓClₓ films. PCBM (23 mg mL⁻¹, chlorobenzene) was spin-coated on top of perovskite films at a rotation speed of 2500 rpm for 30 s. After this, the substrate was annealed at 100 °C for 12 min. Then, the RhB101 layer was then spin-coated on PCBM films at 1500 rpm for 40 s with a concentration of 0.6 mg mL⁻¹. Finally, the films were transferred to a thermal evaporation chamber under a high vacuum (< 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ Pa), then 1.2 nm LiF film and 100 nm Ag film were sequentially deposited on PCBM through a shadow mask.

Devices Characterizations: X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern was recorded by a PANalytical Empyrean diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (  $  \lambda = 1.5406  $   Å), step 0.02 and scan speed 2.3 min  $  ^{-1}  $  . XRD was measured by the model of grazing incidence. Optical absorption spectra and transmission spectra were measured by using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800). The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra were obtained by a fluorescence spectrophotometer (Cary Eclipse, Agilent) with an excitation wavelength of 530 nm. For the PL curves, the slit-width of emission and excitation light were 5 and 10 nm, respectively. The cross section of the devices and the surface topography of perovskite films were characterized by the JEOL JSM-7800F field emission SEM. For the SEM characterization, the test was conducted in low level detector (LED) mode with gun-voltage of 5 kV. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was tested using an MFP-3D-BIO AFM (Asylum Research, an Oxford Instruments company, USA). The time-resolved PL measurements were carried out using a combined fluorescence lifetime and steady-state spectrometer (FLS980, Edinburgh Instruments Ltd.) by using a 510 nm picosecond pulsed laser. Photocurrent–voltage (I–V) curves were measured by using a Keithley 2400 source meter under an AM 1.5G solar simulator (Newport, 2612A) which was calibrated by a silicon reference cell under a light intensity of 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  . IPCE of PSCs was measured by the solar cell quantum efficiency measurement system (Newport, USA). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed on CHI760E Electrochemical Workstation (Chen Hua, China) at a bias of 0.3 V in the frequency range of 1 MHz to 0.1 Hz under dark illumination. The contact angle goniometer (YIKE-360A model; Chengde Precision Test Instrument Factory, China) was employed to measure the contact angles. During the test, the mixed solvents droplets were dripped to the surface of the PEDOT:PSS film and MI/PEDOT:PSS film manually, forming a curved spherical body. Then the camera caught the fixed images. At last, the goniometer automatically calculated the contract angle according to the shape of the sphere. Transient photocurrent (TPC) and transient photovoltage (TPV) measurements were performed with a system excited by a 520 nm (3 nJ, 60 ns) pulse laser. Each solar cell device had an active area of 0.11 cm  $  ^{2}  $   and was measured in air.
