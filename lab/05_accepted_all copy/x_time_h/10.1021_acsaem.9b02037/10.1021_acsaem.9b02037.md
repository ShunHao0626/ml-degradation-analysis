# Improving and Stabilizing Perovskite Solar Cells with Incorporation of Graphene in the Spiro-OMeTAD Layer: Suppressed Li Ions Migration and Improved Charge Extraction

ABSTRACT: Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have achieved a huge success in power conversion efficiency (PCE), although they still suffer from the long-term stability problem caused by the intrinsic sensitivity of perovskites to moisture. 2,2',7,7'-Tetrakis (N,N-di-p-methoxyphenylamine) 9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD) is widely used as the hole transport layer (HTL) in typical PSCs; meanwhile, bis(trifuoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt (Li-TFSI) is necessary as an additive in the Spiro-OMeTAD HTL to improve the hole mobility. However, the Li+ ions bring in high hygroscopicity and a water-uptake effect that both aggravate degradation of the Spiro-OMeTAD HTL and thereby of the perovskite layers. Here, we modify the Li-TFSI-based Spiro-OMeTAD HTL by adding reduced graphene oxide (rGO). We verify that rGO provides adsorption sites for Li+ ions and subsequently suppresses Li+ migration. The water-uptake effect originating from Li+ ions is thus restrained, and unfavorable pinholes in HTL caused by Li+ ion migration are eliminated. Consequently, the rGO-

incorporated HTL remarkably improves the device stability that maintains the initial PCEs within 3% loss after 700 h under 40% humidity; however, the pristine devices almost lose the efficiency after 620 h. In addition, the good conductivity of the rGO favors hole transport in the Spiro-OMeTAD, resulting in a promotion in PCEs from 17.7% to 19.3% by incorporating rGO in HTL. Our work takes an insight into the function of rGO in the HTL and demonstrates an effective way of improving the efficiency and stability of PSCs simultaneously.

YWORDS: perovskite solar cells, moisture stability, reduced graphene oxide (rGO), lithium ions, ion migration, charge extraction

# 1. INTRODUCTION

Organic/inorganic hybrid perovskites have been recognized as a super star in photovoltaics for their high optical absorption coefficients,  $  ^{1-3}  $   high radiative recombination probability,  $  ^{4-6}  $   and especially long diffusion lengths of photoinduced charge carriers.  $  ^{7-10}  $   These advantages allow feasible perovskite solar cells (PSCs) composed of a perovskite light-absorber layer sandwiched between charge carrier (a hole- and an electron-) transport layers.  $  ^{11-14}  $   Although PSCs had a meteoric rise in the recent decade,  $  ^{15-17}  $   rocketing to power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) over 25% for a single-junction,  $  ^{18-20}  $   unsatisfying stability of PSCs limits their further practical applications, and this has become an issue that needs to be broken through urgently.  $  ^{21-25}  $   The intrinsic sensitivity of perovskites to ambient moisture counts for the lability of devices in long-term use.  $  ^{26-28}  $   Roughly, to keep perovskite layers in isolation from water molecules can suppress their chemical degradations leading to an improvement of moisture stability of devices.

Charge carrier transport layers are indispensable modules in PSCs for extraction and transport processes of electron and hole from perovskites to electrodes; and also are natural encapsulation covers separating perovskite layers from ambient environment. For conventional n-i-p structures, the hole transport layer (HTL) is practically exposed to atmosphere (metal electrodes cover HTL but are too thin) thus it is crucial to improve the water-insulation capacity of the HTL. 2,2',7,7'-Tetrakis(N,N-di-p-methoxyphenylamine) 9,9'-bifuorene (Spiro-OMeTAD) is a widely used HTL material for current PSCs as it is of appropriate hole-accepting energy levels and relatively stable chemical activity with respect to most organic/inorganic perovskites.  $  ^{29,30}  $   Pristine Spiro-OMeTAD is poor in device performance, whereas the Li-TFSI (bis-

The addition of Li-TFSI may induce deliquescence of the perovskites, being contrary to more stable PCS devices that one may expect.

To reverse the tide of this disadvantage, we propose a strategy to control the migration of   $  Li^{+}  $   ions by adding reduced graphene oxide (rGO) in the Li-TFSI-based Spiro-OMeTAD HTL. Although rGO has been extensively applied in device fabrication in many ways,  $  ^{39-42}  $   the underlying effects of rGO particularly on the stability of HTLs remain unclear. In our study, we revealed that rGO in HTLs provides adsorption sites for lithium ions in analogy with its effects in lithium batteries and consequently confines migration of   $  Li^{+}  $   ions. We also suggest that the Li-rGO bonding suppresses water-uptake

effects that may be originated from hydrolysis of Li-TFSI. Consequently, the rGO-incorporated HTL remarkably improves the device stability that maintains the initial PCEs after 700 h (under 40% humidity), in comparison with the pristine devices of which the PCEs inevitably fail after 620 h. In addition, the good conductivity of the rGO favors hole transportation in the Spiro-OMeTAD, resulting in a promotion in PCEs from 17.7% to 19.3% by incorporating rGO in HTLs.

It is expected that rGO may enhance hole transportation in HTL as a result of its remarkable charge mobility. We characterized the hole transport capacity of Spiro-OMeTAD layers with and without rGO by using a J-V test. As shown in Figure 2a, it reveals that rGO significantly reduces the resistance of the Spiro-OMeTAD layer by 33.6%, as a result of improved diffusion processes of holes. Photoluminescence (PL) measurements of Spiro-OMeTAD/perovskite films were carried out, and results are summarized in Figure 2b,c. In the presence of rGO, the Spiro-OMeTAD layer exhibits a stronger PL quenching effect than that without rGO, as indicated by the less intense PL emission and shortened PL lifetime. This proves an improvement in hole extraction with incorporation of rGO. To further verify the enhancement in hole mobility with rGO, space charge limited current (SCLC) measurements were performed on the FTO/PEDOT:PSS/Spiro-OMeTAD/Au films. According to the Mott-Gurney equation (eq 1),  $  ^{44}  $  the hole mobility  $  \mu  $  is proportional to the ratio between J and  $  V^{2}  $ . It is worth noting that the transfer resistance is elevated as revealed by the impedance measurement in Figure 2e. We suggest that it may be attributed to the mismatched energy level of rGO (see Figure S2). However, by doping rGO in the HTL, it brings an overall positive effect.

By counting on the benefits of rGO to hole transport, we characterize the photovoltaic performance of the full devices. The Figure 3a inset shows a cross-sectional SEM image of the device with rGO in HTL. In Figure 3a, we compare the  $ J - V $  curves of the best-performing devices with rGO incorporated and pristine Spiro-OMeTAD HTLs under full simulated solar illumination conditions (AM1.5,  $ 100\mathrm{mWcm}^{-2} $ ). In addition, devices show negligible hysteresis upon addition of rGO (see Figure S3), which may be ascribed to the inhibition of  $ \mathrm{Li^{+}} $  migration that will be discussed later. # 3. CONCLUSIONS

In summary, we state that rGO is a promising additive in the Spiro-OMeTAD HTL of PSCs, in favor of device stability and performance. Specifically, we found a promotion in PCEs from 17.7% to 19.3% by incorporating rGO in the Spiro-OMeTAD HTL, which is attributed to the benefits of the graphene structures to hole mobility. Meanwhile, the device stability with rGO has been also significantly improved. Our rGO-based devices show sustained performance in PCEs after a 700-h storage at 25 °C and 40% humidity, remarkably surpassing the pristine devices that fade to failure within 600 h. XPS and TEM results revealed that rGO effectively suppresses Li  $  ^{+}  $  ion migration by providing adsorption sites for Li  $  ^{+}  $  ions in the Spiro-OMeTAD. As a result, the water-uptake effect that originated from Li  $  ^{+}  $  ions is restrained, and detestable pinholes caused by Li  $  ^{+}  $  ion migration are eliminated. Our findings build a further understanding on the function of rGO in the HTL and provide a practical solution to improve the performance and stability of PSCs simultaneously.

Synthesis of rGO. We applied Modified Hummer's method to prepare the materials graphene oxide (GO). Then, 500 mg of GO we made before was put into a microwave oven at a power of 800 W for 30 s, and the black reduced graphene oxide (rGO) powder was collected.  $  ^{51,52}  $

Fabrication of Film and Devices. Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) substrates were predisposed by ultrasonic successively in deionized water, acetone, and alcohol for 20 min each step. Next, 60   $  \mu  $  L of   $  TiO_{2}  $   sol was spin-coated on cleaned FTO substrates and annealed at 500 °C for 30 min to prepare the compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer. Mesoporous (mp)   $  TiO_{2}  $   was then spin-coated at 3800 rpm and calcined at 500 °C for 1 h. The absorption perovskite layer was prepared with a two-step method. First, 65   $  \mu  $  L of lead iodide solution (600 mg   $  PbI_{2}  $   in 1 mL DMF and DMSO (8/2, v/v)) was spin-coated onto the   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer at 4000 rpm for 30 s and cooled down for 15 min. Afterward, 0.15 mL precursor solution (80 mg FAI and 10 mg MABr in 1 mL of isopropyl alcohol) was dripped on the top of the   $  PbI_{2}  $   and spin-coated at 5000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 150 °C for 30 min. As for the rGO-incorporated HTL layer, we first prepared rGO solution in chlorobenzene (0.6–0.7 mg/mL), followed by ultrasonic bath, and then extracted the supernatant. Subsequently, Spiro-OMeTAD (80 mg/mL), Li-TFSI (17.5 mg/mL), and tBP (28.8 mg/mL) were dissolved in rGO-containing chlorobenzene solution and spin-coated on perovskite film at 3000 rpm for 30 s, followed by drying in air with a relative humidity of   $  \sim  $  5% for about 24 h. For the standard HTL, the same recipe of Spiro-OMeTAD, Li-TFSI, and tBP and the same spin-coating procedure as the above were employed except that the chemicals were dissolved into pristine chlorobenzene. Lastly, 100 nm thickness of gold layer was thermally evaporated to serve as the counter electrode.

Characterization. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi S-4300) and atomic force microscopy (AFM, SEIKO SPA300HV) were employed to characterize the film surface morphologies. Transmission electron microscope (TEM, FEI Tecnai G2 F20) was employed to characterize the morphologies of the materials and mapping of the elements. Electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) of the devices were measured by an IM6e Electrochemical Workstation (ZAHNER, Germany). Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements were conducted on a time correlated single photon counting (TCSPC) system (FluoTime 300, PicoQuant). Current density–voltage (J-V) measurements of the devices were carried out on a Keithley 2400 source measurement unit under simulated AM 1.5 illumination (standard 100 mW/cm²). The simulated light was cast by an Oriel 92251A-1000 sunlight simulator, which was calibrated by a standard reference of a Newport 91150 silicon solar cell.
