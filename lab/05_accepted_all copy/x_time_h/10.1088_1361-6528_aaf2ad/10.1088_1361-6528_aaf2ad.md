# $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $ -rGO nanocomposite as an efficient electron transport layer for stable perovskite solar cells on AZO substrate

# Abstract

Electron transport layer (ETL) plays an important role in realizing efficient and stable perovskite solar cells. There are continuous efforts in developing new types of low cost ETLs with improved conductivity and compatibility with perovskite and the conducting electrode. Here, in order to obtain high efficient and stable perovskite solar cells on  $ \mathrm{ZnO} $ : Al (AZO) substrate, reduced graphene oxide (rGO) is incorporated into  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  nanoparticles to form composite ETL. For planar perovskite solar cell on AZO substrates,  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $ -rGO with a low incorporation ratio of  $ 3\mathrm{wt}\% $  rGO significantly enhances the device short circuit current density ( $ \mathrm{J}_{\mathrm{sc}} $ ) and the fill factor (FF) when compared to the device with pristine  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  ETL, leading to an overall power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 16.8\% $  with negligible hysteresis. The effectiveness of the excited charge transfer process of  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $ -rGO ETL is revealed by time-resolved photoluminescence decay (TRPL), and by electrochemical impedance spectrum (EIS) measurements. Furthermore, the solar cell stability is also enhanced due to the incorporation of rGO in the ETL. This work provides a low cost and effective ETL modification strategy for achieving high performance planar PSCs.

Keywords : AZO substrate,  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $ -rGO ETL, PCE, short circuit current density, the fill factor, EIS, TRPL.

# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs), with a PCE reaching  $ 23.3\% $  [1], have attracted great attention in recent years. The remarkable achievement of PSC is owing to the excellent material properties of perovskite, such as suitable bandgap, high carrier mobility and a long diffusion length [2-4]. The improvement of interfacial and hole/electron transporting layer also make a significant contribution to this rapid development.  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  is one of the most widely adopted electron transport layers. Nonetheless, the fact that  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  requires a high temperature sintering process makes it unfavorable

ZnO [6-9] and  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  [10-13] are also being studied as alternative low cost ETL candidates. It is reported that ZnO surface usually contains hydroxide and the residual acetate ligands that can also decompose the perovskite film, adding to the device instability [14]. Among many n-type metal oxide,  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  with suitable conduction band level and excellent mobility is found to be a better electron transportation for perovskite solar cell [15]. Perovskite solar cells with SnO2 ETL has achieved a certified efficiency of  $ 20\% $  [16]. A recent report yttrium (Y) □doped  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  NSAs, results exhibit improved performance with much reduced hysteresis [17]. In addition, lithium (Li) and Stibium (Sb) doped  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  films also have been successfully reported [18,19].

Here, we report our investigation on reduced graphene oxide (rGO) incorporated  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  ETL for perovskite solar cells on aluminum doped zinc oxide (AZO) substrate. AZO is abundant, environment friendly and low cost transparent conducting electrodes for optoelectronic devices. Nonetheless, perovskite solar cells based on AZO substrate is reported with relative low PCE of  $ \sim 13\% $  so far [20, 21], much lower than its ITO and FTO counterparts. The  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO were prepared through spin-coating  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  and rGO mixed solution on the AZO substrate and annealing at  $ 180^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  in air. The rGO possesses excellent charge transport properties, and rGO has proven compatibility with metal oxides [22-24]. However, it has been reported previously that the amount of rGO would affect the photocatalytic properties of the  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO suspension solution [25]. It is suspected that with the right amount of rGO in the composite layer, the rGO would enhance the electron transport behavior while constrain its photodegradation effect on the perovskite thin film. In this study, it is found that the addition of the right amount rGO in the  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  layer effectively reduce electron trap states and passivate the AZO substrate, making the device more stable under the continuous solar illumination compared to pristine  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ . The device based on the  $ 3\mathrm{wt}\% $  rGO mass loading in  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  ETL can reach a PCE of  $ 16.87\% $  with reduced hysteresis.

# 2. Experiment

The AZO glass ( $ R \approx 10 \Omega \mathrm{sq}^{-1} $ ,  $ T \approx 85\% $ ) was cleaned by deionized water, acetone, ethanol sequentially, and dried by nitrogen purging. Before depositing the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  layer, the AZO glass was treated by UV-Ozone for 10min. The  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  precursor was purchased from Alfa Aesar (tin oxide,  $ 15\% $  in  $ \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} $ ). The  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  precursor was diluted by  $ \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} $  to  $ 3\% $ . The rGO nanosheet (diameter  $ \approx 0.5 \sim 5 $  um, thickness  $ \approx 0.8 \sim 1.2 $  nm) was dissolved in GBL with 1mg/ml, and then mixed with  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  solution. The prepared suspension solution with different rGO mass percentage (1wt%, 3wt%, 5wt%) were added to the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  solution to form our final precursor. The mixed precursor is stable over weeks without any precipitation. The ETL precursor were spin-coated at 3000 rpm for 30s, followed by annealing at 180 □ for 30 min. The thickness of films were evaluated by using an alpha-step profiler, as shown in Figure S1, the average thickness of the resulted ETLs were  $ \sim 37 $  nm. The results show that the introduction of a small amount of rGO has insignificant influence on the thickness of perovskite films. For the perovskite layer, 159mg  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{I} $  and 461mg of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  were dissolved in 72 ml of DMSO and 640 ml of DMF solution. The perovskite solution was spin-coated on the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  transfer layer at 4000 rmp for 10 s, and then annealed at 70 □ for 10 min and at 100 □ for another 10 min. The HTM solution was preparation by dissolving 72.3mg Sprio-OMeTAD in 1mL

# 3. Results and discussion

Figure 1(a) shows the device structure of  $ \mathrm{AZO / SnO_2 - rGO / CH_3NH_3PbI_3 / Sprio - OMeTAD / Au} $ , and schematic view of  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO mixture. Figure 1(b) is a cross sectional SEM image illustrates the fabricated device. The perovskite layer consists of the compact microcrystals with the grain size of several hundred nanometers. A uniform  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO film of  $ \sim 30 $  nm forms at the bottom of the AZO substrate. In order to identify the impact of the rGO content on harvesting of the perovskite layer, the optical transmittance of  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  and  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO were measured as a function of rGO content as shown in figure S2. The transmittance decreased with increasing rGO content. It is concluded that excess rGO content would block the light absorption of  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ , and encourage electron-hole recombination [26]. Furthermore, we monitored the degradation of perovskite layers deposited on  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO. Specifically, optical transmittance spectra of perovskite thin films on  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO coated substrate were monitored against time in ambient environment with temperature of  $ 25^{\circ}\mathrm{C}^{\circ} $  and relative humidity of  $ 50\% $ . The results were presented in the Figure S3. Initially, the as deposited perovskite films exhibited black color (Figure S3e) and a significant light absorption edge at about  $ 780\mathrm{nm} $ . It is apparent that the color of the perovskite film on  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  layer become yellowish after  $ 120\mathrm{h} $ , revealing very poor stability of the perovskite on  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  substrate. In contrast, perovskite films with  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO ( $ 1\mathrm{wt}\% $ ) and  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO ( $ 5\mathrm{wt}\% $ ) shows a slower color changing rate. It is worth to note that only slight degradation can be observed for perovskite films deposited on  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO ( $ 3\mathrm{wt}\% $ ) after  $ 120\mathrm{h} $  (Figure S3e). To quantitative the degradation rate, we compared the light transmittance value at the  $ 800\mathrm{nm} $  ( $ \mathrm{T}_{800\mathrm{nm}} $ ) and the  $ 750\mathrm{nm} $  ( $ \mathrm{T}_{750\mathrm{nm}} $ ) near the perovskite bandgap absorption edge. The sample with  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO ( $ 3\mathrm{wt}\% $ ) shows the highest  $ \mathrm{T}_{800\mathrm{nm}} / \mathrm{T}_{750\mathrm{nm}} $  value against time, indicating perovskite thin film on  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO ( $ 3\mathrm{wt}\% $ ) has the best stability under testing condition among these samples (Figure S3f). In addition, the average surface roughness of these  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  films on the AZO substrates were also characterized by atomic force microscope (AFM), which is shown in figure S4. The  $ \mathrm{AZO / SnO_2} $ -rGO ( $ 3\mathrm{wt}\% $ ) surface shows the lowest RMS roughness ( $ \sim 58.1\mathrm{nm} $ ), which is lower than the  $ \mathrm{AZO / SnO_2} $  substrate surface. To obtain optimal doping concentration, different amount of rGO suspension solution, with different mass percent (0, 1, 3,  $ 5\mathrm{wt}\% $ ), were added into  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  solution (Figure 1(c)).

Figure 2(a) and 2(b) compares the photocurrent density-voltage (J-V) characteristics under AM1.5G illumination and the external quantum efficiencies of the champion perovskite solar cells with  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO (3wt%) and with pristine  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ , respectively. For the champion cells, the  $ \mathrm{J_{sc}} $  increases from 21.45 to  $ 22.57\mathrm{mA/cm^2} $ , and the FF increase from  $ 57\% $  to  $ 73\% $ , due to the

To further investigate the charge-transport kinetics at the  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO/MAPbI $ _3 $  interface, the steady-state PL spectra and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurement is employed. The PL spectra were collected from perovskite films fabricated on glass/ETLs in the figure 3(a), PL peak are observed about  $ 780\mathrm{nm} $  for both perovskite films with or without rGO. The  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO/MAPbI $ _3 $  film has a much lower PL quantum yield than the  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ /MAPbI $ _3 $  film, indicating a higher charge extraction rate across the interface between the  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO and MAPbI $ _3 $ . The time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) was measured, adopting the same structures as used for the PL measurements. The TRPL results of devices based on  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  and  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO ETL are presented in figure 3(b). The two TRPL curves can be fitted by a bi-exponential equation [27]: [28]:

$$
f (t) = A _ {1} e ^ {- t / \tau_ {1}} + A _ {2} e ^ {- t / \tau_ {2}} + B
$$

(1)

where the terms  $ \mathrm{A}_1 $ ,  $ \mathrm{A}_2 $  are the decay amplitude, B is the constant for the base-line offset,  $ \tau $  is the decay time. The decay portion including a fast decay  $ (\tau_{1}) $  component reflects the quenching of free carriers in the interface between the perovskite and ETL, and a slow decay  $ (\tau_{2}) $  component represents the recombination of free carriers in radiative channels [28]. The PL decay of the perovskite deposited on the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  film presents a fast decay lifetime  $ (\tau_{1}) $  of 31 ns and a slow decay lifetime  $ (\tau_{2}) $  of 6.4 ns. In contrast, for the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $ -rGO/MAPbI₃ film, the fast decay lifetime and the slow decay lifetime are both shortened, with extracted values of 23 ns and 5.2 ns respectively. A space-charge limited current (SCLC) measurements were also carried out to evaluate the electron mobility of   $  SnO_{2}  $  -rGO ETL, the results of which are shown in figure 3(c). We have fabricated device with   $  AZO/SnO_{2}  $  -rGO/MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  /PCBM/BCP/Au configuration for electron transport investigation. The I-V curves from electron-transport-only measurements can be catalogued into three regions according to the value of the exponent n [29], i.e. n=1 is the ohmic region, n=2 is the SCLC region, and the intermediate region between 1 and 2 is the trap-filled limited region. In the SCLC region, the electron mobility of the PSCs with   $  SnO_{2}  $  -rGO film can be evaluated on the basis of the Mott-Gurney law [29]

$$
J = \frac {9}{8} \varepsilon_ {0} \varepsilon \mu \frac {V ^ {2}}{d ^ {3}}
$$

(2)

where V is the applied voltage, d is the thickness of the ETLs,   $  \varepsilon  $   is the dielectric constant of the   $  SnO_{2}  $   (12.5) film and   $  \mu  $   is the electron mobility. The estimated electron mobility of   $  3.3 \times 10^{-3} \, cm^{2}V^{-1}s^{-1}  $   can be obtained from the Eq (2) from the device based   $  SnO_{2}  $  -rGO film, which is about 5 times higher than that of the   $  SnO_{2}  $   based device (  $  7 \times 10^{-4} \, cm^{2}V^{-1}s^{-1}  $  ). The magnitudes of our mobility values are in the same order to those reported in reference [30]. The SCLC results further confirms that that the photogenerated charge carriers are more efficiently transferred via the   $  SnO_{2}  $  -rGO ETL than the pristine   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL.

Dark current measure is a method to evaluate charge carrier loss through leakage pathways and the recombination of free carriers in devices. Figure 3(d) shows the dark I-V curves for the best PSCs with  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  and with  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $ -rGO, the J-V characteristics in dark can be described by the Shockley diode equation [31]:

$$
J = J _ {0} \left[ \exp \left(\frac {q V}{\mathrm{nKT}}\right) - 1 \right]
$$

(3)

where  $  \mathrm{J}_0  $  is the saturation current density, V the applied voltage, n the ideality factor, T the temperature. K the Boltzaman constant. The dark curves show typical diode properties, with a transition to the space charge limited regime at the build in voltage  $  (\mathrm{V_{bi}})  $ . The ideality factor can be determined from the slope of the exponential regime on the semi-logarithmic plot and can be denoted as:

$$
n = \left(\frac {k T}{q} \frac {\partial \ln J}{\partial V}\right) ^ {- 1}
$$

(4)

In the Eq. (4), n of the device with  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  has a value of 3.7, which is higher than that of the device with  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO of 2.7. n extracted value are significantly large than 1 for both devices, demonstrating that trap assisted recombination plays a mass of role in the recombination process. [30] However, PSC based  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO ETL showed an n value and a  $ R_{s} $  ( $ 1.17\Omega \mathrm{cm}^2 $ ) smaller with regard to the  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  ETL ( $ 8.7\Omega \mathrm{cm}^2 $ ). Suggesting a decreased trap state density at  $ \mathrm{AZO / SnO_2} $  interface when rGO is added to  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ . According to Eq. (3), it can be obtained that the  $ J_0 $  of the device without rGO is  $ 6.32\times 10^{-5}\mathrm{mA / cm}^2 $ , which is one order of magnitude large than that of the device with  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO is  $ 1.3\times 10^{-5}\mathrm{mA / cm}^2 $ . The resistance from lower frequency arc is related to the recombination resistance ( $ R_{\mathrm{rec}} $ ). The  $ R_{\mathrm{rec}} $  of the  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO device is 769.1 Ω, and the  $ R_{\mathrm{rec}} $  of the  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  device is 358.8 Ω. The calculated values of the charge-transfer resistance ( $ R_{\mathrm{tr}} $ ) using the inset equivalent circuit is 373.1 Ω for the device based on  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO, which is much lower than the device based on  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  with 710.4 Ω. The carrier diffusion length  $ L_{\mathrm{d}} $  can be estimated by [34]

$$
L _ {d} = (\frac {R _ {r e c}}{R _ {t r}}) ^ {\frac {1}{2}} L \tag {5}
$$

where  $  \mathrm{R_{rec}}  $  is the recombination resistance,  $  \mathrm{R_{tr}}  $  is the charge transfer resistance,  $  L  $  is the layer thickness. According to the equation (5), with similar  $  L  $  in the devices, the large  $  \mathrm{R_{rec}}  $  and small  $  \mathrm{R_{tr}}  $  in solar cell decrease the possibility for electron-hole recombination.

In order to confirm light stability, the light stability measurements on the perovskite solar cell were investigated. The J-V measure of PSC with  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO electron transfer layer under different continuous illumination time from 100 to 1000s were measured and presented in the figure 4(a). The  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  based PSC degrades with time rapidly, with PCE decreasing from  $ \sim 12\% $  to  $ 11\% $  within 1000s. The most prominent decay is obtained in the plot of  $ \mathrm{V_{oc}} $  (in the figure S7). In contrast, upon light exposure, the  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO device remains more stable with PCE above  $ 16\% $  for more than 1000s. The stability of devices was also tested as a function of storage time in the glove box without capsulation. The PCE is plotted independently in the figure 4(b) to reveal the stability of the devices clearly. The  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO device, the PCE keep  $ 80\% $  after 300h while PCE of the  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  device drops down to  $ < 50\% $ . It is worth noting that the stability of PSCs was tested under AM. 1.5G as light source. The results confirm that the  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO based PSCs is more stable than the  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  device.

The enhanced stability of PSC may be ascribed to the passivation effect of rGO at the interface. For example, the surface of the AZO and low temperature solution processed  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  contain many trap states originated from oxygen vacancies [35], which leads to decomposed of perovskite at the interface and increase carrier recombination. The rGO is suspected to distributed at the boundary of  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  nanocrystals and passivate the electron traps of the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  and the AZO surface.

In this study, we have synthesized a new way to improve the performance of Planar n-i-p PSCs by adding rGO of 3wt% into  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ . The addition of rGO resulted in a high efficiency of  $ 16.87\% $  with a very reduced hysteresis, and the devices deposited on the AZO substrate and treated below  $ 180^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . In addition, the increased  $ \mathrm{J_{sc}} $  of the device based on  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -rGO, which attributes to effective electron extraction from perovskite to AZO. On the other hand, the rGO doped  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  can effectively improve the device stability is due to the stabilization of  $ \mathrm{AZO / SnO_2} $  interface. The introduction of rGO in  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  is a simple and effective way to improve device performances.
