# Bifunctional Molecular Modification Improving Efficiency and Stability of Inverted Perovskite Solar Cells

Yanjun Zhang, Shasha Zhang, Shaohang Wu, Chuanliang Chen, Hongmei Zhu, Zhenzhong Xiong, Weitao Chen, Rui Chen, Shaoying Fang, and Wei Chen*

Kinds of interfacial engineering technologies are explored in perovskite solar cells (PSCs), which are proved to be benefit for the devices to achieve higher efficiency, less hysteresis, and/or better stability. In this work, in an inverted p–i–n structured PSC, a monolayer of 5-aminovaleric acid (5-AVA) molecules with bifunctional groups is introduced to modify the surface of p-NiMgLiO compact hole transport layer (HTL). This key surface modification is not only improving the resultant perovskite film's quality, but also facilitating efficient charge extraction at the HTL/perovskite interface, which result in improved power conversion efficiency (PCE) from 18.0 to 19.4%. Furthermore, the 5-AVA-modified device can withstand much higher poling bias (>1.8 V) for a longer time than the pristine one, showing little nonsteady state current and excellent consistency of forward and reverse photocurrent–voltage curves after high bias poling. Benefitting from the exceptionally stable interface, the 5-AVA-modified device shows much improved long-term stability, which can maintain over 90% of its initial PCE after 300 h continuous light soaking at maximum power point tracking condition.

# 1. Introduction

Organometal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted great attention in recent years, showing a rapid rise of their power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) from 3.8% to currently certified 22.7%.  $  ^{[1-8]}  $   In a typical PSC, the perovskite light-harvesting layer is often sandwiched between the electron transport layer (ETL) and hole transport layer (HTL), and the photogenerated carriers have to transfer across the perovskite/ETL and perovskite/HTL interfaces and transport through ETL and HTL to the electrodes. A high-quality perovskite/charge transport layer interface, which benefits effective charge extraction, is essential for PSC to achieve high PCE. Usually, effective charge extraction means fast charge transfer, limited charge accumulation, and slow charge recombination at the interface.  $  ^{[9-11]}  $

Recent literatures employed 3-aminopropanoic acid and diethanolamine to modify PEDOT:PSS and NiO, respectively, and demonstrated accelerated hole extraction after modification, which therefore led to smaller hysteresis and improved PCE of the devices.  $  ^{[12,20]}  $   However, the detailed mechanisms on how the surface modification effect on hysteresis and long-term stability have rarely been elucidated.

In comparison to PEDOT:PSS, the inorganic NiO is advantageous for its chemical stability and electron blocking capability thanks to its wide bandgap and high conduction band edge.  $  ^{[21]}  $   In our previous work, NiMgLiO was proved as an effective hole transport material with enhanced conductivity and more suitable band alignment with respect to perovskite.  $  ^{[6]}  $   In this work, we further employ an organic monolayer made of bifunctional 5-aminovaleric acid (5-AVA) molecules to modify the surface of NiMgLiO film. Carboxyl functional group of 5-AVA makes it firmly anchor onto the surface of NiMgLiO film, while the amino functional group can strongly coordinate with perovskite.  $  ^{[19]}  $   As expected, the 5-AVA molecular interlayer has strengthened both the electric and chemical interactions between NiMgLiO and perovskite, which not only enhance the device's efficiency but also improve its stability. Furthermore, the most interesting finding is that the 5-AVA-modified device could withstand higher bias (>1.8 V) poling for much longer time than the pristine one, which is important for the device's long-term stability under real working conditions.

# 2. Results and Discussion

Figure 1a illustrates the schematic diagram of the device architecture based on NiMgLiO with a 5-AVA modification layer. As seen, the carboxyl functional groups of 5-AVA molecules could be chemically anchored onto NiMgLiO through esterification with the oxide's surface hydroxyl groups, which has been widely reported in p-type dye-sensitized solar cells; while the amine functional groups could coordinate with the photoactive perovskite layer.[19,22] The Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra have been tested and the results are shown in Figure 1b. The absorption bands at 2923 and  $ 2973~\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $  can be assigned to the asymmetric and symmetric stretches of  $ -\mathrm{CH}_2 $  groups on the alkyl chains. The absorption band at  $ 1572~\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $  can be assigned to the bending modes of  $ -\mathrm{NH}_2 $  groups, and the absorption band at  $ 1403~\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $  in the low-frequency region can be assigned to the stretching of C-O groups.[16,23] These results demonstrate that the modification molecules have successfully absorbed onto the NiMgLiO's surface. Figure 1c shows the effect of 5-AVA modification on the NiMgLiO's surface hydrophilicity and the morphologies of the as-deposited perovskite films. As seen, the contact angles of water drops on the NiMgLiO films with and without 5-AVA modification are tested to be  $ 60.81^{\circ} $  and  $ 21.47^{\circ} $ , respectively, which indicate that the 5-AVA-modified NiMgLiO film has a better wetting property toward the polar  $ N,N $ -dimethylformamide/dimethyl sulfoxide (DMF/DMSO) solution of perovskite precursor. From the top-view scanning electron microscope (SEM) images, it can be seen that the average grain sizes of perovskite become relatively larger after 5-AVA modification (782 vs  $ 513~\mathrm{nm} $ ). This indicates that the 5-AVA modification can largely

influence the growth manner of perovskite crystals. Similar results have been reported in Guang et al.'s work, who employed 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane,   $  \mathrm{H_{2}N(CH_{2})_{3}Si(OC_{2}H_{5})}  $   (3-APTES) to modify   $  SnO_{2}  $   surface, the as-deposited   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   film also presented enhanced crystallinity.  $  ^{[24]}  $   The increased crystallinity associated with 5-AVA modification can be further demonstrated by the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns, which are shown in Figure 1d. The intensities of two strong peaks at   $  14.5^{\circ}  $   and   $  28.4^{\circ}  $ , which are assigned to the (110) and (220) peaks of perovskite, are obviously higher for the 5-AVA-modified sample than the pristine one.

To study the influences of surface modification on hole extraction kinetics at the interface of NiMgLiO/perovskite, we resorted to the steady-state and time-resolved photoluminescence (PL) measurements. From the steady-state PL spectra shown in Figure 2a, it is observed that the NiMgLiO/5-AVA/MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  film exhibits a stronger PL quenching than the NiMgLiO/MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  film. Their quenching ratios are calculated to be 92 and 79% according to the relative peak areas, indicating the enhanced interfacial hole injection efficiency after 5-AVA modification.  $  ^{[25,26]}  $  The transient PL spectra are presented in Figure 2b. The fitted PL lifetime (by the double exponential decay function) is reduced from 142.48 ns for the pristine perovskite film to 8.39 ns and 14.10 ns for the NiMgLiO/5-AVA/MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  sample and the NiMgLiO/MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  sample, respectively. The results further demonstrate that the surface modification of NiMgLiO with 5-AVA can accelerate hole injection from perovskite to NiMgLiO, in good agreement with the inference from the steady-state PL spectra.

Based on the above results, it can be judged that the 5-AVA modification layer can effectively improve hole extraction at the interface and suppress interfacial carrier recombination, leading to the improved performance. For the detailed reasons, three scenarios need be considered. First, the 5-AVA modification layer could improve the perovskite's crystallinity, and thus lower the defect density of the bulk film, which could result in a longer free carrier lifetime. Second, the 5-AVA modification layer could passivate surface traps of NiMgLiO and/or strengthen electric coupling between NiMgLiO and  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  by chemical coordination with both of them, leading to longer carrier lifetime and faster hole injection. Third, the energy level of NiMgLiO could be adjusted due to the chemical interaction between NiMgLiO and 5-AVA, making better hole extraction behavior. To explore the last scenario, the ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy measurement has been carried out, and

the results are presented in Figure 3a. It can be seen that after the 5-AVA modification, the work function (WF) of NiMgLiO slightly increases from 4.78 to 4.83 eV. This difference should be attributed to the decreased work function of NiMgLiO anchored by 5-AVA with terminal electron-donating amine groups.[16] In addition to the slight change in WF, the valence band of NiMgLiO is also slightly lowered from  $ -5.25 $  to  $ -5.30 $  eV after the 5-AVA modification, as shown in Figure 3b. These shifts in energy level could affect hole injection kinetics at the interface, leading to stronger PL quenching and faster PL decay.

First, 5-AVA-modified devices showed a high-quality perovskite film with larger grain size, which is benefit for suppressing ionic migration;  $  ^{[37]}  $  second, the 5-AVA-modified devices possess a much stable HTL/perovskite interface, which can suppress interfacial degradation, leading to improve the device stability.  $  ^{[38]}  $

# 3. Conclusion

In conclusion, we have introduced a monolayer made of bifunctional 5-AVA molecules to modify the interface between HTL (NiMgLiO) and perovskite  $ (\mathrm{MAPbI}_3) $ , and systematically studied its impacts on the device's performance and stability. It is suggested that the 5-AVA modification layer is benefit for the formation of perovskite film with higher crystallization, which also can help to passivation the surface traps of NiMgLiO and lead to a better band alignment at the HTL/perovskite interface. # 4. Experimental Section

Materials: All reagents and solvents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used as received without further purification unless otherwise statement. Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass (TEC-15, 15 Ω □⁻¹) was purchased from Nippon Sheet Glass Co., Japan. PbI₂ (99%) was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Japan. Methyl ammonium iodide was synthesized according to previously reported method.[48] PC₆₁BM (99.5%) was purchased from Lumtec Co., Taiwan. 5-5 AVA was purchased from Aladdin Co., China.

Device Fabrication: The FTO glass was sequentially cleaned with detergent, deionized water, alcohol, and acetone under sonication. The cleaned FTO glass was used to deposit a p-type NiMgLiO compact layer (  $  \approx  $  20 nm thick) by spray pyrolysis according to our previous work.  $  ^{[6]}  $   The prepared NiMgLiO was used to adsorb a monolayer of 5-AVA molecules by immersing in the water solution   $  (0.03 \times 10^{-3} \text{ M})  $   at room temperature for 12 h. After blowing dry with   $  N_{2}  $  , the perovskite layer was deposited

by spin-coating of the perovskite precursor solution at a rotation speed of 5000 rpm for 30 s, followed by quickly drop-casting diethyl ether (200 μL) as antisolvent within 10 s. After that, the films were annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. The perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving PbI₂ (1.5 M) and MAI with 1.05:1 molar ratio in a mixed solvent of DMF/DMSO (4:1, volume ratio). The PCBM layer was deposited on top by spin-coating its chlorobenzol solution (20 mg mL⁻¹) at the rotation speed of 1500 rpm for 30 s. The BCP layer was deposited on top by spin-coating its saturated methanol solution at the rotation speed of 1500 rpm for 30 s. Finally, a 90 nm thick Ag electrode was thermally evaporated under high vacuum (<5 × 10⁻⁴ Pa).

Characterization: FT-IR spectra were characterized on a VERTEX 70 Infrared Fourier transform microscope (Bruker Co., Germany). SEM of the perovskite films was obtained using a Nova Nano 450 scanning electron microscope (FEI Co., the Netherlands). XRD patterns were recorded using an X-ray diffractometer with a Cu Kα radiation source (PANalytical B.V. Co., the Netherlands). The steady-state PL spectra were recorded on an Edinburgh FLS920 fluorescence spectrometer (Edinburgh Co., UK). The time-resolved PL decays were measured at 650 nm using excitation with a 482 nm light pulse at a frequency of 4 MHz from the Horiba Jobin Yvon Fluoromax-4 fluorimeter. The UPS measurement was performed on an AXIS-ULTRA DLD-600W Ultra Spectrometer (Kratos Co. Japan). A solar simulator (Oriel, model 9119) with AM 1.5G filter (Oriel, model 91192) was used to give an irradiance of 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  . The light intensity was precisely calibrated with a standard Si photodiode detector. The active area of solar cell was determined to be 0.09 cm  $  ^{2}  $   by a black metal mask. IPCE was measured on a Newport IPCE system (Newport, USA). The long-term stability was measured with a CHI1000c multichannel electrochemistry workstation (Chenhua, Co., China) in a N  $  _{2}  $  -filled glovebox. The EIS measurements were carried out on an electrochemical workstation (Zahner, Zennium C-IMPS-1) in the frequencies ranging from 10  $  ^{6}  $   to 10  $  ^{-1}  $   Hz, applying 0.85 V voltage without illumination condition.
