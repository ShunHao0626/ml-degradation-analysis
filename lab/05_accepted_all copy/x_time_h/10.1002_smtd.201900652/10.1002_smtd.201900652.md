Inorganic perovskite   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   has been studied as a promising alternative light-absorbing material for photovoltaic application due to its suitable band gap for converting solar light and enhanced stability toward ambient conditions compared to the organic–inorganic halide perovskite. However, the photoactive   $  \alpha  $  -phase of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   can only be stabilized at a high temperature and the phase transition from   $  \alpha  $  -phase to   $  \delta  $  -phase easily occurs at room temperature. Herein, ytterbium (III) chloride (  $  YbCl_{3}  $  ) as a bifunctional additive is introduced into the perovskite precursor to stabilize the black   $  \alpha  $  -phase, and high-quality   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   films are obtained at a temperature as low as 80 °C.   $  Yb^{3+}  $   partly replaces   $  Pb^{2+}  $   to enhance the tolerance factor and favors the formation of   $  \alpha  $  -phase. The Lewis adduct complex of   $  YbCl_{3}\cdot DMSO  $   in the perovskite film can passivate the perovskite film with reduced defects and help enhance the stability of the   $  \alpha  $  -phase. The   $  YbCl_{3}  $  -modified planar-type   $  \alpha  $  -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite solar cells show a champion power conversion efficiency of 12.4% with an impressive stability at ambient conditions. The additive induced controlled crystallization provides a simple and promising way to improve the photovoltaic performance of inorganic perovskite solar cells.

# 1. Introduction

The most commonly used strategies to improve the phase stability can be classified into two categories. One is to tune the structure of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   with a modified tolerant factor.  $  ^{[17]}  $   Ho-Baillie and co-workers obtained low-temperature stable perovskite with a PCE of 13.5% by introducing   $  Ca^{2+}  $  .  $  ^{[18]}  $   Other divalent ions' doping such as   $  Mn^{2+}  $  ,   $  Sr^{2+}  $  , or   $  Sn^{2+}  $   has also been reported to stabilize the   $  \alpha  $  -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $  .  $  ^{[19,20]}  $   Zhang et al. added   $  Bi^{3+}  $   into   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   to partly replace   $  Pb^{2+}  $   to improve the phase stability at a low temperature and achieved a PCE of 13.21%.  $  ^{[21]}  $   The element doping can favor the formation of   $  \alpha  $  -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   but a high annealing temperature is still required and the stability needs to be further improved since most of the reported inorganic devices require an inert condition for stable operation. In a recent report, rare earth   $  Eu^{3+}  $  -doping can reduce the annealing temperature to 85 °C and improve the stability of   $  \alpha  $  -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   at ambient conditions, achieving a PCE of 6.8%.  $  ^{[22]}  $   The other is via controlling the free energy of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   crystal by reducing the grain size or passivating the perovskite surface.  $  ^{[23-25]}  $   Yin et al. demonstrated that poly-vinylpyrrolidone (PVP) has a strong integration with the inorganic perovskite and it can interrupt the periodic structure of perovskite lattice.  $  ^{[26]}  $   The resulted small grain size can contribute to the stabilization of perovskite. It is desirable to avoid use of complicated fabrication processing and develop facile methods for preparing high-quality   $  \alpha  $  -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   films with a stable pure phase.

Except for the phase stability that is a precondition for efficient devices, the morphology of the perovskite film also has a close connection to the photovoltaic performance.  $  ^{[29]}  $   A compact, smooth, and pinhole-free morphology can prevent the direct contact of the sandwiching transport layers and is in favor of the charge transport and recombination suppression. However, a concise control over the crystallization of inorganic perovskite is more difficult compared to the organic–inorganic perovskite.  $  ^{[30]}  $   The transition to black cubic phase of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   typically needs an annealing temperature above 330 °C and the perovskite film has a low coverage at such a high temperature as a result of the fast solvent evaporation. Therefore, it is necessary to reduce the temperature of phase transition to better control the crystallization of perovskite films. Most researchers introduce hydriodic acid-modified lead iodine (  $  PbI_{2}  $  ) to the precursor to reduce the phase transition temperature down to 150 °C and optimize the perovskite film to enhance device performance. This process is complex since the water in hydriodic acid solution should be totally removed.  $  ^{[31-33]}  $   Moreover, the defects at the grain boundaries and surface area of perovskite films may serve as carrier recombination centers, which will reduce the open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ) and fill factor (FF).  $  ^{[34-36]}  $   The defects with a high reactive activity can also accelerate the degradation of the perovskite film when exposed to humid atmosphere. Thus the defect engineering toward the perovskite films is critical to the performance of PSCs.  $  ^{[37]}  $

Herein, we propose a facile and efficient method for preparing high-quality   $  \alpha  $  -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   films with an improved stability and reduced trap density at a low temperature. By introducing a Lewis acid ytterbium (III) chloride (YbCl  $  _{3}  $  ) into the perovskite precursor, Yb partly substitutes Pb in the perovskite structure with an enhanced tolerant factor and   $  \alpha  $  -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   is obtained at a low temperature of 80 °C. Meanwhile, YbCl  $  _{3}  $   reacts with the Lewis base solvent dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to form an adduct complex (YbCl  $  _{3}  $  ·DMSO) in the precursor, which

passivates the perovskite film with reduced defects. The black phase perovskite films can be maintained at room temperature for more than 90 d. The   $  YbCl_{3}  $   modified inorganic PSCs deliver a champion PCE of 12.4% and 66% of its initial PCE is remained after storing at the ambient condition for 400 h. Compared with the previously reported   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film PSCs (Table S1, Supporting Information), our additive engineering strategy enables the inorganic PSCs with a comparable and even higher photovoltaic performance, low-temperature processing (the highest PCE below 100 °C annealing temperature), and impressive stability.

# 2. Result and Discussion

The perovskite precursor solutions with different   $  YbCl_{3}  $   molar ratios were prepared and the corresponding optical images are exhibited in Figure S1 (Supporting Information). The precursor solutions with a low   $  YbCl_{3}  $   concentration are still transparent, while a higher   $  YbCl_{3}  $   concentration of 8% and 10% leads to the formation of turbid precursors. The precursor solutions were spin-coated on the fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) substrates followed by heat treatment at different temperatures and the details can be found in the Experimental Section. After annealing at 80 °C, the resulted film without   $  YbCl_{3}  $   exhibits a yellow color, which suggests the existence of the δ-CsPbI  $  _{3}  $  . When the molar ratio of   $  YbCl_{3}  $   reaches 5%, a black mirror like film can be obtained, indicating the formation of α-CsPbI  $  _{3}  $  . It is noted that a higher annealing temperature can also lead to the formation of black phase with the addition of   $  YbCl_{3}  $   in the precursor as shown in Figure S2 (Supporting Information). Figure 1a shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of perovskite films with different   $  YbCl_{3}  $   contents annealed at 80 °C and the insets are the optical images of the corresponding perovskite films. The diffraction peaks at around 13° and 27° are indexed to the characteristic yellow phase, which exists in the annealed films without or with a small amount of   $  YbCl_{3}  $   doping (3%). The yellow phase disappears when the molar ratio of   $  YbCl_{3}  $   reaches 5% and the diffraction peaks of α-CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   at 14.2°, 20.6°, and 29° dominate the diffraction patterns. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) was used to characterize the composition of the precursor solutions and the perovskite films. As shown in Figure 3a, the pure DMSO has an S=O stretching vibration at 1045 cm  $  ^{-1}  $ , which agrees well with previous reports. It significantly shifts to 1010 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  when YbCl  $  _{3}  $  is added. YbCl  $  _{3}  $  is a typical Lewis acid with an unpaired electron in its 4f orbit and it can easily obtain an electron from a Lewis base. In the precursor that contains

DMSO and YbCl $ _3 $ , a Lewis adduct phase of YbCl $ _3 $ . DMSO is expected to form, which is confirmed by the shift of the S=O stretching vibration related with the bonding between YbCl $ _3 $  and DMSO. The XRD pattern of the YbCl $ _3 $ . DMSO film shows more peaks than YbCl $ _3 $  film, as shown in Figure S5 (Supporting Information). The former was obtained with a YbCl $ _3 $  precursor solution in DMSO while the latter was processed with a YbCl $ _3 $  aqueous solution. We infer that these peaks correspond to the YbCl $ _3 $ . DMSO. A similar Lewis acid base reaction is identified in the organic-inorganic perovskite precursor with the formation of the intermediate phase (PbI $ _2 $ . MAI. DMSO). $ ^{[38-40]} $  The FT-IR spectra of CsPbI $ _3 $  perovskite films with 5% YbCl $ _3 $  annealed at 80 and 150 °C are compared in Figure 3b. Interestingly, the perovskite film annealed at 80 °C shows an obvious stretching vibration at 1010 cm $ ^{-1} $ , while it disappears in the 150 °C annealed sample. The result indicates that the thin layer at the grain boundary of perovskite films with 5% YbCl $ _3 $  annealed at 80 °C is composed of the YbCl $ _3 $ . To further estimate the influence of the adduct complex on the optoelectronic property of the perovskite films, we measured the space charge limiting current (SCLC) of an electron-only structure with an architecture of   $  FTO/TiO_{2}/Perovskite/PCBM/Ag  $   based on perovskite films anneal at 80 and 150 °C. The voltage and current have a linear relationship at a low bias voltage. In the intermediate region that represents the trap filling process, the increase of current injection is followed by the increase of bias voltage. The kink point between the ohmic-type response and the trap-filling process defines the trap-filling limit voltage (  $  V_{TFL}  $  ). When other conditions are similar, the trap density has a positive relationship with the   $  V_{TFL}  $  . Figure 3c shows the   $  V_{TFL}  $   of the 80 °C annealed perovskite film is 0.36 V, which is smaller than that of the 150 °C annealed sample (Figure 3d).

X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) were performed to study the perovskite films based on different concentrations of   $  YbCl_{3}  $  . Without adding   $  YbCl_{3}  $  , the Cs 3d spectra show two peaks at   $  \approx737.5  $   and 723 eV (Figure 4a), and two peaks appear at   $  \approx138  $   and 143 eV in Pb 4f spectra (Figure 4b).  $  ^{[21]}  $   Both the peaks shift to a higher binding energy when the   $  YbCl_{3}  $   concentration increases to 3% because of the formation of the Yb–I bond. The peaks gradually return to a lower binding energy with the

further increase of the   $  YbCl_{3}  $   concentration. No difference of the peak position can be found between the reference and the sample with 10%   $  YbCl_{3}  $ . All the   $  YbCl_{3}  $ -containing samples show a characteristic peak of Yb at the   $  \approx186  $   eV (Figure 4c).  $  ^{[42]}  $   As the increase of   $  YbCl_{3}  $   concentration, the Yb peak gradually shifts toward a lower energy, which suggests the decreased density of electron. Figure S8 (Supporting Information) shows the solid-state   $  {}^{133}Cs  $   MAS nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) of the perovskite films with or without 5%   $  YbCl_{3}  $ . The typical peak of the Yb-doped perovskite film has a 2 ppm shift compared to the corresponding peak of undoped perovskite, which is attributed to the hyperfine interaction of Yb 4f electrons with cesium at an atomic level.  $  ^{[43]}  $   The atomic ratios of the perovskite films with different   $  YbCl_{3}  $   by XPS are listed in Table S3 (Supporting Information). For the reference film without   $  YbCl_{3}  $ , the content of Pb is similar to the Cs, while the content of Pb is lower than Cs for the sample with 3%   $  YbCl_{3}  $   because of the substitution of Pb with Yb and the ratio of   $  (\mathrm{Yb}+\mathrm{Pb})/\mathrm{Cs}  $   is close to unit. However, the ratio of Pb and Cs becomes almost the same if 8% or 10%   $  YbCl_{3}  $   is introduced. The formation of the complex requires the   $  YbCl_{3}  $   molecule to form an adduct phase with DMSO,  $  ^{[40]}  $   while the Yb doping needs to break down the ionic bonding between Yb and Cl.  $  ^{[44]}  $   Collectively, the above results indicate that the formation of Lewis adduct phase dominates for a high   $  YbCl_{3}  $   concentration in the precursor, while a lower   $  YbCl_{3}  $   concentration may favor the   $  Yb^{3+}  $   doping in the final perovskite films. For a moderate level of   $  YbCl_{3}  $   in the precursor, both the   $  Yb^{3+}  $   doping and the existence of adduct phase can be simultaneously realized in the resulted perovskite film. # 3. Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrate that   $  YbCl_{3}  $   as a Lewis acid additive in the perovskite precursor can induce the formation of high-quality inorganic perovskite films. Yb partly replaces the Pb to increase the tolerance factor of perovskite structure and stabilize the black phase of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $  .   $  YbCl_{3}  $   can form an adduct complex with DMSO to passivate the perovskite film with reduced trap density. The modified perovskite films lead to an impressive PCE for inorganic PSCs with improved stability, which can operate in ambient conditions for 400 h. The additive engineering in the precursor may contribute to the further development of inorganic PSCs for obtaining high-performance devices.

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials: PbI $ _2 $ , cesium iodide (CsI), and 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-dip-methoxyphenylamine)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. China. YbCl $ _3 $ , N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), chlorobenzene, and DMSO were purchased from Alfa Aesar. Titanium tetrachloride (TiCl $ _4 $ ) solution (99%) was purchased from Aladdin. Bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide lithium (Li-TFSI) and bis-(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide cobalt (Co-TFSI) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Device Fabrication: The fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) substrate was ultrasonically cleaned with alcohol and deionized water for three times. The dry FTO was immersed into   $  TiCl_{4}  $   aqueous solution (0.1 M) and heated in an oven at 70 °C for 1 h. The   $  TiO_{2}  $   coated FTO was annealed at 150 °C for 20 min to form the compact   $  TiO_{2}  $  . The perovskite precursor was prepared by dissolving 1 M   $  PbI_{2}  $  , 1 M CsI, and different amounts of   $  YbCl_{3}  $   (0%, 3%, 5%, 8%, 10%) into the mixture solvent of DMF and DMSO with a volume

ratio of 1:1. Then the precursor was magnetically stirred at 70 °C for 18 h and the perovskite precursor was filtered by a 0.45 μm filter maintaining stirring before use. 50 μL perovskite precursor with different YbCl₃ was directly spin coated onto the FTO/TiO₂ substrate (after ultraviolet-ozone treatment) at 2000 rpm with accelerated speed of 1000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 80, 150, or 350 °C for 5, 2, or 5 min, respectively. The Spiro-OMeTAD solution was prepared by dissolving 72.3 mg Spiro-OMeTAD in 1 mL chlorobenzene. After that, 29 μL 4-tert-butylpyridine, 18 μL Li-TFSI (520 mg mL⁻¹ in acetonitrile), and 29 μL Co-TFSI (300 mg mL⁻¹ in acetonitrile) were added. This mixed solution was stirred for 3 h before use. After the perovskite film cooled down to room temperature, 35 μL Spiro-OMeTAD was spin coated onto the perovskite film at 2000 rpm for 30 s. At last, 100 nm Ag was deposited by thermal evaporation.

Characterizations: The XRD patterns of all the samples were characterized by X-ray diffractometer (D/MAX-III-B-40 KV, Cu Kα radiation,   $  \lambda = 0.15418  $   nm, X-Pert PRO MPD, Panalytical, Holland). Fourier infrared spectrometer (NICOLET 6700, BRUKER, Hong Kong) was used to characterize the YbCl₃·DMSO adduct in 4000–600 cm⁻¹ range and KBr pellet was used for the powered samples of YbCl₃·DMSO adduct. The morphology of samples was checked by field-emission SEM (Hitachi SU8010, Japan). The photocurrent–voltage (J-V) curves of perovskite solar cells were measured by using a Keithley 2400 Source Meter under AM 1.5G at room temperature. The X-ray photoelectron spectrum was measured by X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The solid state   $  {}^{133}  $  Cs MAS NMR was conducted by AVANCEIII/WB-400 (BRUKER, Switzerland) and 100 mg perovskite powered was used for measurement. The absorption spectra were recorded by a UV–vis spectrophotometer (UV-3600, Shimadzu, Japan). For the aging test, the devices were stored in a sealed container in a dark condition with a humidity of around 25%. The IPCE curves were tested with a Newport QE 200 system. The EIS was measured by an electrochemical workstation (Autolab, PGSTAT 302N, Switzerland) under white light illumination at 0 V bias voltage with an alternative signal amplitude of 5 mV in the frequency range of 800–0.001 kHz.
