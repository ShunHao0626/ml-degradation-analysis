# Surface Treatment of Perovskite Layer with Guanidinium Iodide Leads to Enhanced Moisture Stability and Improved Efficiency of Perovskite Solar Cells

Rohit D. Chavan, Daniel Prochowicz, Mohammad Mahdi Tavakoli, Pankaj Yadav, and Chang Kook Hong*

Interfacial engineering between the perovskite and hole transport layers has emerged as an effective way to improve perovskite solar cell (PSC) performance. A variety of organic halide salts are developed to passivate the traps and enhance the charge carrier transport. Here, the use of guanidinium iodide (Gual) for interfacial modification of mixed-cation  $ (\mathrm{Cs})_x(\mathrm{FA})_{1 - x}\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  perovskite films, which results in the formation of a low-dimensional  $ \delta \mathrm{-FAPbI}_3 $ -like phase on the 3D perovskite surface, is reported. The presence of this thin layer facilitates charge transfer at interfaces and reduces charge carrier recombination pathways as evidenced by enhanced carrier lifetimes and favorable interfacial band alignment. As a result, the power conversion efficiency of the control device is boosted from  $ 19.22\% $  to  $ 20.07\% $ , mainly due to improved open-circuit voltage  $ (V_{\mathrm{oc}}) $  and fill factor. Furthermore, the post-treatment with Gual improves the moisture stability of perovskite polycrystalline films and ambient stability of PSCs.

Compositional engineering by forming 3D/2D  $  ^{[19-21]}  $  or 3D/1D  $  ^{[22]}  $  heterojunctions has been regarded as efficient method for mitigating the nonradiative recombination and improving the optoelectronic properties of the PSCs. The deposition of a bulky and hydrophobic organic cation on the top of the 3D perovskite surface may induce in situ growth of a wide bandgap 2D or 1D perovskites acting as a protecting capping layer. A number of aliphatic and aromatic ammonium cations, e.g., butylammonium,  $  ^{[23]}  $  n-propylammonium,  $  ^{[24]}  $  1,1,1-trifluoroethyl ammonium,  $  ^{[25]}  $  ethylammonium,  $  ^{[26]}  $  2-(4-fluorophenyl)ethylammonium,  $  ^{[27]}  $  cyclopropylammonium,  $  ^{[28]}  $  ammonium valeric acid,  $  ^{[29]}  $  phenylethylammonium,  $  ^{[30-32]}  $  and pentafluorophenylethylammonium  $  ^{[33]}  $  have been utilized as additives to passivate the 3D perovskite surface and improve the ambient stability. The dimensionally graded perovskite layer advantageously changes the interface energy level and reduces the charge recombination at an interface facilitating the interfacial charge transfer.  $  ^{[30]}  $

In other relevant works, several 2D layered lead halide perovskite compounds comprising mixtures of guanidinium with formamidine[39] or cesium cations[40] have been reported.

The compositional engineering of mixed-cation and mixed-halide perovskite compositions with guanidinium iodide (Gual) improved the device performance due to high values of open-circuit voltage   $  (V_{\mathrm{oc}})  $   and reduced the interfacial charge recombination.  $  ^{[41-46]}  $   Notably, these studies investigate the effect of guanidinium cation on the photovoltaic performance by mixing all perovskite precursors together. In turn, Luo et al. reported on the use of guanidinium bromide, which forms a wide bandgap over layer at the interface between the perovskite/hole transport layer (HTL) and consequently improves the value of   $  V_{oc}  $  .  $  ^{[47]}  $

In this work, we report a method for interfacial modification of 3D mixed-cation  $ (\mathrm{Cs})_x(\mathrm{FA})_{1 - x}\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  perovskite using GuaI to reduce trap states and charge carrier recombination pathways. After proper optimization, we find that deposition of GuaI film followed by heat treatment causes the formation of a low-dimensional  $ \delta \text{-FAPbI}_3 $ -like phase on the 3D perovskite surface, which was beneficial in terms of enhancement photovoltaic performance and ambient stability of PSCs. The morphological and optoelectronic characterizations demonstrate that post-treatment with GuaI increases the perovskite films quality by suppressing the charge recombination and increasing carrier lifetime. Consequently, the resultant mesoscopic solar cells exhibit a superior performance with a maximum efficiency of  $ 20.07\% $  and an open-circuit voltage of  $ 1.165\mathrm{V} $  compared to the  $ 19.22\% $  and  $ 1.119\mathrm{V} $  of the unmodified reference device. Moreover, the applied surface passivation method improves the moisture stability of perovskite polycrystalline films and ambient stability of PSCs.

We first evaluated the quality of perovskite films after GuaI post-treatment using thermal annealing at 100 °C for 10 min (referred to as GuaI_100 °C) and with no additional annealing process (referred to as GuaI_RT). The analysis of morphology of these perovskite films measured by scanning electron microscope (SEM) reveals that GuaI post-treatment does not modify the morphology of the surface, which exhibits a similar uniform and pinhole-free texture with average domain size ≈1 μm (Figure 1a). However, the GuaI_100 °C absorber layer has smoother surface with a smaller root-mean square surface roughness of about 17.4 nm compared to reference (24.6 nm) and GuaI_RT (28.1 nm) films as evidenced by atomic force microscopy (AFM) images (Figure S2, Supporting Information). The large RMS of GuaI_RT could be attributed to the formation of unsatisfactory GuaI crystals on the perovskite surface, which increase the roughness of the resulting film. The perovskite layer with low roughness was found to suppress charge recombination and improve charge transport at interface.[15,51] To gain insight into the changes in perovskite structure and composition, powder X-ray diffraction (pXRD), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses are carried out on the investigated samples. Figure 1b shows the pXRD patterns of the reference, GuaI_RT and GuaI_100 °C films coated on glass substrates. In the reference film, the intensive peaks located at ≈14.0° and ≈28.0° originates from the (101) and (202) planes of the 3D perovskite phase, while the small peak at 12.7° indicates the presence of PbI₂ phase, as a result of the decomposition of a small amount of perovskite during annealing at high temperature.[49] After GuaI post-treatment without thermal annealing the diffraction peaks remains the same as for the reference film, suggesting the existence of GuaI crystals itself on the surface.[50] In turn, when the GuaI/IPA solution was coated on the reference film and annealed at 100 °C, the peak corresponding to PbI₂ disappeared likely owing to the reaction of GuaI with remnant PbI₂. A new diffraction peak appeared at 11.8°, which might be related to the low-dimensional perovskite phase. To clarify the nature of the formed capping layer, we prepared the reference films of 1D GuaPbI₃ and 2D Gua₂PbI₄ and collected the pXRD patterns (Figure 1b). We found that the characteristic low-angle diffraction peaks of GuaPbI₃ (main peak at 8.5°) and Gua₂PbI₄ (main peak at 6.7°) are not observed in GuaI_100 °C film indicating that in this case these compositions are not formed. Moreover, there is no obvious shift in characteristic perovskite peak at ≈14.0° in GuaI_100 °C film, which indicates that Gua cation is not incorporated into 3D FA-based perovskite crystal lattice (Figure 1c). To study the role of GuaI-additive and shed more light on the potential mechanism for the observed PV enhancement, we performed ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) for perovskite film without and after GuaI treatment (Figure 4a,b). From the UPS results, the Fermi levels and valence bands maximum were estimated to be -4.62 and -5.77 eV for reference and -4.87 and -5.65 eV for GuaI_100 °C films, respectively. By considering the optical bandgap from UV–vis spectra (Figure 2a) and the valence bands, we calculate the position of conduction bands, which are -4.24 and -4.11 eV for the reference and GuaI_100 °C samples, respectively. Using these results with existing data,[60] we plotted the band diagram of both perovskite solar cells as shown in Figure 4c. Our results reveal that the GuaI-treated perovskite layer plays two critical roles at the interface of perovskite/HTL. This modification not only facilitates the hole transfer from perovskite layer to HTL, but also decreases the possibility of electron–hole recombination at the interface, where the holes located at the highest occupied molecular orbital level of PTAA can potentially recombine with the electrons at the conduction band of perovskite film. This improvement is mainly due to 130 meV band offset between the reference and GuaI_100 °C perovskite. Additionally, the reference perovskite has slightly deeper valence band in compared to that of GuaI-treated perovskite. Therefore, GuaI treatment can make a smooth and desirable path for the holes transferred from the perovskite layer to HTL. These results are in consistent with the TRPL data and are the main reason for observed higher  $  V_{OC}  $  in the modified devices.

# 3. Conclusion

In conclusion, we have demonstrated the method for surface engineering of mixed-cation   $  (\mathrm{Cs})_{x}(\mathrm{FA})_{1-x}\mathrm{PbI}_{3}  $   perovskite films through post-treatment with GuaI. It was found that low-dimensional   $  \delta-FAPbI_{3}  $  -like phase containing small amount of Gua cations was in situ formed on the top of 3D perovskite surface providing better film quality with longer carrier lifetime and favorable interfacial band alignment as compared to reference films. Our results showed that newly formed interface thin layer effectively passivates interface traps/defects on the perovskite surface and/or at grain boundaries reducing nonradiative recombination rather than changes the bulk properties or film morphology (grain size). Consequently, a high PCE of 20.07% (stabilized at 19.73%) along with negligible hysteresis was achieved for the optimized GuaI-treated device. Moreover, the GuaI-treated device also shows improvement in moisture stability than the control cell retaining 95% of its initial PCE over 180 h storage in 40% relative humidity. These results highlight the importance of perovskite surface engineering for developing highly efficient and stable PSCs.

N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%), acetonitrile (99.8%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.9%), anhydrous toluene (99.9%), zinc powder (Zn), hydrochloric acid (HCl, AR, 35–37%), titanium(IV) isopropoxide (99.9%), guanidinium hydroiodide (Gual, 99%), cesium chloride (CsCl, 99%), 4-tert-butylpyridine (TBP, 96%), lithium bis-(trifluoromethylsulphonyl) imide (Li-TFSI, 95%), lead iodide (PbI  $  _{2}  $  , 99.9%), and PTAA were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The TiO  $  _{2}  $   paste (18 NRD) was purchased from Dyesol and formamidinium iodide (FAI, Great-Cell,99.9%).

Device Fabrication: Perovskite solar cells were fabricated on patterned FTO conducting glass. The FTO substrates was ultrasonically cleaned in a soap solution deionized water, acetone and isopropyl alcohol for 20 min, sequentially. The c-TiO  $  _{2}  $  film (20 nm) was spin-coated on cleaned FTO substrate as previously reported in the literature.  $  ^{[49]}  $  The ms-TiO  $  _{2}  $  diluted solution (1:6 weight ratio paste:ethanol) was spin-coated on the c-TiO  $  _{2}  $  film at 5000 rpm for 30 s and sintered at 450 °C for 30 min, after cooling substrates were transport to a nitrogen-filled glovebox for perovskite deposition. A perovskite film composition of FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  with 10 mol% CsCl was prepared by dissolving PbI  $  _{2}  $  (1 mmol; 0.461 g), FAI (1 mmol, 0.172 g), and CsCl (0.1 mmol, 0.016 g) in 1 mL anhydrous mixture of DMF/DMSO (4:1; v:v) at 60 °C for 12 h. The perovskite solution was spin-coated onto the ms-TiO  $  _{2}  $  films at 1000 and 6000 rpm for 10 and 20 s, respectively. Then, 100 μL of chlorobenzene as an antisolvent was dropped on the substrate 10 s prior to the end of the deposition process. Substrates were annealed at 150 °C for 30 min to crystallize the 3D (Cs)  $  _{x}  $  (FA)  $  _{1-x}  $  PbI  $  _{3}  $  perovskite followed by removal of excess FACl.  $  ^{[49]}  $  After cooling down the temperature, Gual in IPA

(1, 2 or 3 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was deposited on the top of surface at 3000 rpm for 30 s. Next, the film was annealed at 100 °C for 10 min or further processed without thermal annealing. PTAA was deposited on top of the perovskite film at 4000 rpm for 30 s. The solution of PTAA was prepared by dissolving PTAA in toluene (15 mg/1.5 mL) and adding 15 μL solution of Li-TFSI in acetonitrile (170 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) and 7.5 μL TBP. Finally, 80 nm of gold electrode was evaporated as a counter electrode using a shadow mask. The active area of this electrode was fixed at 0.09 cm  $  ^{2}  $  .
