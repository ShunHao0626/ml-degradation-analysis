# Suppressing the Phase Segregation with Potassium for Highly Efficient and Photostable Inverted Wide-Band Gap Halide Perovskite Solar Cells

Jiwei Liang, Cong Chen, Xuzhi Hu, Zhiliang Chen, Xiaolu Zheng, Jing Li, Haibing Wang, Feihong Ye, Meng Xiao, Zhengyi Lu, Yuhao Xu, Shunping Zhang, Rui Yu, Chen Tao,  $  ^{*}  $   and Guojia Fang  $  ^{*}  $

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Wide-band gap (WBG) mixed-halide perovskites have drawn much attention because of their excellent optoelectronic properties and the potential to be deployed in tandem solar cells. Nevertheless, the bromine incorporation inevitably leads to photoinduced phase segregation in WBG mixed-halide perovskites. Herein, potassium is used to effectively suppress photoinduced phase segregation, which is visualized with confocal photoluminescence microscopy imaging. Strikingly, the potassium passivation not only inhibits the formation of the narrow-band gap subphase but also enhances the crystallinity of the WBG mixed-halide perovskite. In addition, the potassium-passivated WBG perovskite exhibits lower defect density, longer charge carrier lifetime, and better photostability. As a result, the optimized KI (2 mol %)-passivated WBG perovskite solar cells (PSCs) deliver a champion power conversion efficiency of 18.3% with negligible hysteresis. They maintain 98% of their initial efficiency after 400 h under   $  100 \, mW \cdot cm^{-2}  $   white light illumination in nitrogen.

KEYWORDS: inverted perovskite solar cell, wide band gap, phase segregation suppression, potassium passivation, photostability

# INTRODUCTION

In the past decade, organic–inorganic halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been unprecedentedly developed with a certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) over 25%.  $  ^{1-6}  $   This stems from the outstanding optoelectronic properties of perovskite absorbers such as high absorption coefficient, long carrier diffusion length, and low carrier recombination.  $  ^{7-13}  $   In particular, the tunable band gaps of perovskites make them appealing as the front light absorber in tandem solar cells to overcome the Shockley–Queisser limit.  $  ^{14-27}  $   For example, mixed-halide wide-band gap (WBG) (1.6–1.8 eV) perovskites can potentially combine with a narrow band gap semiconductor such as silicon (Si) or copper indium gallium selenide (CIGS) to construct the tandem configuration.  $  ^{28,29}  $   Unfortunately, the mixed-halide WBG perovskites suffer from undesired phase segregation, which leads to phase instability and thus severely impede the development of perovskite-based tandem solar cells.  $  ^{30-33}  $

Understanding the intrinsic origins of phase segregation in mixed-halide perovskites has crucial significance for achieving high-performance stable PSCs. # RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra of WBG perovskite films illuminated with a  $ 485~\mathrm{nm} $  laser are shown in Figure 1a,b. The pristine WBG perovskite film shows a PL peak at  $ 728~\mathrm{nm} $ , which is consistent with the band gap of the

$ \mathrm{FA}_{0.8}\mathrm{Cs}_{0.2}\mathrm{Pb(I_{0.7}Br_{0.3})_3} $  perovskite absorber. The ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption spectra of  $ \mathrm{FA}_{0.8}\mathrm{Cs}_{0.2}\mathrm{Pb(I_{0.7}Br_{0.3})_3} $  with various KI contents are shown in Figure S1. The band gap of the pristine  $ \mathrm{FA}_{0.8}\mathrm{Cs}_{0.2}\mathrm{Pb(I_{0.7}Br_{0.3})_3} $  is calculated to be approximately  $ 1.71\mathrm{eV} $ , as shown in the inset of Figure S1. However, the potassium-passivated WBG perovskite films exhibit a slight red shift, which may be ascribed to the extra iodine from KI. In addition, we observe that the pristine WBG perovskite obviously appears a vice-PL peak at  $ 789~\mathrm{nm} $  with increasing intensity dependence on the KI content (Figure 1a). The  $ 789~\mathrm{nm} $  PL peak corresponds to the  $ 1.57\mathrm{eV} $  optical band gap of  $ \mathrm{FA}_{0.8}\mathrm{Cs}_{0.2}\mathrm{PbI}_3 $ . This is consistent with the previous reports that the photoinduced halide migration leads to the formation of iodide-rich domains in mixed-halide perovskites. In our case, it is  $ \mathrm{FA}_{0.8}\mathrm{Cs}_{0.2}\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  with  $ 1.57\mathrm{eV} $  band gap. Compared to the pristine one, the potassium-passivated WBG perovskite film does not show any vice-PL peaks under continuous light illumination, which indicates that the addition of the potassium indeed suppresses the halide migration. To verify the suppression of phase segregation originating from the potassium cation, we next measured the PL spectra of the potassium chloride (KCl)-passivated WBG perovskite film under the same condition. To examine the localized distribution of phase segregation in WBG perovskites, confocal PL microscopy imaging measurements (Figure 1c–f) were carried out. As shown in Figure 1c, it is clear that the pristine WBG perovskite film has spots with the PL emission window of   $  785 \pm 5  $   nm after 2 min light exposure. These spots correspond to perovskite polycrystalline with 1.57 eV band gap. From the PL image of the pristine WBG perovskite, we can clearly see that the PL emission at 785 nm is nonuniform, which indicates composition heterogeneity over the perovskite film as a result of the phase segregation. Under the same condition, the potassium-passivated one, however, hardly exhibits PL emission at   $  \sim785  $   nm (Figure 1d), which means that potassium suppresses halide phase segregation and hence the absence of the narrow-band gap iodide-rich domains. Considering the radius of potassium ions, it is impossible for potassium to enter into the 3D perovskite lattices. It might exist as a mixture of KI, KBr, or nonperovskite mixed phases in the film.  $  ^{48,49}  $   To examine the influence of continuous light illumination on WBG perovskite films, we measured other PL emission windows at 730 nm for both pristine (Figure 1e) and potassium-passivated (Figure 1f) WBG perovskite films. Compared to the pristine one, the

potassium-passivated WBG perovskite shows obviously enhanced PL intensity.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were performed to investigate the crystal structures of pristine and potassium-passivated WBG perovskite films. As shown in Figure 2a, both perovskite films show identical diffraction angles, which implies the absence of additional phases despite the incorporation of KI. Subsequently, we examine the evolution of the lattice structure of these two WBG perovskite films before and after continuous light illumination. As shown in Figure 2b, the main diffraction peaks of the pristine WBG perovskite film slightly shifted to lower angles after continuous light illumination. For example, the diffraction angles at 41.5 and 44.2° corresponding to (022) and (212) planes, respectively, shift to 41.4 and 44.1°, as shown in the enlarged pattern in Figure 2d. This shift is consistent with the appearance of the narrow-band gap phase that results from the halide migration. Compared to the pristine one, the potassium-passivated WBG perovskite film does not exhibit any diffraction angle shift after illumination (Figure 2c). Meanwhile, from the enlarged diffraction patterns in the bottom of Figure 2d, the light illumination scarcely induces lattice expansion in the potassium-passivated WBG perovskite film.

To evaluate the influence of KI incorporation on defect passivation in the mixed-halide WBG perovskite, we measured the steady-state PL spectra of the pristine and potassium-passivated WBG perovskite films, as shown in Figure 5a. The potassium-passivated WBG perovskite film exhibits stronger PL emission intensity at  $ 728~\mathrm{nm} $  and suppressed emission peak at  $ 789~\mathrm{nm} $  compared to the pristine one, which suggests that the potassium effectively enhances the crystalline quality and

lowers the defect density. The time-resolved PL spectra, as shown in Figure 5b, give rise to the average carrier lifetime of 69 and 209 ns for the pristine and passivated WBG perovskite films, respectively. This says that KI effectively reduces nonradiative recombination.

$$
E _ {\mathrm{loss}} = k _ {\mathrm{B}} T \times \ln \left(\frac {N _ {\mathrm{c}} \cdot N _ {\mathrm{v}}}{n _ {\mathrm{e}} \cdot n _ {\mathrm{h}}}\right)
$$

$$
\frac {\mathrm{d} n _ {\mathrm{e}}}{\mathrm{d} t} = G - R = 0 \Rightarrow n _ {\mathrm{e}} \approx G \tau_ {\mathrm{SRH}}
$$

Introducing the potassium leads to the excellent enhancement of the performance from 16.7 to 17.8% because of the increase in   $  V_{OC}  $   and FF.

It is well-known that the incorporation of additional ions in perovskite films introduces additional trap states that might result in nonradiative recombination. Therefore, we measure the trap density of perovskite films within the single-carrier device in the dark using the space-charge limited current (SCLC) technique, as shown in Figure 5c. The process of trap-filling comprises the following three regions: the Ohmic one, the trap-filling limited one, and the SCLC one. The carriers are injected into the perovskite film in the trap-filling stage at the ohmic region and then the traps are continuously filled until the bias voltage reaches to the trap-filled limit voltage (  $  V_{TFL}  $  ). The trap density of the perovskite film can be determined using the following equation  $  ^{11}  $

$$
V _ {\mathrm{TFL}} = \frac {e n _ {\mathrm{trap}} L ^ {2}}{2 \varepsilon_ {0} \varepsilon}
$$

(3)

where e is the elementary charge, L is the thickness of the perovskite film (400 nm),   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   is the permittivity of vacuum (  $  \varepsilon_{0} = 8.8 \times 10^{-12}  $   F·m  $  ^{-1}  $  ), and   $  \varepsilon  $   is the relative dielectric constant. We estimated the relative dielectric constant to be 62.23 from the previous literature.  $  ^{[11]}  $   The value of   $  n_{trap}  $   reduces from   $  8.45 \times 10^{15}  $   to   $  6.42 \times 10^{15}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $   with the incorporation of KI. Furthermore, we believe that the decreased trap density stems from the higher quality of the WBG perovskite film with suppressed phase segregation by KI passivation. In addition, the reduced   $  n_{trap}  $   contributes to the decrease in carrier recombination, the prolonged carrier lifetime within perovskite films, and the elevated device performance. # CONCLUSIONS

The confocal PL microscopy images indicated that the potassium suppressed the formation of the iodide-rich phase   $  FA_{0.8}Cs_{0.2}PbI_{3}  $   perovskite. # EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

WBG Perovskite Precursor Preparation. The   $  FA_{0.8}Cs_{0.2}Pb(I_{0.7}Br_{0.3})_{3}  $   perovskite precursor solution was prepared by mixing 0.594 mmol lead iodide (  $  PbI_{2}  $  , purity > 99.9985%, TCI), 0.486 mmol lead bromine (  $  PbBr_{2}  $  , purity > 99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), 0.864 mmol formamidinium iodide (FAI, purity > 99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich), 0.216 mmol cesium iodide (CsI, purity > 99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich), and 0.02 mmol additional lead thiocyanate (  $  Pb(SCN)_{2}  $  , purity > 99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich) in 1 mL of mixed solvent of 0.8 mL of N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, purity > 99.8%, Alfa Aesar) and 0.2 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, purity > 99.8%, Alfa Aesar). In addition, 1 mmol potassium iodide (KI, purity > 99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved in 1 mL of DMSO to form a 1 M KI/DMSO precursor solution. Similarly, the potassium chloride (KCl, purity > 99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved in DMSO formed 0.1 M KCl/DMSO solution. The potassium-passivated WBG perovskite precursor solution was prepared through mixing different volumes of the KI precursor into the WBG perovskite precursor to prepare the molar concentration of 1–4% WBG/KI. Prior to deposition, all of the perovskite solutions were kept stirring at 60 °C for 12 h.

Fabrication of Perovskite Solar Cells. The prepatterned   $  1.5 \times 1.5 \, cm^2  $   indium tin oxide (ITO,   $  15 \, \Omega \cdot sq^{-1}  $  )-covered glass substrates were sonicated in deionized water, acetone, and ethanol by ultrasonication for 15 min, respectively. The substrates were then blown dry with a nitrogen gun and treated with ultraviolet–ozone for 15 min. The poly-TPD (P-OLED,   $  4 \, mg \cdot mL^{-1}  $   in chlorobenzene) solution was spun-cast at 4000 rpm for 30 s and annealed on a hot plate at   $  100 \, ^\circC  $   for 10 min. Then, an ultrathin layer of PFN-  $  P_2  $   (1-material,   $  0.4 \, mg \cdot mL^{-1}  $   in methanol) solution was spun-cast at 5000 rpm for 20 s to enhance the wettability of Poly-TPD. Subsequently, the perovskite layer was deposited using the antisolvent process as follows:   $  60 \, \muL  $   of the perovskite precursor solution was homogeneously dropped on the substrate and then spun-cast at 1000 rpm for 10 s and at 4000 rpm for 30 s with   $  200 \, \muL  $   of chlorobenzene dripping at the 10 s of the end step. Immediately, the as-prepared sample was annealed at   $  60 \, ^\circC  $   for 2 min and then   $  100 \, ^\circC  $   for 30 min. All perovskites were prepared under the same condition. Finally, the samples were transferred to the evaporation chamber and   $  C_{60}  $   (20 nm), BCP (5 nm), and copper (80 nm) were deposited in sequence at a vacuum of   $  5.0 \times 10^{-5} \, Pa  $   to accomplish the PSCs' fabrication.

Perovskite Films and PSC Characterization. The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectra measurements were carried out with a DeltaFlex fluorescence spectrometer (HORIBA), and the power of laser is about   $  0.7 \mu W  $   and the excitation wavelength is 481 nm. XRD measurements were performed on a Bruker AXS D8 ADVANCE. The UV–vis absorption spectra of the perovskite materials were measured with a SHIMADZU mini 1280 UV–visible spectrophotometer. Current density–voltage (J–V) characterizations of the PSCs were measured under air mass 1.5 global (AM 1.5G) conditions (Enli Technology Co. Ltd). Also, the maximum power point (MPP) trace of PSCs was carried out under AM 1.5G in a nitrogen atmosphere. The long-term photostability measurement was performed in a sealed chamber filled with nitrogen gas under a Newport Solar Simulator Sol3A 94063A 1000 W xenon lamp that illuminated the samples at normal one sun. The top-view and cross-sectional images of the perovskites were observed with a Zeiss SIGMA field emission scanning electron microscope. SCLC of the PSCs was measured using a CHI 660D electrochemical work station (Shanghai Chenhua Instruments, China). EQE spectra were recorded with a QE/IPCE system (Enli Technology Co. Ltd). XPS spectra were recorded with an XPS/UPS system (Thermo Scientific, Escalab 250Xi). The PL images were measured using a confocal PL imaging microscope. A tunable narrow band-pass filter (  $  785 \pm 5  $   nm;   $  730 \pm 5  $   nm) and a long-pass filter (532 nm) were fitted before the CCD detector to block the excitation component of transmitted light (532 nm). The light was focused by a   $  100\times  $   objective (Olympus, NA = 0.8). The sample illuminated spot size was about   $  7 \mu m  $   in diameter. The laser power was   $  0.3 \mu W  $  . In order to prevent the influence of the WBG perovskite film from irradiation light, we use a normal perovskite film for focusing and the sample used for focusing was thrown away. Then, a new sample was used for measuring the PL image to prevent the influence of irradiation light.
