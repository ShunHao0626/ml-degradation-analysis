# Cyclopentadithiophene-based Hole-Transporting Material for Highly Stable Perovskite Solar Cells with Stabilized Efficiencies Approaching 21%

ABSTRACT: There is an urge to develop new hole transporting materials (HTMs) for perovskite solar cells (PSCs), which can yield comparable power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) yet mitigate the issue of stability associated with the state-of-the-art HTM spiro-MeOTAD. Herein, we designed and prepared   $  C_{2v}  $  -symmetric spiro-configured HTM-1 comprising a central acridine-cyclopentadithiophene core unit flanked with triarylamine moieties. PSCs containing a 40 nm thin HTM-1 layer for hole extraction yielded a stabilized PCE approaching 21% under standard illumination. Owing to its higher hole mobility (  $  \mu_{h}  $  ) at low electric field, an impressive short circuit current density (  $  J_{sc}  $  ) of 24.7 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   and a high fill factor (FF) of 0.77 have been achieved. More importantly, HTM-1-based PSCs presented an excellent long-term operational stability under continuous illumination for 400 h and thermal stability at 80 °C, which can be ascribed to its high glass transition temperature of 168 °C and superior moisture tolerance. Arguably, the confluence of high performance and remarkable stability will lead to the development of technologically interesting new, stable, and efficient PSCs.

Keywords: Perovskite solar cells, hole-transporting material, spiro-bicyclopentadithiophene, photostability, thermal stability

# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have become one of the most exciting topics in the photovoltaic field,  $  ^{1-5}  $  undoubtedly due to the stunning increase in their power conversion efficiency (PCE) from 3.8% to over 25%.  $  ^{6}  $  Although different architectures have been reported for PSCs, high performance and good batch-to-batch reproducibility renders n-i-p type mesoscopic structure more attractive.  $  ^{7}  $  Further development of n-i-p architecture mainly relies on the hole transporting material (HTM), which not only plays a key role in facilitating efficient hole collection,  $  ^{8}  $  but also plays a critical role in realizing high operational stability by protecting the perovskite layer from moisture and metal diffusion.  $  ^{9}  $  The most commonly used HTM in mesoscopic PSCs is 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis[N,N-di(4-methoxyphenyl)amino]-9,9'-spirobifluorene (spiro-MeOTAD) owing to its desirable properties such as appropriate ionization potential, low light absorption, and reasonable solubility.  $  ^{3,9}  $  However, spiro-MeOTAD is still under debate primarily because (i) its high cost;  $  ^{10}  $  (ii) operational stability: the amorphous spiro-MeO-TAD with a glass transition temperature (Tg) of 120 °C is prone to crystallization at elevated temperatures.  $  ^{11,12}  $

Recent efforts have focused on the above-mentioned requirements to realize practical applications of PSCs. A wide range of new HTMs, both organic and inorganic, has been designed and investigated to replace the well-explored “gold-standard” spiro-MeOTAD. Nevertheless, only very few HTMs reached or surpassed the efficiency of spiro-MeOTAD-based devices.  $  ^{13-19}  $   Therefore, the development of HTMs allowing for fabrication of highly efficient and stable PSCs is still in great demand.  $  ^{20,21}  $

Herein, we have developed a stable new hole conductor (abbreviated as HTM-1) comprising a central   $  C_{2\nu}  $  -symmetric spiro acridine-cyclopentadithiophene core flanked with triarylamine moieties. The rational design of HTM-1 was based on our published spiro-bicyclopentadithiophene-based HTM (spiro-CPDT), which represents a thiophene analogue of spiro-MeO-TAD (Chart 1) and exhibited a PCE of 13.4% without the use of any dopants and additives.  $  ^{22}  $   In this respect, we wanted to simplify the complex structure of spiro-CPDT by replacing one half of the molecule by an acridine moiety and simultaneous keeping the favorable spiro connection. PSCs containing HTM-1 layer for hole extraction yielded a stabilized PCE approaching 21% under standard illumination displaying the potential of HTM-1 as a promising and technologically interesting hole conductor for highly-efficient and stable PSCs.

# 2. Results and Discussion

2.1. Synthesis of HTM-1. As outlined in Scheme 1, the target molecule HTM-1 was synthesized starting from commercially available 2-bromotriphenylamine 1 and trimethylsilyl (TMS)-protected cyclopentadithiophenone 2, which was synthesized according to a literature procedure in three steps.  $  ^{[23-25]}  $   2-Bromotriphenyl-amine 1 was lithiated using n-butyl lithium (n-BuLi) and subsequently added to TMS-protected ketone 2. A subsequent intramolecular ring-closure reaction was carried out with   $  BF_{3}  $  -etherate as Lewis acid catalyst to afford spiro-cyclopentadithiopheneacridine 3 in 72% yield. A twofold bromination was carried out using mild bromination agent N-bromosuccinimide (NBS) to generate the brominated intermediate 4 in 98% yield. Targeted HTM-1 was synthesized in 72% yield in a   $  Pd^{0}  $  -catalyzed Suzuki-type cross-coupling reaction of 4 with triarylamine boronic ester 5, which was synthesized in three steps according to literature procedures.  $  ^{[26-28]}  $

2.2. Structural and thermal properties of HTM-1. The structure of HTM-1 was confirmed by NMR-spectroscopy (Figure S1-S6, Supporting Information, SI), high-resolution mass spectrometry (Figure S7-S9, SI), and single crystal X-ray structure analysis. Single crystals suitable for X-ray diffraction measurements were obtained by slow diffusion of n-hexane into a dichloromethane solution of HTM-1. The crystal belongs to the monoclinic space group   $  P2_{1}/c  $   and key parameters of the crystal structure refinement are summarized in the SI. Figures 1 and S10 show the molecular structure of HTM-1. The unit cell (a = 10.2586(1), b = 18.1730(2), c = 29.9845(3) Å;   $  \alpha = 90.00  $  ,   $  \beta = 92.7628(9)  $  ,   $  \gamma = 90.00^{\circ}  $  ) contains four equivalent molecules and selected molecular bond lengths, angles, and torsion angles are shown in Figure S11 and compiled in Tables S1 and S2.

Figure 1. Single crystal structure analysis of HTM-1. Kink angle between the cyclopentadithiophene (CPDT) and acridine moiety and selected torsion angles are shown.

The phenyl rings of the triarylamine units connected to the CPDT core are distorted by   $  32.9^{\circ}  $   and   $  8.8^{\circ}  $   and consequently a twist of   $  60^{\circ}  $   of the two triarylamine ‘arms’ relative to one another results. Interestingly, the conformation of the spiro core in HTM-1 crystals differs from the ideal tetrahedral geometry like it is reported for spiro-MeOTAD.  $  ^{29}  $   The acridine moiety in HTM-1 is not perpendicularly arranged with respect to the CPDT-plane, but bent by   $  31^{\circ}  $  . Comparing the geometry of the acridine unit with values recently obtained from X-ray crystal structure analysis of the related hole transport material spiro-bisacridine (SBA),  $  ^{30}  $   we found a similar slight boat conformation, but a stronger deviation from the tetrahedral geometry which was   $  19^{\circ}  $   in the case of SBA. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of HTM-1 (Figure S13) revealed a total mass loss of 46% with a decomposition onset at 417 °C. After determination of the degradation temperature, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) (Figure S14) was used to determine the glass transition and possible melting temperature.  $  ^{[31]}  $   The signals at around 86 °C and 255 °C correspond to artefacts, which were also visible if an empty crucible was heated. No visible signals are observed during the cooling phase. From the first scan, a   $  T_{g}  $   of 168 °C was obtained, which is significantly higher than that measured for spiro-MeOTAD (  $  T_{g} = 120\;^{\circ}C  $  ).  $  ^{[11]}  $   In the second and third scan, slightly lower   $  T_{g}  $   of 167 °C and 163 °C were recorded. Furthermore, no melting was observed for HTM-1. The elevated   $  T_{g}  $   of HTM-1 is expected to lead to better long-term thermal stability of the PSCs.

2.3. Optoelectronic properties of the HTMs. Hole transporting material HTM-1 showed an absorption band with a maximum at 445 nm and an extinction coefficient of 65 000 M  $  ^{-1}  $  cm  $  ^{-1}  $  (Figure S15). The low energy absorption band of HTM-1 is red-shifted by 59 nm (~0.43 meV) compared to spiro-MeOTAD. This effect is attributed to the electron-rich nature of the formal bithiophene moiety compared to the biphenyl of spiro-MeOTAD. Film spectra revealed the same trend (Figure S16, Table S4). Cyclic voltammograms (CV) of HTM-1 showed two reversible one-electron oxidation waves at -0.01 V and 0.13 V (vs. Fc/Fc  $  ^{+}  $ ), which correspond to the consecutive oxidation of the  $  \pi  $ -conjugated triarylamine-substituted cyclopentadithiophene core under formation of radical cations and dications. In addition, a reversible third oxidation wave at 0.69 V comprising the simultaneous transfer of two electrons, which we address to the formation of triarylamine dications, and a quasi-reversible fourth wave at 1.02 V corresponding to the oxidation of the spiro acridine unit by one electron is observed (Figure 2a). The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy level of -5.01 eV for HTM-1 and of -5.04 eV for spiro-MeOTAD was calculated from the onset of the oxidation wave and a value of -5.1 eV for Fc/Fc  $  ^{+}  $  vs vacuum.  $  ^{32}  $

2.5. Hydrophobic and photoluminescent properties of the HTMs. To explore the hydrophobic nature of HTM-1, we conducted contact angle measurements (CAM) as a function of water loading time. The contact angles of the perovskite/spiro-MeOTAD film dropped from 71 to 39° within 30 min demonstrating the vulnerability of the system towards humid ambience (Figure 3a and b). In contrast, no substantial change of the contact angle of the perovskite/HTM-1 film was observed for 15 min. Even after 30 min, the perovskite/HTM-1 film still exhibited a contact angle of >60° which is a promising feature to potentially improve the moisture resistance of PSCs.  $  ^{36}  $

To gain insight into the charge extraction properties of novel HTM-1, we investigated the emission of perovskite using photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy. As shown in Figure 3c, the bare perovskite film exhibited a very strong PL emission, inferring high radiative recombination of photogenerated carriers. In contrast, the PL intensity is drastically reduced when the perovskite film came in contact with the HTM. To understand such a drop in the PL intensity further, and to evaluate the charge extraction kinetics at the perovskite/HTM interface, we recorded the time-resolved PL (TRPL) decay (Figure 3d). The pristine perovskite showed long-lived carriers with a decay time  $ \tau_{10} $ ; (at which the maximum PL intensity decreases by a factor of 10) of 137 ns, correlating with the predominant radiative recombination of carriers shown by the steady-state PL (Figure 3c). However, the charge carriers present in the perovskite films capped by HTMs exhibited a much shorter  $ \tau_{10} $  establishing that the drop in the PL intensity could be associated with the hole extraction from the valence band of perovskite into the HOMO of HTM. Specifically, spiro-MeOTAD and HTM-1-coated perovskite films showed a  $ \tau_{10} $  of 15.9 ns and 15.1 ns, respectively, indicating similar hole extraction kinetics for both the HTMs.

2.6. Photovoltaic properties of the HTMs. To evaluate the potential of HTM-1 in a solar cell configuration, we fabricated PSCs consisting of the following architecture: fluorine-doped tin-oxide (FTO) substrate/ c-TiO $ _2 $ / mp-TiO $ _2 $ / (CsI) $ _{0.05} $  (FAPbI $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.90} $ (MAPbBr $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.10} $ / HTM/Au. We first optimized the concentration of HTM solution to exploit the maximum potential of HTM-1 and the best PCEs were obtained with 30 mM solution (Figure S17). The scanning electron microscope (SEM) micrographs (Figure 3e and f) show that all layers are interconnected with well-defined interfaces. The thickness of spiro-MeOTAD layer is  $ \sim $ 240 nm whereas that of HTM-1 is only  $ \sim $ 40 nm. Despite a much lower thickness, HTM-1 uniformly covers the surface of perovskite layer, which could minimize the parasitic recombination and minimizing the current leakage.

The current density-voltage   $  (J-V)  $   curves of the best-performing PSCs are shown in Figure 4a, and extracted photovoltaic parameters are summarized in Table S5. For the HTM-1-based champion cell, we achieved a   $  J_{SC}  $   of   $  24.7\ mA\cdot cm^{-2}  $  , a   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.10 V, and an FF of 0.77 resulting in PCE of 21.0%, while the spiro-MeOTAD-based champion cell exhibited a   $  J_{SC}  $   of   $  24.4\ mA\cdot cm^{-2}  $  , a   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.14 V, and a FF of 0.76, yielding a PCE of 21.1%. Barring   $  V_{OC}  $  , it is noteworthy that   $  J_{SC}  $   and FF values of HTM-1-based PSCs are relatively higher. # 3. Conclusions

Spiro-configured and cyclopentadithiophene-based HTM-1 has been tailor-made by a multi-step synthesis and successfully employed as hole conductor in highly efficient and stable PSCs. HTM-1 exhibited a higher hole mobility, higher thermal, and better moisture stability as compared to well-known spiro-MeOTAD. In the light of these properties, PSCs based on HTM-1 showed an impressive PCE of 21.0%, which is one of the highest efficiencies for organic HTMs reported to date. In addition, PSCs employing HTM-1 exhibited insignificant hysteresis and excellent reproducibility. Importantly, HTM-1-based devices revealed an excellent long-term operational stability after aging over 400 h. We strongly believe that this study highlights the potential of asymmetric spiro-type molecular HTMs for high performance PSCs and for potential commercialization of perovskite solar panels.
