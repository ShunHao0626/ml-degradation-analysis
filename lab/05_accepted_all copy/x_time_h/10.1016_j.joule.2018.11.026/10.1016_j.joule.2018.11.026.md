# Dion-Jacobson Phase 2D Layered Perovskites for Solar Cells with Ultrahigh Stability

Dion-Jacobson (DJ) phase 2D layered perovskites are developed by removing the van der Waals gap between organic layers and inorganic slabs in Ruddlesden-Popper (RP) phase counterparts. The hydrogen bonding formed at both sides of diammonium cations with perovskite layers in the DJ phase 2D perovskite endows it with extremely high structural stability, compared with that at only one side in the RP phase one. The devices exhibit a PCE of 13.3% with unprecedented stability, even when subjected to very harsh testing conditions.

# SUMMARY

2D layered perovskites have emerged as potential alternates to traditional 3D analogs to solve the stability issue of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). However, van der Waals gaps in reported Ruddlesden-Popper (RP) phase 2D perovskites with monoammonium cations provide weak interactions between layers, potentially destabilizing the layered perovskite structure and thus the device. Here we eradicate such gaps by incorporating diammonium cations into MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  , developing a series of Dion-Jacobson phase 2D perovskites that afford a cell efficiency of 13.3% with ultrahigh device stability. Unencapsulated devices retain over 95% efficiency upon exposure to various harsh stresses including ambient air (40%-70% relative humidity [RH]) for 4,000 hr, damp heat (85°C and 85% RH) for 168 hr, and continuous light illumination for 3,000 hr. The improved device stability over the RP counterpart is attributed to alternating hydrogen bonding interactions between diammonium cations and inorganic slabs, strengthening the 2D layered perovskite structure.

# INTRODUCTION

3D perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have achieved certified power conversion efficiencies (PCE) over 23%, but the unsatisfactorily low stability upon exposure to humidity, heat, and light hinders their commercialization.  $  ^{1-5}  $   To surmount this issue, besides efforts from perspectives of interface engineering and encapsulation,  $  ^{6-8}  $   seeking alternatives to 3D perovskites is more crucial to intrinsically improve the stability of photoactive materials. Recently, it is reported that Ruddlesden-Popper (RP) phase 2D layered perovskites exhibit higher environmental stability compared with the 3D counterparts,  $  ^{9-12}  $   because the hydrophobic nature of the organic cations can resist the penetration of water molecules into the perovskite lattice.  $  ^{9,10}  $   Several organic cations have been explored for 2D PSCs, e.g., phenethylammonium (PEA  $  ^{+}  $  ),  $  ^{9}  $   butylammonium (BA  $  ^{+}  $  ),  $  ^{3,8}  $   iso-BA  $  ^{+}  $  ,  $  ^{13}  $   and iodoethylammonium.  $  ^{14}  $   Typically, Tsai et al. used a hot-casting method to improve the charge transport in BA-based 2D PSCs, achieving a PCE of 12.5%.  $  ^{3}  $   Unencapsulated devices could retain 60% of their initial PCE after 2,250 hr light illumination and only 20% after 60 hr storage under a 65% relative humidity (RH).  $  ^{3}  $   The PCE of BA-based 2D PSCs was further improved by Cs  $  ^{+}  $   doping to 13.7% by Zhang et al. However, devices lost 15% PCE after storing under a 65% RH for 36 hr, and only kept stable for 16 hr when subjected to heat stress (80°C) even under inert atmosphere.  $  ^{15}  $   Although the stability of these 2D PSCs is higher than that of the 3D counterparts under such moderate testing conditions, there is apparently a big room for PSC stability to be further improved.

# Context & Scale

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted tremendous academic and industrial interests because of their rapidly increased power conversion efficiency (PCE) in the past few years, but the intrinsic instability of commonly used 3D perovskites induces the issue of low device stability. Ruddlesden-Popper (RP) phase 2D layered perovskites have recently been reported to show enhanced stability. However, weak van der Waals interactions between interlayers cannot sufficiently stabilize their 2D layered structure. By removing the van der Waals gaps in the RP case, we herein develop a series of Dion-Jacobson phase (DJ) 2D layered perovskites with higher structural stability for PSCs. A maximum PCE of  $ 13.3\% $  is achieved from the DJ phase 2D PSCs, and unencapsulated devices are extremely stable, retaining more than  $ 95\% $  of initial PCE upon exposure to ambient air (4,000 hr), damp heat  $ (85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  and  $ 85\% $  RH, 168 hr), and continuous light illumination (3,000 hr).

In principle, these RP phase 2D perovskites consist two sheets of interlayer organic cations, in which monoammonium cations such as BA $  ^{+}  $  and PEA $  ^{+}  $  only interact with inorganic perovskite layers at one side and hence there is a van der Waals gap between adjacent cations (see illustration in Figure 1). $  ^{16}  $  Seemingly, hydrophobic chains can protect the perovskite slabs from the moisture penetration, but the very weak van der Waals interaction between the layers cannot guarantee the structural stability of the 2D perovskite framework, leading to its degradation upon exposure to external harsh stresses.

Elimination of the van der Waals gap is thus a reasonable way to enhance the stability of 2D perovskites. Diamine compounds with two amino groups at both ends can play such a role via alternately forming hydrogen bonds with the inorganic slabs without any gaps (only one sheet of interlayer), having potential to stabilize the layered perovskite structure.  $  ^{16-18}  $   These perovskites are known as Dion-Jacobson (DJ) phase 2D perovskites represented by the formula of A'A  $  _{n-1}  $  Pb  $  _{n}  $  I  $  _{3n+1}  $  , where A' represents a divalent organic cation.  $  ^{19}  $   Several diamines such as ethylenediamine and 1,4-butanediamine were reported to prepare such perovskites; however, their device efficiencies were very low (<2%), and their advantage in terms of stability over RP phase 2D perovskites was not demonstrated yet.  $  ^{20,21}  $   Recently, a series of DJ phase 2D perovskites containing 3-(aminomethyl)piperidinium or 4-(aminomethyl)piperidinium cations were reported for 2D PSCs with a PCE of 7.32%; nonetheless, the device stability was also not mentioned.  $  ^{19}  $   During the preparation of this manuscript, Ma et al. claimed that reducing the distance between inorganic perovskite slabs by using 1,3-propanediamine in 2D perovskite framework could enhance the efficiency and stability of PSCs.  $  ^{22}  $   However, the stability of unencapsulated devices under high humidity and continuous light illumination were not investigated and the critical role of absence of the van der Waals gap in the 2D perovskite was not discussed.

Herein, we demonstrate that the DJ phase 2D perovskites exhibit greater material and device stabilities than the RP phase counterparts due to the elimination of the van der Waals gap in the former, by comparing the 2D perovskites derived from the 1,3-propanediamine (PDA) and the propylamine (PA) cations, respectively. Relative to the PSCs based on the RP phase 2D perovskite, the DJ phase analogs not only afford a higher PCE of 13.3%, one of the highest values for 2D PSCs so far, but also exhibit more outstanding stability, retaining more than 95% PCE when subjected to very harsh testing conditions (ambient air [40%–70% RH], heating at 85°C and 85% RH, and continuous light soaking).

# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The DJ phase 2D perovskites (PDA) (MA)  $  _{n-1}  $  Pb  $  _{n13n+1}  $   (n = 1–4) were prepared from a stoichiometric reaction between PbI  $  _{2}  $  , MAI, and (PDA)I  $  _{2}  $   (see Experimental section for details). The UV-vis absorption spectra show continuously red-shifted and broadened bands with increasing n values (Figure 2A), which coincides with the color change of the (PDA) (MA)  $  _{n-1}  $  Pb  $  _{n13n+1}  $   film (Figure 2A inset). Due to the dimension reduction effect, their band gap progressively decreases with the increased n values, with E  $  _{g}  $   = 2.46, 1.77, 1.69, and 1.63 eV for n = 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively. This variation of band gap with dimensionality is a universal trend and similar to other 2D Pb-based perovskites.  $  ^{[23,24]}  $   Furthermore, for the cases of lower n values (n = 1 and 2) the spectra show small excitonic peaks which become unobvious for higher n values. In particular, the (PDA) (MA)  $  _{3}  $  Pb  $  _{4}  $  I  $  _{13}  $   (n = 4) does not present any excitonic peaks, coinciding well with previous reports.  $  ^{[19,25]}  $   This is due to the decrease in the excitonic binding energy with

It has been claimed that the RP phase 2D perovskites usually exhibit different emissions when measured from the front and back sides of the film because of the presence of lower layer numbers.  $  ^{26,27}  $   To detect the situation in the DJ phase 2D perovskites, we measured the photoluminescence (PL) spectra of the   $  (\mathrm{PA})_{2}(\mathrm{MA})_{3}\mathrm{Pb}_{4}\mathrm{I}_{13}  $   and   $  (\mathrm{PDA})(\mathrm{MA})_{3}\mathrm{Pb}_{4}\mathrm{I}_{13}  $   films from both sides. While the PA-based case (RP phase) unsurprisingly displays shifted emission peaks (Figure S2), the PDA-based one (DJ phase) interestingly does not show any changes in the PL spectra obtained from both sides of the film. These results suggest that the DJ phase 2D perovskite possesses higher phase purity than the RP one. This is because that, compared with the RP case, the strong hydrogen bonding between the diammonium cation and the perovskite slab at both sides in the DJ case enables less distorted perovskite layers, reducing the formation of additional phases with smaller n values.  $  ^{28}  $

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was carried out to investigate the crystallinity of the DJ phase 2D perovskite films. Two dominant peaks appear around 14.2° and 28.4°, representing the scattering from crystallographic planes (111) and (202) of (PDA) (MA) $ _{n-1} $ Pb $ _{n} $ I $ _{3n+1} $  perovskites, respectively (Figure 2B). $ ^{3,8,10,11,14} $  Slight shifts of the two peaks toward larger angles are observed with increased n values (Figure 2B inset), owing to the extension in crystal lattice constant and hence the crystal size. $ ^{14,15} $  It has been found that a hot-casting technique can enable a preferential out-of-plane alignment of conducting inorganic slabs in the 2D perovskite with respect to the substrate, in favor of improving charge transport and thus the device efficiency. $ ^{3,23,24} $  This technique was also employed to prepare our 2D perovskite films, and the XRD measurement was conducted on the (PDA) (MA) $ _{3} $ Pb $ _{4} $ I $ _{13} $  films fabricated at different hot-casting temperatures ranging from 130°C to 190°C (Figure 2C). We find that, as the temperature increases, the full-width at half-maximum at ~14.2° gradually narrows from 0.181° to 0.124°, while the crystal size increases from 0.57 to 2.5 μm, as illustrated in Figure 2D and Table S1. These results imply the formation of the 2D structure of PDA-based perovskites. $ ^{29,30} $  In addition, the scanning electronic microscopic (SEM) images (Figure S3) show a Volmer-Weber growth mode of the DJ phase 2D perovskites, where the nucleation of small crystals takes place on the surface of the substrate at low temperature, and then gradually grows into closely packed large crystals with increased temperature. $ ^{24} $  This process is consistent with the XRD analysis.

To further confirm the orientation of our DJ phase 2D perovskite with respect to substrate, we have conducted grazing-incidence XRD (GI-XRD) measurements of the (PDA)  $ (\mathrm{MA})_{3}\mathrm{Pb}_{4}\mathrm{I}_{13} $  films at various hot-casting temperatures (Figure 3). The GI-XRD patterns reveal two major characteristics of the 2D perovskite films, similar to those in previous reports.[3,13,31] The sample fabricated at room temperature presents continuous diffraction rings, indicating the random orientation of polycrystalline in thin film. By applying the hot-casting method, the characteristic patterns become more prominent and sharp Bragg spots start appearing within the same ring when the temperature reaches  $ 170^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . The intensity of such Bragg spots along the  $ q_{z} $  direction is dramatically enhanced at  $ 190^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  (Figure 3F). These sharp Bragg spots reflect highly oriented crystal grains with vertical growth of inorganic perovskite layers bearing PDA cations relative to substrate, which will consequently increase the charge transport.

We further tested the stability of devices based on MAPbI $ _3 $ , (PA) $ _2 $ (MA) $ _3 $ Pb $ _4 $ I $ _{13} $ , and (PDA) (MA) $ _3 $ Pb $ _4 $ I $ _{13} $  under stresses of humidity, heat, and light illumination, respectively. Noted that all devices for stability tests are unencapsulated. First, the devices are stored under open atmosphere with an RH of 40%–70% for the humidity test. The normalized PCEs versus storage time are illustrated in Figure 6A. The MAPbI $ _3 $ -based devices undergo a significant degradation in the first few days, while the (PA) $ _2 $ (MA) $ _3 $ Pb $ _4 $ I $ _{13} $ -based ones show a slower degradation, retaining 80% of their original PCE after 360 hr and only 25% after 4,000 hr. In contrast, our champion devices based on the PDA-containing 2D perovskite exhibit a negligible degradation, retaining almost 97% PCE after 360 hr and more than 95% after 4,000 hr. The stability of these perovskite thin films was also detected under the same humidity condition by XRD analysis (Figures 7A–7C). This shows that the 3D perovskite film gives a lead iodide peak after 10 days, while the PA-based perovskite film displays such a peak after 45 days, but our PDA-based perovskite film does not show any signs of decomposition up to 90 days, quite well consistent with the humidity stability for PSCs. In addition, the color changes of such films after 170 days (Figures 7A–7C inset) visually reflect the degradation process of these perovskites. We also fabricated a device based on the PDA-based 2D perovskite in air (RH of 40%–70%), yielding a PCE of 11.9% (Figure S13). These results further verify the high humidity stability of DJ phase 2D perovskite materials and devices.

Second, heat stability is investigated by placing the devices at   $  85^{\circ}  $  C on a hot plate in an open atmosphere with an RH of 40%–70%. Not surprisingly, the PCE of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   PSCs drastically reduces by half within 1 hr (Figure 6B). For the PA-based 2D PSCs, they can maintain 95% of their initial PCE after several hours, but only less than 30% after 168 hr. Notably, our champion PDA-based 2D PSCs are extremely stable and maintain about 95% of their initial PCE after continuous heating in air for 168 hr. Encouraged by these exciting results, we further applied harsher conditions by placing the unencapsulated devices inside an environmental chamber at constant temperature and humidity of   $  85^{\circ}  $  C and 85%, respectively, which is a widely used aging condition in the photovoltaic industry. Very intriguingly, our PDA-based 2D PSCs still retain more than 95% PCE after continuous aging for 168 hr, whereas the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  - and PA-based counterparts lost almost 95% and 80% PCEs, respectively (Figure 6C). Considerably high heat stability of PDA-based perovskite thin films is also observed (Figures 7D–7F). The higher stability of monoamine-based 2D PSCs than 3D ones was attributed to the hydrophobic and insulating natures of the organic cations (such as BA $  ^{+}  $  and PEA $  ^{+}  $  in the literature), $  ^{3,7}  $  which can resist or minimize the effect of humidity on the device. However, under very harsh testing conditions as we exerted, the monoamine (PA in this work)-based 2D PSCs actually exhibit modest device stability for any of the stresses. The hydrophobic and insulating properties of the monoammonium cations may to some extent facilitate the humidity stability, but the presence of the van der Waals gap between the cations is not beneficial to the structural stability of the 2D perovskite, which results in the disappointing performance when their devices are subjected to stresses in the high-temperature and high-humidity environment. # Conclusions

In summary, we have developed a highly efficient and stable homologous series of DJ phase 2D perovskites, (PDA)  $ (\mathrm{MA})_{n - 1}\mathrm{Pb}_n\mathrm{l}_{3n + 1} $ . By using the hot-casting method, a maximum PCE of  $ 13.3\% $  was achieved from the (PDA)  $ (\mathrm{MA})_3\mathrm{Pb}_4\mathrm{l}_{13} $ -based device. The unencapsulated devices can retain about  $ 95\% $  of their initial PCE after 4,000 hr exposure to ambient air (40%-70% RH), 168 hr to damp heat stress (at  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  and  $ 85\% $  RH), and 3,000 hr to light illumination. We attribute the high efficiency and outstanding stability of diamine-based 2D PSCs to the strong hydrogen bonding of organic layers with perovskite layers at both sides, excluding the van der Waals gap. This leads to the formation of a less-distorted crystal structure with tight stacking of perovskite layers, inducing increased crystallinity, higher phase purity, and improved charge mobility of the DJ phase 2D perovskites compared with the RP phase analogs. Our findings demonstrate that PSCs based on the DJ phase 2D perovskites have superior efficiency and stability than those based on the RP phase ones. Further improvement in device PCE can be expected by doping additional cations or by further optimizing the processing conditions, which together with remarkably enhanced device stability will advance the application of PSCs.

# Synthesis of (PDA)I $ _2 $  and PAI

1,3-Propanediammonium iodide salt (PDA)  $  _{I_{2}}  $  was prepared by taking 1 mole equivalent of 1,3-propanediamine (2.6 mL) followed by dropwise adding 2 mole equivalents solution of hydroiodic acid (8 mL) with continuous stirring at 0°C for 2 hr. Using a rotary evaporator, the white precipitate of diammonium iodide salt was recovered by evaporation of the solvent at 55°C. The precipitate was washed with ethanol and diethyl ether three times. # Device Fabrication and Measurement
Preparation of Substrate

The devices were fabricated on 2-mm-thick FTO-coated glasses. The substrates were sequentially cleaned by ultrasonication in ethanol, isopropanol, and acetone each for 30 min and finally dried under an   $  N_{2}  $   flow. Then the substrates were treated in a UV-ozone cleaner for 15 min.

# Fabrication of ETL

The treated substrates were immersed in a 0.2 M aqueous solution of   $  TiCl_{4}  $   and placed in oven at 70°C for 1 hr. The glass/FTO/  $  TiO_{2}  $   substrates were then washed with deionized water three times and finally treated with a steam of   $  N_{2}  $  . Before further use, the   $  TiO_{2}  $   thin film was annealed at 200°C for 1 hr in air.

# Fabrication of 2D Perovskite Layer

The precursor solutions of DJ phase 2D perovskites (PDA)  $ (\mathrm{MA})_{n - 1}\mathrm{Pb}_n\mathrm{I}_{3n + 1}(n = 1 - 4) $  were synthesized from a stoichiometric ratio of MAI,  $ \mathrm{PbI_2} $ , and  $ (\mathrm{PDA})_2 $  in  $ 1\mathrm{mL} $  mixed solvent of  $ \gamma $ -butyrolactone and DMSO (7:3 volume) with various concentrations based on  $ \mathrm{PbI_2} $ . The RP phase 2D perovskite  $ (\mathrm{PA})_2(\mathrm{MA})_3\mathrm{Pb}_4\mathrm{I}_{13} $  solution was prepared from a stoichiometric ratio of MAI,  $ \mathrm{PbI_2} $ , and PAI. Synthetic procedures in details for each  $ n $  value are given in the Supplemental Information. The 2D perovskite layers were prepared using a hot-casting method. On the substrate of glass/FTO/TiO $ _2 $  heated at a specific temperature, a  $ 70~\mu \mathrm{L} $  precursor solution was dropped then spin-coated at  $ 4,000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 40~s $ . The color of the thin film quickly changed from pale yellow to orange yellow for  $ n = 1 $ , to red for  $ n = 2 $ , to dark red for  $ n = 3 $ , and to black for  $ n = 4 $ , during the evaporation of solvent. For all  $ n $  values and PA-based perovskite layer, the same conditions were applied.

# Electrode Evaporation

A 100-nm-thick counter gold electrode was thermally evaporated under a vacuum of   $  4 \times 10^{-4}  $   torr.

# Device Measurement

The J–V measurement was performed via a solar simulator (SS-F5-3A, Enlitech) along with AM 1.5G spectra, the intensity of which was calibrated by a certified standard silicon solar cell (SRC-2020, Enlitech) at   $  100 \, mV/cm^{2}  $   under ambient conditions. A black metal mask with an aperture was used to control the light scattering and focus the light on the active device area (  $  0.066 \, cm^{2}  $  ) during measurement. The EQE data were obtained using a solar-cell spectral-response measurement system (QE-R, Enlitech).
