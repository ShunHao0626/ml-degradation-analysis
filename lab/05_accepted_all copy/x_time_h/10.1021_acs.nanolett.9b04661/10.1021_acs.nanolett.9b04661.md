# Abstract

3D/2D hybrid perovskite systems have been intensively investigated to improve the stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Whereas, undesired crystallization of 2D perovskite during the film formation process could undermine the structural stability of 2D perovskite materials, which cause serious hysteresis of PSCs after aging. This issue is however rarely studied. Stability study for 3D/2D hybrid systems to date are all under one-direction scan, and the lack of detailed information on the hysteresis after aging compromises the credibility of the stability results. In this work, by correlating the hysteresis of the hybrid PSCs with the 2D crystal structure, we find that the prompt 2D perovskite formation process easily induces numerous crystal imperfections and structural defects. These defects are susceptible to humidity attack and decompose the 2D perovskite to insulating long chain cations and 3D perovskite, which hinder charge transfer or generate charge accumulation. Therefore, large hysteresis is exhibited after aging the 3D/2D hybrid PSCs in ambient, even though reverse-scan power conversion efficiency (PCE) is found to be well-preserved. To address this issue, alkali cations  $ \mathrm{K}^+ $  and  $ \mathrm{Rb}^+ $  are introduced into the 2D perovskite to exquisitely modulate the crystal formation, which gives rise to higher crystallinity of 2D perovskite and better film morphology with less defects. We achieved PCE beyond  $ 21\% $  due to the preferable charge transfer process and reduced non-radiative recombination losses. The structural features also bring about impressive moisture stability, which results in the corresponding PSCs retaining  $ 93\% $  of its initial PCE and negligible hysteresis after aging in ambient atmosphere for 1200 hours.

Key words: 2D perovskite; alkali cation doping; improved 2D perovskite structural stability; hysteresis; long-term device stability.

Organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite materials, with extraordinary optical and electronic properties, have revolutionized the next-generation photovoltaics.  $  ^{1-4}  $   Significant efforts have been devoted to the optimization of perovskite compositions and device architectures, the development of electron/hole transport layers and interfacial engineering.  $  ^{5-9}  $   Up to now, certified power conversion efficiencies (PCE) of up to 25.2% have been achieved, which surpasses that of any other thin film solar cell technology.  $  ^{10}  $   However, the sensitivity of perovskite materials to moisture, illumination and heat still remain as major hindrances to the commercialization of perovskite solar cells (PSCs), despite their rapid progress in PCEs.  $  ^{11-13}  $

Among all the factors that cause instability of PSCs, humidity, which irreversibly decomposes the perovskite to   $  PbI_{2}  $   and sublimated organic amine components, is especially detrimental to the 3D bulk perovskite.  $  ^{[11]}  $   Lower-dimensional (2D) perovskites, derived by isolating the corner-sharing lead halide octahedra sheets with long-chain hydrophobic cations, on the other hand, have been demonstrated to exhibit much better moisture stability than their 3D counterparts.  $  ^{[12-14]}  $   However, PCEs of 2D PSCs are generally compromised by the larger exciton binding energy and narrower absorption range of 2D perovskite materials.  $  ^{[12-15]}  $   Therefore, to have a trade-off between the moisture tolerance and PSC performance, a hybrid system of 3D/2D mixture as light absorber has been developed.  $  ^{[16-24]}  $   Among the methods fabricating 3D/2D hybrid systems, selectively growing 2D perovskite on top of 3D bulk film, as the most facile and straightforward route, has attracted significant attention in the recent years.  $  ^{[21-24]}  $   The top 2D perovskite layer serving as both defect passivator and moisture barrier simultaneously improves efficiency and stability of PSCs. Whereas, it is notable that the 2D layered structure also brings about some adverse characteristics to the 3D/2D hybrid system: undesired orientation or crystallization of 2D perovskite during film formation process could hinder charge transport, resulting in charge accumulation at the perovskite/hole transport material (HTM) interface, and causing severe hysteresis of PSCs. This issue is lacking of rational considerations and has rarely been investigated.

In this work, by correlating the crystallinity/morphology of layered perovskites with hysteresis, we found that prompt formation of 2D perovskite during the spin-casting process readily induces crystal imperfections and structural defects, which are prone to the degradation of the 2D perovskite crystals under humidity. The deteriorated 2D perovskite obstruct the charge transport from 3D perovskite towards the HTM, which leads to unbalanced electron/hole charge collection, thus causing severe hysteresis in 3D/2D hybrid PSCs after aging in ambient. This phenomenon has however never been reported. Stability tests for 3D/2D hybrid systems to date are all under one-direction scan, absence of the hysteresis information after aging compromises the accuracy of the stability results. Hence refining the 2D crystal formation in this type of stacking layered 3D/2D hybrid system could be highly beneficial.

Herein, we incorporate alkali cations   $  K^{+}  $  ,   $  Rb^{+}  $   into the 2D precursor, as they have been proved as effective additives in 3D perovskite for tuning the perovskite microstructure and passivating the halide vacancies in the meantime.  $  ^{25-29}  $   The 2D perovskite crystallization and opt-electronic properties upon alkali cation doping are analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD), grazing

We evidence that the alkali cations doping induces higher crystallinity, preferable crystallite orientation, better film morphology and superior moisture stability of 2D perovskite thin film, which result in improved photovoltaic performance of the hybrid PSCs and hysteresis-associated stability. Preparation of the perovskite layers are described in detail in the experimental section. In brief, mixed triple cation 3D perovskite   $  Cs_{0.05}FA_{0.79}MA_{0.16}PbI_{2.49}Br_{0.51}(FA^{+}=CH(NH_{2})_{2}^{+}, MA^{+}=CH_{3}NH_{3}^{+})  $   was prepared by anti-solvent method: FAI (1M),   $  PbI_{2}  $   (1.1M), MABr (0.2M),   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (0.22M) and CsI (0.065M) were deposited from anhydrous dimethylformamide (DMF):dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (vol% 4:1) solvent with a concentration of 1.32 M. For the 3D/2D hybrid perovskite layer, 100 μL butylammonium iodide (BAI) in isopropanol (IPA) with a concentration of 10 mg/mL was deposited onto the 3D perovskite layers. After thermal annealing at 100°C for 10 min, a 3D/2D hybrid layer can be formed. The structural crystallinity and phases of the 3D and 3D/2D hybrid perovskite films are analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns. From the results shown in Figure 1a, it can be seen that the   $  Cs_{0.05}FA_{0.79}MA_{0.16}PbI_{2.49}Br_{0.51}  $   perovskite (referred to as 3D in the following context) shows a peak at 12.6° indicative of the presence of unreacted   $  PbI_{2}  $ . This peak disappears with the   $  BA^{+}  $  incorporation on the top (the film referred to as 3D/2D), suggesting   $  BA^{+}  $  reacts with the remnant   $  PbI_{2}  $ . Aside from the typical diffraction peaks of the tetragonal 3D phases, 3D/2D films exhibit additional reflections at 9.0° and 13.6° corresponding to lattice planes of (040) and (060) in 2D perovskite   $  \mathrm{BA}_{2}(\mathrm{Cs}_{0.05}\mathrm{FA}_{0.79}\mathrm{MA}_{0.16})\mathrm{Pb}_{2}(\mathrm{I}_{0.83}\mathrm{Br}_{0.17})_{7}  $ .  $  ^{17,28}  $   The tiny diffraction peak at 11.7° in the 3D perovskite and 3D/2D-K perovskite films corresponds to the δ-phase of   $  FAPbI_{3}  $ .  $  ^{30,31}  $   We further conducted XRD analysis with fixed incident angles at 0.2°, 0.4° and 1.0° to elucidate the surface and internal perovskite structure. From Figure 1c, it is apparent that at low incident angle of 0.2°, the top layer is dominated by the 2D crystal diffractions (9.0° and 13.6°). With increasing the incident angle to 0.4° and 1.0°, 3D diffractions (14.1°, 28.3° and 31.8°) become substantially stronger. The variations from surface to bulk diffractions verify the 2D perovskite majorly forms on the top of the 3D film.

The structural stability of the 3D/2D hybrid perovskite film was then tested. Figure 1b shows the XRD patterns of perovskite films stored under Relative Humidity (RH) @ 85% for 5 days. In Figure 1b, 3D perovskite shows increased PbI  $  _{2}  $  reflection peak and decreased reflections from 3D tetragonal phases, illustrating the partial decomposition of the 3D perovskite under high humidity. From the XRD pattern of the 3D/2D film, the absence of the PbI  $  _{2}  $  peak and the presence of prominent 3D phase reflections indicate the intact 3D perovskite under the protection of 2D layer. The diffraction peaks from 2D perovskite (at 9.0°, 13.6°), however, are significantly decreased or even disappeared, which implies the degradation of the top 2D layer into BAI and 3D perovskite. To obtain an in-depth understanding of the crystal orientation of the 2D perovskite in the hybrid perovskite films, grazing incident wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) measurements are performed. Figure 2a-d and Figure S1 present the 2D GIWAXS images for 3D/2D, 3D/2D-K, 3D/2D-Rb and 3D/2D-Rb&K films taken as a function of X-ray incident angle to probe film microstructure at different depth. Decreasing scattering intensity for 2D phase (q ~ 0.33 Å $  ^{-1}  $ ) at higher X-ray incident angle indicates formation of 2D phase on the film surface, which agrees with the finding from GIXD measurement. As the 2D phase predominantly resides on the film surface, we focus further GIWAXS analysis based on data taken at shallow X-ray incident angle of 0.1°. Based on Figure 2a-d, the 1D scattering profiles averaged about the out-of-plane (OOP) direction of the films are plotted, as shown in Figure 2e. The observation of strong OOP 2D peaks from the 3D/2D film indicates that the 2D crystallites are predominantly packing parallel to the substrate, which agrees well with previous reports.  $  ^{24}  $  The OOP packing of 2D perovskite crystal is justified by the inorganic octahedra layer sandwiched between two long-chain organic layers, which confines the crystal growth of the inorganic sheets parallel to the substrate. In contrast, it is seen from Figure 2e that the addition of K $  ^{+}  $  and Rb $  ^{+}  $  cations into the 2D perovskite suppress the 2D diffractions along the OOP direction. As it is widely acknowledged that the parallel orientation of 2D crystallites severely blocks charge transport, the suppressed OOP packing suggests decreased charge transfer barrier. To further explore the dominating crystallite orientation of 2D phase in 3D/2D with alkali doping perovskite films, we further integrated the GIWAXS pattern azimuthally over the scattering ring at q=0.33Å $  ^{-1}  $  corresponding to (020) plane of the 2D perovskite (n=2), the results are shown in Figure 2f. For 3D/2D film, it reveals a high ordering of 2D perovskite parallel to the substrate. With the incorporation of Rb $  ^{+}  $ , the 2D crystallites exhibit significantly enhanced ordering along ~40° relative to the OOP direction. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurement is further carried out to elucidate the surface composition of the perovskite films. Figure S2a,b show the high-resolution XPS spectra of N 1s, C1s respectively. The full survey of XPS spectra of the five films are shown in Figure S2c, and the relative atomic ratio of the elements are summarized in Table S1. The N 1s spectrum of the 3D perovskite shows two peaks at 402.5 eV and 400.8 eV originated from MA $  ^{+}  $  (or BA $  ^{+}  $  and MA $  ^{+}  $  in 2D perovskite) and FA $  ^{+}  $ , respectively. $ ^{36} $  The measured FA $  ^{+}  $ /MA $  ^{+}  $  ratio (Table S1) of 89:12 matches the theoretical ratio of 91:9 in the perovskite precursors (note here that the molecular ratio of FA $  ^{+}  $ /MA $  ^{+}  $  in the material is 83:17 corresponds to a nitrogen atomic ratio of 91:9 as FA $  ^{+}  $  contains two nitrogen atoms). $ ^{37} $  In the 3D/2D film, the measured FA $  ^{+}  $ /(MA $  ^{+}  $ +BA $  ^{+}  $ ) ratio of 61:47 conforms with the 2D formula BA $  _{2}  $ (Cs $  _{0.05}  $ FA $  _{0.79}  $ MA $  _{0.16}  $ ) Pb $  _{2}  $ (I $  _{0.83}  $ Br $  _{0.17}  $ ) $ _{7} $ . As the doping ratios of the alkali cations are very low, we are unable to detect   $  Rb^{+}  $  and   $  K^{+}  $  in the perovskite film prepared under the experimental condition. The spectra illustrate that   $  Rb^{+}  $  and   $  K^{+}  $  are concentrated on the top of the 3D/2D perovskite film, which suggests that the alkali cations doping majorly affects the 2D crystallization and the defect passivation.

Steady-state and time-resolved photoluminescence (PL) measurements are conducted to study the charge extraction and recombination process. From the steady-state PL spectra shown in Figure S4a, it is observed that the 2D perovskite layer capping drastically increases the PL intensities, which testifies the role of 2D layer in passivating the 3D perovskite trap states. With the introduction of alkali cations doping, the PL intensity slightly increases in the 3D/2D-K, 3D/2D-Rb and 3D/2D-Rb&K perovskite films, suggesting the alkali cations further suppress the defects in the 2D perovskite layer. The time-resolved PL (TRPL) spectra shown in Figure 4b consists of two distinct procedures. The first (fast) decay should be associated with the instrument response of the excitation light, so we focus on comparing the second decay. The second decay processes are fitted by the bi-exponential decay of I(t) =  $  I_0 + A_1 \exp(-t/\tau_1) + A_2 \exp(-t/\tau_2)  $ , ( $  \tau_1  $  and  $  \tau_2  $  give the fast and slow decay time constant,  $  A_1  $  and  $  A_2  $  are fractions of the two decay processes, the average lifetime  $  <\tau> = \alpha_1 \tau_1 + \alpha_2 \tau_2  $ ,  $  \alpha_i = \frac{A_i \tau_i}{\sum A_i \tau_i}  $ ), the fitting details are summarized in Table S2. $ ^{38} $  Compared to 3D perovskite film with  $  \tau  $  of 18 ns, 3D/2D layer shows an elongated  $  \tau  $  of 72 ns, which is indicative of the suppressed trap-assisted non-radiative recombination losses by the passivation of 2D perovskite. Upon the addition of alkali cations K $ ^+ $ , Rb $ ^+ $  and Rb $ ^+ $ &K $ ^+ $ , the  $  \tau  $  further enhances to 98 ns, 85 ns and 88 ns, respectively, which could be ascribed to the passivation of halide vacancies or the refined crystallization of 2D perovskite by alkali doping.

Table 1 summarizes the photovoltaic performances of PSCs (40 devices for each condition). It is seen that PSCs based on 3D perovskite films show an average reverse-scan PCE of  $ 17.5\% $  with a large hysteresis index (HI) of  $ 28.2\% $ . In comparison, 3D/2D hybrid PSCs yield enhanced average reverse-scan PCE of  $ 18.1\% $ , with marginally changed  $ \mathrm{J_{SC}} $ , and notably increased  $ \mathrm{V_{OC}} $  from 1.08 to 1.12V. A slight loss in  $ \mathrm{J_{SC}} $  is attributed to the inhibited charge transport by the 2D perovskite capping layer. This is evidenced by the significant loss in  $ \mathrm{J_{SC}} $  for PSCs with thicker 2D layer (Figure S8), in which, the photogenerated charges need to overcome larger number of organic insulating sheets. The enhancement in  $ \mathrm{V_{OC}} $ , as we have elaborated previously, can be ascribed to the suppressed back electron transfer at the perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD interface. Above all, the mixture of 3D and 2D systems significantly reduces the HI to  $ 14.8\% $ . The decreased hysteresis could be explained that the 2D perovskite passivates the trap states on the surface or grain boundaries of the 3D perovskite film, which suppresses the hysteresis-related trapping/detrapping process and the ion migration process.[23,26,39-41] The incorporation of alkali cations  $ \mathrm{Rb^{+}} $  and  $ \mathrm{K^{+}} $  into the 2D perovskite further boosts the PCE to  $ 19.3\% $  and  $ 19.6\% $ , respectively (Table 1). We attribute the simultaneously improved  $ \mathrm{V_{OC}} $ ,  $ \mathrm{J_{SC}} $  and FF to the improved crystallinity, preferable crystallization/orientation and less structural defects in the 2D perovskite, as have been evidenced by the previous thin film characterizations, which give rise to reduced electron-hole recombination, facilitated hole transport and balanced electron/hole mobility. Whereas, PSCs based on 3D/2D-K still show noticeable HI of  $ 14.8\% $ . Noted that in 3D perovskite,  $ \mathrm{K^{+}} $  cations cannot fit into the 3D lattices due to their small cationic size, they are sitting on the grain boundaries and are movable under applied biases. As the 2D perovskite has a relatively more relaxed crystal structure compared to their 3D counterparts, the small  $ \mathrm{K^{+}} $  cation would be more mobilized in 2D perovskite under external voltages, which leads to the noticeable HI in PSCs based on 3D/2D-K. This assumption is also attested by the device characterizations of PSCs based on 3D/2D-K with a series of  $ \mathrm{K^{+}} $  doping ratios. Figure S9 reveals that raising the  $ \mathrm{K^{+}} $  doping ratio over the optimized ratio ( $ 5\% $ ) adversely increases the hysteresis. In contrast, HI remains almost constant when increasing the  $ \mathrm{Rb^{+}} $  doping ratio in PSCs based on 3D/2D- $ \mathrm{Rb^{+}} $ . It can be explained that  $ \mathrm{Rb^{+}} $  with larger cationic size would be less mobilized due to the steric hindrances, as compared to the small  $ \mathrm{K^{+}} $  cations. However, from

Moisture stability of the PSCs based on both 3D and 3D/2D perovskites is tested under ambient atmosphere (with RH of 30%-60%) without encapsulation. Ultimately, transient photovoltage decay (TVD), transient photocurrent decay (TCD) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements are conducted to elucidate the effects of alkali cation doping in 2D perovskite layer on the charge recombination and extraction processes. The recombination lifetime under modulated illumination intensities, extracted from the exponential fitting of TVD spectra, are shown in Figure 5a. Compared to PSCs based on 3D perovskite, PSCs based on 3D/2D perovskite exhibits an overall elongated recombination lifetime under a series of illumination intensities from 0.01-1.2 sun, which is indicative of the passivated trap state in 3D/2D hybrid perovskite film. Introducing alkali cations into the 2D perovskite further retards the charge recombination process, which might be attributed to the synergistic effects of alkali cations on both improving the 2D crystallization and passivating the halide vacancies. In addition, EIS measurement is conducted to uncover the recombination kinetics at the interface under illumination, the Nyquist plots are shown in Figure S13a-e. As the middle frequency zone of the EIS semicircle is dominated by the junction capacitance and recombination resistance of the perovskite/HTM (or electron-transport material, ETM) interfaces, the carrier recombination lifetimes extracted from the EIS ( $  \tau_{rec} = R_{rec}C_g = \frac{1}{f}  $ ) should be equivalent to the recombination lifetimes, $ ^{57} $  the results have been summarized in Figure S13f. It shows increased carrier lifetime with the 2D passivating the surface of 3D perovskite, and further elongated lifetime with alkali cation doping in 2D perovskite, which are consistent with the TVD results.

TCD measurements are performed to reveal the kinetics of carrier extraction within the PSCs. Exponentially fitting the decay curves in Figure 5b affords the transient time  $ (t_{tr}) $  of PSCs based on 3D, 3D/2D, 3D/2D-K, 3D/2D-Rb and 3D/2D-Rb&K to be  $ 5.0~\mu \mathrm{s} $ ,  $ 3.7~\mu \mathrm{s} $ ,  $ 3.6~\mu \mathrm{s} $ ,  $ 3.2~\mu \mathrm{s} $  and  $ 3.2~\mu \mathrm{s} $ , respectively. Compared with 3D perovskite film, the enhanced mobility of 3D/2D film can be explained on the basis of the passivation of defects on the 3D perovskite surface. Further enlarged mobility with alkali doping in 2D perovskite is ascribed to the more desirable crystal orientation for charge transfer and the passivated halide vacancies in 2D perovskite. In conclusion, via investigating the hysteresis-related stability of 3D/2D hybrid PSCs, we find that performance degradation and significant changes in the hysteresis behavior can be observed after aging the devices in ambient atmosphere, despite the fact that the PCE derived from one-direction scans appears to be well-preserved. This could severely compromise the accuracy of stability tests for 3D/2D hybrid PSCs. By correlating the hysteresis of the hybrid PSCs with 2D crystal structure, we uncover the origin of the increased hysteresis to be the numerous crystal imperfections created during the rapid formation of 2D perovskite. These crystal defects are extremely susceptible to humidity attack and accelerate the decomposition of the 2D perovskite to insulating long chain cations, which severely blocks hole transfer to the HTM and causes unbalanced electron/hole extraction. Therefore, to have a superior control over the crystal formation, alkali cations  $ \mathrm{K}^+ $  and  $ \mathrm{Rb}^+ $  are introduced into the 2D perovskite, which induce higher crystallinity of 2D crystallization and better film morphology with less defects. As a result, we obtained an outstanding moisture structural stability of 3D/2D hybrid perovskite, and a corresponding PCE over  $ 21\% $  with impressive moisture and photo stabilities of the hybrid PSCs.

# Materials

Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)-coated glass, formamidinium iodide (FAI), methylammonium bromide (MABr) and butylammonium iodide (BAI) were purchased from Greatcell;   $  PbI_{2}  $   and   $  PbBr_{2}  $   were purchased from Alfa Aesar; bathocuproine (BCP), CsI, RbI, KI, dimethyl sulphoxide (DMSO), isopropanol (IPA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. # Device Fabrication

FTO-coated glasses are cleaned by ultra-sonication in 1% Helmanex surfactant aqueous solution, water and ethanol in sequence (15 min for each). Compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   (c-  $  TiO_{2}  $  , thickness ca. 10-20 nm) blocking layer was deposited onto FTO by spray pyrolysis of 1 mL bis (isopropoxide)-bis(acetylacetonate)titanium (IV) dissolved in 19 mL anhydrous isopropanol (IPA) at 500°C. After cooling down to room temperature, a mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer (m-  $  TiO_{2}  $  , thickness ca. 100-150 nm) was deposited by spin-coating at 4000 rpm for 20s from 30 nm particle paste (Dyesol 30 NR-D) in ethanol (weight ratio 1:6). The film was drying at 100 °C for 10 min followed by sintering at 500°C for 30 min.

For the fabrication of both 3D and 3D/2D hybrid PSCs, the 3D perovskite was developed first. The 3D perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving FAI (1M), PbI $ _2 $  (1.1M), MABr (0.2M), PbBr $ _2 $  (0.22M) and CsI (0.065M) in anhydrous dimethylformamide (DMF):dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (vol% 4:1) solvent with a concentration of 1.32 M. The 3D perovskite thin film was formed by spin-coating the perovskite precursor solution onto the FTO/c-TiO $ _2 $ /m-TiO $ _2 $  substrate using a two-step program: 1000 rpm (ramp 1000 rpm/s) for 10 s and then 6000 rpm (ramp 6000 rpm/s) for 20 s. 200 μL. chlorobenzene (CBZ) was poured onto the substrate 5s prior to the end of the second step. The substrate was then annealing at 100°C for 1 h. After forming the 3D perovskite, the top 3D layer was converted to 2D perovskite by depositing the BAI/IPA solution (with concentration varying from 3 mg/mL to 20 mg/mL) onto the spinning 3D perovskite film at 6000 rpm for 20 s, followed by annealing at 100°C for 10 min. For alkali cation doping, KI, RbI or both KI and RbI were added into BAI/IPA solution with alkali cation/BAI weight ratio varying from 1% to 10%.

For the hole-transporting layer deposition, 60 mM spiro-OMeTAD with 198 mM 4-tert-butylpyridine (tBP), 30 mM bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) and 1.8 mM tris(2-(1H-pyrazol-1-yl)-4-tert-butylpyridine) cobalt (III) tris(bis (trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imde) (FK209) in CBZ was spin-casting onto the perovskite film at 3000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, gold electrode with a thickness of 80 nm was thermally deposited onto the substrate. The active area of the device was 0.16 cm  $  ^{2}  $  .

# Characterizations
