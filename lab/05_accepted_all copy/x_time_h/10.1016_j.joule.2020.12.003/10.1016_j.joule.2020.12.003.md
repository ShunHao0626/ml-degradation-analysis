# D-A-  $  \pi  $  -A-D-type Dopant-free Hole Transport Material for Low-Cost, Efficient, and Stable Perovskite Solar Cells

We report a D-A-π-A-D molecular configuration for the low-cost and efficient dopant-free hole transport materials (HTMs). The suitable energy level and surface passivation effects of DTB-FL HTM effectively inhibit recombination loss and improve charge collection property at hole extraction interface. By replacing the p-doped Spiro with DTB-FL HTM, the perovskite solar cells exhibit higher efficiencies of 21.5% (0.04 cm $  ^{2}  $  active area) and 19.6% (1.0 cm $  ^{2}  $ ) with superior long-term stability under different harsh environments.

# SUMMARY

The development of low-cost and efficient hole transport materials (HTMs) is important for the commercialization of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Comparing with the widely studied D-A-D and D-π-D linear-type small molecule HTMs, DTB-FL with a D-A-π-A-D molecular design is proposed, featuring facile synthesis and excellent optoelectronic properties. Moreover, the HTM with efficient surface passivation effects and proper energy level alignment at the hole extraction interface effectively inhibits recombination loss and improves the charge collection property. As a result, the champion efficiencies of 21.5% and 19.6% for active areas of 0.09 and 1.0 cm², respectively, with superior operational stability are achieved by using DTB-FL HTM. In addition, DTB-FL can also be used as efficient HTM for all-inorganic PSCs, producing an impressive PCE of 17.0% with a high   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.30 V. These results underscore the promising potential of the D-A-π-A-D molecular design in preparing low-cost dopant-free HTMs toward stable and efficient PSCs.

# INTRODUCTION

Hole transport materials (HTMs), as one of the key components in perovskite solar cells (PSCs), can not only provide efficient electron blocking, hole transport, and extraction at the perovskite/HTM interface but can also protect the absorber from the detrimental environmental factors, such as moisture and metal diffusion from ambient and electrode, respectively.  $  ^{1-6}  $   HTMs with good material durability and chemical compatibility with the perovskite can effectively improve device longevity.  $  ^{7-9}  $   Therefore, the development of efficient HTMs becomes an increasingly important strategy to both enhance the performance of PSCs and mitigate device degradation. Currently, Spiro-OMeTAD (abbreviated as Spiro) is still the key HTM used for most of the high-efficiency PSCs, despite its deficiencies being widely discussed. Spiro is expensive as it is synthesized by tedious synthetic routes, which also suffers from the amorphous to crystalline phase transition under thermal stress and causes instability of the thin film morphology.  $  ^{8,10,11}  $   Moreover, additional dopants such as 4-tert-butyl pyridine (TBP) and lithium bis(tri-fluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (Li-TFSI) are required to improve the electrical conductivity of Spiro film for high-performance PSCs, which complicates the device fabrication process. In addition, the ionic diffusion/migration issues of the dopants and their hygroscopic nature will also accelerate the device degradation process.  $  ^{12}  $   These adverse effects limit the application of Spiro for practical PSCs. # Context & Scale

Hole transport material (HTM) serves as one of the main components of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Currently, the development of alternative dopant-free molecular HTMs with facile synthetic routes, desired optoelectronic properties, and good fabrication compatibility for high-performance PSCs is still in urgent need. Herein, we report an efficient and low-cost dopant-free HTM named DTB-FL with a D-A- $  \pi  $ -A-D molecular configuration, which can synergistically improve the interfacial energy level alignment and surface defect passivation effects. Incorporation of the new HTM into MA-free hybrid, large-area (1.0 cm $  ^{2}  $ ), and all-inorganic PSCs produces remarkable power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of 21.5%, 19.6%, and 17.0%, respectively, with superior long-term stability under humidity, thermal, and operational conditions. This work provides important insights into the molecular design of HTM with both practical value and material properties that are required for economic, efficient, and stable PSCs.

Polymer-based HTM is one of the good options as, generally, polymers have good film formation property, but one main drawback is the difficulty to precisely control their molecular weight and polydispersity, leading to batch-to-batch variation in the material property. Conjugated small molecules with more well-defined molecular structures and tunable energy levels are, therefore, being extensively explored to be the dopant-free HTMs for PSCs.  $  ^{13-16}  $

Among the small molecule HTMs that have been developed for PSCs, we can categorize them into three different classes based on their molecular geometries, including 3D spiro-orthogonal-, 2D star-shaped/discotic-, and 1D linear-type small molecules. While all three types of HTMs have been extensively investigated, more attention has recently been concentrated on the linear type HTMs for dopant-free PSCs as devices based on these HTMs showing generally higher PCEs.  $  ^{17}  $   In addition, due to the simpler molecular geometry, a wider variety of molecular building blocks can be selected to construct the linear-type HTMs. Two common design strategies for linear HTMs are D-A-D and D-  $  \pi  $  -D structures, where D, A, and   $  \pi  $   have corresponded to the electron-rich donor, electron-deficient acceptor, and   $  \pi  $  -bridge with relatively weak electron-donating or accepting property, respectively.  $  ^{18-23}  $   For the D-A-D molecules, their highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) can be readily tuned by selecting appropriate D and A building blocks. Usually strong intramolecular charge transfer characteristic is presented in this type of molecules, which not only affects their band-gap and absorption properties but may also enhance the intermolecular packing in the thin film as well as the charge carrier mobility.  $  ^{19,24,25}  $   For the D-  $  \pi  $  -D type HTMs, a   $  \pi  $  -bridge is introduced to increase the   $  \pi  $  -conjugation length of the molecule, aiming to further enhance the packing between the molecules and improve their charge transport property.  $  ^{14}  $

In this work, we propose an efficient dopant-free HTM for PSCs with a D-A-π-A-D molecular configuration that combines the advantages of easy tuning of the energy levels and also the extension of π-conjugation length for improving charge transport property. The chemical structure of the studied HTM, DTB-FL, is shown in Figure 1A. The donor (D) unit based on 4-methoxy-N-(4-methoxyphenyl)-N-phenylaniline has been considered as the efficient terminal group in HTM designs owing to its strong electron-donating, good hole transport, and film formation properties.  $  ^{26}  $   The acceptor (A) unit based on benzothiadiazole features structural planarity, benefiting the enhanced intermolecular stacking.  $  ^{27,28}  $   A 9,9-dialkylfluorene unit with weak electron-donating strength is selected as the middle π-bridge, and such modification not only can ensure good solubility and film formation properties of the molecules, electronically, but also extends the overall π-conjugation length of the molecule and deepens its HOMO energy level. Meanwhile, the 2,7-substitution on 9,9-dialkylfluorene core contributes to a more linear conjugated structure of HTM, which may feature better material properties in contrast to the twisted one created by the 3,6-substitution.  $  ^{29}  $   Modulation of the energy levels is important as appropriate energy level alignment between the valence band of the perovskite and the HOMO of the HTM is required to achieve PSCs with high open circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ). Another two interfacial effects that may affect the performance of the PSCs are the interfacial charge selectivity and surface trap passivation. An energy barrier presented between the conduction band of perovskite and the LUMO of the HTM can improve the hole selectivity by effectively blocking the undesired electrons from reaching the anode and, therefore, reduce interfacial charge recombination at the anode interface. # Molecular Structures and Electronic Properties

The synthetic routes of DT-BT and DTB-FL are shown in Figure 1A, and the synthetic details are provided in the Supplemental Information (Figures S1–S3). Single crystal structure analysis was conducted to provide better understanding of the molecular packing of the two different HTMs. As for the molecular conformation, DT-BT and DTB-FL crystallize with monoclinic and triclinic structures, respectively. The different molecular configurations of DT-BT and DTB-FL in crystal state are presented in Figures 1B–1E. Further crystallographic details are shown in Tables S3–S17. The torsion angles between the benzothiadiazole core and the adjacent phenyl rings in DT-BT were determined to be   $  32.6^{\circ}  $   and   $  42.2^{\circ}  $  , respectively. In contrast, the torsion angles between the benzothiadiazole unit and the adjacent phenyl/fluorenyl rings in DTB-FL are   $  23.0^{\circ}  $  ,   $  31.3^{\circ}  $  ,   $  41.9^{\circ}  $  , and   $  33.5^{\circ}  $  , respectively, indicating a slightly enhanced planarity comparing to that of DT-BT. More importantly, significantly different molecular packing motifs were identified in their single crystals. As shown in Figures 1D–1E, DT-BT presents an obvious angle between the benzothiadiazole cores in the adjacent layers with a herringbone-like packing mode, whereas the fluorene cores of DTB-FL are parallel to each other, forming a lamellar packing structure that is usually more favorable for charge transport, which is in good agreement with the space charge limit current (SCLC) hole mobility results discussed below.

The electronic properties of the HTMs were then investigated by a combination of theoretical and experimental studies. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were used to evaluate the molecular geometries and frontier molecular orbital (FMO) energy levels of different HTMs. Based on the results shown in Figure S9, the backbones of DT-BT and DTB-FL are not planar and a small twisting exists between the building blocks. The HOMO of DT-BT and DTB-FL are mainly localized on the triphenylamine moieties and are partially delocalized to the acceptor units, while the LUMO are concentrated on the acceptor units. It is worth noting that with the introduction of the extra 9,9-dialkylfluorene unit in DTB-FL, a lower calculated HOMO level of -4.61 eV with respect to -4.47 eV of DT-BT was obtained. The calculated HOMO level of Spiro is -4.23 eV (Figure S10), which is shallower than those of the proposed molecules. Deep HOMO level is particularly important for increasing the   $  V_{oc}  $   of PSCs, and the calculated results suggest that DTB-FL shows good potential to achieve this goal.

To evaluate the band gaps and energy levels of the DT-BT and DTB-FL molecules, further studies including cyclic voltammetry, photoelectron spectroscopy, and optical absorption studies were performed. Electrochemical cyclic voltammetry (CV) was first performed to obtain the oxidation potentials of DT-BT and DTB-FL in solution state (Figure S11). With the saturated calomel electrode (SCE) as the reference electrode, the energy level of the molecules was calculated according to the equation:   $  E_{HOMO} = -\left(E_{ox} + 4.5 + 0.24\right)  $   (eV), where   $  E_{ox}  $   is the initial oxidation potential in CV measurement. The calculated HOMO levels of DT-BT and DTB-FL are -5.29 and -5.33 eV, respectively, which is in good agreement with the trend obtained from DFT calculations. To take on step further, we also studied the energy levels of the molecules in a solid thin film using atmospheric photoelectron spectroscopy, which is considered to be a more representative method to determine the HOMO of HTMs.  $  ^{41}  $   Figure 2A shows the photoelectron emission spectra of the DT-BT and DTB-FL films and the HOMO levels are determined to be -5.32 and -5.47 eV, respectively. The UV-vis spectra of both DT-BT and DTB-FL films and solutions show obvious bimodal absorption, which are presented in Figures 2B and S12. # Thin Film Structures and Optoelectronic Properties of Different HTMs

The thin film morphology, electrical properties, and the surface passivation effects of the HTMs were studied. To better understand the thin film structure, the thermal property of the molecules was first evaluated. The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was exploited under a nitrogen atmosphere to study the thermal stability of two synthetic molecules. As shown in Figure S16A, the decomposition temperatures corresponding to 5% weight loss were determined to be   $  429^{\circ}  $  C and   $  439^{\circ}  $  C of DT-BT and DTB-FL, respectively, indicating both small molecules are thermally stable HTMs for PSCs. Upon evaluating these materials in differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurement, a clear glass transition at   $  154^{\circ}  $  C was observed for the DT-BT. As for the DTB-FL, the thermal behavior during the first heating scan exhibits a glass transition with enthalpic relaxation at   $  87^{\circ}  $  C and a cold crystallization of amorphous component at   $  136^{\circ}  $  C, followed by melting at   $  190^{\circ}  $  C (Figure S16B). The surface morphology and molecular ordering of the HTM thin films were investigated by atomic force microscopy (AFM) study. As shown in the AFM topography and phase images in Figure S17, both the DT-BT and DTB-FL molecules form smooth, homogeneous, and pin-hole free thin films with root-mean square roughness (  $  R_{rms}  $  ) less than 1 nm when coated on ITO, suggesting the good film formation property of the molecules. We then coated the molecules on the perovskite film, which has a relatively rough surface with   $  R_{rms}  $   of over 30 nm (Figure S18), to further evaluate their film formation property. Both the molecules form a uniform, good surface coverage with much reduced   $  R_{rms}  $   down to 4.5 and 4.1 nm for DT-BT and DTB-FL films, respectively, which is important to prevent current leakage and facilitate better charge-collection efficiency in the PSCs.  $  ^{49}  $

# Conclusion

In summary, we have reported a low-cost and efficient dopant-free HTM named DTB-FL with a D-A- $ \pi $ -A-D molecular configuration for fabricating stable and high-performance MA-free PSCs. The good film formation quality and the extended  $ \pi $ -conjugation length of DTB-FL ensure the efficient charge transport property of the HTM. More importantly, it was found that the well-matched band alignment and surface passivation effects endow the DTB-FL layer with great benefits in charge transport and extraction property at the perovskite/HTM interface with reduced interfacial recombination loss. As a result, the DTB-FL based PSCs deliver the champion PCEs of  $ 21.5\% $  and  $ 19.6\% $  for small-area  $ (0.09~\mathrm{cm}^2) $  and large-area  $ (1.0~\mathrm{cm}^2) $  devices, respectively, which outperform those based on commonly used p-doped Spiro HTM. Meanwhile, DTB-FL presents a good compatibility to the all-inorganic PSCs. An excellent PCE of  $ 17.0\% $  with a high  $ V_{oc} $  of  $ 1.30\mathrm{V} $  is achieved, which is among the highest reported values for  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_{3 - x}\mathrm{Br}_x $  PSCs based on dopant-free HTM. Furthermore, significant improvements in long-term stability under different aging environments are achieved in the DTB-FL based PSCs, which maintained  $ 76\% $  of the initial PCE value after  $ 600\mathrm{h} $  ambient exposure, maintained  $ 68\% $  of the initial PCE after  $ 240\mathrm{h} $  thermal stress, and maintained  $ 87\% $  of initial PCE after  $ 600\mathrm{h} $  continuous illumination measured at MPP, which are much more stable than the Spiro based PSCs measured under the same condition. Overall, our results demonstrate a promising route toward the facile-fabrication, low-price, and efficient dopant-free HTM for synergistically improving the photovoltaic performance and longevity of PSCs.

Single crystal X-ray crystallography of DT-BT and DTB-FL were collected at 100 and  $ 150\mathrm{K} $ , respectively, on a Rigaku XtaLAB FR-X(Cu) diffractometer with  $ \mathrm{CuK}\alpha $  radiation ( $ \lambda = 1.54184\AA $ ). Data reduction and empirical absorption correction were performed using the CrysAlisPro program. The structure was solved by a dual-space algorithm using SHELXT program. All non-hydrogen atoms could be located directly from the difference Fourier maps. Framework hydrogen atoms were placed geometrically and constrained to the parent atoms using the riding model. Final structure refinement was done using the SHELXL program by minimizing the sum of squared deviations of  $ \mathbf{F}^2 $  using a full-matrix technique.

# Mobility and Trap density Measurements

# Grazing Incident Wide-Angle X-Ray Scattering (GIWAXS)

Measurements were performed at the WAXS beamline at the Australian Synchrotron using 15 keV photons with the 2-dimensional scattering patterns collected by a Dectris Pilatus 2M detector.  $  ^{52}  $   Images taken at the critical angle were used for further data analysis, with the critical angle identified by scanning the X-ray angle of incidence from 0° to 0.15° with intervals of 0.01°, as well as a separate measurement at a high angle of 0.2°. Before starting the measurement on a fresh sample with the predefined series of incident angles, the sample was leveled by iterating a beam alignment procedure, which involves coarse height scan, tiling angle scan, and fine height scan, subsequently. The X-ray exposure time was chosen as 1 s to maximize signal-to-noise ratio and at the same time minimize beam damage. Gapless mode of Pilatus 2M detector was utilized to eliminate the blank gap intrinsic to the detector. The Pilatus 2D detector was mounted within a vacuum tube to minimize the air scattering. The samples, at the same time, were also kept in a separate vacuum chamber during the measurement. Silver behenate was used as the scattering standard to calibrate the physical dimension of detector-sample distance and beam center position.
