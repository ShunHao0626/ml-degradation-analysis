# Abstract

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have drawn significant attention because of high power conversion efficiency, solution processability, large-area device fabrication and low cost. Recently, PSCs have been considered as a promising candidate for the power source of the spacecrafts and satellites in space missions due to their high specific power and cosmic radiation resistance. Herein, we review the main challenges for achieving space-grade perovskite solar cells: light instability, thermal cycling stress and high vacuum-induced issues, as well as the technical advantages with respect to ultra-lightweight, radiation tolerance and upscaling potential. Finally, an outlook on the future development is presented.

# 1. Introduction

The human's curiosity of the universe has only grown as the technology advances. Since Soviet Union launched the first artificial satellite in 1957, more than 6000 spacecrafts have been sent into space for space exploration, earth observation, communication and military uses [1]. To supply the power for space missions which generally last for several years, photovoltaic (PV) technologies have largely overtaken batteries, fuel cells and nuclear power owing to sustainability, safety and cleanliness [2]. For instance, the International Space Station is powered by four sets of solar arrays consisting of 262400 silicon solar cells, generating up to  $ 120\mathrm{kW} $  of electricity [3]. Currently,  $ \square -\square $  multi-junction solar cells are most commonly used in this field as they possess much superior power conversion efficiency (PCE) than conventional crystalline silicon solar cells. However, the thickness of a typical GaInP/GaAs/Ge three-junction space cell in the market is above  $ 200~{\mu\mathrm{m}} $ , which implies high weight and volume [4]. These factors may be insignificant for the terrestrial solar cells, but they impose constraints on the design of space solar arrays and increase the energy consumption of rocket launch. Moreover, the scarcity of material sources and complex fabrication processes of  $ \square -\square $  multi-junction solar cells lead to higher cost by orders of magnitude as compared to the silicon solar modules [5]. Thus, new PV technologies are in great demand for the future space competition.

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs), as the potential game-changer among the terrestrial PVs, have achieved boosted efficiency of  $ 25.2\% $  at an unprecedented pace over the last ten years [6], which is attributed to the understanding on charge carrier dynamics, defect physics and interface energetics, as well as unremitting efforts on the compositional engineering, crystallization control, defect passivation and interface modification [7-14]. Most importantly, PSCs hold potential to be the low-cost leader in market in case the modules can guarantee a lifetime of 15 years [15]. Nevertheless, the longest lifetime of PSCs reported so far is only about 1 year, confined by the instability issues while operating in the earth atmosphere, especially upon oxygen and moisture exposure [16-19]. Thus, an interesting question emerges: can PSCs survive longer in space where the air density is extremely thin and no water exists? Recent studies demonstrate significant advantages of PSCs in lightweight and tolerance under high-energy particle irradiations, which exhibit the potential qualification as a space market entrant [20-22]. To this end, it is needed to seriously evaluate the prospect of PSCs for space application.

In fact, the space environment brings other hazards. As shown in Figure 1, according to the altitude with reference to the earth, the satellite orbits can be divided into Low Earth Orbit (LEO, below  $ 2000\mathrm{km} $ ), Medium Earth Orbit (MEO, 2000 -

Without protection of the earth atmosphere, the sun's damaging ultraviolet (UV) radiation would directly cast on the solar panels, which is a real challenge for the stability of materials.

The possible solutions and proposed research directions are also provided. We hope that this review can draw more attention of academic researchers and manufacturers on these aspects.

# 2. Challenges

# 2.1 Light instability

Extraterrestrial solar cells are usually characterized using air mass zero (AM0) solar spectrum. The major difference compared with AM1.5G used terrestrially is that AM0 contains higher extent of UV radiation, which is a big challenge for PSCs going into space. In terrestrial condition, this may not be a problem since a UV filter can easily protect devices. However, for a space-grade solar cell, any additional weight should be considered to avoid in order to cut down the cost, and any “filtered-out” PCE is economic as well as uncompromising.

It is generally agreed that the UV instability of PSCs originates from the photocatalysis of inorganic metal oxides, and UV-B (310-317 nm) has been claimed more detrimental than UV-A (360-380 nm) [26]. Historically, molecular oxygen in the atmosphere was believed to participate in the UV degradation process of   $  TiO_{2}  $  -based PSCs. Oxygen absorption and desorption at the oxygen vacancy sites in   $  TiO_{2}  $   create deep level traps leading to charge recombination and photocurrent degradation [27]. However, recent study by Ji et. al revealed a two-stage detailed UV degradation process of PSCs even in an inert atmosphere, which argues the possibility of oxygen-free space environment being the harbor (Figure 2a) [28]. First, the   $  Ti^{3+}  $   defects caused by inherent oxygen vacancy transform to   $  Ti^{4+}  $   defects as a result of the charge recombination with UV-generated holes in the valence band of   $  TiO_{2}  $ . The   $  Ti^{4+}  $   defect states serve as electron traps that provide extra nonradiative recombination centers in devices. Then, increasing   $  Ti^{4+}  $   defects turn to oxidize I and lead to irreversible degradation of perovskites upon formation of   $  I_{2}  $   or polyiodide   $  I_{3}^{-}  $ .

Similar behavior can happen to other metal oxides depending on their photocatalysis ability. Thus, it is critical to utilize UV-inert materials as charge extraction layers for space solar cells, e.g.   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   [27],   $  Zn_{2}SnO_{4}  $   [29], La-doped   $  BaSnO_{3}  $   [30],   $  ZnTiO_{3}  $   [31], etc., Even so, the efficiency changes in those materials-based PSCs are over 10% after exposing to UV radiation for only hundreds of hours, which is far from the requirement of space missions.

The impact of UV radiation on bare perovskites is usually considered optimistic. Theoretically, the photon energy in the UV range could be high enough to break the chemical bonds in organic materials, especially polymers. The photostability of perovskites was found to closely relate to the optical absorption of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ , which implies that blue light at a wavelength of  $ 465~\mathrm{nm} $  brings no more damage than green

The study highlights the important role of hole conductors in addressing the UV stability of PSCs, and indicates the severe lack of understanding in the UV degradation mechanism regarding a complete device.

# 2.2 Thermal cycling stress

Although the satellites are always equipped with powerful thermal control systems to maintain the components at acceptable temperatures, the current PV modules must pass a standard thermal cycling test before considering their use in space (American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics, AIAA S-111A-2014 [34]). The test includes subjecting the modules to a cold extreme with temperature down to -185 □ and a hot extreme with temperature up to 150 □. This is the second challenge for PSCs as they usually suffer efficiency loss at a testing temperature of 85 □ in the terrestrial-grade stability assessment.

Dedicated investigations on the thermal cycling behavior of PSCs are lacking, and the few existing reports follow the terrestrial standard with temperature range between -40 □ and 85 □ [35-37]. Rather than the direct damage of high or low temperature, mechanical failure (interfacial delamination) dominates the performance decay during the cycles, which is attributed to the mismatched thermal expansion coefficients of each layer in a device. Robust encapsulation is thus indispensable to maintain the mechanical stability.[38] Cheacharoen et. al performed comparative trials between two commonly used encapsulants, Ethylene vinyl acetate (EVA) and Surlyn (Figure 3a) [35]. It was found that EVA with low elastic modulus provide better protection during the temperature cycles (Figure 3b). Further adoption of polyolefin together with an outer butyl rubber edge seal enabled the efficiency unchanged after 200 temperature cycles, which makes a decent start of PSCs before facing the markedly enlarged temperature range in space [36].

Bear in mind that the hot extreme is up to 150 □. Even though the mechanical stability could be guaranteed, under such thermal stress, perovskite devices can undergo material degradation [39, 40], ion diffusion [41, 42], and metal migration [43], all leading to notable performance loss. Therefore, each layer in the device stack should be designed targeting higher thermal stability than the current state of studies.

Our earlier work revealed that the decomposition reaction of a typical mixed perovskite   $  Cs_{0.05}(MA_{0.17}FA_{0.83})_{0.95}Pb(I_{0.83}Br_{0.17})_{3}  $   (FA: formamidinium) starts at the surface followed by gradual permeation into the bulk, which originates from the intrinsic dangling bonds and defects on the imperfect surface [44]. During the degradation process, the loss of iodide element was found to accompany with the decomposition of MA, while the other components remained stable (Figure 3c). Similarly, Long et al. reported the synchronized disappearance of   $  FA^{+}  $  and   $  Br^{-}  $  in   $  Cs_{x}FA_{1-x}PbI_{y}Br_{3-y}  $   above 100 □ [45]. Therefore, to survive at the hot extreme,

Further decreasing the temperature to -173 would increase the charge transfer barrier between the perovskite and adjacent charge transport layers [57], possibly due to the contact detachment. More evidence of the PSCs performance at low temperature conditions is still needed to address these challenges.

# 2.3 High vacuum

The high vacuum of space brings potential challenges since many volatile materials are involved in perovskite devices. Molecules can easily outgas in high vacuum and are prone to deposit on the cold surface during the thermal cycling, which leads to contamination and influenced properties of sensitive optics. Specific outgassing test methods have been established, known as the ASTM E1559 standard, to access the information related to the total mass loss and volatile condensable materials so as to predict the contamination outgassing characteristics [58]. PSCs have not yet come to this stage of productions, however, the effects of vacuum on the PSCs should be unraveled first.

In our previous work, perovskite films underwent much more violent degradation under UHV (  $  \sim10^{-9}  $  Torr) than at normal pressure [59]. As shown in Figure 4a, a large number of pores appear on the perovskite film after 24 h white-light illumination in UHV, and X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) confirms that 50% amount of the N and I related species escape from the MAPb(  $  I_{0.83}Br_{0.17}  $  )  $  _{3}  $  films as gas product, together with 38% Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  reduced to metallic Pb (Figure 4b). Similar phenomena were observed in more sensitive two-dimensional perovskite films, which points to the fact that the vacuum acts as catalyst in the degradation process by causing outgassing [60].

In the case of complete devices, vacuum was revealed to accelerate the ion migration even in dark condition, leading to reaction between halide ions and metal electrode [61]. Doubtlessly, this can be more profound in working devices under illumination. Recently, Jiang et. al observed a ten-times-faster degradation of devices kept in the pressure of  $ 5 \times 10^{3} $  Pa than  $ 9 \times 10^{4} $  Pa during operation [62]. By introducing robust interlayers composed of zinc-oxide (ZnO) and aluminum doped ZnO (AZO), devices realized a low PCE loss rate of  $ 0.007\%/\mathrm{h} $  at low pressure, which is attributed to the mitigation of ion mobilization (Figure 4c, d).

On the basis of these findings, it is crucial to design devices with minimal ion migration and outgassing issues, as well as ensure that the encapsulation techniques (including the edge seal) can effectively screen the vacuum effect.

# 3. Technical advantages

# 3.1 Ultra lightweight and flexibility

Apart from high efficiencies, the key metric of space solar arrays is the specific power (power-to-weight ratio), which to a large extent determines the weight of payloads and thus the launch cost. Flexibility, on the other hand, can improve the compatibility of the energy-generating system in order to simplify the rocket design. These metrics are particularly important for the growing market of high-altitude UAVs, where the power input is relatively less demanding due to the reduced life expectancies. In these regards, the high absorption coefficient and soft material nature of perovskites enable

9

Journal Pre-proof

PSCs to surpass their rivals.

Generally, perovskite active layer with a thickness of  $ \sim 400\mathrm{nm} $  is sufficient for light harvesting, which is 3 orders of magnitude thinner than that of crystalline silicon absorbers [63]. Flexible PSCs based on a  $ 20 - \mu \mathrm{m} $ -thick PET substrate could yield high specific power of  $ 5.07\mathrm{kWkg^{-1}} $  [64]. As the thickness of the substrates reduces to 1.4  $ \mu \mathrm{m} $ , the areal density of PSCs could be as low as  $ 5.2\mathrm{g}\mathrm{m}^{-2} $  and the specific power remarkably increased to  $ 23\mathrm{kWkg^{-1}} $ , overtaking any other PVs (Figure 5a, b) [65]. The overall thickness of the PSCs is less than  $ 3\mu \mathrm{m} $  (including  $ 1\mu \mathrm{m} $  polymer capping), which is one order of magnitude thinner than other thin film PVs. In a recent study, the record high specific power of  $ 29.4\mathrm{kWkg^{-2}} $  of a flexible PSC with a PCE of  $ 15.18\% $  was reported by Kang et al. via the employment of  $ 1.3\mu \mathrm{m} $ -thick polyethylene naphthalate (PEN) foils and orthogonal silver nanowire transparent electrodes [20].

It should be noted that the specific power of manufactured products is in fact driven by the weight of package and interconnects, which is not involved in the reports of flexible PSCs mentioned above (Figure 5c) [63]. As a relative fair comparison, the leading manufacturer of thin-film GaAs PVs, Alta Devices, present their state-of-the-art products with an areal density of  $ 114\mathrm{g}\mathrm{m}^{-2} $  and specific power of 3.02 kW  $ \mathrm{kg}^{-1} $  without package [66]. MicroLink Devices display their best triple junction space solar cell with an excellent efficiency of  $ 37.75\% $ , featuring the similar unpackaged specific power of  $ 3\mathrm{kwkg}^{-1} $  [67]. By considering a minimizing package at  $ 300\mathrm{g}\mathrm{m}^{-2} $ , an upper bound of  $ 1.167\mathrm{kWkg}^{-1} $  for  $ 35\% $  efficiency  $ \square -\square $  solar modules has been calculated and elucidated by Reese et. al [63]. These values already stand for the best performance among the space market incumbents, which instead highlight the superiority of PSCs in lightweight.

So far, the efficiency of flexible PSCs has exceeded  $ 19\% $  [68], and wonderful sustainability under 10000 cycles of bending at a  $ 0.5\mathrm{mm} $  radius has been demonstrated [69]. However, the metric of specific power is usually neglected among the research community. We thus call for niche products which are fully designed for space application to rigorously describe the specific power performance of extraterrestrial perovskite solar panels.

# 3.2 High-energy particle irradiation tolerance

Spacecrafts and satellites always face the particulate and ionizing radiation caused by the galactic cosmic rays and solar particle events. 1 MeV electrons or 10 MeV protons are commonly used as the equivalent fluences to evaluate the damage effect [34, 70]. One of the main reasons why GaAs-based space solar cells cannot reach high specific power is these materials promptly degrade under the high-energy particle irradiation. Cumbersome radiation shielding is thus indispensable and brings in additional mass to the solar arrays. For PSCs, the cosmic radiation hardness has been reported with regard to electron [22, 71-73], proton [21, 71-77], gamma-ray [78-81] and neutron [82], which delivers positive signals that PSCs could withstand the harsh radiation environment in space.

As the forerunners on this topic, Miyasaka and co-workers reported stable  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -based PSCs retaining  $ 92\% $  of their initial efficiency after irradiation with 1 MeV electron at a particle fluence of  $ 1\times 10^{16}\mathrm{cm}^{-2} $ , and the external quantum efficiencies (EQE) remained nearly constant (Figure 6a) [71]. As a contrast, the silicon and  $ \square -\square $  compound solar cells retained only  $ 60\% $  and  $ 62\% $  efficiencies after the same treatment, respectively. Nickel and co-workers observed that under proton

irradiation at 68 MeV, the damage threshold of proton dose to perovskite absorber is  $ 10^{12} $  p cm $ ^{-2} $ , which is 3 orders of magnitude higher than that of crystalline silicon (Figure 6b) [75]. Meanwhile, there is no loss in either open-circuit voltage ( $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $ ) or fill factor (FF) even at a higher proton dose of  $ 10^{13} $  p cm $ ^{-2} $ .

Beyond those observations, the fundamental mechanisms governing this radiation tolerance has not been well studied and understood. A possible explanation is that the photodegradation-induced deep traps could be partially compensated by the unintentional doping effects upon proton irradiation, resulting from the displacements of atoms in the inorganic Pb–I framework [77]. Recently, Lang et. al established a kinetic model to describe the prolonged photoluminescence (PL) decay observed in   $  Cs_{0.05}MA_{0.17}FA_{0.83}Pb(I_{0.83}Br_{0.17})_{3}  $   perovskite after radiation treatment [21]. It was found that the proton irradiation indeed creates trap states that locate   $  \sim0.3  $   eV within the bandgap of perovskite. However, the detrapping of the trapped minority charge carriers is so efficient that leaves neglect contribution to the recombination kinetics in devices (Figure 6c).   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   is generally less stable than the mixed-cation perovskites, but it still produces strong resistance to gamma rays due to the rapid self-healing process (Figure 6d) [80].

Though perovskite absorber could withstand high-energy particle irradiation, some reports indicated the potential concerns on charge transport layers in such environment. For example, spiro-OMeTAD was found to degrade at the proton dose of  $ >10^{14} $  p cm $ ^{-2} $ , evidenced by the decreased characteristic-peak intensity in Raman spectra [74]. On the other hand, the devices using pre-exposed phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PC $ _{61} $ BM) layers under gamma ray exhibited lower current densities and efficiencies than the reference samples, implying the radiation instability of such widely employed electron transport material [81]. Therefore, more efforts should be focused on the properties of charge transport layers to make high-performance perovskite space solar cells. Overall, the significant features in the radiation hardness not only indicate better long-term reliability of the perovskite space solar cells, but also promise the reduction of the radiation-shielding layer, further reducing the cost.

# 3.3 Upscaling potential

III-V multi-junction solar cells are fabricated using the metal-organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD) reactors, which provide good reproducibility but higher cost and prolonged growth durations [83]. In this regard, solution-processed PSCs exhibit an impressive compatibility for high-throughput industry-scale fabrication via multiple strategies, such as drop casting, inkjet printing, spray coating, slot die and blade coating [84-87]. For example, Lin et al. obtained micrometer-scale perovskite grains via a solution-printing technic and the PCE of the corresponding devices exceeded  $ 20\% $  [88]. PV module manufacturers have already been trying to bring the

Panasonic Corporation (Japan) fabricated the world's largest PSC module of  $ 802~\mathrm{cm}^2 $  with a certified efficiency of  $ 16.1\% $  [90].

In view of the challenges in acquiring space-grade PSCs, we propose that the further upscaling efforts should take the elimination/replacement of metal electrodes, organic hole conductors and the photocatalytic   $  TiO_{2}  $   into account. As substitutes, carbon-based materials are cost-effective, thermally stable, highly conductive and large-scale processable. The efficiency of carbon-electrode hole-conductor-free PSCs has exceeded 17% [91], and the superior stability makes the all-air fabrication available [92]. Noticeably, carbon-based devices in most reports still contain   $  TiO_{2}  $   as electron transport layer for the purpose of better efficiencies [93]. An upscaling attempt by this approach demonstrated a PCE of 12.1% on a monolithic perovskite module with an active area of   $  70\ cm^{2}  $   (Figure 7b) [95]. Although the thermal and UV stability was untested, the modified devices retained performance for over 6 months of storage in ambient air without encapsulation and 60 h of maximum power point tracking, which encourage further investigations in this technic.

# 4. Outlook

The real-space experiments of PSCs are in progress. In 2018, Cardinaletti et al. first launched PSCs to the stratosphere at an altitude of  $ 32\mathrm{km} $  in a so-called OSCAR

Unfortunately, the encapsulation layers were damaged first even at moderately low pressure, leading to severe performance loss. More recently, Zhu and co-workers performed the similar experiments by using well-encapsulated PSCs with a UV filter [97]. After 10-years development, PSCs have reached the initial phase of industrialization. The key challenges for the space application are instabilities under UV illumination, thermal cycling and high vacuum. To address these issues, some comments on future research directions are given below.

(i) Down-conversion materials can absorb high-energy photons in the UV range and convert them to visible light, which was claimed to not only protect devices under UV radiation, but also improve PCEs [98, 99]. Such effect may be magnified under AM0 spectrum and thus is worth further investigations.

(ii) Cs-based inorganic perovskites are expected to play a crucial role for perovskite space solar cells, as they currently present the best thermal stability and decent phase stability under high and low temperature, respectively, among the perovskite families. There is still large room for improving their performance.

(iii) The efficiency for carbon-based perovskite solar modules reached  $ 10\% $  in 2017. The past three years witnessed no significant progress due to the difficulties of attaining high-quality large-area perovskite films. A breakthrough in this field may rewrite the rules for both terrestrial and extraterrestrial applications.

(iv) Since it is unlikely for the efficiency of PSCs to challenge that of □-□ multijunction solar cells, how to take full advantages of high specific power is critical for PSCs to start their way to specialized markets such as UAV and micro-satellites. Scalable fabrication of flexible PSCs requires more endeavor from our research community.

(v) Minimizing the package weight while sustaining the stability requires improvement in the encapsulation technics as well as standardized stability assessment under simulated space environment. Particularly, the thermal-cycling test of PSCs is essential before considering applications in space.

Unlike the 20-years demand from the terrestrial market, at the present stage, the relatively short lifetime of PSCs may satisfy pseudo-satellites/LEO satellites, usually designed for a few months or less than 5 years. The promising features of PSCs match
