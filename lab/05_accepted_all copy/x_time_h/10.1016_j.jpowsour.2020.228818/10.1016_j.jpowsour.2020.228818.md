# 1. Introduction

Over the past few years, solid-state photo-voltaic devices based on hybrid organic-inorganic lead halide perovskite active layers have been developed as one of the most promising candidates for a renewable clean solar technology   $  [1-4]  $  . The power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been rapidly increased from 3.8% to the recently reported highest value of 25.2%   $  [5-11]  $  .

The high performances of PSCs were attributed mainly to the wide range of visible spectrum absorption, large carrier diffusion length, and

Organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells, whose highest power conversion efficiency (PCE) already exceeds 25%, have been attracting considerable attention. However, there are still various challenges, such as their low repeatability, large number of intrinsic defects, and low environmental stability. In this study, we dope monovalent thallium (Tl  $  ^{+}  $  ) into the precursor to form a pinhole-free perovskite film with improved photo-voltaic performance and environmental stability. The incorporation of Tl  $  ^{+}  $  into CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}  $  PbI  $  _{3}  $  can improve the crystal quality of perovskite film by adjusting the rate of crystalline growth. Due to the non-equivalent doping of Tl  $  ^{+}  $ , the Fermi level is lowered by 0.31 eV toward the highest occupied molecular orbital, indicating that Tl behaves as a p-type dopant. An improved carrier dynamics and balanced charge transportation can be observed in MAPb  $  _{0.99}  $  Tl  $  _{0.01}  $  I  $  _{3}  $ . After the doping of 1 mol% Tl into the perovskite film, the average PCE of the cells is increased from 15.1 to 17.8%. Furthermore, the thallium-doped perovskite solar cell exhibits outstanding environmental stability because of the improved moisture resistance. After 200 h of storage under ambient conditions, the Tl-doped perovskite solar cell retains 82.7% of its initial power conversion efficiency, which is higher than the retention of the intrinsic perovskite device (55.9%).

However, these technologies were not focused on the energy alignment of the perovskite or alteration of the intrinsic character of the light absorber. In this regard, element substitutions have been studied to tune the band gap, improve the crystallinity, and thus increase the device performance [12].

In general, perovskite materials have the common chemical formula of   $  ABX_{3}  $  , in which A is an organic or inorganic cation, B is commonly Pb, and X is usually a halogen atom [30–38]. Extensive studies have been carried out on the substitutions of elements or chemical groups, e.g.,   $  Cs^{+}  $  ,   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}^{+}  $   (  $  MA^{+}  $  ), or   $  \mathrm{CH(NH_{2})_{2}^{+}}  $   (  $  FA^{+}  $  ) at A sites,   $  Pb^{2+}  $  ,   $  Sn^{2+}  $  ,   $  Ge^{2+}  $  ,   $  Sr^{2+}  $  ,   $  Cd^{2+}  $  ,   $  Mn^{2+}  $  , or   $  Ca^{2+}  $   at B sites, and   $  I^{-}  $  ,   $  Br^{-}  $  ,   $  Cl^{-}  $  ,   $  BF_{4}^{-}  $  ,   $  BF_{6}^{-}  $  , or   $  SCN^{-}  $   at X sites, which have significantly improved the performances of PSCs [12,39–41]. For example, monovalent cesium is a good element to replace the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}^{+}  $   group in perovskites with [40]. Nevertheless,   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   does not have a suitable band gap (1.73 eV) as a light absorber [42]. The large band gap leads to a narrowed light absorption range despite the high open-circuit voltage [43]. Furthermore, the   $  \alpha  $  -phase of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   uncontrollably degrades to the   $  \delta  $  -phase when the temperature decreases from 300 °C to room temperature (around 25 °C) [42]. Therefore, the dual-halide perovskite   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   was developed owing to its improved environmental stability [44]. In addition, some other types of perovskite partially substituted at A or X sites (e.g.,   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $   or   $  FAPbI_{3}  $  ) suffer from low structural stabilities or improper band gaps [45,46]. In general, the substitutions of elements or groups at A or X sites obviously change the unit cell and thus, lead to significant changes in band gap and phase stability [12]. Owing to the large changes, it is usually challenging to carefully balance the various performances of the PSCs.

Recently, Chen et al. have used Ag to partially substitute Pb as a P-type dopant in a perovskite. In their study, a 0.5%-doped sample has exhibited the highest performances including a PCE, open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ), short-circuit current density (  $  J_{sc}  $  ), and fill factor (FF) of 18.4%, 1.1 V, 20.6 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and 81%, respectively [32]. In contrast, their control device has exhibited lower performances including a PCE,   $  V_{oc}  $  ,   $  J_{sc}  $  , and FF of 16.0%, 1.09 V, 19.3 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and 76%, respectively. Similarly, Hu et al. have used Bi as a dopant to partially replace Pb in FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  , which has yielded an improved PCE of 17.78%, 36.56% higher than that of the control device [30]. Their study was focused only on the substitution with trivalent Bi  $  ^{3+}  $   for a conventional device structure. However, the energy alignment shift issues related to the p- or n-type behaviors were not analyzed.

In this study, we use monovalent thallium (Tl  $  ^{+}  $  ), which is a Group III element in the periodic table, to partially replace Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  at B sites. The photovoltaic performance and environmental stability of devices are improved simultaneously. The non-equivalent doping of Tl  $  ^{+}  $  can downshift the Fermi level of the light absorber toward the highest-occupied molecular orbital (HOMO), which improves the carrier dynamics and balances the charge transport. Due to the incorporation of Tl  $  ^{+}  $ , the perovskite grain size is increased from 300 to 500 nm on average, suggesting an improved crystal quality. The experimental group exhibits an improved PCE of 17.8% (1 mol% incorporation of TII), 17.9% higher than that of the control group. Furthermore, the Tl-doped light absorber exhibits an increased moisture resistance, which improves the environmental stability. The optimized sample exhibits an excellent environmental stability. It retains 82.7% of the initial PCE after 200 h of storage at 25 °C and a humidity of 35%; this may be attributed to the improved perovskite crystal quality. In comparison, the control group retains 55.9% of the pristine PCE under the same conditions.

# 2. Experimental methods

# 2.1. Chemicals and reagents

$ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ , TII, chlorobenzene (CB), dimethylsulphoxide (DMSO, anhydrous,  $ 99.8\% $ ), and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Phenyl- $ \mathrm{C}_{61} $ -butyric acid methyl esters

(PCBM) was purchased from Nano-C Inc. (USA). Methylammonium iodide (MAI) and 2,9-dimethyl-4,7-diphenyl-1,10-phenanthroline (BCP) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corporation (China). Patterned indium tin oxide (ITO) glasses were purchased from Ying Kou You Xuan Trade Co. Ltd. (China).

# 2.2. Device fabrication

The PSCs were fabricated on the ITO glasses with configurations of ITO/poly(3,4-ethylenedioxylenethiophene):poly(styrene sulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS)/perovskite/PCBM/BCP/Ag, as shown in Fig. 1(a). The glass substrates with ITO anodes were sequentially cleaned with detergent, deionized water, and isopropanol (IPA), and then treated with ultraviolet ozone (UV–O $  _{3}  $ ) for 20 min. PEDOT:PSS (Clevious P VP AI4083, Heraeus, Germany) was spin-coated on the ITO glass at 4000 rpm for 30 s, and then thermal annealing was carried out at 140 °C for 15 min. The perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving PbI $  _{2}  $ , TII, and MAI in anhydrous DMF in a molar ratio of 1–x:x:1 (0 ≤ x ≤ 0.05) to obtain a solution with a concentration of 1 M, which was then stirred for 15 h at 60 °C. The perovskite film was deposited by spin-coating the precursor solution onto the PEDOT:PSS-coated sample at 4000 rpm for 30 s in a nitrogen-filled glove box. During the spin coating, CB (200 μL) was quickly dropped onto the sample at a delay time of 8 s. The sample was then annealed on a hot plate at 100 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, 50 μL of a PC $  _{61}  $ BM solution (30 mg mL $  ^{-1}  $  in CB) was spin-coated at 1500 rpm for 30 s to form an electron-transport layer (ETL). Subsequently, 70 μL of a BCP solution (0.5 mg mL $  ^{-1}  $  in IPA) was spin-coated onto the surface of the ETL at 4000 rpm. Finally, a 100-nm-thick Ag electrode was coated on top of the sample (working area of 0.1 cm $  ^{2}  $ ) by using a thermal evaporator under a high vacuum (<6.0 × 10 $  ^{-6}  $  Torr).

# 2.3. Characterization

Top-view and cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were acquired by using a JSM-7500F field-emission SE microscope (JOEL, Japan) at an acceleration voltage of 20 kV. Steady-state photo-luminescence (PL) and time-resolved photo-luminescence (TRPL) spectra were measured by using a spectrometer (FLS920, Edinburgh Instruments, UK). Current-density-voltage (J-V) characteristics of the PSCs were measured at an irradiation intensity of 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  (AM 1.5). The external quantum efficiencies (EQEs) of the PSCs were measured by using an incident-photon-to-current efficiency measurement system (Solar Cell Scan 100, Zolix, China). The space-charge-limited current (SCLC) of a hole-only device (ITO/PEDOT/(MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  or MAPb  $  _{0.99}  $  Tl  $  _{0.01}  $  I  $  _{3}  $  )/MoO  $  _{3}  $ /Ag) was measured by using a 2400 Source Meter in a dark environment at a bias voltage of 1 V. Electro-chemical impedance spectroscopy was performed in a dark environment by using an electro-chemical workstation (CH Instruments, USA). Ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption spectra were recorded by using a UV-vis-near-infrared 3600 spectrometer (Shimadzu, Japan). X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurement was carried out by using a Rigaku Smart Lab X-ray diffractometer (Japan) with Cu K  $  _{a}  $  radiation (  $  \lambda = 1.5418  $  Å) at room temperature. Ultraviolet photo-electron spectroscopy (UPS) was carried out by using a Kratos Axis Ultra X-ray photo-electron spectroscope (Kratos Analytical, UK). X-ray photo-electron spectroscopy (XPS) was carried out by using a PHI 5300 ESCA PerkinElmer spectrometer.

The 1%-Tl-incorporated sample exhibited the largest grain size, increased from approximately 300 to 500 nm on average, which may be attributed to the retarded crystal growth by the Tl doping. The larger grain size of the 1%-Tl-doped perovskite film may imply a denser structure with less grain boundaries compared to the pristine perovskite film [31]. The 5%-Tl-doped group showed an obviously degraded spectrum, especially at wavelengths near 600 nm, indicating that excessive Tl doping can degrade the crystal quality. The   $  J_{sc}  $   values calculated from integration of the EQE spectra matched well with the electrically measured values with differences of 2.1–3.4%, indicating the high reliability of our optical method for measuring the EQEs. The second factor is related with the energy alignment shift of the perovskite contributing to the photo-electronic performance improvement of the device. To study the changes in photo-electric properties, PL and TRPL spectra of samples with configurations of glass/perovskite (original or Tl-doped   $  MAPbI_{3}  $  ) were measured. As shown in Fig. 4(a), the Tl-doped samples with doping ratios of 0.5 and 1% exhibited increased peaks. The peak intensity decreased with the increase in Tl doping ratio in the range of 2–5%. The TRPL data were consistent with the PL spectra.   $  MAPb_{0.99}Tl_{0.01}I_{3}  $   exhibited the largest lifetime of 64.7 ns, larger than that (25.3) of the pristine sample. The higher peak intensity in the PL spectrum and larger lifetime in the TRPL data suggest a higher photovoltaic performance of   $  MAPb_{0.99}Tl_{0.01}I_{3}  $  , consistent with the SEM and XRD results. At the Tl concentration of 5.0%, the PL lifetime was decreased to 19.7 ns. These data are consistent with the J-V characteristics in Fig. 3(a) and suggest that a proper doping ratio (1%) of Tl might passivate the defects in the perovskite crystal and reduce the number of nonradiative recombination centers. However, excess concentration of Tl could affect the original perovskite lattice and lead to more intrinsic defects and decreased crystallinity.

As discussed above, the Tl incorporation into the perovskite could improve the morphology and crystallinity and reduce the number of intrinsic defects. # 4. Conclusions

We propose a B-site doping of monovalent Tl to obtain a  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $

PSC with improved PCE and environmental stability. The non-equivalent   $  Tl^{+}  $  lowered the Fermi level by 0.31 eV, indicating the enhanced p-type character. Among the PSCs with various Tl doping ratios, the optimized device with the doping ratio of 1% exhibited a PCE,   $  J_{sc}  $  ,   $  V_{oc}  $  , and FF of 17.8%, 21.5 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , 1.06 V, and 0.78, higher than those of the pristine PSC (15.1%, 19.2 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , 1.05 V, and 0.75, respectively). An appropriate doping ratio (  $  \leq  $  1%) of Tl into the perovskite likely suppressed the nucleation during the deposition, and finally improved the crystal quality of perovskite. An increased grain size from 300 to 500 nm on average can be observed from SEM images. SCLC data indicated that the Tl incorporation decreased the trap density in the perovskite film, from   $  8.74 \times 10^{15}  $   to   $  4.68 \times 10^{15}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  , suggesting suppressed inherent defects. A higher environmental stability of PSCs can be observed due to the enhanced hydrophobic properties of perovskite film. Notably, 82.7% of the initial PCE was retained after 200 h of storage, making its retention higher than that (55.9%) of the control sample.
