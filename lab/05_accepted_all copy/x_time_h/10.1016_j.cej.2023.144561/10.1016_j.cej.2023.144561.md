# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic metal halide perovskites with the  $ \mathrm{ABX}_3 $  structure (where A is  $ \mathrm{MA}^+ $ ,  $ \mathrm{FA}^+ $ ,  $ \mathrm{Cs}^+ $  or  $ \mathrm{Rb}^+ $ , B is  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  or  $ \mathrm{Sn}^{2+} $ , and X is  $ \mathrm{Cl}^- $ ,  $ \mathrm{Br}^- $  or I) have gained significant attention in the photovoltaic field due to their fascinating optoelectronic properties [1-5]. # ABSTRACT

Inverted perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted tremendous attention due to their compatibility in flexible and tandem devices, thus with great commercialization potential. However, abundant crystal defects and energy level mismatch still remain throughout the devices, which cause nonradiative recombination and become the two main issues impeding the performance that need to be solved urgently. In this work, we have adopted a co-passivation strategy by incorporating PEACl (phenethylammonium chloride) into perovskite films and adding PPF (2,8-Bis(diphenyl-phosphoryl)-dibenzo[b,d]furan) in anti-solvent. It is found that PEACl could significantly passivate the defects in the perovskite bulk and grain boundaries. PPF could reinforce the passivation efficacy by forming coordination bond between PPF and   $  Pb^{2+}  $   (  $  P=O\rightarrow Pb  $  ). It also forms a thin n-type hole-blocking layer between perovskite film and electron transport layer, which increases photo-generated charge carrier lifetimes and decreases nonradiative recombination at carrier transport interfaces. Furthermore, after being co-passivated with PEACl and PPF (PEACl/PPF treated), an energy level gradient is formed between the perovskite and passivated layer, which is conducive to electron extraction and hole blocking, further resulting in less energy loss in the charge transfer process. As a result, the PEACl/PPF treated device obtained a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 22.70% with an impressive open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ) of 1.19 V, much higher than the corresponding values (19.51% and 1.06 V) of the control device.

Since the   $  V_{oc}  $   of PSCs is obtained from the quasi Fermi level splitting of electron and hole carriers, the defect density has the greatest impact on the open-circuit voltage [19]. Furthermore, structural defects also degrade the fill factor (FF), finally lowering the PCE of PSCs [20]. In addition, the ions (e.g.,   $  MA^{+}  $   and   $  I^{-}  $  ) in perovskite film have a low activation energy for migration (<0.1 eV) and can easily move [22]. Also the volatile organic components will easily evaporate from the defect points under moisture, oxygen, high temperature or high light intensity atmosphere, leading to the degradation of perovskite materials and then the PSC device [23–26]. These structural defects will not only affect photovoltaic parameters negatively but also decrease the device operational stability. Thus, effective strategies to reduce trap defects across perovskite films are crucial and urgently needed.

Another kind of passivation method is surface post treatment of the crystallized perovskite films with the passivating agent. You's group spin-coated PEAI on annealed perovskite film, which as a result increased the PCE to  $ 23.56\% $  [20]. However, only the top surface area can be passivated via posttreatment since the annealed perovskite film is difficult to be penetrated by PEAI [18]. To solve this issue, in-film treatment strategy was proposed by dissolving the passivating materials into the antisolvent. This facilitates the penetration of passivation materials into the perovskite film during mesophase formation [31], which could passivate the upper grain boundaries and surfaces of perovskite films. Meng's group dissolved tribenzylphosphine oxide (TBPO) into CB as antisolution. In view of this, we proposed that co-passivation method which combines with two methods will be an effective strategy to passivate the whole area of perovskite films.

Another issue for inverted PSCs is the improper valence band of   $  PC_{61}BM  $   or   $  C_{60}  $   to perovskite film when compared with   $  TiO_{2}  $   or   $  SnO_{2}  $  . Therefore, the hole blocking ability of   $  PC_{61}BM  $   or   $  C_{60}  $   may be insufficient [33–35], which cause nonradiative recombination at the interface between perovskite and ETL [36], So it is important to improve the hole-blocking capability between perovskite film and ETL. To solve this, we elaborately select proper n-type materials (i.e., PPF) into CB during the anti-solvent step, which is anticipated to function as a hole-blocking layer [36].

Here, based on the two issues mentioned above, we adopt the method of co-passivation combine with bulk treatment and in-film treatment to increase the photovoltaic performance of inverted PSCs. In bulk treatment, we added zwitterion PEACl in perovskite precursor.  $ \mathrm{PEA^{+}} $  groups occupy the A-site vacancies,  $ \mathrm{Cl^-} $  ions can optimize the crystallization of perovskite film [37]. # 2. Results and discussion

As shown in Fig. 1a, the control perovskite films were prepared via typical anti-solvent-assisted method. For the bulk treated sample (referred to as PEACl), small amount of PEACl was added into the perovskite precursor, while for the co-passivated sample (referred to as PEACl/PPF), PPF small molecule was dissolved in antisolvent. Subsequently, the impact of PEACl and PPF on the crystallinity and morphology of perovskite films was investigated. In Fig. 1b, X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns verified three samples were successfully crystallized into cubic perovskite phase   $  Cs_{0.05}MA_{0.07}FA_{0.88}Pb(I_{0.93}Br_{0.07})_{3}  $  , where the primary peaks at   $  28.10^{\circ}  $  ,   $  24.30^{\circ}  $  ,   $  19.81^{\circ}  $  , and   $  14.04^{\circ}  $   represent to (200), (111), (110), and (100), respectively [36]. It can be found that the   $  PbI_{2}  $   peak at   $  12.59^{\circ}  $   obviously exists in the control sample, which disappears after passivated with PEACl or PEACl/PPF. This result verifies that PEACl can promote the crystallization of perovskite grain [36]. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were shown in Fig. 1c–h. A lot of white dots appear in the control sample which can be attributed to unreacted   $  PbI_{2}  $   [38–40], agreeing well with the XRD results. While these white dots disappeared in PEACl treated film, further verifying PEACl can facilitate the perovskite crystallization. Meanwhile, the co-passivated sample shows larger perovskite grains and a more compact morphology than the other samples. We speculate that the PPF material is beneficial to the crystal growth of perovskite. This

In contrast, the co-passivated perovskite layer displayed more n-type surface with VBM of  $ -5.70\mathrm{eV} $ , CBM of  $ -4.14\mathrm{eV} $  and Fermi level of  $ 4.30\mathrm{eV} $ . To gain more insight into charge-carrier recombination dynamics in the perovskite films the steady state photoluminescence (PL) and time resolved PL (TRPL) tests were performed. In Fig. 3d, PL spectra of various samples were collected from the top side of the perovskite films. The PL peak of control perovskite film was located at  $ 785~\mathrm{nm} $ , while the peak was blue shifted to  $ 780~\mathrm{nm} $  for PEACI treated and PEACI/PPF treated perovskite films. This blue-shift PL could be considered as an evidence of defect passivation, since surface defects redshift the PL peak [36,37]. As the defects of perovskite film will cause nonradiative recombination and reduce the PL intensity, the increased PL intensity means less defects. The PL intensity of PEACI/PPF treated perovskite film was higher than that of PEACI treated perovskite film and the latter higher than that of the control sample, indicating lower defect density after treated with PEACI and the PPF could further reduce the defect density in perovskite film. This result should be caused by the coordination bond between PPF and  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} (\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{O} \rightarrow \mathrm{Pb}) $  [32]. TRPL profiles were fitted (Fig. 3e) and the results were listed in Table S1. The decay lifetime is  $ 0.15~\mu s $  for the control sample,  $ 0.56~\mu s $  for PEACI treated sample and  $ 1.35~\mu s $  for PEACI/PPF treated sample, which further indicates that the trap-assisted nonradiative recombination has been significantly suppressed in the same order as the observed  $ V_{oc} $  enhancement of the PSCs after passivated with PEACI and PPF [45,46].

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Fig. 2f and Fig. S6) measurements show that passivation with PEACl or PEACl/PPF caused a shift in the peak position of Pb 4f, C 1s, I 3d, and N 1 s in perovskites. The binding energies of Pb 4f  $  _{7/2}  $   and Pb 4f  $  _{5/2}  $   for the control sample were 137.6 and 142.6 eV, respectively, while those for the passivated samples were shifted to the higher values of 138.2 and 143.2 eV. This should be caused by the interaction between passivation agents and the Pb  $  ^{2+}  $   and changes in the Pb-I bond length [36,47,50,51]. Similar shifts were observed in I 3d, C 1s, and N 1s, which was also attributed to the interaction between functional groups and the perovskite film [50,51].

As mentioned before, the hole blocking capability of   $  PC_{61}BM  $   or   $  C_{60}  $   is not enough in inverted PSCs, which will cause charge recombination at the interfaces of perovskite/ETL and ETL/electrode. An additional hole-blocking layer such as BCP were deposited before evaporation electrode has been widely used to enhance hole-blocking capability [36,46,52–54]. But it cannot suppress charge recombination between perovskite and   $  PC_{61}BM  $  . Improving the hole blocking capability between perovskite and   $  PC_{61}BM  $   will be a good strategy to further improve the performance of PSCs. The control inverted PSC devices were typical structure of ITO/PTAA/perovskite/  $  PC_{61}BM  $  /BCP/Cu, here we added four n-type hole blocking materials (TPBi, BCP, TAZ and PPF) in antisolvent and form hole-blocking layer (HBL) upon perovskite film. # 3. Conclusion

In summary, we have successfully demonstrated a co-passivation strategy to effectively passivate the structural defects inside and outside of the perovskite grains. Meanwhile, the joint action of PEACl and PPF enabled the formation of an energy level gradient between the bulk and surface of perovskite film, which is conductive to the electron transfer and hole blocking. As an encouraging result of the suppressed charge recombination, the open circuit voltage of the optimized device was increased from 1.06 V to 1.19 V, and the PCE of the device is increased from 19.51% to 22.70%. Furthermore, the reduced trap states and promoted charge collection also improved the operational stability of the device. This study opens a new avenue to simultaneously mitigate defects inside and outside of perovskite films towards the fabrication of high efficiency and stable PSCs.
