# Compositional Engineering for Thermally Stable, Highly Efficient Perovskite Solar Cells Exceeding 20% Power Conversion Efficiency with 85 °C/85% 1000 h Stability

Taisuke Matsui,  $  ^{*}  $   Teruaki Yamamoto, Takashi Nishihara, Ryosuke Morisawa, Tomoyasu Yokoyama, Takashi Sekiguchi, and Takayuki Negami

Perovskite solar cells have received great attention because of their rapid progress in efficiency, with a present certified highest efficiency of 23.3%. Achieving both high efficiency and high thermal stability is one of the biggest challenges currently limiting perovskite solar cells because devices displaying stability at high temperature frequently suffer from a marked decrease of efficiency. In this report, the relationship between perovskite composition and device thermal stability is examined. It is revealed that Rb can suppress the growth of   $  PbI_{2}  $   even under   $  PbI_{2}  $  -rich conditions and decreasing the Br ratio in the perovskite absorber layer can prevent the generation of unwanted RbBr-based aggregations. The optimized device achieved by engineering perovskite composition exhibits 92% power conversion efficiency retention in a stress test conducted at 85 °C/85% relative humidity (RH) according to an international standard (IEC 61215) while exceeding 20% power conversion efficiency (certified efficiency of 20.8% at 1 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ). These results reveal the great potential for the practical use of perovskite solar cells in the near future.

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted considerable attention because of their unprecedented progress in power conversion efficiency (PCE), which now reaches 23.3%, exceeding those of commercial thin-film solar cells based on copper indium gallium selenide (CIGS) (22.6%) and CdTe (22.3%) and approaching that of highly efficient crystal silicon solar cells (26.7%).  $  ^{[1]}  $   One of the biggest concerns for commercialization of PSCs is their limited stability. In particular, the compatibility of high efficiency and thermal stability is a major challenge because devices that are stable at high temperature frequently suffer from a decrease in efficiency to below 15%.  $  ^{[2-4]}  $   Recently, several devices showing relatively high efficiency of up to 18% and high thermal stability (85 °C) have been produced using copper phthalocyanine,  $  ^{[5]}  $   copper thiocyanate  $  ^{[6]}  $   or poly[bis(4-phenyl)

methylammonium (MA), Cs, Rb, Pb, I, and Br should be controlled. Furthermore, not only effect of single element but also combination effect of these elements should be important. Thus, careful studies are required for further development. Indeed, even though more and more studies have been reported on multication approach,  $  ^{[18-21]}  $  there are still no reports of PSCs that meets international standard (IEC 61215); 85 °C/85% relative humidity (RH) stress test with high efficiency. Here we focus on the dependence of device thermal stability on perovskite composition by using state-of-the-art highly efficient PSCs (  $  \approx  $  20%) to achieve compatibility of high efficiency and high thermal stability.

We tested the thermal stability of devices with a structure of indium tin oxide (ITO)/compact titanium dioxide   $  \left(\mathrm{TiO}_{2}\right)  $  /mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $  /perovskite/PTAA/gold (Au). The starting perovskite composition used here was   $  Cs_{0.05}(MA_{0.17}FA_{0.83})_{0.95}\mathrm{Pb}\left(\mathrm{I}_{0.83}\mathrm{Br}_{0.17}\right)_{3}  $   because this composition has been reported to display high efficiency and high operational stability.  $  ^{[17]}  $   We chose PTAA as the HTM because its high efficiency  $  ^{[22-24]}  $   and high thermal stability  $  ^{[7]}  $   have been reported respectively. In addition, we used a low doping concentration of 3 mol% lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in PTAA in this experiment to minimize any adverse effects of the additives on the device thermal stability reported so far.  $  ^{[25-27]}  $

First, time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) was used to roughly assess any compositional changes in the depth and plane directions of the devices. Because the TOF-SIMS elemental mapping areas of Rb and Br were different, we conducted scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometry (EDX) measurements to further analyze these agglomerations. In summary, we revealed that the addition of Rb and the halide composition of the perovskite absorber layer affects the thermal stability of PSCs. The optimized device with engineered perovskite composition achieved a PCE exceeding 20% and 92% retention after stress testing at 85 °C/85% RH for 1000 h according to an international standard (IEC 61215). This is the first PSC to exhibit good stability in a damp heat test with exceeding 20% efficiency, showing great potential for practical use in the near future.

# Experimental Section

Substrate Preparation: Glass substrates (25 mm × 25 mm) coated with ITO (150 nm) and TiO₂ (10 nm, Geomatec) were cleaned by sonication in isopropanol for 10 min. After drying under an N₂ stream, the substrates were further cleaned by UV/ozone treatment for 30 min. A mesoporous TiO₂ layer was deposited by spin coating for 10 s at 4000 rpm with a ramp rate of 2000 rpm s⁻¹ using a 30 nm TiO₂ particle paste (Dyesol 30 NR-D) diluted in ethanol to achieve a layer that was 150–200 nm thick. After spin coating, the substrates were immediately dried at 100 °C for 10 min and then sintered at 500 °C for 30 min. After cooling to 150 °C, the substrates were transferred to an N₂-filled glove box to deposit the perovskite films.

Perovskite Precursor Solution and Film Preparation: The organic cations were purchased from Dyesol, the lead compounds from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (TCI) and Csl and Rbl from Iwatani Corporation. The perovskite precursors  $ (\mathrm{PbI}_2,\mathrm{PbBr}_2 $  formamidinium iodide (FAI), methylammonium iodide (MAI), and MABr) were dissolved in anhydrous dimethyl formamide (DMF)/dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (4:1 v:v) at  $ 1.2\mathrm{M} $ .

The perovskite solution was spin coated by a two-step program at 1000 and 4000 rpm for 10 and 30 s, respectively. During the second step, chlorobenzene (200   $  \mu  $  L) was poured on the spinning substrate 5 s prior to the end of the program. The substrates were annealed at 115 °C for 15 min and then 100 °C for 30 min in an   $  N_{2}  $  -filled glove box.

Hole Transport Layer and Top Electrode: After perovskite annealing, the substrate was cooled for few minutes and then a PTAA (Sigma-Aldrich) solution (10 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  in toluene) was spin coated on the substrate at 4000 rpm for 20 s. PTAA was doped with Li-TFSI (Sigma-Aldrich) and tBP (Sigma-Aldrich). The molar ratio of additives to a single PTAA unit was 3% and 24% for Li-TFSI and 18% and 144% for tBP for the devices presented in Figures 1,2 and 3,4, respectively. Finally, an Au top electrode (150 nm) was thermally evaporated on each substrate under high vacuum.

Encapsulation: A glass cover with water-absorbent sealant (HD-S051414W-40, Dynic) was put on the device under dry air conditions (<2% RH). The substrate and glass cover were glued with epoxy resin (XNR 5516Z-B1, Nagase ChemteX Corporation). The epoxy resin was cured by UV treatment for 15 min.

Photovoltaic Device Testing: The solar cell performance was measured using a triple-A xenon light source (BPS X300BA, Bunkou-Keiki). The light intensity was calibrated with a Si photodiode before device testing. J-V characteristics of the cells were obtained using an electrochemical analyzer (ALS440B, BAS Inc. (BAS)). The voltage scan rate was 100 mV s  $  ^{-1}  $  and devices were not preconditioned by a process such as light soaking or prolonged forward voltage bias application before starting the measurement. The cells were defined by a black metal mask (0.1 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ) to fix the active area and decrease the influence of scattered light. Measurements were conducted in dry air (<2% RH) at room temperature.

Perovskite Characterization: A field-emission scanning electron microscope (SU8220, Hitachi) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer

(QUANTAX FlatQUAD, Bruker AXS) were used to characterize the morphology and elemental distribution from the device top. XRD patterns were recorded on a Rigaku SmartLab X-ray diffractometer (Cu anode,  $ \lambda = 1.54060\AA $ ) in the angle range of  $ 2\theta = 5^{\circ} - 40^{\circ} $  under ambient conditions. TOF-SIMS was used to characterize the depth profile and conduct elemental mapping of the top view of the devices. Profiling was accomplished with a gas cluster ion beam with an area of  $ 100\mu \mathrm{m}\times 100\mu \mathrm{m} $ . During profiling, all the spectra were collected at or below a primary-ion dose density of  $ 1\times 10^{12} $  ions  $ \mathrm{cm}^{-2} $  to remain at the static SIMS limit. Before the above characterizations, the Au electrode was removed from the devices using Scotch tape.
