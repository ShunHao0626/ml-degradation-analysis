# Anti-solvent engineering via potassium bromide additive for highly efficient and stable perovskite solar cells

# ABSTRACT

# ARTICLEINFO

Anti-solvent assisted crystallization is commonly employed method to achieve high-quality perovskites attributed to its great operability. Herein, we report an anti-solvent engineering approach via simply using potassium bromide (KBr) additive with commonly used chlorobenzene (CB) in triple-cation perovskite solar cells (PSCs). We show that the KBr additive in the CB anti-solvent not only increases the crystallinity and passivates the perovskite top surface defects, but also leads to suppressed nonradiative recombination and facilitates charge extraction at interfaces. Interestingly, due to the halide vacancies filling with  $ \mathrm{R}^+ $  ions, hysteresis behavior in the treated perovskite layer was suppressed. Consequently, a champion power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 18.29\% $  was yielded for anti-solvent engineering employing KBr (an  $ 20\% $  improvement in PCE compared to the CB-only anti-solvent device). Furthermore, the optimized device based on KBr demonstrated improved stability, maintaining  $ 80\% $  of its original efficiency after aging in an environment with a relative humidity of  $ 30 - 50\% $  for 1080 h. Our study reports the significant role of anti-solvent engineering in improving perovskite's quality for efficient PSCs and develops the potential for PSC commercialization.

# 1. Introduction

Nowadays, Organometal hybrid perovskites have attracted considerable attention as a new photoactive absorber for next-generation solar cells   $  [1,2]  $  . Since PSCs based on methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ) were first fabricated with a PCE of 3.7% in the last decade, the current efficiency of PSCs has reached 25%   $  [3]  $  . Besides the high PCE, perovskite provides an adjustable bandgap, high absorption coefficient, long charge diffusion length   $  [4]  $  . In order to attain the industrialization of PSC, tremendous attempts have been done to facilitate the stability of PSCs and upscaling   $  [5–7]  $  . The optimization of the perovskites morphology represents a crucial factor to enhance performance of PSCs. Optoelectrical features of the perovskites such as light harvesting, charge transfer, and recombination are directly dependent on perovskite morphology   $  [5,8–10]  $  . In this content, different deposition techniques such as anti-solvent spin-coating method, doctor blade, vacuum evaporation, two-step sequential spin-coating, spray deposition, and flash infrared annealing have been reported to realize suitable perovskites   $  [11–16]  $  . Among these methods, anti-solvent approach is commonly used because of the inexpensive and feasible operability of PSCs

[17-20].

It is well-known that the types of anti-solvent engineering and deposition routes seriously influence the nucleation speed during the perovskite fabrication [21]. Particularly, enlarges in grains size because the nature of the employed anti-solvent could improve the electrical properties, carrier's extraction ability, energy-level alignment at interfaces, and recombination rates [22]. Joel et al. studied four different types of anti-solvents for the crystallization of spinning  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskites. Anti-solvent acts as a humidity resistor during deposition, defending sensitive perovskite structures from degradation by water during film formation and annealing [23]. Jian et al. reported six types of commonly utilized and low cost anti-solvents with various physicochemical merits to modify the spin-coating perovskites deposited from a mixed DMSO and DMF solutions. They showed that the anti-solvents reveal a crucial impact on the perovskite crystal growth kinetics [10]. Daniel et al. used the anti-solvents of CB and isopropanol (IPA) to treat the perovskite films during spin-coating process. The IPA-based PSC resulting in highly homogenous, pinhole-free with larger grains triple-cations perovskite layers compared to CB processed perovskites [19]. # 2. Experimental section

# 2.1.Materials

Unless otherwise, chemicals and solvents were provided from Greatcell Solar and Sigma-Aldrich, respectively. Lead halides  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and  $ \mathrm{PbBr}_2 $  were ordered from TCI. Cesium iodide (CsI) was bought from Alfa Aser.

# 2.2. Solution preparation

A 1.35 M   $  Cs_{0.05}(FA_{0.95}MA_{0.05})_{0.95}Pb(I_{0.95}Br_{0.05})_{3}  $   precursors were synthesized by dissolving formamidinium iodide (FAI, 99.99%),   $  PbI_{2}  $  , methylammonium bromide (MABr, 99.99%), lead bromide (  $  PbBr_{2}  $  , 99.99%, TCI), and cesium iodide (CsI, 99.999%) in a mixed solvents of N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.7%, Sigma-Aldrich) (4:1 v/v). With diluting of commercial   $  TiO_{2}  $   paste in ethanol (99.5%, Sigma-Aldrich) with a weight ratio of 1:6, the mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   (mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $  ) stock solution was prepared. To prepare a potassium bromide (KBr, 99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich) doped anti-solvent, 0–2 mg of the KBr salt were dissolved in chlorobenzene (CB, 99.8%, Sigma-Aldrich) and sonicated at room temperature for 30 min. The hole transport layer (HTL) solution was fabricated by addition 73.2 mg of Spiro-OMeTAD (99%) in CB with bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide lithium salt (Li-TFSI, 98%) solution (520 mg/mL in acetonitrile), 4-tert-butylpyridine (tBP, 98%) additives.

# 2.3. Devices fabrication

The pre-patterned FTO   $  SnO_{2}  $   (15   $  \Omega/sq  $  ) glasses were washed with deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol (IPA) for 15 min in an ultrasonic bath [31]. A compact hole-blocking   $  TiO_{2}  $   (c-  $  TiO_{2}  $  ) layer with thickness of   $  \sim40  $   nm was spin-coated on FTO substrates with speed of 4000 rpm for 60 s, with an annealing step at 500 °C for 60 min. The c-  $  TiO_{2}  $   solution was prepared as reported elsewhere. The mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $   solution was deposited on the c-  $  TiO_{2}  $   layer at 5000 rpm for 30 s, then baked at 500 °C for 60 min. For perovskites, 100   $  \mu  $  l of perovskite solution was added on the mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $   film and deposited first at 1000 rpm for 10 s and then 4000 rpm for 60 s. In the last 20 s of the faster step, 200   $  \mu  $  l of CB with or without KBr additive immediately was dripped on the intermediate perovskite layer. After that, the perovskite layers were annealed at 100 °C for 45 min. As an HTL layer, the Spiro-OMeTAD mixture was deposited at 4000 rpm for 30 s over the perovskite layers. Finally, a 100 nm Au was deposited on the HTLs as electrodes.

# 2.4. Characterizations

The micro-morphology of the perovskites was characterized by FESEM (TESCAN, Mira 3) images. The visualized grain size was analyzed using ImageJ software. The X-ray diffraction pattern of the perovskite was collected by a Bruker, D8 advance X-ray diffraction spectrometer. The absorption spectra of the perovskites were studied with a Perkin Elmer spectroscopy. The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) responses of layers were monitored with a HORIBA Fluorolog-III device. The PV measurements of the PSCs under simulated irradiation of AM 1.5 (450 W Xenon light source from Oriel) were measured with a Keithley Model 2400. The PV devices were masked with an active area of  $ 0.08\mathrm{cm}^2 $ . The voltage step in I-V tests was fixed at  $ 50\mathrm{mV / s} $ . The light of the simulator was calibrated with a Si-reference solar cell. Incident photon-to-electron conversion efficiency (IPCE) response of devices was monitored using a calibrated Newport system.

# 3. Results and discussion

Herein, the performance and ambient stability of the PSCs were boosted through a facile anti-solvent engineering approach using the KBr additive. For this aim, the triple-cations perovskite films are deposited on the  $ \mathrm{mp - TiO_2} $  substrate via a one-step deposition method using anti-solvent assist [32], as shown in Fig. 1. After that, the KBr additive is introduced to the anti-solvent source for improving the crystallinity of the perovskite layer and tailoring the interfacial of perovskite and HTL layers.

At first, the effects of the suggested approach on the optical features of the perovskites were investigated with UV–Vis and steady-state PL techniques (Fig. 2a–c). Fig. 2a shows the UV–Vis spectra of the grown perovskite layers utilizing CB anti-solvent sources with different amounts of KBr additive. By introducing KBr additive into the anti-solvent source, the absorbance ability of the perovskite layer increases in the range of 400–600 nm. In the case of 1 mg additive, the absorbance ability of the perovskite layer reaches its maximum. This phenomenon brings the hypothesis that anti-solvent engineering improves the light-harvesting property of the perovskite layer [33,34]. Notably, with increasing the amount of KBr additive to 2 mg in the anti-solvent source, the absorbance spectra reduced with a clear peak around 500 nm, which is related to   $  PbI_{2}  $   surplus in the corresponding perovskite layer [35]. The tauc curve of different perovskite layers is shown in Fig. 2b, which suggests presence of the KBr additive doesn't change bandgap energy (  $  E_{g}  $  ) of the perovskite layer. PL responses of the fabricated perovskites with various contents of KBr on the FTO glasses are revealed in Fig. 2c. As expected, the PL signal of the perovskite film with 1 mg KBr is higher than the others. It indicates the non-radiative recombination rate reduced in corresponding perovskite film and brings more electron-hole pairs during light irradiance [36,37]. To further insight, crystalline characteristics of perovskite layers were monitored by XRD equipment. Fig. 2d describes the XRD patterns of different perovskite layers. In the control layer (i.e., 0 mg case), two peaks at 12.7° and 38.80° are observable, referring to the   $  PbI_{2}  $   contents. Besides, two dominant peaks at about 14.2°, 28.4° could be seen, which assign to the black-phase of   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   in (001) and (002) crystal planes [38–40], respectively. Interestingly, after KBr adding to the anti-solvent source, the   $  PbI_{2}  $   peaks are weakened, and   $  \alpha  $  -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   peaks in the perovskite layer are intensified. In the case of 2 mg KBr, as was deduced from the UV–Vis test,   $  PbI_{2}  $   peaks in the XRD pattern intensify, indicating an undesirable crystallinity for PV application. Besides, in none of the perovskite films, any   $  \delta  $  -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   peaks did not appear in their XRD patterns, which reveals anti-solvent method is a practical way to produce a favorable   $  \alpha  $  -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   phase.

Top-view FE-SEM images of the perovskite film with and without KBr additive are demonstrated in Fig. 3. Through the anti-solvent engineering approach, the micromorphology of perovskite layers is changed in appearance, indicating the effects of the KBr additive on the growth of grains of the perovskite layer. The ambient air stability of the control and modified PSCs were monitored in an environment with a relative humidity of 30–50% (Fig. 6b). Through anti-solvent engineering, the stability behavior of the perovskite layer increased. The PCE of the KBr-based device maintains 80% of its initial value after 1080 h, while the control device only remains 40% of its initial PCE. The enlarged grains size with passivated GBs caused by the KBr additive is the reason for stability improvements (See Fig. 3). When GBs in a perovskite layer are passivated, pathways are blocked for humidity diffusion into the perovskite structure and keep the perovskite layer safe from degradation [36]. Besides, assist of the KBr, perovskite crystallinity improved (Fig. 2d). This phenomenon leads to a higher humidity resistance in the perovskite layer. PbI  $  _{2}  $  content in a perovskite layer faster reacts with humidity of air and then opens the degrading ways of the perovskite layer. As can be seen in Fig. 2d, for the KBr-based devices, PbI  $  _{2}  $  peaks are weakened compared to the control device, indicating higher humidity resistance in them.

# 4. Conclusions

In conclusion, we have developed an effective mixed anti-solvent (CB and KBr) assisted crystallization strategy to fabricate high-quality perovskite layers. Formative processes of perovskites with various morphologies achieved by dripping different CB/KBr amounts were analyzed. KBr additive in CB anti-solvent is beneficial to obtaining highly crystalline perovskites with less grain boundaries, which is helpful to slow down recombination processes. Besides, due to the halide vacancies filling with   $  K^{+}  $  ions, hysteresis behavior in the treated perovskite layer was suppressed. As a result, theses pinholes-free and dense polycrystalline perovskites mainly facilitated to achieve significant improvement in FF from 70.8% to 73.8% for an average of 15 PV cells. The remarkably enhanced PSC performance was obtained by the perovskite fabricated by adding 1 mg of KBr to CB and the best PCE of 18.29% was recorded. The excellent performances of PSCs are attributed to the decreased charge recombination and enhanced charge injection at interfaces. In this regard, the unencapsulated KBr-based devices can retain 80% of their initial PCEs after 1080 h in ambient environment.
