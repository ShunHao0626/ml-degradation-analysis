# Interfacial Modification and Defect Passivation by the Cross-Linking Interlayer for Efficient and Stable CuSCN-Based Perovskite Solar Cells

Jinhyun Kim, $ ^{\dagger} $  Younghyun Lee, $ ^{\dagger} $  Alan Jiwan Yun, $ ^{\dagger} $  Bumjin Gil, $ ^{\text{ID}} $  and Byungwoo Park $ ^{*} $

Department of Materials Science and Engineering, Research Institute of Advanced Materials, Seoul National University, Seoul 08826, Korea

Supporting Information

ABSTRACT: The study of the inorganic hole-transport layer (HTL) in perovskite solar cells (PSCs) is gathering attention because of the drawback of the conventional PSC design, where the organic HTL with salt dopants majorly participates in the degradation mechanisms. On the other hand, inorganic HTL secures better stability, while it offers difficulties in the deposition and interfacial control to realize high-performing devices. In this study, we demonstrate polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) as an ideal polymeric interlayer which prevents interfacial

degradation and improves both photovoltaic performance and stability of CuSCN-based PSC by its cross-linking behavior. Surprisingly, the PDMS polymers are identified to form chemical bonds with perovskite and CuSCN, as shown by Raman spectroscopy. This novel cross-linking interlayer of PDMS enhances the hole-transporting property at the interface and passivates the interfacial defects, realizing the PSC with high power-conversion efficiency over 19%. Furthermore, the utilization of the PDMS interlayer greatly improves the stability of solar cells against both humidity and heat by mitigating the interfacial defects and interdiffusion. The PDMS-interlayered PSCs retained over 90% of the initial efficiencies, both after 1000 h under ambient conditions (unencapsulated) and after 500 h under 85 °C/85% relative humidity (encapsulated).

KEYWORDS: perovskite solar cell, inorganic hole-transport layer, cross-linking interlayer, defect passivation, stability

# INTRODUCTION

Attention on photovoltaics has rapidly grown with the growing concern for future energy supply.  $  ^{1-3}  $   Among the various materials and designs, the perovskite solar cell (PSC) has recently been a popular research object because of its outstanding potential as the next generation photovoltaics.  $  ^{6-14}  $   The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSC has already become comparable to that of the conventional ones, and extensive studies for its commercialization are in progress.  $  ^{14-26}  $   Even though the PCE growth is quite satisfactory, the stability of the device is still below the industrial standard, demanding more advances in materials and designs.

In general, PSC has a hierarchical structure of the perovskite with an electron-transport layer and hole-transport layer (HTL), and the spiro-OMeTAD is one of the most common materials for the HTL.  $  ^{20-23,25-30}  $   This organic material enables outstanding PCE, but has low stability because of its high reactivity and the salt dopants.  $  ^{31-35}  $   To overcome the limitation of the organic-based HTL, various inorganic p-type semiconductors including metal oxides and copper-based materials have been recently introduced.  $  ^{36-45}  $

Among them, CuSCN is one of the promising inorganic materials for the HTL, which has decent hole mobility   $  (10^{-1}\;\mathrm{cm}^{2}\;\mathrm{V}^{-1}\;\mathrm{s}^{-1})  $  , enabling solar cells with high theoretical and experimental PCEs.  $  ^{36-38,43,46-49}  $   However, despite its great potential, the deposition of CuSCN with polar solvents still

remains a challenge in the n–i–p-structured PSC. As diethyl sulfide (DES) is the most common solvent for the solution-deposition of CuSCN, the polar property of DES is reported to induce damages on the perovskite surface.  $  ^{43,50}  $   The decomposition of the organic–inorganic perovskite because of its high reactivity to the polar solvents and consequential trap formation have been reported.  $  ^{43,50,51}  $   In addition, the degradation of CuSCN has been observed with the formation of CuI at the perovskite/CuSCN interface, resulting in negative impacts on the solar cell performance.  $  ^{38}  $   Therefore, a novel strategy to prevent the degradation of materials and mitigating the trap formation at the interface will play a significant role realizing efficient and stable PSCs.

Herein, we utilize the polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS)   $  (\mathrm{CH}_{3}[\mathrm{Si}(\mathrm{CH}_{3})_{2}\mathrm{O}]_{n}\mathrm{Si}(\mathrm{CH}_{3})_{3})  $   polymers for an interlayer between the perovskite and CuSCN to stabilize the film deposition and device operation. PDMS is a super hydrophobic and thermally resistive polymer,  $  ^{52-55}  $   and the presence of the PDMS interlayer successfully blocks the decomposition of the perovskite at the surface during the deposition of the upper layer. # EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

Device Fabrication. The indium tin oxide (ITO) substrate (AMG) was etched with zinc powder (TCI Chemicals) and sonicated in acetone, ethanol, and deionized water, for 20 min, respectively. Afterward, the substrate was UV–ozone treated for 15 min. The   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloidal dispersion (15 wt % in   $  H_{2}O  $  , Alfa Aesar) diluted with DI water was spin-coated on the ITO substrate and annealed at 120 °C for 30 min. The substrate for the hole-only device was fabricated by spin-coating PTAA solution (2 mg/mL in chlorobenzene) on ITO glass with a spin rate of 5000 rpm for 40 s, with annealing at 120 °C for 20 min. For the   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   perovskite film, the 1.92 M   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   precursor was prepared by dissolving methylammonium iodide (Great Solar Laboratory) and lead iodide (  $  PbI_{2}  $  ; TCI Chemicals) in mixed solvents of N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF; Sigma-Aldrich) and dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich) with a 9:1 volume ratio. In the case of the   $  Cs_{0.05}(FA_{0.83}MA_{0.17})_{0.95}\mathrm{Pb(I_{0.83}Br_{0.17})_{3}}  $   perovskite (CsFAMA) film, formamidinium iodide (Great Solar Laboratory), methylammonium bromide (MABr; Great Solar Laboratory),   $  PbI_{2}  $  , and lead bromide (  $  PbBr_{2}  $  ; TCI Chemicals) were dissolved in DMF/DMSO with a 4:1 volume ratio with the addition of the CsI/DMSO solution. The perovskite solution was spin-coated on the substrate at 3000 rpm for 20 s with diethyl ether used as a secondary solvent. The PDMS (Sigma-Aldrich) interlayer was prepared by dispersing 12.25 mg of PDMS in 1 mL n-hexane (Daejung) and spin-coated on the perovskite film. The hole transporting material was prepared by dissolving 25 mg of CuSCN (Sigma-Aldrich) in 1 mL of DES (Sigma-Aldrich) and spin-coated on the substrate with annealing at 50 °C for 10 min.

Characterization. Optical properties of various films were analyzed with UV–vis spectroscopy (V-770; JASCO). An X-ray diffractometer (New D-8 ADVANCE; Bruker) was used to observe the phases and impurities in perovskite films. A field-emission scanning electron microscope (Merlin Compact; Zeiss) was utilized to observe the vertical structures of devices. Raman and steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra were obtained via LabRAM HV Evolution, Horiba, using lasers of   $  \lambda_{excitation} = 325  $   and 532 nm. Time-resolved PL (TRPL) spectra (FluoTime 300; Picoquant) were

obtained using   $  \lambda_{excitation} = 398  $   nm laser. The films were deposited on glass substrates and analyzed with incident light on the perovskite or CuSCN surface. Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectroscopy (TOF.SIMS-5; ION-TOF) was performed to analyze the compositional depth profile of the PDMS-interlayered device. The four-point probe was used to measure the electric conductivity of films fabricated on quartz substrates. Work functions of argon-sputtered films were measured by multipurpose X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (Sigma Probe; Thermo VG Scientific) with a photon energy of   $  h\nu = 21.22  $   eV. A potentiostat (Zive SP-1; WonATech) was utilized for both space-charge-limited current (SCLC) and admittance analyses. For the SCLC measurement, the voltage was applied from 0 to 10 V. For the admittance analysis, the impedance of the device was measured under ac voltage perturbation with 10 mV of amplitude and frequency from   $  10^{-2}  $   to   $  10^{5}  $   Hz. The devices had been kept in the dark to drop the voltage below 3 mV before each measurement. The solar cell measurement system (K-3000; McScience) was used to test the device performance with an active area of   $  0.09 \, cm^2  $  . The voltage was swept from 1.2 to -0.1 V for the reverse scan, or vice versa for the forward scan (scan rate of   $  100 \, mV s^{-1}  $  ).

# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

To clarify the degradation of the perovskite layer during the CuSCN deposition, the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 / \mathrm{CuSCN} $  films are investigated with different prewetting times of the CuSCN solution from 0 to  $ 90\mathrm{s} $  followed by a spin-coating step. As the CuSCN solution is wetted longer on the perovskite substrate, the degradation of the perovskite occurs more severely at a level that can be easily compared visually. The features of each film including optical images, absorbance spectra, and X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns are compared in the Supporting Information, Figure S1. With clear evidence of surface degradation, it is necessary to improve the quality of the perovskite/CuSCN interface.

As an ideal strategy to suppress the perovskite decomposition, a buffer interlayer is designed with super hydrophobic PDMS, and the perovskite surface is successfully protected from the damage by the CuSCN-DES solution (Figure S2). In addition, polymers with hydrophobic properties are also expected to boost the stability of the solar cell without hindering its photovoltaic performance, regarding that the utilization of the hydrophobic upper layer is reported to improve the device stability under humid air.  $  ^{56,57}  $   Furthermore, polymers with a lone pair of electrons are reported to passivate defects forming a Lewis adduct with the perovskite,  $  ^{58,59}  $   indicating the possible passivation of the interfacial defects by PDMS. The schematics with cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images for the synthesis of CuSCN-based PSCs are shown in Figure 1. It should be noted that 200 nm PDMS layer looks diminished after the deposition of CuSCN.

The PDMS polymers basically have insulating properties which can hinder the charge extraction and transfer at the interface. Therefore, PL spectroscopy has been conducted for each structure deposited on a glass substrate to compare the hole-extraction property from the perovskite to the CuSCN HTL. As shown in Figure 2b, a PL peak at  $ 766\mathrm{nm} $  is identified for every case, which represents the radiative recombination of excitons in  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ . Without any electrode in contact, bare  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  shows strong PL intensity with the majority of photocarriers dissipating via radiative recombination. On the other hand, with CuSCN as the upper layer, a large proportion

of holes are extracted by the CuSCN HTL resulting in the decreased PL intensity. Notably, when the PDMS interlayer is incorporated, more effective PL quenching is observed, implying that holes are effectively extracted by the CuSCN HTL. The TRPL has been also measured, where  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3/ $  PDMS/CuSCN shows more rapid PL decay compared to the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3/ $  CuSCN sample. The PL decay curves are fitted to the biexponential functions, and the average carrier lifetime is significantly reduced with the PDMS interlayer (as shown in Table S1), implying the improved hole-extracting properties by the PDMS/CuSCN HTL. In addition, even though the PDMS itself has deficient electrical properties, the PDMS/CuSCN film shows negligible decreases in both electrical conductivity and hole mobility as shown in Figures S3 and S4. Considering that the energy level of CuSCN remains unchanged with or without the PDMS interlayer (Figure S5), the utilization of the PDMS cross-linking interlayer barely blocks the carrier transfer but rather improves the photovoltaic property.

In our previous work, we have confirmed that reduction of the interfacial deep traps plays a significant role enhancing   $  V_{OC}  $   and   $  J_{SC}  $   by mitigating nonradiative recombination at the interface, which causes loss in both   $  V_{OC}  $   and charge extractions.  $  ^{51,66}  $   Recalling that the electronic trap states are considerably reduced as observed by impedance analysis and J-V characteristics in Figure 3, improved photovoltaic performance of PDMS devices are majorly because of the mitigation of interfacial traps by the PDMS cross-linking interlayer. Better hole-extraction efficiency of the PDMS/CuSCN HTL observed by PL analyses in Figure 2b is also believed to play an important role in improving   $  J_{SC}  $   and   $  \eta  $  .

Furthermore, the PDMS interlayer improves the air stability. The PDMS-interlayered PSC maintains its performance stable at 25 °C/over-55% relative humidity (RH) for more than 1000 h without any encapsulation (Figure 4c). The PDMS/CuSCN device also endures extreme humidity (95% RH), much better than the bare CuSCN device (Figure S8). It is astonishing that the device with PDMS sustains the perovskite layer protected even when dipped in water, while the device without the PDMS interlayer immediately turns yellow because of the decomposition of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  into PbI  $  _{2}  $  (Figure 4d). It confirms that the interlayer of PDMS suppresses the penetration/diffusion of external H  $  _{2}  $  O molecules through the interface.

# CONCLUSIONS

Herein, we suggest the PDMS interlayer as an ideal and facile strategy to prevent the interfacial degradation in the CuSCN-based PSC. We have fabricated a PDMS interlayer by a simple solution-process, and most importantly, identified a cross-linking behavior of PDMS polymers at the interface. The chemical bonds formed by PDMS polymers are believed to mitigate traps/defects at the interface, improving the photovoltaic performance of the device with the enhanced hole-extraction property. The PDMS cross-linking interlayer enables a highly efficient CuSCN-based PSC with a PCE over 19.0%, which is among the top levels reported so far. Moreover, the PDMS-modified interface allows the solar cell to maintain over 90% of its initial PCE both after 1000 h storage under ambient conditions (25 °C/over-55% RH, unencapsulated) and after 500 h storage under rigorous conditions (85 °C/85% RH, encapsulated). Further studies on the correlation between the defect states of the PDMS-interlayered PSC and its degradation will provide insights to realize commercialization of highly stable PSC.
