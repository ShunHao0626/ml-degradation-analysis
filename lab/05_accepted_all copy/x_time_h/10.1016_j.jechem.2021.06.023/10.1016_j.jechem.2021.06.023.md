# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite materials exhibit distinguished optoelectronic properties such as high light absorption coefficients, large carrier diffusion length, big defect tolerance, etc. and have attracted extensive research interest. In the past ten years, the power conversion efficiency of single-junction perovskite thin-film solar cells has been improved from  $ 3.8\% $  to  $ 25.2\% $  [1-4]. Additionally, this type of cell can be fabricated by facile solution processes, which made it to be a promising candidate to realize high-efficiency and low-cost solar cells for future commercial application. The crystallinity quality of the perovskite films determines the photovoltaic properties of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). However, the perovskite films depoited by low-temperature solution-processes are polycrystalline and have high defect densities [5-7]. The defects such as undercoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  and halide  $ (1^{-}) $  ions at the perovskite film surface, at the grain

High-quality perovskite films with larger grain size and fewer defects is a prerequisite for high-performance perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Antisolvent-assisted crystallization is an effective approach to obtain compact and uniform perovskite films; however, the majority of antisolvents currently applied have strong toxicity, and the control of perovskite crystallization is not easy through single antisolvent. In this work, a green antisolvent of ethyl acetate (EA) with acetylacetone (AA) additive is used to fine-tune perovskite crystallization and passivate defect, which produces uniform and compact  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  perovskite films having larger grain and fewer grain boundaries and reduced defect density. Meanwhile, the interfacial hydrophobic characteristic of the perovskite films is enhanced. At the optimized concentration of AA in EA, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  PSCs was improved from  $ 19.2\% $  to  $ 21.1\% $  and their stability in air was also enhanced. These results present a green antisolvent additive engineering strategy to enhance the crystallinity, passivate defects, and fabricate efficient and stable PSCs.

develop environmentally-friendly nontoxic green antisolvents to prepare the perovskite films. Recently, several green antisolvents—including diethyl ether (DE) [26], ethyl acetate (EA) [25–28], sec-butyl alcohol (SBA) [29], anisole (ANS) [30], ethanol [31], methyl benzoate (MB) [32], etc. have been developed to prepare perovskite films. EA has a higher polarity (0.228) and lower boiling point (77 °C), which enables rapid perovskite crystallization. The contacts among functional layers and stability of the PSCs are also improved using EA antisolvent [33].

The quality of perovskite film depends on the nucleation and crystal growth process. Traditional single antisolvent can rapidly extract the precursor solvents from perovskite films during the spin-coating process. However, antisolvent remaining in the perovskite films could adversely affect the perovskite crystal's growth during the thermal-annealing phase. Furthermore, the organic components such as methylammonium iodide (MAI), in the perovskite precursors are easily volatilized during the annealing process, producing   $  PbI_{2}  $   instead of perovskite phase and lowering the properties of PSCs [34]. Recently, antisolvent additive engineering (AAE), which involves adding functional additive in the antisolvent, has been developed to address this problem. Different antisolvents and additives have been used to regulate perovskite films growth dynamics and improve its quality, such as CB/ethanol [35], CB/acetonitrile [36], CB/acetic acid (Ac) [37], CB/Rubrene [38], CB/  $  I_{2}  $   [39], CB/Methylamine (MA) [40], CB/4-tert-butylpyridine (tBp) [41], EA/hexane [28], EA/(PbCl  $  _{2}  $  , PbBr  $  _{2}  $  , and MAI) [42,43], EA/petroleum ether (PE) [28], diethyl ether (DE)/  $  I_{2}  $   [44], DE/SnCl  $  _{2}  $   [45], ether/Methanol [46], toluene/2-hydroxyethyl acrylate (HEA) [47], ethanol(Eth)/methylamine bromide (MABr) [31], etc. For example, EA/hexane leads to slower growth of perovskite crystals, thus impeding the production of cracks in the films [28]. However, compared to conventional additive engineering through adding additives into perovskite precursor solutions, there are few studies on AAE and limited additives, even with its better prospects.

In this paper, using the AAE approach, we apply acetylacetone (AA) as additive in the green antisolvent ethyl acetate (EA) to treat   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   (  $  MAPbI_{3}  $  ) film in a one-step preparation process, and find that the AA plays an important role to improve the quality of the perovskite films. The C = O functional groups of the AA passivate uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   defects and enhance the interaction of cations and anions in perovskite by coordinate bonding, facilitating the formation of dense films with large crystalline size. Consequently, the solar cell based on   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   treated with AA shows the highest PCE of 21.1% in comparison to that of the untreated device (19.2%). Additionally, the ambient stability of the AA-treated PSCs is also increased due to the better perovskite film quality and hydrophobicity, and the cell keeps 87% of its initial efficiency for 1224 h in air without encapsulation. This study affords a green approach for fabricating high-quality perovskite films, and it is promising to obtain environmentally friendly, high-efficiency, and stable PSCs.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1. Films preparation and solar cell fabrication

FTO glass substrates were first rinsed ultrasonically for 30 min using Hellmanex solution in a volume ratio of 1:100, and then rinsed with ultrapure water for three times. Subsequently, the FTO glasses were dried by flowing nitrogen gas, followed by treating in an ultraviolet-ozone treatment (UVO-Cleaner, Jelight Company USA) for 15 min. Then a compact 30-nm thick TiO  $  _{2}  $  layer was deposited by chemical bath using 0.2 M TiCl  $  _{4}  $  aqueous solution at 70 °C. The CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}  $  PbI  $  _{3}  $  perovskite precursor solutions were prepared by dissolving 461 mg PbI  $  _{2}  $  and 159 mg CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}  $  I in a mixed

solvent of 71 μL dimethylformamide (DMF) and 762 μL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) with stirring at room temperature for 6 h. The perovskite solution (60 μL) was spin-coated onto the prepared FTO/TiO₂ substrate in a nitrogen-filled glove box at room temperature by a typical one-step anti-solvent method, at a speed of 1000 rpm for 5 s with a rate of 1000 rpm s⁻¹ followed by 4000 rpm for 30 s with a rate of 4000 rpm s⁻¹. During the second spin-coating step, 0.3 mL EA/AA with different volume ratios of AA/EA = 0, 0.5%, 1%, 2% was dripped onto the spinning substrate 25 s prior to the end of the program. Then the samples were heated at 130 °C for 10 min on a hotplate. As a control sample, we refer to the traditional CH₃NH₃PbI₃ films without AA treatment as CT-PSCs. The hole transport layer solution containing 90 mg spiro-OMeTAD was dissolved into the mixture solution of 36 μL 4-tert-butylpyridine and 22 μL of lithium bis (trifluoromethylsulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI) solution (520 mg LiTSFI in 1 mLacetonitrile) in 1 mL of chlorobenzene. The solution (50 μL) was deposited by spin-coating at 5000 rpm for 30 s. All the spin-coating procedures were completed in a nitrogen filled glovebox. Gold electrode (3 mm × 3 mm) with a thickness of 80 nm were deposited onto the spiro-OMeTAD film through vacuum thermal evaporation.

# 2.2. Characterization and measurement

X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra were recorded by an x-ray diffractometer (XRD, DX-2700) using Cu Kα radiation (  $  \lambda = 0.154  $   nm). Top-view and cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were gotten on a field emission SEM (FESEM, SU-8020, Hitachi). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) images were recorded by a Bruker Dimension FastScan Scanning Probe Microscope (SPM) in the tapping mode. Ultraviolet–visible spectra were obtained using a spectrophotometer (Lambda 950, PerkinElmer). Photoluminescence (PL) spectra and time-resolved PL (TRPL) spectra were collected by exciting the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   films deposited on compact TiO  $  _{2}  $  /FTO substrate using a PicoQuant Fluo Time 300 with 510 nm laser excitation pulsed at a frequency of 40 MHz for PL or 1 MHz for TRPL. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were acquired with a Bruker VERTEX 70 in the range of 4000–400 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  . The elemental chemical states of the perovskite films were measured by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo K-Alpha VGS) using the Fermi edge as the energy reference. The J-V curves of the PSCs were measured on a Keithley 2400 SourceMeter in ambient conditions at room temperature. The simulated sunlight source was a 450-W xenon lamp and the illumination intensity was calibrated to 100 mWcm  $  ^{-2}  $  . The power output of the AM 1.5G Oriel solar simulator was calibrated with an NREL-traceable KG5-filtered silicon reference cell. The fabricated PSCs were tested from 2 to -0.1 V at a scan rate of 0.1 Vs  $  ^{-1}  $  , scan of 0.02 V, and scan delay time of 10 ms. External quantum efficiency (EQE) measurement was performed using a QTest Station 2000ADI system (Crowntech Inc.), including a tungsten-halogen lamp, a Si detector and a monochromator. Electrochemical Impedance spectroscopy (IS) was measured by an electrochemical workstation (IM6ex, Zahner, Germany) in the frequency range from 1 MHz to 1 Hz at 0.9 V under dark. The water contact angles of perovskite films were measured by a DataPhysics OCA 20.

To explore the effects of the various concentrations of AA in EA antisolvents on the morphologies of the perovskite films, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements were performed on the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  films. Fig. 2a-h shows the surface morphology evolution of the perovskite films with different concentrations of AA. All the perovskite films possess dense and uniform surface morphologies, and there are no pinholes on the perovskite films. This uniform and compact morphology of the perovskite films by the antisolvent EA is ascribed to the uniform nucleation and crystallization. EA is a polar solvent with a relative polarity (0.228) greater than that of other common halogenated antisolvents, including CB (0.188) and (toluene) TL (0.099). EA is miscible with the perovskite solvent (DMF and DMSO) but insoluble with the perovskite precursors  $ (\mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{-}\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{I}) $ . During spin coating, EA could extract the residual solvents quickly and promote the nucleation of the perovskite films. Besides, EA is also fairly volatile due to its low B.P. of  $ \sim 77^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ , which helped to expedite the removal of DMF and DMSO solvent more effectively than antisolvents with relatively higher BP such as CB (BP  $ \sim 132^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ ) and TL (BP  $ \sim 111^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ ). Subsequently, an abundance of nuclei was formed and uniformly distributed on the substrate, which grew into uniform grains and ultimately coalesced into dense and uniform polycrystalline films under thermal annealing.

The AA additive can adjust the grain size of the perovskite films. As shown in Fig. 2i, the average grain size increases from 276 to  $ 361~\mathrm{nm} $  as the AA concentration increases from 0 to  $ 1\% $ . Upon further increasing AA concentration, the grain size decreases to  $ 319~\mathrm{nm} $ . Fig. S1 (Supporting information) gives the grain size distributions of perovskite films without and with AA treatment. This reflects that an appropriate amount of AA in the EA antisolvent can increase the grain size of the perovskite films. The morphology variation suggests that AA can affect the growth of the perovskite crystals. The larger grain size means fewer grain boundaries, and this is desired to decrease the density of defects, impede ion migration, suppress nonradiative carrier recombination, and improve the carrier-transfer characteristics, thus enhancing the device performance and stability [48,49]. To further study the modification of the surface morphology of the perovskite films treated with AA, we used atomic force microscopy (AFM) to assess the roughness of the samples. Fig. 2j and k show AFM images of the samples without and with AA treatment, respectively. The root-mean-square roughness  $ (Rq) $  value of the perovskite films shows a decrease from 17.6 to  $ 15.0~\mathrm{nm} $  with the AA treatment.

In AAE process, the additive AA could permeate the perovskite liquid precursor film [50], resulting in the combination of AA and perovskite in the perovskite phase formation. Furthermore,   $  PbI_{2}-  $   AA intermediate phases and/or an organic cation (MAI)-AA complex can be formed with the help of the C = O groups in AA [48,49,51]. Based on this, we assumed that the combination of AA and perovskite can produce intermediate phases that can postpone the nucleation of the perovskite and facilitate grain growth.

The crystal structure of perovskite films treated with different concentrations of AA on FTO/TiO $ _2 $  substrates were studied by XRD analysis, as shown in Fig. 3a. All the perovskite films present similar XRD patterns. There are no new additional peaks and obvious peak shifts, which suggests that the AA does not affect the crystal structure of the perovskite phase and it situates on the surface and in the grain boundaries of perovskite films [52]. The diffraction peaks located at 14.12°, 20.06°, 23.50°, 24.54°, 28.46°, 31.89°, and 43.22° correspond to the (110), (112), (211), (202), (220), (310), and (224) planes of the MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite, respectively, which suggests the MAPbI $ _3 $  films are the photoactive tetragonal black phase [53,54]. All the perovskite films grew preferentially along the (110) orientation plane and there is not the representative PbI $ _2 $  peak at 2θ = 12.5°, reflecting a complete transformation to perovskite phase. The variations of the peak intensity and full-width-at-halfmaxima (FWHM) of the (110) peak are shown in Fig. 3b. It is noted that the perovskite film treated with 1% AA exhibited the maximum peak intensity and narrowest FWHM value of the (110) peak, which implies better crystallinity. This shows that AA can effectively improve perovskite film crystallinity.

In addition to the morphology variation, the application of AA also influenced the chemical bonding state of the perovskite films, as demonstrated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra measurements. Fig. 4a shows that for the perovskite film with AA treatment the characteristic binding energy peaks of Pb (Pb  $ 4\mathrm{f}_{7/2} $ , Pb  $ 4\mathrm{f}_{5/2} $ ) moved to higher binding energy compared to those of the perovskite film without AA treatment, while the O 1 s peaks moved to lower binding energy, which indicates chemical interaction between the AA and the perovskite (Fig. S2). The interaction is mainly from the O of the C = O functional group of AA, which affords lone pair electrons to the uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  of the perovskite [49,55,56]. On the other hand, for the perovskite films without AA treatment, the strong Pb  $ 4\mathrm{f}_{5/2} $  peak at  $ 143.5\mathrm{eV} $  and Pb  $ 4\mathrm{f}_{7/2} $  peak at  $ 138.6\mathrm{eV} $  are characteristic of the  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  state in the perovskite [57], whereas the weak  $ 4\mathrm{f}_{5/2} $  peak at  $ 141.7\mathrm{eV} $  and  $ 4\mathrm{f}_{7/2} $  peak at  $ 136.9\mathrm{eV} $  are characteristic of the  $ \mathrm{Pb}^0 $  state. The  $ \mathrm{Pb}^0 $  state indicates that there is metallic lead in the perovskite film, which is from the unsaturated  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  [58,59]. The existence of metallic lead is ascribed to iodide deficiency in the perovskite lattice, which is caused by the decomposition of the perovskite during annealing [60]. However, the metallic lead peaks vanished in the AA treated film. This observation indicates that the molecular interaction between AA and Pb can restrain the production of metallic lead and prevent the decomposition of the perovskite film during annealing.

The  $ \tau_{\mathrm{avg}} $  of the AA (1%) treated film (237 ns) is longer than that of the untreated film (96 ns). It is reported that the carboxyl group (C = O) has a Lewis base property, and it could afford an electron pair from C = O double bond to uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  in perovskite [66,67], thus passivating defects in the perovskite film.

The trap state density of the perovskite films was investigated by space-charge-limited current (SCLC) method using electron type devices (FTO/TiO $ _2 $ /MAPbI $ _3 $ /PCBM/Ag) to reveal the influence of AA on the defects in the perovskite films. Fig. 5d shows the dark current–voltage properties of the devices fabricated using the perovskite films without and with AA treatment. In the region of low bias voltages, the linear relationship between the current and voltage indicates ohmic behavior. For bias voltages above the kink point (trap-filled limit, TFL), the current quickly rises nonlinearly, indicating that the trap states are fully filled via the infused carriers [68]. The trap density ( $ N_t $ ) was estimated from the trap-filled limit voltage ( $ V_{\text{TFL}} $ ) by Eq. (3).

$$
V _ {\mathrm{TFL}} = \frac {e N _ {\mathrm{t}} L ^ {2}}{2 \varepsilon \varepsilon_ {0}}
$$

(3)

where  $ e $  is the elementary charge,  $ N_{\mathrm{t}} $  is the trap-state density,  $ L $  is the thickness of the perovskite film,  $ \varepsilon $  is the relative dielectric constant of perovskite  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  ( $ \varepsilon = 32 $ ), and  $ \varepsilon_0 $  is the vacuum permittivity. The trap-state densities were calculated to be  $ 9.83 \times 10^{15}~\mathrm{cm}^{-3} $  and  $ 6.29 \times 10^{15}~\mathrm{cm}^{-3} $  for the perovskite film without and with AA treatment, respectively. The remarkably lower trap-state density suggests that the AA treatment can decrease the defect density in the perovskite film.

This is beneficial for the reduction of carrier recombination losses in the PSCs, which resulted in the improvement of   $  J_{sc}  $  . A comparison of the J-V curves measured in the reverse and forward scan directions for PSC devices without and with AA treatment is shown in Fig. 5S. The AA treatment can greatly reduce the hysteresis (the hysteresis index is reduced from 0.16 to 0.08). The hysteresis of a PSC predominantly arose from the perovskite absorber and the contact materials. There are three possible origins for the hysteresis: (1) bulk or surface defects of perovskite acting as traps (carrier trapping/detrapping), (2) ferroelectric polarization, and (3) iodide ion and organic cation ion migration. Usually, the hysteresis of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite film solar cell is larger than that of the mixed cations FA  $  _{x}  $  MA  $  _{(1-x)}  $  PbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite film solar cell [69–71]. The main cause is that MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite has lower activation energy barrier for ions migration than that of FA  $  _{x}  $  MA  $  _{(1-x)}  $  PbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite [72].

In Fig. 6c, the devices with AA treatment have higher and more-concentrated PCE distributions compared with the control device, based on PCE statistics of 30 control devices and AA-treated devices. The average PCE of the PSC devices was improved from 18.8% (0% AA) to 20.6% (1% AA). The time-dependent stabilized PCEs under bias at the maximum power point were measured under 100 mWcm  $  ^{-2}  $  AM1.5G illumination. The AA-treated and untreated devices display stabilized photocurrents of 22.8 and 20.9 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  and stabilized efficiencies of 20.5% and 18.4%, respectively (Fig. S6), which are close to the  $  J_{sc}  $  and PCE from the J-V curves. Fig. 6d gives the external quantum efficiency (EQE) of the PSCs without and with AA treatment. The device with AA treatment has increased EQE in the wavelength scope from 350 to 760 nm. The devices without and with AA treatment have integrated current densities of 22.1 and 23.6 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $ , respectively, which are consistent with the  $  J_{sc}  $  from the J-V measurements. Similarly, the EQE of the device with AA treatment is higher than that of the device without AA treatment, reflecting increased photogenerated carrier collection of the AA treated device.

In order to further investigate the charge transport dynamics of the PSCs, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were conducted in the dark over the 0.1 Hz–100 kHz frequency range. Fig. 6e gives the dark Nyquist plots of the PSCs without and with AA treatment, and the corresponding equivalent circuits (shown in the inset) were used to analyze the measurement results [73]. The electrical parameters of the PSCs were obtained by fitting, and the series resistance ( $ R_{\text{s}} $ )/recombination resistance ( $ R_{\text{rec}} $ ) values of the PSCs fabricated with and without AA are 22.2/3180 and 32.4/2501 Ω, respectively. Compared with the control cell, the AA treated cell has a smaller  $ R_{\text{s}} $  and larger  $ R_{\text{rec}} $ . A lower  $ R_{\text{s}} $  suggests more effective carrier transfer from the perovskite layer to the electron transport layer (ETL) and hole transport layer (HTL), whereas a higher  $ R_{\text{rec}} $  reflects fewer defects in the perovskite layers and at the interfaces. Since the ETL and HTL are the same in the fabricated PSCs, a lower  $ R_{\text{s}} $  and higher  $ R_{\text{rec}} $  for the AA-treated PSCs are due to the improved crystal quality and defect passivation of the perovskite film with AA treatment, as discussed earlier with regards to the SEM, XRD, PL, and TRPL results. Therefore, reduced defect density and less recombination in the AA treated MAPbI $ _3 $  film improved the performance of the PSCs [73].

Long-term stability is an important parameter for PSCs as well, especially during operation in ambient air. We also investigated the air stability of the PSCs in the ambient environment (25 °C; RH: 30%). As shown in Fig. 6f, the PSCs with AA treatment had better stability than the untreated one. After 1224 h, the PCE of PSCs with AA treatment maintained 87% of their initial efficiency, while the PCE of the untreated PSC was 80% of its initial efficiency. # 4. Conclusions

In conclusion, we applied an effective AAE approach to enhance the efficiency and stability of planar MAPbI $ _3 $  PSCs. The AA, which contains C = O functional groups, was added into EA green antisol-vent, and its role and effect on the perovskite film's quality and corresponding photovoltaic properties were systematically stud-

ied. By adding AA, uniform and compact perovskite films having larger grain, fewer grain boundaries, and lower defect density were prepared. This is beneficial for the reduction of carrier recombination losses in the PSCs. The higher-quality perovskites films could be ascribed to the intermolecular interaction between the AA and the perovskite. The PSC incorporating the AA-modified  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  film shows a higher PCE of  $ 21.1\% $  than the unmodified PSC  $ (19.2\%) $ . Additionally, the air stability of the modified PSC was improved as a result of the enhancement of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  film quality and hydrophobicity, and it retained  $ 87\% $  of its initial PCE after  $ 1224\mathrm{h} $  in the ambient atmosphere (30% humidity) without capsulation. This work affords an environmentally friendly AAE approach to fabricate high-quality perovskite film and enhance the corresponding device properties.
