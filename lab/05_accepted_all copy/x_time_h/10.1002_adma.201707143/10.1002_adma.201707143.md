However, the popularly applied perovskite films are polycrystalline, and therefore the trap states at the grain boundaries still exist, which becomes one of the significant obstacles that hamper further improvement. These shallow defects at the grain boundary can scatter the carriers during their diffusion. At the same time, the heterogeneous contacts with the corresponding buffer layers (hole transport layer and electron transport layer) can lead to distortion of the interfacial crystal structure owing to the large lattice mismatch,  $  ^{[3,4]}  $   which always produces deep trap states and decreases the output

Effective passivation and stabilization of both the inside and interface of a perovskite layer are crucial for perovskite solar cells (PSCs), in terms of efficiency, reproducibility, and stability. Here, the first formamidinium lead iodide  $ (\delta\text{-FAPbI}_3) $  polymorph passivated and stabilized MAPbI $ _3 $  PSCs are reported. This novel MAPbI $ _3 $ / $ \delta\text{-FAPbI}_3 $  structure is realized via treating a mixed organic cation MA $ _x $ FA $ _{1-x} $ PbI $ _3 $  perovskite film with methylamine (MA) gas. In addition to the morphology healing, MA gas can also induce the formation of  $ \delta\text{-FAPbI}_3 $  phase within the perovskite film. The in situ formed 1D  $ \delta\text{-FAPbI}_3 $  polymorph behaves like an organic scaffold that can passivate the trap state, tunnel contact, and restrict organic-cation diffusion. As a result, the device efficiency is easily boosted to  $ 21\% $ . Furthermore, the stability of the MAPbI $ _3 $ / $ \delta\text{-FAPbI}_3 $  film is also obviously improved. This  $ \delta\text{-FAPbI}_3 $  phase passivation strategy opens up a new direction of perovskite structure modification for further improving stability without sacrificing efficiency.

A variety of “interlayer” materials have been developed to passivate the traps and enhance the charge carrier selectivity. There are two main categories of materials that can be used for this purpose. The first one is extrinsic materials such as   $  PbI_{2}  $  ,  $  ^{[5]}  $   polymers,  $  ^{[6-8]}  $   fullerene derivatives,  $  ^{[9]}  $     $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $  ,  $  ^{[10]}  $   surfactant,  $  ^{[11,12]}  $   and amino acids.  $  ^{[13]}  $   The other one is 2D/3D mixed multi-dimensional (MD) hybrid perovskites.  $  ^{[14-16]}  $   This interface engineering can help achieve improved voltage output through realizing a preferred energy level alignment and/or passivation of defects.  $  ^{[8,17]}  $

Inspired by the structure of highly efficient heterojunction with intrinsic thin layer silicon solar cells, the passivation of a photoactive layer on both sides with larger band gap but compositionally identical (intrinsic) materials (a-Si:H) can enable a closer contact and minimized barrier at their interface.  $  ^{[18-20]}  $   Therefore, the intrinsic passivating materials are theoretically superior to the extrinsic ones regarding compositional and/or structural compatibility. The MD perovskite modified light absorber is a successful example of the enhancement of device stability, but unfortunately it failed to maintain low band gap advantage of the 3D perovskite materials.  $  ^{[14,15,21-25]}  $

This study presents a new approach and a novel concept of induction intrinsic passivating phase within a host perovskite film toward improved efficiency and stability.

The distinct effect of MA gas treatment on various perovskite films can be seen from the ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) measurement as presented in Figure 1b. For the pure MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  film, no significant change in the absorbance onset is observed after the MA gas treatment (Figure 1b, r-MA and g-MA). In contrast, the MA gas treatment on pure FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  (r-FA) leads to a dramatic decrease of light absorbance over the whole visible range and the resulting yellow film (g-FA) has identical absorption behavior to the  $  \delta  $ -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ( $  \delta  $ -FA) prepared by spin-coating and low-temperature annealing.  $  ^{[30]}  $  r-FA20 film with a composition of MA  $  _{0.8}  $  FA  $  _{0.2}  $  PbI  $  _{3}  $  showed absorption onset at 800 nm between those of r-MA and r-FA. Interestingly, a blueshift of the absorbance onset from 800 to 780 nm was observed when we treat the r-FA20 perovskite film with the MA gas, which is a clear indication of the formation of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  phase in the film. This perovskite phase transition after the gas treatment was also proved by the photoluminescence (PL) spectra with the corresponding emission peak of FA20 sample blueshifted from 800 to 780 nm as shown in Figure 1c (blue to green line), which is in agreement with the PL curve of g-MA. Together with the results of XRD analysis, we can conclude that the MA gas treatment on the mixed-organic-cation r-FA20 perovskite film resulted in a mixed phase of  $  \delta  $ -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  inside MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  perovskite film, which is denoted as g-FA20 in the following discussion.

Figure 1d shows the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image of the as spin-coated  $ \mathrm{MA}_{0.8}\mathrm{FA}_{0.2}\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  ( $ r $ -FA20) perovskite film appearing translucent with rod-like structures. There are many void areas between them that are typical of one-step-processed perovskite films using DMSO solvent. Inside MA gas atmosphere, the raw perovskite film behaves like a sponge and uptakes excess MA molecules to form a liquid with an empirical formula of  $ \mathrm{MA}_{0.8}\mathrm{FA}_{0.2}\mathrm{PbI}_3\cdot x\mathrm{MA} $  ( $ x \approx 3 $ ) with mass concentrations around  $ 87\% $ . In the liquid, the perovskite crystals can rearrange to fill the void area. After removal from the MA gas environment and the following heat treatment, a dense, smooth film can be formed as shown in Figure 1e, which is responsible for the visual evolution of the film from dull to shiny  $ g $ -FA20 film (Figure S1, Supporting Information). The fine structure of  $ g $ -FA20 was also studied by the high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM) as illustrated in Figure 1f. The sample was prepared by direct deposition of precursor solution

on an amorphous carbon covered copper mesh for HRTEM and treated with MA gas as mentioned above. The different morphology on the carbon film is due to the hydrophobicity of the carbon surface, but it is still helpful in understanding the phase separation behavior. The g-FA20 has a polycrystalline nature showing clearly two lattice fringes separation of 3.17 and 2.38 Å, which could be well indexed to the (014) plane of MAPbI $ _{3} $  and the (121) plane of δ-FAPbI $ _{3} $ , respectively. This is a direct proof of the induced phase separation after MA gas healing method and reconstruction of the MAPbI $ _{3} $ /δ-FAPbI $ _{3} $  structure in the g-FA20 film. As evidence of the δ-FAPbI $ _{3} $  structure at room temperature is thermodynamically stable than the α-FAPbI $ _{3} $  perovskite phase, one plausible explanation about the phase separation is the different precipitating kinetics of MA-based and FA-based hybrids due to their different ion radius and hydrogen bond number to the Pb–I framework.

To further check the distribution of the gas induced   $  \delta  $  -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   phase in the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   film, grazing-incidence XRD (GIXRD) was performed on the treated film with different incidence angles varying from 0.5° to 7° and the peak intensities were normalized to the (110) diffraction of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   (Figure 2a). At low incidence angles, the evanescent wave penetrating depth of X-rays is limited. Therefore, most of the scattering signal mainly comes from the top layers. The GIXRD with the incidence angle of 0.5° showed the strongest (100) diffraction from   $  \delta  $  -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  , which gradually decreased when the incidence angle increased from 0.5° to 7°. The change of ratios between the two intensities is presented in the inset of Figure 2a. This is an indication that the formation of   $  \delta  $  -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   is dispersed throughout the whole g-FA20 film with slightly higher distribution on the surface than the inside.

The following work function measurements gave consistent information (Figure 2b). The surface work function was measured by the photoelectron spectroscopy in the air, which can give the electronic properties up to several nanometers so that only the surface of the g-FA20 film can be studied. The pure phase  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  and  $ \delta $ -FAPbI $ _3 $  were also checked for comparison. As expected, the work function of the g-FA20 film surface is  $ 5.51\mathrm{eV} $ , which is between the work function of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  ( $ 5.36\mathrm{eV} $ ) and  $ \delta $ -FAPbI $ _3 $  ( $ 5.60\mathrm{eV} $ ). The interior part (denoted as film inside), scratched out top half perovskite layer by a razor, shows a work function of  $ 5.49\mathrm{eV} $ , which is very close to that of the film surface ( $ 5.51\mathrm{eV} $ ).

To further check the depth-dependent properties of the g-FA20 film, the PL spectra from different excitation wavelength (514 and 633 nm) are shown in Figure S3 (Supporting Information). It is known that the longer wavelength laser has larger penetration ability. Therefore the 514 nm laser mainly excites near the film surface, while the 633 nm laser can excite deeper part of the film. Upon excitation by laser irradiations at 514 and 633 nm, the PL intensities are enhanced by 33% and 67%, respectively, after introducing δ-FAPbI $ _{3} $ . The more significant enhancement of the PL by 633 nm laser indicates that the inside of the perovskite films is also well passivated. This phenomenon is very different from the PbI $ _{2} $  passivation behavior, where only the film surface is passivated. $ ^{[5]} $

This effect can be evidenced by photophysical characterizations of the materials. The continuous wavelength and time-correlated single-photon counting (CWPL and TCSPC) spectra of both g-MA and g-FA20 films with different contact layers are illustrated in Figure 2c,d. The CWPL spectra were measured by an excitation at  $ 460~\mathrm{nm} $  for glass/perovskite:poly(methyl methacrylate) and glass/perovskite:(spiro-OMeTAD) specimens. The PL intensity of the g-FA20 film on the glass substrate without spiro-OMeTAD is 1.9 times and 1.6 times higher than that of the reference g-MA films measured from the film surface (Figure 2c) and glass side (Figure S4, Supporting Information), respectively. Therefore, even there is an electronic insulating   $  \delta  $  -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   phase presented within the perovskite film, it does not restrict the carriers injection to the neighboring layers (Figure 2c).

Additionally, TCSPC measurements were performed with the same samples to compare the lifetime of the photoexcited species in both types of perovskite films (Figure 2d). The PL decays show biexponential behavior with both fast and slow components, and the detailed trace fit data are listed in Table S2 (Supporting Information). We assigned  $ \tau_{1} $  as a decay component closely related to nonradiative recombination by the traps on the film surface and grain boundaries, and  $ \tau_{2} $  as a component of radiative recombination from bulk perovskite. The induction of the alien  $ \delta $ -FAPbI $ _{3} $  phase in MAPbI $ _{3} $  significantly increased the  $ \tau_{1} $  and  $ \tau_{2} $  from 0.78 and 7.5 ns to 1.72 and 10.24 ns, respectively, further indicating the effective trap passivation by the formed  $ \delta $ -FAPbI $ _{3} $ . Additionally, when spiroOMeTAD was used as the hole quencher and contacted into the perovskite film, both  $ \tau_{1} $  and  $ \tau_{2} $  were significantly shortened (Figure 2d and Table S2, Supporting Information).

In Figure 3b, photocurrent density–voltage (J-V) characteristics of the devices fabricated with g-FA20 as light harvesting layer are presented and compared with those of a control device using g-MA, which our group has reported recently.  $  ^{[27]}  $   As shown in the J-V curves, the device based on g-FA20 exhibited an open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) of 1.13 V, a short-circuit current density (  $  J_{SC}  $  ) of 23.7 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and a fill factor (FF) of 79.6%, giving an overall PCE of 21.25%, whereas the control device under the same conditions showed an overall PCE of 18.26% with a   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.05 V, a   $  J_{SC}  $   of 23.3 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and an FF of 74.9%. The superior performance of the g-FA20 device is mainly attributed to a higher   $  V_{OC}  $   and FF and is among the highest efficiencies reported based on the devices made by MA gas treatment. The significant enhancement of the   $  V_{OC}  $   from 1.05 to 1.13 V is due primarily to the interface engineering by the formation of the insulating   $  \delta  $  -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   phase within the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   domains. The passivation of the surface trap highly reduces the energy loss during the carrier injecting to the hole and electron transport materials.

The improvement of FF value from 0.75 to 0.79 is because of the suppression of nonradiative recombination or trap density through the type II passivation.  $  ^{[41]}  $   It is evident that the densities of hole and electron trap states are reduced in the case of g-FA20 film based on the space-charge-limited-current results as presented in Figure S5 and Table S3 (Supporting Information). # Experimental Section

Device Fabrication: Chemically etched FTO glass (Nippon Sheet Glass) was cleaned with a detergent solution, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol. To form a 20–25 nm thick   $  TiO_{2}  $   blocking layer, diluted titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetylacetonate) (TAA) solution (Sigma-Aldrich) in ethanol (0.2 mL TAA in 6 mL of anhydrous ethanol) was sprayed onto the glass surface at 450 °C. A 200 nm mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   was coated on the substrate by spin-coating at a speed of 2000 rpm for 10 s with a ramp-up of 1000 rpm s  $  ^{-1}  $   from a commercially available   $  TiO_{2}  $   paste (Dyesol-30NRD) in ethanol; the weight ratio of   $  TiO_{2}  $   paste to ethanol was 9:1. After spin-coating, the substrate was immediately dried on a hotplate at 80 °C, and the substrates were then sintered at 500 °C for 20 min before the deposition of the perovskite layer. A 1.4 M perovskite precursor solution was prepared by mixing   $  PbI_{2}:MAI:FAI  $   (1:0.8:0.2, molar ratio) in DMSO (the control one comprises   $  PbI_{2}:MAI  $   1:1 only). The resulting perovskite solutions were spin-coated on the substrates at 4000 rpm for 30 s, followed by a heat treatment at 100 °C for 10 min. The perovskite films were placed in a   $  CH_{3}NH_{2}  $   gas environment for 2–3 s at room temperature, followed by an additional heating for 15 min at 100 °C. The HTM (spiro-OMeTAD) solution was prepared by dissolving 91 mg of spiro-OMeTAD (Merck) with additives in 1 mL of chlorobenzene (additives: 21 μL of Li-bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide from a stock solution, 520 mg in 1 mL of acetonitrile, 16 μL of FK209, tris(2-(1H-pyrazol-1-yl)-4-tert-butylpyridine)-cobalt(III) tris(bis(tri fluoromethylsulfonyl)imide, (375 mg in 1 mL of acetonitrile) and 36 μL of 4-tertbutyl pyridine were added. The HTM layer was formed by spin-coating the solution at 4000 rpm for 20 s and followed by the deposition of an 80 nm thick Au electrode by thermal evaporation. Deposition of the perovskite and spiro-OMeTAD was conducted inside a glove box under nitrogen.

Photocurrent J-V Measurements: J-V characteristics were recorded by applying a potential external bias to the cell while recording the generated photocurrent with a digital source meter (Keithley Model 2400). The light source was a 450 W xenon lamp (Oriel) equipped with a SchottK113 Tempax sunlight filter (Praezisions Glas & Optik GmbH) to match the emission spectrum of the lamp to the AM1.5G standard. The light intensity was calibrated with an NREL certified KG5 filtered Si reference diode. The solar cells were masked with a metal aperture of   $  0.16 \, cm^2  $   to define the active area. Hysteresis curves were routinely recorded.

Stability Measurements: The cells were placed in a sealed cell holder that was flushed with a flow of argon of 30 mL min  $  ^{-1}  $  . J-V curves were characterized by an electronic system using 22 bits delta-sigma analogic to digital converter. For I-V curves measurement, a scan rate of 25 mV s  $  ^{-1}  $   with a step of 5 mV was used, maintaining the device nearly at room temperature. Between each measurement, the cells were maintained at the maximum power point using an MPPT algorithm under 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $   with an LED lamp without UV light. A reference Si-photodiode was placed in the holder to verify the stability of the light.

XRD and Wide-Angle GIXRD: XRD was performed using a D8 Advance diffractometer (Bruker AXS) model (Bruker Corporation, Billerica, MA, USA). The machine was equipped with an air blade to remove the air scattering. Background was extracted automatically.

UV–Vis Absorption: UV–vis absorption spectra were recorded on a PerkinElmer lambda 850 with 150 mm InGaAs integration sphere. Samples were illuminated with by a 450 W Xe lamp filtered through a double monochromator (5 nm landpass) directed onto the sample.

Steady PL: Steady PL spectra were recorded on a Perkin LS-55 fluorescence spectrometer excited at 500 nm.

SEM: Scanning electron microscopy was performed using a high-resolution scanning electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV (ZEISS Merlin).

HRTEM: High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (JEM-2100plus, JEOL) was performed at an acceleration voltage of 200 KV. g-FA20 sample was deposited on the TEM grid with amorphous carbon support, and MA gas treatment was done directly on the TEM grid.

Time-Resolved Photoluminescence: Experiments were performed with a Fluorolog using a pulsed source either at 406 nm (NanoLED 402-LH from Horiba, pulse width <200 ps, 11 pJ per pulse,   $  \approx1~mm^{-2}  $   spot size) or at 460 nm (Ps diode lasers BDS-SM, pulse with <100 ps, from photonic solutions, 0.2 pJ per pulse,   $  \approx1~mm^{-2}  $   spot size), and the signal was recorded at 780 nm using the TCSPC technique. The samples were excited from the mesoporous   $  SiO_{2}  $   side under ambient conditions. A mono-exponential fitting was used to analyze the background-corrected PL decay signal.

Ultraviolet Photoelectron Emission: Spectra were measured with a Riken Keiki AC-2 spectrometer in the air. A gold electrode was connected to the sample, and the substrate was then illuminated with the monochromatic light energy of 0.05 eV step.

TPV: The device was serially connected to a digital oscilloscope (Tektronix DPO5104B) and the input impedance of the oscilloscope set to 1 MΩ, to form the open-conditions, for monitoring the charge density decay. The test was measured under 0.3 sun illumination. An attenuated UV laser pulse (SRS NL 100 Nitrogen Laser) was used as a small perturbation to the background illumination on the device. The laser-pulse-induced photovoltage variation (ΔV) was smaller than 5% of the   $  V_{OC}  $   produced by the background illumination. The wavelength of the   $  N_{2}  $   laser was 337 nm, the repeating frequency was 10 Hz, and the pulse width was less than 3.5 ns.
