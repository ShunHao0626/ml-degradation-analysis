# Induced Crystallization of Perovskites by a Perylene Underlayer for High-Performance Solar Cells

Zhao-Kui Wang, $ ^{\dagger, \S} $  Xiu Gong, $ ^{\ddagger, \S} $  Meng Li, $ ^{\dagger} $  Yun Hu, $ ^{\dagger} $  Jin-Miao Wang, $ ^{\dagger} $  Heng Ma, $ ^{\ddagger} $  and Liang-Sheng Liao $ ^{*, \dagger} $

$  ^{\dagger}  $  Jiangsu Key Laboratory for Carbon-Based Functional Materials & Devices, Institute of Functional Nano & Soft Materials (FUNSOM), Soochow University, Suzhou, Jiangsu 215123, China

$  ^{\ddagger}  $  College of Physics and Electronic Engineering, Henan Normal University, Xinxiang, Henan 453007, China

ABSTRACT: Perovskite crystallization and interface engineering are regarded as the most crucial factors in achieving high-performance planar heterojunction (PHJ) perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Herein, we demonstrate a thin perylene underlayer via a solution-processable method. By using branch-shaped perylene film as a seed-mediated underlayer, crystalline perovskites with fabric morphology can be formed, which allows for obvious improvement in absorption by a light scattering effect. With its deep highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) level, perylene also plays an important role in the energy-level tailoring of poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrenesulphonate) (PEDOT:PSS) and  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_x\mathrm{Cl}_{3 - x} $ . In addition, perylene and perovskites form a fully crystalline heterojunction, which is

beneficial for minimizing the defect and trap densities. Due to these merits, a maximum power conversion efficiency of  $ 17.06\% $  with improved cell stability is achieved. The finding in this work provides a simple route to control perovskite crystallization and to optimize the interfaces in PHJ PSCs simultaneously.

KEYWORDS: perovskite solar cells, interface engineering, perylene underlayer, induced crystallization, stability

Organic-inorganic halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) provide great potential for the photovoltaics industry due to their unique advantages, such as high efficiency.  $  ^{1-4}  $   Nevertheless, PSCs are still in the early stages of commercialization compared with other mature solar cell technologies due to existing concerns such as the operational mechanism,  $  ^{5-7}  $   lead toxicity,  $  ^{8-10}  $   material stability,  $  ^{11,12}  $   and interfacial engineering.  $  ^{13-15}  $   Recently, planar heterojunction (PHJ) PSCs with controllable interface engineering demonstrated cell performance comparable to that of mesoscopic structure based PSCs.  $  ^{16-18}  $   In typical PHJ PSCs, carrier behaviors of traveling through a transport pathway, including the perovskite active layer, the electron and hole transport layers (ETL and HTL, respectively), the electrodes, and the corresponding interfaces, can be manipulated effectively by choosing suitable interfacial layers. According to the layer-stacking sequence, PHJ PSCs are divided into two groups: the conventional n-i-p structure in which the ETL is deposited on the bottom cathode and the p-i-n structure in which the HTL is deposited on the bottom anode. Particularly, the p-i-n configuration is regarded as more suitable for flexible devices because these devices can be fabricated at low temperature (<150 °C). The first p-i-n-type PHJ PSC was reported in

2013 and referred to the concept of donor/acceptor in conventional organic solar cells.  $  ^{19}  $   Noticeably, many conventional hole transport materials are difficult to apply in the p-i-n-type PHJ PSCs because the general solvents used for the perovskites, such as N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), have very good solubility, which can wash off most of the underlayer of perovskites. Poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene): poly(styrenesulphonate) (PEDOT:PSS) is mostly employed as the p-type layer for hole extraction in p-i-n-type PHJ PSCs. However, the cell stability suffered from the acidic nature of the PEDOT:PSS dispersions.  $  ^{20,21}  $   In addition, the low work function of PEDOT:PSS caused a relatively small open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ) of around 0.90 V in these p-i-n-type PHJ PSCs.  $  ^{22,23}  $   In addition to some inorganic HTLs, such as   $  NiO_{x}  $  , CuO, and graphene oxide (GO),  $  ^{24-26}  $   some organic HTLs,  $  ^{27-31}  $   such as poly[N,N'-bis(4-butylphenyl)-N,N'-bis(phenyl)benzidine] (poly-TPD) and poly(triaryl amine) (PTAA), which demon-

Crystallization of the perovskites, including the orientation, grain size, and morphology, determines the device performance of mainly PSCs. A sequential deposition method and process techniques, such as two-step deposition techniques,  $  ^{32}  $  dual-source vacuum vapor deposition,  $  ^{33}  $  and addition of additives,  $  ^{34-37}  $  have been developed to achieve high-quality perovskite films. In general, crystallization starts from a relatively slow nucleation step, which is associated with the condition of the underlayer of the perovskite active layer.  $  ^{38-40}  $  Therefore, besides the concentration, composition, solvent choice, and annealing temperature of the perovskite precursor itself, the underlayer also strongly affects the morphology and the crystallization of the perovskite films.  $  ^{36-41}  $  Recently, some other routes to improve the perovskite crystallization by the underlayer modification, such as inserting an interlayer between the PEDOT:PSS and perovskite layers and adding DMSO to PEDOT:PSS,  $  ^{27,41}  $  have also been reported. Therefore, new hole interfacial layers with suitable energy levels, which can act as the underlayer to tune and control the growth of the perovskite films, are necessary to develop high-performance and stable PHJ PSCs (Supporting Information Table S1).

Perylene   $  \left(\mathrm{C}_{20}\mathrm{H}_{12}\right)  $   is one of the simple aromatic hydrocarbons and exhibits a high hole mobility in the crystalline state.  $  ^{42}  $   A crystalline perylene interfacial layer is expected to exhibit high performance in PHJ PSCs due to fewer grain boundaries and low defect and trap densities. In addition, perylene has a deep highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) level, which is situated near the HOMO level of the perovskite   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{x}Cl_{3-x}  $   (-5.5 eV).  $  ^{43}  $   The step energy difference among PEDOT:PSS, perylene, and   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{x}Cl_{3-x}  $   is beneficial for hole transport and collection. In this work, we report a solution-processed perylene interfacial layer inserted between PEDOT:PSS and   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{x}Cl_{3-x}  $  . Crystalline perylene films were formed by an atmosphere-controlled spin-coating method with optimized solvent and annealing conditions. Fabric and dense   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{x}Cl_{3-x}  $   films were formed by a one-step coating

of a perovskite precursor solution onto the underlayer of perylene films. A resulting PHJ PSC device presents the best performance, with a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 17.06%, a   $  V_{oc}  $   of 0.98 V, a short-circuit current density (  $  J_{sc}  $  ) of 22.61 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  , and a fill factor (FF) of 77%. There is remarkable improvement in PCE compared with the reference device (12.67%) based on a PEDOT:PSS-only underlayer.

# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Perylene is a widely used small molecule in optoelectronic devices.  $  ^{42}  $   The packing perylene molecules can result in various physical and chemical properties. Solution-based deposition methods have been developed to deposit different micro/nanostructures of perylene.  $  ^{44,45}  $   Herein, we report a facile method for the preparation of perylene micro/nanostructure films by first dissolving perylene powder directly into different solvents of ethyl alcohol, chloroform, acetone, or chlorobenzene. Perylene films are formed by spin-coating the prepared solutions onto PEDOT:PSS in atmospheric conditions. Due to the interaction between the polarity of solvent and perylene, the morphology of perylene films is strongly dependent on the used solvent (Supporting Information Figure S1). Aligned strip-like perylene nanorods were obtained in chloroform-based samples compared with the poor configuration in the other solvent-based samples. With further device tests, PHJ PSCs with a chloroform-based perylene interfacial layer also demonstrated the best performance (Supporting Information Figures S2 and S3 and Tables S2 and S3). Noticeably, an obvious increase of   $  V_{oc}  $   (from 0.91 to 0.96 V) appeared in all perylene interfacial layer based PHJ PSCs regardless of the solvents used, which is associated with the relatively deep HOMO level of perylene compared with that of PEDOT:PSS.  $  ^{43}  $   No large difference in   $  J_{sc}  $   (from 19.73 to 20.36 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  ) was observed among these perylene-based (different solvent) PHJ PSCs. It means that the enhanced PCE in perylene-based (chloroform) PHJ PSC was mainly attributed to the improved FF (from 72 to 77%). Actually, the FF in PHJ solar cells is strongly related to the interface conditions between the active layer, the ETLs and HTLs, and the electrodes.

Therefore, a detailed investigation of the effect of a different underlayer on the device performance of PHJ PSCs is necessary.

As is known, interface modification and surface treatment are usually utilized for performance optimization in PHJ PSCs.  $  ^{14,15,46,47}  $   Ideal contact matching between the underlayer and the perovskite active layer, including the surface, interface, and the energy levels, is desired for achieving high performance. Herein, we carried out a study on how perovskite crystallization relies on the different underlayers. Four cases of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{x}Cl_{3-x}  $   deposition on bare ITO, ITO/perylene, ITO/PEDOT:PSS, and ITO/PEDOT:PSS/perylene were investigated. A flow diagram of the device fabrication with the cell structure is shown in Figure 1. Perovskite precursor solutions were prepared by mixing methylammonium iodide (MAI) and lead chloride (  $  PbCl_{2}  $  ) powder in anhydrous DMF with a molar ratio of 3:1. After deposition and annealing of the perovskite films, phenyl-  $  C_{61}  $  -butyric acid methyl ester (  $  PC_{61}BM  $  ) and bathophenanthroline (Bphen) were spin-coated sequentially. A Ag electrode was finally vacuum-evaporated through a shadow mask with a defined device area of 0.0725 cm  $  ^{2}  $   or 4.0 cm  $  ^{2}  $  .

Figure 2a–d shows the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of bare ITO, ITO/perylene, ITO/PEDOT:PSS, and ITO/PEDOT:PSS/perylene films. Perylene was dissolved in chloroform with a concentration of 1 mg/mL. The ITO presents a rough surface morphology, as shown in Figure 2a. Random distribution of perylene micro/nanostructures appears in the sample of ITO/perylene (Figure 2b), which was ascribed to the coarse surface of the ITO substrate.  $  ^{19}  $   The PEDOT:PSS film on ITO exhibits a relatively smooth morphology (Figure 2c) because of the good film-forming ability of PEDOT:PSS on the ITO substrate. As shown in Figure 2d, aligned perylene nanorods were formed on the ITO/PEDOT:PSS films due to the flat surface morphology of the PEDOT:PSS layer. The SEM images of corresponding   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{x}Cl_{3-x}  $   films deposited on these underlayers are shown in Figure 2e–h. The film coverage of the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{x}Cl_{3-x}  $   films on the bare ITO substrate is very poor, which is less than 70% (Supporting Information Table S4). Many large voids (over 1   $  \mu  $  m in size) (Figure 2e) can be seen, which might be generated during the crystallization process of the perovskites due to the rough ITO surface. The film coverage is improved (81.5%) when the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{x}Cl_{3-x}  $   film is deposited onto the ITO/perylene (Figure 2f). However, the large voids still exist due to the disordered and deformed perylene microstructures. Good   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{x}Cl_{3-x}  $   crystalline films with a surface coverage of 91.7% are obtained on the ITO/PEDOT:PSS underlayers (Figure 2g). Nevertheless, there are still many gaps among the perovskite crystalline grains. By utilizing aligned perylene microstructure film as the underlayer, the quality of the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{x}Cl_{3-x}  $   films (Figure 2g) is obviously improved with decreased voids and gaps, which resulted in improved film coverage of 94.2%.

# CONCLUSION

In summary, we have demonstrated a solution-processed microstructure perylene layer acting as the underlayer for   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{x}Cl_{3-x}  $   perovskite deposition. The crystalline perylene underlayer plays several important roles in improving the device performance of PHJ PSCs. (1) Branch-shaped crystalline perylene acts as a seeding layer to grow fabric perovskites with improved crystalline quality. (2) Perylene works as a hole interfacial layer with the role of energy-level tailoring for PEDOT:PSS and   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{x}Cl_{3-x}  $  , which is beneficial for hole transport and collection. (3) Perylene and   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{x}Cl_{3-x}  $   form a fully crystalline heterojunction with fewer grain boundaries and low defect and trap densities. The multifunctions of perylene result in significant enhancement of PCE from 12.67 to 17.06%, compared with the control device without the perylene underlayer. This simple method is commensurate with simultaneous improvements in perovskite crystallization and interface optimization for PHJ PSCs.

# METHODS

Preparation of Perylene and   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{x}Cl_{3-x}  $   Solutions. Perylene solutions were prepared by dissolving perylene (99.6%) powder directly into four solvents of ethyl alcohol, chloroform, acetone, and chlorobenzene with different concentrations (1–5 wt %). The solutions were stirred in air at room temperature for 10 h. Methylammonium iodide was synthesized by reacting 20 mL of hydroiodic acid (57 wt % in   $  H_{2}O  $  ) and 48 mL of methylamine (40 wt % in methanol) in a 250 mL round-bottom flask under stirring in an ice bath for 2 h. The reactant products were distilled in a rotary evaporator at 55 °C to remove the solvents, and then the precipitate was washed with diethyl ether three times. Finally, white-colored powder was collected and dried at 60 °C for 24 h in a vacuum. Then, MAI and lead chloride (  $  PbCl_{2}  $  , 99.999%) powder were mixed in anhydrous DMF (99.9%) in a molar ratio of 3:1. The mixture solutions were stirred at 60 °C overnight in a glovebox. All solutions were filtered with a 0.45 μm polytetrafluoroethylene filter before use.

Device Fabrication. Poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):polystyrenesulfonate (PEDOT:PSS, Baytron PVP Al 4083) was spin-coated onto cleaned ITO substrates (4500 rpm, 40 s) and annealed at 140 °C for 15 min. The perylene solution was spin-coated on the PEDOT:PSS layer at 3000 rpm for 40 s. According to the boiling points of the solvents, the annealing temperature of the perylene film was chosen to be 50, 50, 40, and 100 °C for ethyl alcohol, chloroform, acetone, and chlorobenzene, respectively. Both the spin-coating and the annealing were carried out in atmospheric conditions. Different micro/nanostructure perylene films could be deposited by controlling the solution concentration and the annealing time. The samples were then transferred into a glovebox for perovskite deposition. The   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{x}Cl_{3-x}  $   precursor (DMF) solution was deposited onto
