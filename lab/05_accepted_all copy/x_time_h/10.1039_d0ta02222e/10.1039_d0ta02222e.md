# Abstract

Surface passivation is a proven strategy for preparing high efficiency planar perovskite solar cells. Herein, we report an effective surface passivation strategy using the multifunctional small organic ionic compound 1-Ethylpyridinium chloride (EPC) in combination with  $ (\mathrm{FAPbI}_3)_{0.95}(\mathrm{MAPbBr}_3)_{0.05} $ . It is found that the nitrogen atom in the pyridine group forms chemical bonds with under-coordinated lead ions in the perovskite film whereby the defect density is significantly reduced. The organic groups, including ethyl and pyridine, furthermore provide higher hydrophobicity for improved moisture stability. Finally, the chlorine anions were also found to play an important role in the defect passivation while further improving the perovskite crystallinity. Detailed theoretical study confirms that the EPC is indeed a good passivation agent for multiple defects. As a result, the power conversion efficiency is increased from  $ 19.52\% $  to as high as  $ 21.19\% $  via the EPC passivation and the devices show reduced hysteresis and increased stability.

# 1. Introduction

In past decade, organic-inorganic halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have developed rapidly owing to their superior optoelectronic properties such as appropriate bandgap, long charge carrier lifetime, high absorption, and excellent charge carrier mobility. The power conversion efficiency (PCE) has quickly increased from 3.8% to 25.2%.  $  ^{1-17}  $   Meanwhile, the theoretical efficiency, based on the Shockley-Queisser limit,

Huang’s group reported that the main reason for charge recombination in polycrystalline perovskite film is the large number of defects at the perovskite films surface and grain boundaries.  $  ^{19}  $   Clearly, defect passivation is an essential aspect when it comes to further improving the efficiency of the PSCs.

Two kinds of approaches are used to suppress recombination and improve the efficiency of charge carrier transport and exciton separation. One option is to introduce additives into the perovskite precursor solution. For example, Sargent's group introduced   $  [6,6]  $  -phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) into the perovskite film. Here, the PCBM was reported to cover the perovskite grain boundaries and significantly reduced recombination losses and hysteresis.  $  ^{20}  $   Liu's group developed   $  NbF_{5}  $   as an additive to be used in FA-based perovskite films.   $  NbF_{5}  $   can bind to the constituents of the perovskite, which decreases the trap-state density and improves, both, stability and performance; efficiencies as high as 20.56% have been achieved this way.  $  ^{21}  $   Zhan's group prepared the fused ring electron acceptor doped perovskite film which showed enhanced electron extraction and suppressed bimolecular recombination, yielding a PCE of 21.7%.  $  ^{22}  $   Zhu's group introduced a diboron compound into the perovskite absorber layer, which was reported to reduce the defect density by reacting with residual FAI and increased the efficiency up to 21.11%. Seok's group added additional iodide ions into the organic cation solution forming the perovskite film, which also decreased the defects density of the perovskite film; here, solar cell devices reached 22.1%.  $  ^{17}  $   Grätzel's group designed a new bifunctional molecular additive (the mercapto-tetrazolium and phenylammonium (SN) moieties), the introduction of SN not only passivates the surface defects, but also act as a structure-directing function for high quality perovskite film formation, consequently, achieving a PCE exceeding 20% for 1 cm  $  ^{2}  $   active device area.  $  ^{23}  $

Another approach is surface passivation. When photogenerated charge carriers pass from the perovskite into the hole transport layer (HTL), defects at the surface of the perovskite film can trap these charges and decrease, both, the open circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) and fill factor (FF). The function of surface passivating species is to reduce or eliminate those defects, thereby inhibiting the charge carrier recombination and improving the efficiency of the device. For example, Grätzel's group added Polymethyl methacrylate into the antisolvent solution by which surface defects were effectively passivated, improving the device performance up to 21.6%.  $  ^{24}  $   Huang's group used quaternary ammonium halide to passivate the surface defects and obtained a certified PCE of 20.45%.  $  ^{19}  $   Liu's group introduced tris(pentafluorophenyl)boron into the antisolvent to modify the perovskite surface, which helped to form large grain sizes and efficiently reduced the defect density; a high PCE of 21.6% was obtained.  $  ^{25}  $   Zhu's group used guanidinium bromide to form a stable capping layer on top of the perovskite film. They found that this layer reacts with excess   $  PbI_{2}  $  , thereby passivating the defects and improving the performance of the device to 21.51%; notably a   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.21 V was reached using an inverted PSC structure.  $  ^{26}  $   Similarly, You's group adopted PEAI to react with excessive   $  PbI_{2}  $  . By reducing the defect density and inhibiting the non-

In these studies additives or surface passivation strategies were used to improve the perovskite film quality and thereby the device performance. Here, most of these additives or passivators can only passivate one type of defect, either the positive or negative charged ones. However, perovskite can exhibit a wide range of defects, such as antisite defects   $  PbI_{3}^{-}  $  , under-coordinated halide ions, under-coordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $  , iodine vacancy, etc. Thus, it would be desirable to have a passivator that could simultaneously passivate multiple kinds of defects.

In the present work, a multifunctional small organic ionic compound, 1-Ethylpyridinium chloride (EPC), was developed to passivate defects at the surface and grain boundaries of perovskite films. Compared to other the state-of-the-art studies in literature, this material combines several properties in a unique way: (1) the nitrogen atom in the pyridine group forms chemical bonds with under-coordinated lead ion on the perovskite surface which significantly reduces the defect density. (2) The organic groups, including ethyl and pyridine, provide increased hydrophobicity for improved moisture stability. (3) The chlorine is also assisting in the defect passivation and in addition improves the perovskite crystallinity. Overall, we can show that the hole extraction was improved and charge accumulation and recombination at the perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD interface were suppressed via EPC passivation. Employing this approach in PSC, the hysteresis was reduced and both the   $  V_{OC}  $   and FF were increased. Overall, the PCE was increased from 19.52% to 21.19%.

# 2. Results and Discussion

The mixed   $  (\mathrm{FAPbI}_{3})_{0.95}(\mathrm{MAPbBr}_{3})_{0.05}  $   perovskite films were prepared via a spin-coating approach. The surface of the perovskite film was then passivated using solutions of EPC in iso-propanol (IPA) at different concentrations. We first investigated the effect of EPC concentration on crystallinity of the perovskite film. Figure 1a shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the perovskite film that were treated with these different concentrations. Clearly, the perovskite films show a high degree of orientation as only few reflexes are visible. No impurity phase, such as remaining   $  PbI_{2}  $  , can be observed in any of the samples. We further analyzed the crystallinity of perovskite film by comparing for the various EPC concentrations the intensities of the (110) reflexes, as well as their full width half max (FWHM) as shown in Figure 1b. The relative intensity of (110) signal first rises for concentrations up to 1 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  , while beyond that point it decreases again. The trend observed for the FWHM is exactly the opposite, since for a better crystallinity (i.e. less disorder) a narrower FWHM is expected.  $  ^{[27]}  $   Overall, the perovskite film with the best crystallinity is achieved for an EPC concentration of 1 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  . # 3. Conclusion

In this work, the multifunctional small organic ionic compound EPC was developed which is able to enhance the photovoltaic performance and reduce hysteresis effects. Furthermore, the stability of PSCs could be improved. The pyridine group in this passivating compound forms a chemical bond with under-coordinated lead ions at the perovskite surface which significantly reduces the density of defect state. The organic groups, including ethyl and pyridine, provide higher hydrophobicity for improved moisture stability. Furthermore, the chlorine anions also play an important role in defect passivation, in addition to its assistance in better perovskite crystallinity. Based on above merits, the hole extraction and charge transfer were improved at the perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD interface by reducing recombination and preventing charge accumulation. In PSCs this improved the average FF from  $ 0.753 \pm 0.012 $  to  $ 0.789 \pm 0.008 $ . As a consequence, a high PCE of  $ 21.19\% $  was obtained via EPC passivation, along with the decreased hysteresis. This work provides an effectively approach for improving the performance of the PSCs.

# Device fabrication

FTO coated glass substrates were first cleaned ultrasonically for 30 min with 2% Hellmanex aqueous solution and then rinsed with ultrapure water and ethanol. Before being used, the substrates were treated by an   $  O_{2}  $   plasma for 20 min. The compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer was prepared by a chemical bath deposition process with 0.2 M   $  TiCl_{4}  $   aqueous solution at 70 °C for 1 h. The substrate was annealed at 200 °C for 30 min and treated by   $  O_{2}  $   plasma for 20 min before use.

The precursor solution was prepared by adding   $  PbI_{2}  $   (647.4 mg), FAI (241.6 mg),   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (25.3 mg), MABr (7.7 mg), and MACl (33 mg) into 1 mL DMF/DMSO (8:1, v/v) which was then stirred for 5 h. For the surface passivation, EPC was dissolved at various concentration in IPA.

The perovskite precursor solution was spin-coated onto   $  TiO_{2}/FTO  $   substrates at a speed of 1000 rpm for 5 s with a ramping rate of 1000 rpm   $  s^{-1}  $   followed by 5000 rpm for 25 s with a ramping rate of 2500 rpm   $  s^{-1}  $  . 0.5 mL diethyl ether was dripped onto the spinning substrate 10 s prior to the end of the program, then the perovskite film was immediately heated at 150 °C for 10 min on a hotplate. In the TA setup, used for characterization of the charge carrier dynamics, the excitation source was a regeneratively amplified Ti:Sapphire laser with a wavelength of 775 nm (Light Conversion, 1030 nm, 150 fs, and 100 kHz repetition) and a high-speed spectrometer was used to detect the probe beam (Ultrafast systems, HELIOS).
