# Surface Passivation of Perovskite Film by Sodium Toluenesulfonate for Highly Efficient Solar Cells

Mengmeng Zhang, Wanpei Hu, Yanbo Shang, Weiran Zhou, Wenfeng Zhang,* and Shangfeng Yang*

Ionic defects at the surfaces of organolead halide perovskite films are detrimental to both the efficiency and stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Herein, sodium  $ p $ -toluenesulfonate (STS) is applied during the surface modification of perovskite layer for the first time, leading to the efficient surface passivation of the perovskite film and consequently significant enhancements in both efficiency and stability of mixed-cation PSC devices. Upon incorporating STS atop the perovskite layer, the power conversion efficiency of the  $ \mathrm{Cs_{0.05}MA_{0.12}FA_{0.83}PbI_{2.55}Br_{0.45}} $  (abbreviated as CsMAFA) mesoporous-structure mixed-cation PSC devices improves from  $ 18.70\% $  to  $ 20.05\% $  with reduced hysteresis. The sulfonate  $ (-\mathrm{SO}_3^-) $  anion of STS coordinates with the  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  of CsMAFA perovskite, and the  $ \mathrm{Na}^+ $  cation of STS electrostatically interacts with the anions  $ (\mathrm{I}^{-}/\mathrm{Br}^{-}) $  of CsMAFA perovskite, resulting in the surface passivation of the CsMAFA perovskite film with reduced electron and hole trap state densities. In addition, STS modification induces an upshift of the valence band of perovskite, facilitating hole extraction from the perovskite layer to the hole transport layer with suppressed interfacial charge recombination. Moreover, such a trap state passivation of perovskite film leads to improvement of the ambient stability of PSC devices.

The intrinsic property and quality of the perovskite absorber layer are critical for the performance of PSC device.  $  ^{[5]}  $   So far most of the reported PSC devices with a PCE over 21% are based on mixed-cation lead mixed-halide perovskites   $  (\mathrm{FA}_{x}\mathrm{MA}_{1-x}\mathrm{PbBr}_{y}\mathrm{I}_{3-y})^{[6,7]}  $   and such inorganic cations as   $  Cs^{+}  $  ,   $  K^{+}  $  , and   $  Rb^{+}  $   are usually added into the MA/FA perovskites so as to enhance further the

device efficiency as well as ambient and thermal stabilities.  $  ^{[8-12]}  $   However, as the complex crystallization process of Cs/MA/FA perovskites resulted from multicomponent mixing, an inhomogeneous composition distribution with even the retaining of unreacted components like formamidinium iodide (FAI) often appears in the bulk phase and/or at the film surface of the resulting polycrystalline perovskites.  $  ^{[13,14]}  $   As the organic components can easily evaporate from the surface during the thermal annealing process,  $  ^{[15]}  $   a high density of unfavorable surface defects and grain boundaries is inevitably generated.  $  ^{[16]}  $   These result in the existence of ionic defects such as under-coordinated Pb atoms, Pb clusters, and/or vacant iodine sites which act as the recombination centers capturing the photogenerated charge carriers.  $  ^{[2,17]}  $   In addition, grain boundaries in polycrystalline perovskite are found to be responsible for ion migration which is correlated with current–voltage hysteresis.  $  ^{[18,19]}  $   Hence, passivating the defects at the surface of perovskite film and/or grain boundaries is an effective way to improve the efficiency and prolong the durability of PSC devices.  $  ^{[18,20]}  $

On the basis of massive efforts on engineering, the composition, phase, morphology, and surface defect of perovskite absorber layer as well as the perovskite/electrode interfaces in the past decade, the certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs has reached 25.2%,  $  ^{[4]}  $   promising PSC alternatives to the commercialized crystalline Si and inorganic semiconductor thin-film solar cells.

Hence, it is highly desirable to develop novel nonhalogen organic salts for surface modification of the perovskite film toward strong interactions with perovskite.

Herein, for the first time, we apply a simple and cheap organic salt sodium p-toluenesulfonate (STS) in surface modification of the perovskite film, achieving significant enhancements of both efficiency and stability of mixed-cation PSC devices. The effects of STS modification on the surface morphology, crystallinity, trap states, and energy band structures of the perovskite film are investigated systematically. The sulfonate   $  (-SO_{3}^{-})  $   anion and   $  Na^{+}  $  cation of STS are found to interact, respectively, with the   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and   $  I^{-}/Br^{-}  $  ions of perovskite, resulting in surface passivation of the perovskite. As a result, after STS modification, the PCE of   $  Cs_{0.05}MA_{0.12}FA_{0.83}PbI_{2.55}Br_{0.45}  $   (abbreviated as CsMAFA) mesoporous-structure PSC devices improves from 18.70% to 20.05% with reduced hysteresis, and the ambient stability of PSC devices obviously improves as well.

# 2. Results and Discussion

The surface modification of CsMAFA perovskite is fulfilled by spin coating an STS (see Figure S1a, Supporting Information, for the chemical structure) solution dissolved in isopropanol (IPA) onto the annealed CsMAFA perovskite film. Using CsMAFA perovskite as the light-absorber layer and a Spiro-OMeTAD (2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-di-p-methoxyphenylamine)-9,9'-spirobifluorene) layer as the hole transport material (HTM), mesoporous-structure PSC devices with a structure of FTO/c-TiO $ _2 $ /m-TiO $ _2 $ /CsMAFA perovskite/STS/Spiro-OMeTAD/Au are fabricated (Figure 1a), and the corresponding cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image is shown in Figure 1b, from which the CsMAFA perovskite layer with a thickness of  $ \approx $ 400 nm looks compact and pinhole free. Under the optimized concentration of STS solution in IPA (0.25 mg mL $ ^{-1} $ , see Figure S2, Supporting Information), the current density-

voltage (J-V) curve of the PSC device with STS modification layer measured under one sun illumination (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  intensity, AM 1.5G spectrum) is shown in Figure 1c, which includes also that of the control device without STS layer for comparison. The photovoltaic parameters including open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ), short-circuit current (  $  J_{sc}  $  ), fill factor (FF), PCE, series resistance (  $  R_{s}  $  ), and shunt resistance (  $  R_{sh}  $  ) are shown in Table 1, which includes also the averaged parameters based on 30 devices fabricated independently. The best control device without STS modification shows a  $  V_{oc}  $  of 1.113 V,  $  J_{sc}  $  of 23.01 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $ , FF of 73.03%, and a PCE of 18.70%. After STS modification, the champion PCE dramatically increases to 20.05% with a  $  V_{oc}  $  of 1.139 V,  $  J_{sc}  $  of 23.52 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $ , and FF of 74.83%. Such a PCE increase is further confirmed by comparing the averaged PCEs with and without STS modification (see Figure 1d and Figure S3, Supporting Information), revealing that STS modification results in simultaneous increases of all three parameters of  $  V_{oc}  $ ,  $  J_{sc}  $ , and FF. Among them, the increase in  $  J_{sc}  $  is verified by external quantum efficiency (EQE) measurement (Figure 1), revealing that the STS-modified device exhibits higher EQE responses in the region of 380–750 nm.

The influence of STS modification on the hysteresis behavior is examined by comparing the J-V curves under reverse and forward scans, from which the hysteresis index defined as   $  [PCE(reverse) - PCE(forward)]/PCE(reverse)  $   is calculated. As shown in Figure 1f and Table S2, Supporting Information, after STS modification, the hysteresis index significantly reduced from 11.39% to 5.39%. It is known that the hysteresis of PSCs essentially results from charge accumulation at the interfaces and existence of trap states within the perovskite layer.  $  ^{[43,44]}  $   Hence, the significantly prohibited hysteresis after STS modification is believed to originate from the decreased trap states, as discussed further in the following paragraphs.

To unveil the factors responsible for the efficiency enhancement of PSC device after STS modification, we conducted a series of spectroscopic and morphological characterizations. We first used X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to deduce the interactions between STS with the underneath CsMAFA perovskite layer. Comparing the high-resolution Pb 4f XPS spectra of CsMAFA perovskite films with and without STS modification (Figure 2a,b), we found that both of the Pb 4f  $  _{7/2}  $  and Pb 4f  $  _{5/2}  $  signals at 143.31 and 138.46 eV detected for the pristine CsMAFA perovskite film shift negatively to 142.92 and 138.07 eV, respectively, after STS modification. Such downshifts of Pb 4f  $  _{7/2}  $  and Pb 4f  $  _{5/2}  $  binding energies suggest the decrease in the cationic charge of Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  of CsMAFA perovskite, resulting in the donation of the lone electron pair on the oxygen (O) atom within the sulfonate group (  $  -SO_{3}^{-}  $  ) of STS to the empty 6p orbital of Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  via coordination bonding.  $  ^{[23,45]}  $  We further compared the XPS signals of Na 1s for the pristine STS and STS atop CsMAFA perovskite. Interestingly, the Na 1s signals at 1071.47 eV detected for the pristine STS shift positively to 1071.84 eV after depositing STS atop CsMAFA perovskite (see Figure S4a, Supporting Information). This suggests the increase in the cationic charge of Na  $  ^{+}  $  of STS, which presumably results from electrostatic interaction with the anions (I  $  ^{-}  $ /Br  $  ^{-}  $ ) of CsMAFA perovskite. This assumption is verified by the negative shifts of the I 3d  $  _{3/2}  $  and I 3d  $  _{5/2}  $  signals for the pristine CsMAFA perovskite film from 630.87 and 619.37 eV to 630.39 and 618.91 eV (Figure 2c,d),

We then investigate the influence of STS modification on the surface morphology and crystallinity of the CsMAFA perovskite film. Surface morphologies of CsMAFA perovskite films with and without STS modification are compared by SEM and atom force microscopy (AFM). According to the SEM image of the pristine CsMAFA perovskite film (Figure 3a), the polycrystalline grains are uniform and dense without obvious pinholes, and there are some protruded substances with different contrasts, which are likely the residual PbI $ _{2} $ . $ ^{[18]} $  After STS modification, although negligible change on the overall surface morphology of CsMAFA perovskite film is observed, in addition to the protruded substances, many tiny bright particles with sizes of  $ \approx $ 25 nm appear (Figure 3b), which are assigned to the aggregates of STS. In addition, the average crystalline grain size is almost

the same after STS modification (see Figure S6, Supporting Information). The existence of such tiny bright particles is confirmed by AFM characterizations, indicating that STS modification leads to a slight increase in the root-mean-square (RMS) roughness from 10.43 to 11.30 nm (Figure 3c,d). As the STS layer is deposited on the surface of CsMAFA perovskite film after complete crystallization, STS modification also imposes a negligible change on the crystallinity of CsMAFA perovskite film, as confirmed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) characterization (see Figure S7, Supporting Information). Similarly, according to UV–vis absorption spectroscopic characterization, no change of the optical absorption of CsMAFA perovskite film as well as its optical bandgap (1.60 eV) is found (see Figure S8, Supporting Information). Hence, the increase in   $  J_{sc}  $   is not attributed to the increase in optical absorption.

Given that STS modification induces negligible changes on the surface morphology, crystallinity, and optical absorption of CsMAFA perovskite film as discussed earlier, the increases in photovoltaic parameters must originate from other factors. We next conducted steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) characterizations to probe the influence of STS modification on the trap states of CsMAFA perovskite film. To definitively unravel the influence of STS modification on the trap states of CsMAFA perovskite film, we used the space charge limited current (SCLC) method to quantitatively measure the electron and hole trap state densities.  $  ^{[23,49]}  $   We fabricated an electron-only device with a structure of ITO/PCBM/CsMAFA perovskite/PCBM/Ag, and the corresponding J-V curves for the devices with and without STS modification measured in the dark are shown in Figure 4c, from which the measured electron trap state densities (  $  n_{t,e}  $  ) of CsMAFA perovskite films before and after STS modification are   $  3.79 \times 10^{16}  $   and   $  1.10 \times 10^{16}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  , respectively. In contrast, based on a hole-only FTO/NiOx/CsMAFA perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD/Au device (Figure 4d), the measured hole trap state densities (  $  n_{t,h}  $  ) of CsMAFA perovskite films before and after STS modification are   $  1.18 \times 10^{16}  $   and   $  8.15 \times 10^{15}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  , respectively (see Supporting Information S11 for detailed analyses). Therefore, both electron and hole trap state densities of CsMAFA perovskite film are reduced after STS modification, revealing the effectiveness of our surface passivation strategy.

An intriguing question is whether the reduced trap state density of CsMAFA perovskite film affects further the charge recombination kinetics. According to the dependence of   $  J_{sc}  $   on the incident light intensity (I), following a power law of   $  J_{sc} = I^{\alpha}  $  , the extent of bimolecular recombination can be deduced from the slope (a).  $  ^{[50]}  $   The slightly larger slope of the STS modified-device suggests the suppressed bimolecular recombination during sweeping out the charge carriers at the perovskite/HTM interface (see Figure S9, Supporting Information). Moreover, the dependence of   $  V_{oc}  $   on the natural logarithm of the incident light intensity   $  [\ln(I)]  $   can be fitted according to the equation   $  V_{\mathrm{oc}} = \varepsilon k T \ln(I)/e + \text{constant}  $  , where   $  \varepsilon  $   represents the ideal factor, k represents the Boltzmann constant, T represents the kelvin temperature, and e is the elementary charge. A slope close to 1kT/e means that the bimolecular recombination is dominant, whereas a slope larger than 1kT/e indicates the presence of the additional trap-assisted Shockley–Read–Hall (SRH) recombination.  $  ^{[50,51]}  $   As shown in Figure 4, the slope of the control and STS-modified devices is 1.413kT/e, which is smaller than that of the control device (1.686kT/e). As the slope of the STS-modified device is more close to 1kT/e, STS modification induces a more effective suppression of the monomolecular SRH recombination, beneficial for the enhancements of   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF.

We finally evaluated the effect of STS modification on the stability of the PSC device. First, the stabilized power outputs of the devices with and without STS modification are examined by applying a constant potential at maximum power point, indicating that the PCE of the STS-modified device can be stabilized at 19.67% at maximum power point (see Figure S10, Supporting Information). Then we monitored the ambient stability of the unencapsulated devices by storing them under ambient conditions (temperature: 25 °C, relative humidity: 40–70%) for 950 h. As shown in Figure 6a, PCE of the control device degrades obviously by ≈53% after 950 h, indicating that the control device is sensitive to   $  H_{2}O  $   and   $  O_{2}  $   in air, causing the decomposition of CsMAFA perovskite. The PCE degradation of the control device is mainly due to the decrease in FF (see Figure S11, Supporting Information). After STS modification, the PSC device shows improved stability, as reflected by a much lower degradation rate of ≈21% under the same aging conditions. This originates from the reduced trap states within CsMAFA perovskite film and improved hydrophobicity of CsMAFA perovskite film, as discussed in the following paragraphs, rendering the enhanced durability against the invasion of the moisture.  $  ^{[23,28]}  $

To understand the underlying mechanisms of the improved stability of the STS-modified device, we investigated the influence of STS modification on the hydrophobicity of CsMAFA perovskite film by water-contact angle measurements. According to a comparison shown in Figure 6b, the water-contact angle of CsMAFA perovskite film increases dramatically

from 45.9° to 68.1°, indicating the improved hydrophobicity of CsMAFA perovskite film after STS modification due to the hydrophobicity of the outward-facing toluene group, which is beneficial for protecting CsMAFA perovskite from the attacks of   $  H_{2}O  $   and   $  O_{2}  $   and the consequently improved stability of the device. Accordingly, a schematic protection mechanism of STS surface passivation for CsMAFA perovskite film is shown in Figure 6c, highlighting the role of the hydrophobic toluene groups within STS in resisting   $  H_{2}O  $   and   $  O_{2}  $   in air.

As STS modification induces the change of the intrinsic property of CsMAFA perovskite film, especially passivation of the trap states, we monitored the changes of optical absorption property and composition of CsMAFA perovskite film so as to unravel the degradation mechanism of the device. It has been reported that perovskite degrades and releases   $  PbI_{2}  $   and   $  I_{2}  $   in the presence of oxygen and light.  $  ^{[18,52,53]}  $   We used the UV–vis absorption spectrum to detect the change of iodine content after immersing the CsMAFA perovskite films in toluene in a continuous illumination at 1 sun for 4 h in air (see Supporting Information S17 for detailed analyses). The results show that the black control CsMAFA perovskite film gradually turns into red brownish, whereas the STS-modified CsMAFA perovskite film remains dark brownish. In addition, a significant decrease in the absorption peak at   $  \approx500  $   nm (  $  I_{2}  $   absorption) was observed for the STS-modified perovskite film, revealing a decreased iodine content that resulted from hindered reactions with oxygen due to decreased trap states within perovskite films. Furthermore, XRD characterization was conducted to probe the compositional changes of CsMAFA perovskite film after STS modification. After storing the device under ambient conditions for 45 days, a severe degradation of the pristine CsMAFA perovskite film is observed as reflected by the dramatic enhancement of the   $  PbI_{2}  $   diffraction peak at   $  12.6^{\circ}  $ , whereas the   $  PbI_{2}  $   peak exhibits a much lower intensity for STS-modified CsMAFA perovskite film (see Figure S13, Supporting Information), suggesting the inhibited decomposition of CsMAFA perovskite. These results confirm that the STS modification effectively protects CsMAFA perovskite from decomposition as a result of the attacks of   $  H_{2}O  $   and   $  O_{2}  $   in air, promising its practical applications in PSC device.

# 3. Conclusions

In summary, for the first time, sodium p-toluenesulfonate (STS) was applied in the surface modification of CsMAFA perovskite film, affording trap state passivation and consequently a significant PCE enhancement of PSC device from 18.70% to 20.05% with reduced hysteresis. XPS characterizations reveal that the sulfonate   $  \left(-\mathrm{SO}_{3}^{-}\right)  $   anion and   $  Na^{+}  $  cation of STS can interact, respectively, with the   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and   $  I^{-}/Br^{-}  $  of perovskite. As a result, both electron and hole trap state densities of CsMAFA perovskite film are reduced after STS modification, and this further leads to suppressed charge recombination. Furthermore, STS modification induces an upshift of the valence band of CsMAFA perovskite, facilitating hole extraction from the perovskite layer to the hole transport layer. Due to efficient trap state passivation and improved hydrophobicity of the perovskite film, the ambient stability of the device is obviously improved after STS modification. Sulfonate organic salt is cheap yet effective in surface passivation of the perovskite film, promising its potential application in achieving high-efficiency perovskite solar cells.

Materials: FTO-coated glass substrates with a sheet resistance of   $  13 \pm 1.5 \Omega sq^{-1}  $   were purchased from NSG Group, Japan. Dyeso), titanium(IV) isopropoxide (Alfa Aesar), TiCl₄ (GR, 99.5%, Aladdin), Li-bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (Li-TFSI, Sigma), and Spiro-OMeTAD (1 M company). Dimethylformamide (DMF), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and chlorobenzene were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Sodium p-toluenesulfonate (STS) was purchased from TCI and p-toluenesulfonic acid was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Device Fabrication: A patterned FTO glass substrate was cleaned by sequential ultrasonic treatment in detergent, deionized water, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol. A   $  TiO_{2}  $   compact layer was spin coated onto FTO with a mixture solution of 65 mL of HCl (2 M), 350 mL of titanium isopropoxide, and 5 mL of ethanol at 2000 rpm for 40 s and dried at 100 °C for 10 min, followed by annealing at 500 °C for 30 min. The   $  TiO_{2}  $   (Dyesol, 30 NR) paste was dissolved in ethanol (1:6) and stirred overnight. The well-dispersed   $  TiO_{2}  $   paste precursor was spin coated at 5000 rpm on the compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer and preannealed at 120 °C for 10 min, followed by annealing at 510 °C for 30 min. After cooling down to 70 °C, these samples were transferred to an aqueous solution of 40 mM   $  TiCl_{4}  $   and heated at 70 °C for 30 min. The   $  TiCl_{4}  $  -treated substrates were thoroughly washed with deionized water and annealed at 500 °C for 30 min. The following coating steps were performed in nitrogen atmosphere inside a glovebox. The CsMAFA triple cation precursor solution (1.3 M) was prepared with   $  PbI_{2}  $   (1.1 M),   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (0.2 M), FAI (1 M), and MABr (0.2 M). About 1.5 M of CsI was dissolved in DMSO as a stock solution for the source of Cs. The CsMAFA perovskite precursor solution was spin coated on m-  $  TiO_{2}  $   substrates at 2000 rpm for 10 s and 6000 rpm for 30 s, and 100 μL chlorobenzene was dropped before the end of the procedure. The substrate was then immediately transferred on a hotplate and heated at 100 °C for 60 min, followed by cooling down to room temperature naturally. The surface passivation layer was achieved by spin coating a 0.25 mg mL STS dissolved in IPA at 3000 rpm for 30 s onto the annealed perovskite layer. The hole transport layer was subsequently deposited on top of the perovskite film by spin coating at 3000 rpm for 30 s, using a chlorobenzene solution which contained 73.2 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   of
