# Creating a Dual-Functional 2D Perovskite Layer at the Interface to Enhance the Performance of Flexible Perovskite Solar Cells

Caoyu Long, Keqing Huang, Jianhui Chang, Chuantian Zuo, Yuanji Gao, Xin Luo, Biao Liu, Haipeng Xie, Zhihui Chen, Jun He, Han Huang, Yongli Gao, Liming Ding,* and Junliang Yang*

Flexible perovskite solar cells (f-PSCs) have been attracting tremendous attention due to their potentially commercial prospects in flexible energy system and mobile energy system. Reducing the energy barriers and charge extraction losses at the interfaces between perovskite and charge transport layers is essential to improve both efficiency and stability of f-PSCs. Herein, 4-trifluoromethylphenylethylamine iodide  $ \mathrm{(CF_3PEAI)} $  is introduced to form a 2D perovskite at the interface between perovskite and hole transport layer (HTL). It is found that the 2D perovskite plays a dual-functional role in aligning energy band between perovskite and HTL and passivating the traps in the 3D perovskite, thus reducing energy loss and charge carrier recombination at the interface, facilitating the hole transfer from perovskite to the Spiro-OMeTAD. Consequently, the photovoltaic performance of f-PSCs is significantly improved, leading to a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 21.1\% $  and a certified PCE of  $ 20.5\% $ . Furthermore, the long-term stability of f-PSCs is greatly improved through the protection of 2D perovskite layer to the underlying 3D perovskite. This work provides an excellent strategy to produce efficient and stable f-PSCs, which will accelerate their potential applications.

# 1. Introduction

Flexible and light-weight photovoltaics possess potentially commercial prospects in flexible energy system and mobile energy system, such as aerospace industries, smart buildings and automobile, as well as powering smart devices including wearable and portable electronics.  $  ^{[1,2]}  $   Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have become one of the most promising next-generation photovoltaics because of unique advantages, such as decent light absorbing ability,  $  ^{[3]}  $   high electron and hole mobility,  $  ^{[4]}  $   excellent photovoltaic properties, and low cost.  $  ^{[5,6]}  $   The certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) has been improved to 25.5%.  $  ^{[7]}  $   Thus, flexible PSCs (f-PSCs) are promising candidates for low-cost, light-weight, flexible, mobile energy system processed via low-temperature, large-scale, and high-throughput printing techniques.  $  ^{[8-11]}  $

Very recently, the 4-trifluoromethylphenylethylamine iodide (CF₃PEAI, Figure 1a) or CF₃PEAI-based 2D layered perovskite was used to optimize the performance of PSCs on rigid substrate, exhibiting excellent effects on passivating defects and facilitating hole transfer.  $  ^{[22-24]}  $   Herein, efficient f-PSCs were constructed via interface engineering of 2D/3D perovskite, i.e., the incorporation of a 2D perovskite (CF₃PEA)₂(FA)ₙ₋₁PbI₃n₊₁ on top of the 3D perovskite FA₁₋ₓMAₓPbI₃. The ultra-thin 2D perovskite upshifted the energy level of 3D perovskite and decreased the energy gap between 3D perovskite and the hole transport layer (HTL) 2,2“,7,7”-tetrakis-(N,N-di(4-methoxyphenyl-amino)-9,9’-spirobi-fluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD), which facilitated the hole transfer due to the reduced energy barriers and charge extraction losses at the interface. Meanwhile, the 2D perovskite also passivated the defects in 3D perovskite, thus improving the open-circuit voltage (Vₒc), short-circuit current density (Jsc), and fill factor (FF), leading to a PCE up to 21.1% and a certified value of 20.5%, which is one of the best efficiencies reported for f-PSCs to date. Moreover, the 2D/3D f-PSCs exhibited excellent ambient stability owing to the hydrophobicity of 2D perovskite. The research suggests that interface engineering with a 2D/3D stacked structure is an excellent route to fabricate efficient and stable f-PSCs.

# 2. Results and Discussion

The 3D perovskite  $ \mathrm{FA}_{1 - x}\mathrm{MA}_x\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  was deposited using a two-step spin-coating deposition,[25,26] where the mixed FAI/MAI reacted with  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  during the coating and annealing process (FA:  $ \mathrm{NH}_2\mathrm{CH} = \mathrm{NH}_2^+ $ ; MA:  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3^+ $ ). Compared with the traditional high-performance perovskite material (FAPbI₃)₁₋ₓ(MAPbBr₃)ₓ, the bromine-free  $ \mathrm{FA}_{1 - x}\mathrm{MA}_x\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  perovskite has lower bandgap while keeping the  $ \alpha $ -phase of FAPbI₃.[25] To prepare a 2D perovskite layer on 3D perovskite, CF₃PEAI in isopropanol was spin-coated onto the surface of  $ \mathrm{FA}_{1 - x}\mathrm{MA}_x\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  and annealed at  $ 140^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 10\mathrm{min} $  (Figure 1b). It reacted with the residual  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  of 3D perovskite surface, forming 2D perovskite film.

To reveal the formation of 2D perovskite on the surface of 3D perovskite, X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to characterize the perovskite films deposited on PEN/ITO (Figure 1c and Figure S1 in the Supporting Information). For pristine 3D perovskite, a   $  PbI_{2}  $   peak at   $  12.7^{\circ}  $   can be observed.  $  ^{[25,26]}  $   After treating with   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $   and annealing for 10 min, the peak intensity for   $  PbI_{2}  $   decreased and a new peak located at   $  4.8^{\circ}  $   appeared, which is marked with a red four-pointed star, indicating the formation of 2D perovskite. A 2D perovskite film was also prepared for the reference, in which the peaks at   $  4.8^{\circ}  $ ,   $  9.8^{\circ}  $ ,   $  14.6^{\circ}  $ , 19.5,   $  24.4^{\circ}  $ ,   $  29.4^{\circ}  $ , and   $  34.5^{\circ}  $   can be assigned to (100), (200), (300), (400), (600), and (700) lattice planes, respectively. Thus, the new peak located at   $  4.8^{\circ}  $   for 2D/3D perovskite film should stem from the (100) lattice plane of 2D perovskite, and the decreased intensity of   $  PbI_{2}  $   peak indicated that the   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $   reacted with   $  PbI_{2}  $ , forming 2D film on the surface of 3D perovskite film.

Morphology and ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption characterizations were performed to explore the properties of pristine 3D and 2D/3D perovskite films. From top-view scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of 3D and 2D/3D perovskite films (Figure S2a,b in the Supporting Information), it was found that a thin 2D perovskite treatment did not significantly affect the surface morphology of the perovskite film. Similar grain sizes were observed. However, the grain texture of 2D/3D perovskite film arose differently, which revealed a cover of 2D perovskite layer.  $  ^{[28]}  $   On the other hand, the root mean square (RMS) roughness of perovskite films was decreased from 28.1 to 24.4 nm on the basis of atomic force microscopy (AFM) results (Figure S2c,d in the Supporting Information), indicating that more 2D perovskite exists at the grain boundary and therefore reduced the height difference between the grain surface and grain boundary, resulting in a more flat film. The UV–vis absorption spectra (Figure S3 in the Supporting Information) displayed that 2D/3D perovskite possessed a little higher light absorption. Besides, the absorption onset was almost the same as pristine 3D perovskite film, indicating that the   $  CF_{3}PEAI-modification  $   didn't significantly change the bandgap of 2D/3D perovskite film (inset in Figure S3 in the Supporting Information).

The XPS depth profiling was used to obtain the thickness information of 2D perovskite layer in 2D/3D film by detecting the F element. As shown in Figure S4 in the Supporting Information, when the concentrations of  $ \mathrm{CF_3PAEI} $  were 1 and  $ 2\mathrm{mgmL^{-1}} $ , the signal of F element only existed at the surface. The signal disappeared as a  $ 10~\mathrm{nm} $  layer was peeled off by Ar ion beam. Therefore, it could be speculated that the thickness of 2D perovskite was less than  $ 10~\mathrm{nm} $  when the concentrations of  $ \mathrm{CF_3PAEI} $  were 1 and  $ 2\mathrm{mgmL^{-1}} $ . Furthermore, it was found that the 2D perovskite made by using  $ 2\mathrm{mgmL^{-1}}\mathrm{CF}_3\mathrm{PAEI} $  was thicker than the 2D perovskite made by using  $ 1\mathrm{mgmL^{-1}} $   $ \mathrm{CF_3PAEI} $  according to the stronger signal of F element. In the same way, the thickness of 2D perovskite made by using  $ 4\mathrm{mgmL^{-1}}\mathrm{CF}_3\mathrm{PEAI} $  was between 10 and  $ 15\mathrm{nm} $  (Figure S4c in the Supporting Information).

In addition, the steady-state photoluminescence (PL) was used to disclose the component of the 2D species in 2D/3D perovskite film. A typical emission peak at about 635 nm was found for the 2D perovskite (Figure S5a in the Supporting Information). Therefore, it could conclude that the molecular formula of the 2D perovskite in 2D/3D film is   $  (\mathrm{CF}_{3}\mathrm{PEA})_{2}\mathrm{FA}_{2}\mathrm{Pb}_{3}\mathrm{I}_{10}  $   (  $  (\mathrm{CF}_{3}\mathrm{PEA})_{2}(\mathrm{FA})_{n-1}\mathrm{Pb}_{n}\mathrm{I}_{3n+1}  $   for n = 3).  $  ^{[29]}  $   Furthermore, the PL scanning measurement was performed to map the distribution of 2D perovskite in 2D/3D film (Figure S5b in the Supporting Information). An area of 6   $  \mu  $  m × 6   $  \mu  $  m was selected to perform the PL scanning on 2D/3D perovskite film under 488 nm laser with a step of

0.2   $  \mu  $  m. It's worth noting that the wavelength of the PL receiver is only from 597 to 652 nm. Thus, the PL signal was only from 2D perovskite. Figure S5b (Supporting Information) showed a homogeneous PL intensity, revealing the homogeneous distribution of 2D component on 3D perovskite film. Besides, two typical regions with different luminescent intensities, marked with "1" and "2", were selected to get their PL peaks. As shown in Figure S5c (Supporting Information), their PL peaks are almost identical at   $  \approx  $  635 nm, proving the uniform PL emissions from 2D perovskite layer on 3D perovskite film.

Furthermore, steady state and time-resolved PL were used to study the charge transfer dynamics of pristine 3D and 2D/3D perovskite films with the structures of 3D perovskite/HTL and 2D/3D perovskite/HTL (Figure 2a,b and Table S1 in the Supporting Information). It is interesting that the PL intensity of 2D/3D perovskite/HTL was significantly reduced (Figure 2a), which revealed that the 2D perovskite promoted hole transfer from 2D/3D perovskite to the HTL Spiro-OMeTAD as compared with pristine 3D perovskite, thus boosting the quenching of the PL. The time-resolved PL decay kinetics was fitted by tri-exponential decay function [30,31]

$$
F (t) = A _ {1} \exp (- t / \tau_ {1}) + A _ {2} \exp (- t / \tau_ {2}) + A _ {3} \exp (- t / \tau_ {3}) + \gamma_ {0}
$$

(1)

where   $  A_{1}  $  ,   $  A_{2}  $  , and   $  A_{3}  $   are the time-independent coefficient of amplitude fraction,   $  \tau_{1}  $  ,   $  \tau_{2}  $  , and   $  \tau_{3}  $   are fast decay time, intermediate decay time and slow decay time, respectively. The time-resolved PL followed the same trend. The 2D/3D perovskite film shortened the PL lifetime, and the average carrier lifetime of perovskite/HTL decreased from 12.44 to 1.43 ns (Figure 2b and Table S1 in the Supporting Information). It also proved that 2D perovskite promoted the hole extract/transfer efficiently. Moreover, the steady state and time-resolved PL for control 3D and 2D/3D perovskite films without Spiro-OMeTAD HTL (Figure S6 and Table S1 in the Supporting Information) showed an obviously enhanced PL intensity and carrier lifetime, revealing that the 2D perovskite layer also passivated the defects on 3D perovskite surface.  $  ^{[32,33]}  $

In order to reveal the mechanism of elevated hole extract, ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) measurement was performed to acquire energy levels of 3D and 2D/3D perovskite films. Specifically, the work functions (WF) can be obtained from the secondary electron cutoff of UPS in Figure 2c (left) by the expression of WF = 21.22 eV -  $  E_{\mathrm{cutoff}}  $ . In this way, the WFs of 3D and 2D/3D perovskite films were estimated to be 4.59 and 4.24 eV. In other words, the WF is clearly upshifted when 3D perovskite changes to 2D/3D perovskite. Moreover, Figure 2b (right) shows the valence band maximum (VBM) of 3D and 2D/3D perovskite films, which were determined by linear extrapolation of the leading edge and the background. The valence band ( $  E_v  $ ) can be acquired by the formula of  $  E_v = \mathrm{WF} + \mathrm{VBM}  $ , and the calculated values are 5.43 and 5.31 eV for 3D and 2D/3D perovskite films, respectively. Accordingly, the process of hole extraction of pristine 3D and 2D/3D perovskite films was depicted in Figure 2d. The upshifted  $  E_v  $  and reduced energy level gap between the  $  E_v  $  of 2D/3D perovskite and the HOMO of Spiro-OMeTAD would decrease the energy loss and facilitate the hole transfer from perovskite to Spiro-OMeTAD, reducing interface charge recombination.[34,35]

Due to the existence of insulating organic cations and spatial confinement, too thick 2D perovskite can prevent the charge transport from highly conductive 3D perovskite.  $  ^{[39,40]}  $   Thus, it is very important to control the thickness of the 2D perovskite within the effective charge carrier diffusion length, so the decent charge-carrier transport can be attained in 2D/3D

perovskite stacking layer.  $  ^{[41,42]}  $   When 3D perovskite was modified with a concentration of   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $   at 2 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  , the average   $  J_{sc}  $   of 2D/3D devices increased from 23.17 to 23.93 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   as compared with 3D devices (Table 1). The increase of   $  J_{sc}  $  , FF, and   $  V_{oc}  $   resulted from the 2D perovskite, which optimized the energy level between perovskite and HTL, enhanced the hole transfer (Figure 2 and Figure S7 in the Supporting Information), and decreased the defect state density on the surface of 3D perovskite film (Figure S6 in the Supporting Information), resulting in the enhancement of average PCEs from 18.8% to 20.5% (Table 1 and Figure S8 in the Supporting Information). The current density–voltage (J-V) curves for the champion 3D and 2D/3D f-PSCs under 1 sun illumination are shown in Figure 3b. The champion 3D PSC showed a PCE of 19.5%, with a   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.121 V, a   $  J_{sc}  $   of 23.31 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   and an FF of 74.8%. Our work offers a simple and effective route to achieve f-PSCs with splendid performance and high stability, narrowing the performance gap between f-PSCs and rigid PSCs.

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials: All materials were used directly without any purification, including   $  SnO_{2}  $   precursor (Alfa Aesar, 15% in   $  H_{2}O  $   colloidal dispersion),   $  PbI_{2}  $   (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.99%),   $  HC(NH_{2})_{2}I  $   (Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp., 99.5%),   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}Cl  $   (Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp., 99.5%),   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $   (Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp., 99.5%), Spiro-OMeTAD (Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp., 99.5%), isopropanol (J&K Scientific, 99.5%), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.8%), chlorobenzene (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.8%), dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9%) and acetonitrile (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.95%).

Device Fabrication: The PEN/ITO (Peccell, Japan) was cleaned by sonication in detergents/H₂O, distilled water, and isopropanol for 20 min sequentially, then dried by N₂ blowing and treated by UV–ozone for 25 min. The 5% SnO₂ colloidal solution diluted by H₂O was spin-coated onto PEN substrate with a speed of 3000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 145 °C for 30 min. Before the deposition of perovskite films, SnO₂ layers were treated by UV–ozone for 20 min. A two-step spin-coating method was employed to prepare the perovskite films in glovebox. 1.5 × 10⁻³ M PbI₂ was dissolved in 1 mL mixed solvent (DMF:DMSO = 9:1) and stirred at 75 °C for 8 h. The precursor was spin-coated onto the SnO₂ layer for 30 s at a speed of 1500 rpm, followed by a heat-treatment at 70 °C for 1 min. After cooling down, the solution of FAI: MAI: MACl (90 mg: 6.39 mg: 9 mg in 1 mL isopropanol) was spin-coated at 2000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 145 °C

for 20 min in ambient conditions (relative humidity of  $ \approx 40\% $ ). For the 2D/3D devices, the  $ \mathrm{CF}_3\mathrm{PEAl} $  in isopropanol with a concentration of 1, 2, or  $ 4\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1} $  were spin-coated onto 3D perovskite films, then annealed at  $ 140^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for 10 min. For the hole-transport layer, the Spiro-OMeTAD solution in chlorobenzene  $ (90\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1}) $  with the addition of  $ 10~\mu \mathrm{L} $  tBP and  $ 45~\mu \mathrm{L} $  Li-TFSI/acetonitrile  $ (170\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1}) $  was spin-coated at  $ 4000\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 30\mathrm{s} $  after the perovskite film had cooled down. Ultimately, a  $ 100\mathrm{nm} $  Ag counter electrode was deposited via thermal evaporation at a pressure of  $ 8\times 10^{-4}\mathrm{Pa} $ , resulting in an active area of  $ 0.09~\mathrm{cm}^2 $ . A  $ 0.06514~\mathrm{cm}^2 $  mask was used to define the accurate active area during measurements.

Characterization: X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku D, Max 2500, Japan) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Al K $ _{\alpha} $  X-ray source, 1486.6 eV) were characterized to investigate crystallographic properties and element distribution of perovskite. XPS depth profiling was performed to obtain the thickness information of the 2D perovskite, in which Ar ion beam was used to peel off a certain thickness of the 2D/3D perovskite film and the F element could be detected. Atomic force microscopy (Agilent Technologies 5500 AFM/SPM System, USA) and scanning electron microscope (FEI Helios Nanolab 600i SEM, America) were utilized to characterize the morphology of perovskite films. The steady-state PL was measured by an FLS980 spectrometer. Time resolved PL was obtained through time-correlated single-photon counting (TCSPC, MS3504i) measurements. The PL mapping was performed on 2D/3D perovskite film under 488 nm laser with a step of 0.2  $ \mu $ m. The UV–vis spectrophotometer (Puxi, T9, China) was employed to characterize the absorption properties of perovskite films. Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS, He I, 21.22 eV) was employed to explore the detailed energy-level. Current density–voltage (J–V) characteristics of the devices were measured from +1.2 to 0 V with a 50 ms scanning delay, 60 points in reverse-scan modes by a digital Source Meter (Keithley, model 2420). The measurement was under a Xenon-lamp-based solar simulator (Newport 91160s, AM 1.5G), which was calibrated by a standard silicon solar cell with a light intensity of 100 mW cm $ ^{-1} $ . The contact angle measurement was executed by a drop shape analyzer (Sheng ding, SDC-100) at ambient temperature to analyze the hydrophilicity of perovskite surfaces.
