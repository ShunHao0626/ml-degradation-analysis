# Abstract

Due to the sensitivity of the perovskite solar cells to the subtle changes under the ambient atmosphere conditions, many problems have aroused great attention such as collapse of the perovskite structure and efficiency quickly drops. Among them, internal defect also is a big obstacle for high-quality poly-crystalline perovskites, at present it is still difficult to control the density of the trapping sites. Herein, we use a bidentate chelating agent, thenoyltrifluoroacetone (ttfa), to effectively controls the crystallization kinetics, grain sizes and crystal defect of Cs/MA/FA perovskite materials by a nucleation and growth process of perovskite crystals. Realization of crystalline-state-tuning during crystallization process obtain better quality perovskite thin film, with no additional operation that suit the needs of modern industrial production and management, therefore this is an industry produce oriented method. The chelating agent can effectively passivate the defects in perovskite films, leading to a low defect density and a long charge carrier lifetime. As a result, the ttfa-passivated perovskite solar cell demonstrates a high PCE of 19.70% with superior stability retaining over 64% of the initial PCE after two weeks unencapsulated storage in an adverse atmosphere with about 50% relative humidity.

# 1. Introduction

Organometal halide perovskite materials (AMX $ _3 $ , A = Cs $ ^+ $ , CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3^+ $ , NH=CHNH $ _3^+ $ ; M = Pb $ ^{2+} $ , Sn $ ^{2+} $ ; X = Cl $ ^- $ , Br $ ^- $  and I $ ^- $ ) are attractive as light-harvesting absorbers because of their simple synthesis, good long-range carrier diffusion lengths, and excellent optical absorption. $ ^{[1-5]} $  Therefore, perovskite-based materials have naturally been considered as candidates for construction of low cost solar cells. Notably, their power conversion efficiency (PCE) rapidly increased from 3.8% to the current record of 24.2% over the past seven years. $ ^{[6-10]} $  Consequently, perovskite solar cells (PSCs) are now considered to be the most favorable candidates for realizing high PCE-low-cost solar cells with long-term stability.

The properties of perovskite layers are known to be vital for achieving high performance of PSCs and to date, various techniques have been developed to improve the performance of perovskite films. Among these, optimization of solar energy absorbers for PSCs has mainly been focused on attempts to achieve a defect-free perovskite film,  $  ^{[11]}  $   including surface passivation, increasing the grain size, reducing the grain boundaries, and adjusting the crystal dimensions of the perovskite film.  $  ^{[12-14]}  $   Additive engineering has proven to be an effective strategy to fabricate high-quality perovskite layers resulting in PSCs with increased efficiency. For example, Han et al. revealed that the addition of methylammonium acetate (MAAc) and thiosemicarbazide (TSC) to the precursor solution, affords to a large crystal size and low density of defects during the preparation of large-area uniform perovskite films.  $  ^{[15]}  $   Qi et al. fabricated an improved perovskite film with hydrophobic ammonium-based additives which can effectively increase crystalline grain size and reduce charge carrier recombination of the double cation perovskite film.  $  ^{[16]}  $   In addition, Bi et al. introduced an approach to prepare perovskite films of high electronic quality by using poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) as a template to control nucleation and crystal growth.  $  ^{[17]}  $   Overall, the compatible with simple, high efficiency, low cost and low-temperature processable methods, such as additive engineering to control the crystallization process of perovskite films, is of great value. Additive engineering has applied to control film morphology, such as surface uniformity, surface coverage, and crystal size for high performance.  $  ^{[20]}  $   In addition, additives blending into a perovskite film can effectively improve stability towards commercialization.  $  ^{[21]}  $   Therefore, controlling the crystallizing of perovskites with no additional steps, which suit the needs of modern industrial production and management, can promote the industrialization of perovskite solar cells.

During the preparation of perovskite films, the morphology of perovskite films is often successfully controlled by the crystal grain nucleation and growth processes, which highlight the importance of introducing a suitable additive in the perovskite film during crystallization. Herein, we report the use of thenoyltrifluoroacetone (ttfa), a small molecule popularly used as an extraction agent or a chromogenic agent in metal-ions testing   $  [22, 23]  $  , into Formamidinium (FA)-based perovskite. The strong interactions between   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ions and ttfa were used to control the crystallization process of perovskite film, resulting in accurately passivated halogen vacancies, obtaining larger grain size and improved stability of the devices. The passivation mechanism was investigated. As a result, the prepared films demonstrated a champion PCE as high as 19.70%. Furthermore, the high-quality film suppressed the degradation, leading to much improved device stability.

# 1.2 PSCs fabrication

Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) substrates (10  $ \Omega\cdot $ sq $ ^{-1} $ ) were pre-cleaned in an ultrasonic bath with deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol in sequence for 20 min each, followed by drying under a nitrogen flow. The FTO substrates were then treated with UV-ozone for 25 min. The TiO $ _2 $  blocking layer ( $ \sim $ 50nm) was prepared by a chemical bath deposition method, and the details of its processing can be found in a previously published report. [24] Perovskite layers were fabricated using a method similar to that reported by Anaraki et al. [25] The perovskite precursor solutions containing the additives were prepared by the addition of 1.5% molar ratios of the additives which were added to the above-prepared perovskite precursor solution (based on Pb $ ^{2+} $ ). The spin-coating procedure was done in a glovebox with two step spin coating process and 130  $ \mu $ L methoxybenzene as anti-solvent was dripped during the second step. The perovskite film annealed at 110  $ ^{\circ} $ C for 10 min. A 101 mg Spiro-OMeTAD solution was dissolved in 1 mL of chlorobenzene with dopants of 40  $ \mu $ L 4-tert-butylpyridine, 24.5  $ \mu $ L lithium bis(trifluoromethylsulphonyl) imide (520 mg·mL $ ^{-1} $  in acetonitrile) and 49  $ \mu $ L [tris(2-(1H-pyrazol-1-yl)-4-tert-butylpyridine)cobalt(III) bis(trifluoromethylsulphonyl) imide] (FK209) (300 mg·mL $ ^{-1} $  in acetonitrile). The Spiro-OMeTAD solution was spin-coated onto the perovskite films at 5000 rpm for 25 s, and the samples were kept in a desiccator overnight. Finally, a gold thin film ( $ \sim $ 80 nm) was deposited on the Spiro-OMeTAD-coated film using a thermal evaporation using a thermal evaporator through a shadow mask under vacuum (ca. 10 $ ^{-4} $  Pa). The effective areas for the perovskite solar cells are 7 mm $ ^{2} $ .

# 2. Discussion

Figure 1(a) shows the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of the pristine perovskite (control group) and the perovskite with ttfa. Figure 1(b) depicts the stretching vibrations related to the C=O bonds of pure ttfa appeared at   $  1,654 \, cm^{-1}  $  . Upon addition of ttfa into perovskite film, it was observed that the C=O stretching shifted from 1,654 to   $  1,681 \, cm^{-1}  $  , which indicates the existence of ttfa in the perovskite film after annealing. The ttfa likely formed chelate with perovskite via the interaction between   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and the C=O bonds in ttfa.  $  ^{[26]}  $   Figure 1(c) shows the XRD patterns of the perovskite and perovskite-ttfa films. All films display characteristic diffraction peaks corresponding to the crystal planes of the trigonal perovskite phase. Additionally, the amount of residual   $  PbI_{2}  $   disappeared upon introduction of ttfa, indicating that the residual lead ions can be effectively chelated by ttfa in a bidentate fashion.  $  ^{[27,28]}  $   When introducing excess ttfa, as detailed in Supporting Information, Figure S2, the distinct diffraction peak in   $  7.46^{\circ}  $   indicated that a new structure in the 3D halide perovskite film, which is likely to be 1D structure FAI-  $  PbI_{2}  $  -ttfa.  $  ^{[29]}  $   At the same time, an additional diffraction peaks emerged in   $  11.4^{\circ}  $   which belongs to the perovskite yellow phase.  $  ^{[30]}  $   This is probably due to the chelation with   $  Pb^{2+}  $   destroyed the correct chemical proportion of the perovskite. The interactions between ttfa (bidentate) and   $  Pb^{2+}  $   are stronger than the interaction of   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and DMSO, along with the photographs of perovskite precursors w/o, w/ ttfa (Figure S3a), pure   $  PbI_{2}  $  , pure ttfa and ttfa with   $  PbI_{2}  $   mixed solution (Figure S3b). The pure   $  PbI_{2}  $   with ttfa additive exhibit an orange color, apparently the color darker than the pure   $  PbI_{2}  $   solution and the perovskite precursor without ttfa additive, probably due to the strong chelation ability between   $  PbI_{2}  $   and additives. An illustration of the possible passivation mechanism is presented in Figure 1(d). We infer that the ttfa with its rather low steric hindrance and strong   $  Pb^{2+}  $   chelating

To confirm the existence of the ttfa additives in the perovskite, we investigated the interaction between the additive and the perovskite. We used ultrafast transient absorption spectroscopy (TA) measurements, to monitor the dynamics of energy transfer and thereby reveal the differences between these material configurations. In the control group (without ttfa), we observed the typical photobleaching (PB) at about 740 nm from this 2D contour plot (Figure 2a). In the perovskite film sample with  $ 1.5\mathrm{mol}\% $  ttfa (Figure 2b), we observed the distinct bleach peaks at about  $ 740~\mathrm{nm} $  which is almost the same with the control group. Whereas in the perovskite film sample with  $ 7.5\mathrm{mol}\% $  ttfa, we observed the bleach peak spread towards the short wavelength, it means new bleach peak appeared. The new bleach band is considered to excess ttfa chelating with  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and FAI that form a mixed phase film.

Figure 3(a) and (b) show SEM images with a top-view of perovskite and perovskite-ttfa films indicated that the perovskite grain size increases with the use of ttfa additive, confirmed that ttfa in the precursor has exerted a crucial impact on the nucleation and crystal growth processes. This is probably because the introducing of the chelating agents-ttfa can slow down the nucleation and crystal growth rate, leading to the formation of larger grains. Figure S4 shows AFM height images of the perovskite and perovskite-ttfa, it is apparent that a  $ 100\% $  surface coverage and a flat surface was achieved. The top surface of perovskite-ttfa was found to be smoother than that of the pure perovskite. Upon introduction of ttfa, the RMS roughness decreased to  $ 25.7~\mathrm{nm} $  from the  $ 43.9~\mathrm{nm} $  observed for the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  substrate. Figure 3(c) shows the grain size distribution of these two samples, and clearly indicates that the average grain size in the perovskite-ttfa film is significantly larger than the pure perovskite film. Figure 3(d) shows the water contact angle increased from  $ 51^{\circ} $  to  $ 66^{\circ} $  upon introduction of ttfa. This result points to the beneficial use of ttfa inside the perovskite, which allows it to function as a barrier for preventing moisture permeation.

The photovoltaic devices were fabricated with a n-i-p planar structure, as depicted in Figure 4(a). Pure perovskite and with ttfa were employed as the active layer. The open-circuit voltage  $ (V_{OC}) $ , short-circuit current  $ (J_{sc}) $ , fill factor  $ (FF) $ , and reproducibility were systematically enhanced (Figure 4c) and the summary of the best device performance is presented in Table S1 (Supporting Information). The significantly larger Jsc and FF are attributed to good crystallinity and large grain size, resulting in effective charge transport and less carrier recombination at grain boundaries. Figure S5 (Supporting Information) displays statistical distribution of key J-V parameters including Jsc, Voc, FF, and PCE of 30 individual devices. We observed a slight Voc enhancement for the highest device. This result shows that the improved film morphology(larger perovskite grain size and smoother surface) reduce the surface defects at the HTL/perovskite interface, which can result in suppressing the recombination and reducing charge accumulation at interfaces.  $  ^{[31,32]}  $

The external quantum efficiency (EQE) for the perovskite solar cell is shown in Figure 4(d). The integrated photocurrent densities were measured to be 20.59 and 21.19 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  for devices without and with ttfa, respectively, which are approximately equal to the  $  J_{SC}  $  values extracted from the corresponding J-V curves. This phenomenon is ascribed to the improved perovskite-ttfa film, which allowed a further increase of light absorption in perovskite layer (Figure S6, Supporting Information).

The electron trap-state density in the perovskite films was calculated from the dark current-voltages   $  (I-V)  $   of the electron-only device (Figure S1). Figure 5(a) shows the I-V curves of the electron-only devices based on perovskite films with and without ttfa. The current exhibited linear correlation with the voltage at the low bias voltage, indicating an Ohmic response. However, the I-V quickly increased sharply while the bias voltage exceeded the kink point, the trap-filled limit voltage   $  (V_{\mathrm{TFL}})  $  , which indicates that the trap states are absolutely filled. The perovskite films generated from the pristine precursor perovskite solution exhibited an electron trap-state density of   $  8.40 \times 10^{15} \, cm^{-3}  $  , whereas the perovskite film with ttfa exhibited a decreased electron trap-state density of   $  3.55 \times 10^{15} \, cm^{-3}  $   (The details are shown in the Supporting Information). Therefore, it is expected that the reduced trap density leads to an increased Jsc and FF for the devices.  $  ^{[33, 34]}  $

To further understand the dynamics of charge transfer and recombination in the devices, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were carried out (Figure 5d). Nyquist plots show the intercept on the real axis at high frequency range is ascribed to the electron transfer process ( $ R_{\text{s}} $ ), and the semicircle in the low frequency range is attributed the recombination process ( $ R_{\text{rec}} $ ). The fitting parameters are listed in Table S3(Supporting Information). When the ttfa additive is used, the device displays a lower  $ R_{\text{s}} $  and higher  $ R_{\text{rec}} $  compared to the control group, and the corresponding chemical capacitance of  $ R_{\text{rec}} $  also increased. This result indicates a faster carrier transfer and a lower recombination rate in perovskite-ttfa device, which is consistent with other characterization results. $ ^{[39-41]} $

Finally, we investigated the effect of the additive on stability. Figure 6(a) shows the ambient stability of the unencapsulated device, where the bare perovskite solar cells were stored under in ambient conditions at about 50% humidity in the dark. Under these conditions, the PCE of the perovskite-ttfa device maintained 64% of its initial efficiency after two weeks, while the solar cells without the ttfa additive degraded by 43% when stored under the same condition. After two weeks of storage, two obvious diffraction peaks emerged on the XRD patterns in the vicinity of 11.4° and 12.4° for the control group which belong to yellow-phase and PbI  $  _{2}  $  . On the other hand, only tiny diffraction peak appeared in about 11.4° in perovskite with ttfa film. The excellent environmental stability of the PSCs is attributed to the larger grain size, strong chelation ability between PbI  $  _{2}  $   and ttfa, and reduced trap state density, which is known to suppress the moisture permeation at grain boundaries.  $  ^{[42]}  $

We employed a simple and effective chelate-assisted strategy by a bidentate additive, for modulating grain growth and passivating defects in perovskite films. The ttfa additive significantly improved the photovoltaic performance, with a highest PCE of 19.70% attained. Furthermore, incorporation of the ttfa additive reduced moisture sensitivity, and improved the ambient stability for developing high-quality perovskite films. Additionally, the manufacture technology is low-temperature (  $  \leq  $  110 °C) process using a nontoxic and inexpensive additive and greener anti-solvent, which will reduce the cost and some adverse environment impact. This approach will also minimize the health risks faced by researchers working on the technology. In addition, additive engineering with no additional operation is an industrial production method.
