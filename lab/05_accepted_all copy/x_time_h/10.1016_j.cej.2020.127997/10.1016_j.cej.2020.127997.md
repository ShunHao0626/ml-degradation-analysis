# 1. Introduction

The metal halide perovskite solar cell (PSC) has proved to be a photovoltaic technology with great commercial application potential. In the past decade, PSC's power conversion efficiency (PCE) has increased from 3.8% [1] in 2009 to the current 25.5% [2], and it seems that the PCE will continue to increase. Huge and rapid progress comes from high-quality perovskite films [3,4] and ingeniously designed device structures [5–7]. As a superstar of photovoltaic materials, the perovskite not only has adjustable band gap and high absorption coefficient in the UV–vis range [8], but also has better carrier mobility [9], ultra-long carrier diffusion length (>1   $  \mu  $  m) [10] and small exciton binding energy close to thermal kinetic energy [11]. # ABSTRACT

There are many defect states at the interface between   $  SnO_{2}  $   and perovskite due to the preparation process and the lattice mismatch. These defects will trap photo-generated carriers, resulting in low carrier extraction efficiency and loss of device stability. Many researches are underway to further improve the performance of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) based on   $  SnO_{2}  $   electron transport layer (ETL), including doping of ETL and effective passivation of interface defects. Here, the   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   ETL is used in planar perovskite solar cells to simultaneously dope ETL and passivate the defects at the ETL/perovskite interface. First, Cl ions can passivate the defects on the   $  SnO_{2}  $   surface, and part of the chloride ions can also diffuse into the perovskite to passivate the grain boundaries. Additionally, In ions themselves are suitable dopants and can provide better doping performance for   $  SnO_{2}  $ . The result shows that the simultaneous ETL doping and interface passivation result in a significant increase of PCE from 19.1% to 20.8% due to the enhancement of short-circuit current density. This method expands the ETL/perovskite interface optimization work by using anions and cations for passivation and doping, respectively, and has some inspiration for the delivery of high efficiency and stable planar structure PSCs based on   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL.

$ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  ETL is too low (ca.  $ 10^{-4}\mathrm{cm}^2\mathrm{V}^{-1}s^{-1} $ ) to match with high hole mobility of commonly used hole transport layers (HTLs) (ca.  $ 10^{-3}\mathrm{cm}^2 $   $ \mathrm{V}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1} $ ). In contrast,  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  has the wonderful features required by ETL: suitable energy level, high carrier mobility and good anti-reflection capabilities [16]. More importantly, the  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -based PSC prepared by low-temperature method shows outstanding performance, and its efficiency is comparable to that of the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ -based device [17,18]. The certified PCE of PSCs based on  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  ETLs is over  $ 23\% $  [19], implying the great potential of  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  as ETLs in PSCs. Moreover, compared with  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ ,  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  based PSCs show better stability [20].

The main problem faced when using   $  SnO_{2}  $   as ETL is the defect existing at the interface between   $  SnO_{2}  $   and perovskite, which not only causes severe charge recombination limiting the photocurrent density, but also exacerbates the instability of the interface [4,21]. The photocurrent density of the solar cell is determined by the incident photon-electron conversion efficiency (IPCE) of the specific equation:

$$
I P C E (\lambda) = \alpha \lambda \varphi_ {\mathrm{inj}} \varphi_ {\mathrm{c}}
$$

where   $  \alpha  $   is the absorption coefficient,   $  \varphi_{inj}  $   is the charge injection efficiency and   $  \varphi_{c}  $   is the charge collection efficiency. Therefore, to obtain a high photocurrent density, it is important to maximize charge injection at the interface. From the perspective of perovskite, perovskite films grown through the low-temperature process are polycrystalline, and inevitably have grain boundaries (GBs). The lattice mismatch between perovskite and ETL could make this undesirable situation even more worse [4]. The passivation of perovskite grain boundaries is often fulfilled by introducing interface modified materials, such as PCBM [22], C60 [23], etc. As far as the metal oxide ETL is concerned, it should be noticed that the existence of non-stoichiometric defects related to oxygen vacancies which are easy to occur during the thermal annealing process [24].   $  SnO_{2}  $   films prepared at high temperature could cause severe degradation, while low temperature annealing usually forms oxygen vacancies related defects in the   $  SnO_{2}  $   lattice (eg   $  SnO_{2-x}  $  , SnO) [25]. Doping ETL with heterovalent metal cations or organic molecules has been proven to be an effective way to minimize the oxygen vacancy density in ETL or passivate these traps to improve the charge transport performance. Currently, numerous metal cations such as Li [26], Ga [27], Nb [28], and Sb [29] have been explored as ETL dopant to passivate   $  SnO_{2}  $   surface defect and enhance conductivity.

Recently, the passivation effect of Cl on interface defects has begun to attract extensive attention. Sargent et al. used Cl-capped   $  TiO_{2}  $   as the ETL and found that Cl can significantly passivate the deep trap state at the perovskite interface and reduce the carrier recombination at the   $  TiO_{2}  $  /perovskite interface [30]. Tan et al. doped   $  SnO_{2}  $   with KCl, pointing out that K/Cl ions could effectively passivate the ETL/perovskite contact, thereby achieving impressive device performance [31]. Guo et al. treated ZnO ETL with   $  NH_{4}Cl  $   and filled the vacancy of oxygen on the surface of ZnO with Cl, which greatly reduced the charge recombination at the perovskite/ZnO interface [32]. CsCl [33] and   $  PbCl_{2}  $   [34] were also introduced as intermediate layers between ETL and perovskite to passivate interface defects. Herein, we devise a strategy that deactivating oxygen vacancies on the surface of   $  SnO_{2}  $   and doping   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL simultaneously by treating the   $  SnO_{2}  $   surface with   $  InCl_{3}  $ . It is worth mentioning here that Cl can also passivate the grain boundaries and deep defect states of the perovskite interface. Through the doping and passivation process, the PCE of the device treated with   $  InCl_{3}  $   greatly increases from 19.1% to 20.8% due to the short circuit current density (  $  J_{sc}  $  ) enhancement. Furthermore, the effective interface trap passivation also promotes the stability of the device.

# 2. Results and discussion

Our work is based on the control device with the structure of ITO/  $ \mathrm{SnO_2 / Cs_{0.05}MA_yFA_{0.95 - y}PbI_{3 - x}Cl_x / spiro - OMeTAD / Ag} $  using  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  as ETL. Two-step spin coating method is used to form a perovskite layer through

the interdiffusion of lead halide and organic salt [35,36]. Apart from this, compositional engineering and solvent annealing [3] to optimize the crystallization process of perovskite are also applied to obtain high-quality perovskite films. Fig. 1a shows the structure of the device with InCl $ _{3} $  treatment. To obtain the best performance, the ETL should fully cover the ITO substrate, and should not be so thick as to introduce a high series resistance. Therefore, a decent concentration of InCl $ _{3} $  should be considered. In our case, the optimized concentration of InCl $ _{3} $  is 0.05 M, and the resultant SnO $ _{2} $ :InCl $ _{3} $ . The optimal annealing temperature (200 °C) is also explored to prepare decent SnO $ _{2} $ :InCl $ _{3} $  film. Fig. S1 and Table S1 show the performance of PSC devices prepared with different annealing temperatures and various InCl $ _{3} $  precursor concentrations. Fig. 1b briefly describes the energy level information of each functional layer in the device which is determined by ultraviolet photoelectron spectrometer (UPS) measurement and discussed later.

In order to analyze the surface properties of   $  SnO_{2}  $   film treated with   $  InCl_{3}  $  , atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurement was conducted. As shown in Fig. 2a-b, we can clearly see that the surface morphology of   $  SnO_{2}  $   treated with   $  InCl_{3}  $   has not changed, evenly and densely covering the ITO surface. There is no obvious difference in the root mean square roughness (RMS), where the   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   film is 1.15 nm, and the   $  SnO_{2}  $   film is 1.06 nm. The presence of   $  InCl_{3}  $   can also prevent possible direct contact between the perovskite and ITO, thereby reducing the possibility of electron-hole recombination and current leakage, improving the performance of the device. Since the surface energy of   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETLs seriously affects the crystal of perovskite film, we measured the contact angle of   $  SnO_{2}  $   and   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   films to DMF, as shown in Fig. S2. Both films have good surface wettability, the low contact angle of the DMF solvent is beneficial to the spreading of the   $  PbI_{2}  $   solution and the formation of high-quality perovskite film [37,38].

As a significant part of the device structure, electron transport layers need to have good light transmittance to ensure minimum incident light loss. Therefore, we measured the transmittance of   $  SnO_{2}  $   and   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   films on sapphire to evaluate the effect of   $  InCl_{3}  $   treatment on the utilization of incident light, as shown in Fig. 2c. The   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   shows the same transmittance as   $  SnO_{2}  $   film in the range of 400 ~ 800 nm. While in the wavelength range of 200 ~ 400 nm, the   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   film shows lower transmittance compared to   $  SnO_{2}  $   film, which may be caused by the increased light reflection caused by the increased surface roughness and the absorption of   $  InO_{x}:Cl  $   layer. Combined with the transmission spectrum, optical band gap of the ETL can also be obtained, which requires the relationship between the absorption coefficient and the photon energy. The optical coefficient   $  \alpha  $   mentioned above can be obtained by the formula:   $  T = Aexp(-\alpha d)  $  , where T is the film transmittance, A can be treated as approximately 1, and d is the film thickness. By employing the Tauc model, the optical bandgap of the films can be calculated from the high absorption region. The Tauc model is a very classic model with the formula:   $  ah\nu = D(h\nu - E_{g})^{n}  $  , where h\nu stands for the photon energy,   $  E_{g}  $   represents the optical bandgap, D is a constant, and n equals to 1/2 here. By plotting   $  (ah\nu)^{2}  $   versus h\nu, direct optical bandgaps of   $  SnO_{2}  $   and   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   films are extracted, as plotted in Fig. 2d. The band gap of   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   slightly decreases to 3.87 eV compared to that of   $  SnO_{2}  $   (3.91 eV).

The energy level change of   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL also needs to be considered after   $  InCl_{3}  $   doping and passivation. For this reason, we carried out the UPS measurement of   $  SnO_{2}  $   and   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   films, as shown in Fig. 2e. Since the perovskite growth process has not changed from our previous reports [4], the energy level of perovskite is consistent. The secondary-electron cut-off of UPS spectra is used to determine the work function (  $  W_{F}  $  ) which can be calculated from the photon energy (21.22 eV) and the cut-off energy (  $  E_{cutoff}  $  ) by using the equation   $  W_{F} = 21.22 - E_{cutoff}  $   [39–41]. As shown by the dotted line in Fig. 1b, the Fermi levels (  $  E_{F}  $  ) of   $  SnO_{2}  $   and   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   are -4.39 eV and -4.52 eV, respectively. The introduction of   $  In^{3+}  $   as an acceptor dopant and Cl surface passivation resulted in the reduction of the   $  SnO_{2}  $   Fermi level. As we all know, both radiative recombination and non-radiative recombination limit the charge transfer of PSC [45]. The high-quality perovskite film is very important to reduce the current loss caused by non-radiation recombination. In addition, perovskite films with better crystalline quality have fewer grain boundaries, which also contributes to the charge transport and stability of perovskite [30]. The UV-Vis absorption spectrum of the perovskite film was also carried out. As shown in Fig. S5, the perovskite films have strong absorption in the UV to near infrared range before and after introducing  $ \mathrm{InCl}_3 $ , and the absorption edge is extended to  $ 800~\mathrm{nm} $ . Furthermore, in the wavelength range of 400 to  $ 500~\mathrm{nm} $ , the perovskite film prepared on  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $ :  $ \mathrm{InCl}_3 $  shows stronger light absorption than that on  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $ . This may be due to the increased crystalline quality of perovskite film, which has been mentioned above.

The electron-only devices with the structure of ITO/SnO  $  _{2}  $  (or SnO  $  _{2}  $ : InCl  $  _{3}  $ )/perovskite/PCBM/BCP/Ag were fabricated and characterized by the space charge limited current (SCLC) method to further evaluate the

density of defect states (  $  N_{defects}  $  ) in perovskite films. As shown in Fig. 4d, the curve could be divided into three parts: the first part is the ohmic region, and the current and voltage in this region have a linear relationship. The second part is called the trap-filling region, where the voltage is in medium value and the curve slope is relatively high. Furthermore, all of the trap states are filled with injected carriers in this region. The voltage between the first region and the second region is called   $  V_{TFL}  $   (trap-filled limit voltage). The (  $  N_{defects}  $  ) can be calculated by formula:

$$
N _ {\text { defects }} = 2 \varepsilon_ {0} \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{r}} V _ {\mathrm{TFL}} / e L ^ {2}
$$

In the formula,   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   is the vacuum dielectric constant,   $  \varepsilon_{r}  $   is the relative dielectric constant (perovskite is 6.5 [46]), e is the basic charge, and L is the thickness of the perovskite film. As can be seen from Fig. 4d, the   $  V_{TFL}  $   of the   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL device is 0.27 V, and after treatment with   $  InCl_{3}  $ , the   $  V_{TFL}  $   of the device drops to 0.19 V. Through calculation, the defect density of the perovskite film decreases from   $  7.76 \times 10^{14}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $   to   $  5.46 \times 10^{14}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $   after   $  InCl_{3}  $   treatment. SCLC results further confirm that the introduction of   $  InCl_{3}  $   not only can passivate   $  SnO_{2}  $ , but also can effectively reduce the defect state density of perovskite films.

Next, we conducted steady-state photoluminescence (PL) test to focus on the charge transfer and recombination behavior at the interface between perovskite and ETL. As shown in Fig. 4e, the weak PL peak intensity indicates that the carrier transfer is faster and the extraction efficiency is higher at the  $ \mathrm{SnO_2:InCl_3} $ /perovskite interface. In addition, there is a slight blue shift in the peak position of PL, which also indicates the reduced defect in perovskite film [4,47]. At the same time, because Cl in the ETL leads to the formation of Pb-Cl bonds at the ETL/perovskite interface, the chemical interaction between ETL and perovskite is stronger [30], which also helps to reduce the charge recombination on the perovskite side and improve the interface charge transfer. With the superior optoelectronic properties discussed above, it is expected that the   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   would make a better ETL in the PSCs. Thus, two kinds of PSCs were fabricated using   $  SnO_{2}-ETL  $   and   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}-ETL  $   respectively to comprehensively evaluate device performance. Fig. 5a shows the corresponding current J-V curves of two representative PSCs using   $  SnO_{2}  $   and   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   ETLs, and the average photovoltaic parameters are listed in Table 1. The device based on   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL has a   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.07 V, a   $  J_{sc}  $   of 22.1 mA/cm², and an FF of 0.78, yielding an average PCE of 18.3%. While the device based on   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   ETL shows a   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.07 V, a   $  J_{sc}  $   of 23.7 mA/cm², and an FF of 0.76, yielding an average PCE of 19.4%. The hysteresis index (HI) of PSCs could be calculated from the equation: HI = (PCE  $  _{rev}  $   - PCE  $  _{for}  $  )/PCE  $  _{rev}  $  , where PCE  $  _{rev}  $   and PCE  $  _{for}  $   are PCEs of reverse scan and forward scan. The PSC based on   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   ETL (5.37%) exhibits reduced hysteresis compared to that based on   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL (15.01%) (Table S3). The suppressed hysteresis is due to the enhanced charge transfer at ETL/perovskite interface. We also measured the J-V curves of PSCs in dark, as shown in Fig. S6. The PSC based   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   ETL shows slightly higher reverse dark current than that based on   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL, and the increased dark current and decreased shunt resistance (  $  R_{sh}  $  ) caused the slightly decreased fill factor. The improvement of PCE is mainly due to the enhancement of   $  J_{sc}  $  . After interface passivation, the average   $  J_{sc}  $   of the devices fabricated in same batch increases significantly, from 22.1 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $   to 23.7 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  . The higher   $  J_{sc}  $   of the device based on   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   ETL can be ascribed to the enhanced charge transfer at perovskite/ETL interface and increased film quality of perovskite absorbing layer deposited on   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   ETL, which has been demonstrated before. The J-V curve of the best performance device based on   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   ETL is shown in Fig. 5c. The   $  J_{sc}  $   of the device has increased to a remarkable 24.12 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  , with a high   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.10 V and an FF of 0.78, reaching a high PCE of 20.8%. Fig. 5d exhibits the steady-state output of   $  J_{sc}  $   and PCE of the device based on   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   ETL at maximum power point as a function of time. It is clear seen that the device shows a stable current output of 20.76 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  , and a stable PCE of 19.1% under a voltage bias of 0.92 V. The lower steady-state PCE is caused by the hysteresis behavior of PSC.

In a typical planar-type PSC with an n-i-p structure, the energy level drop between the quasi-Fermi levels of ETL and HTL determines the   $  V_{oc}  $   of the device. As can be seen from Fig. 1b, due to the passivation of   $  InCl_{3}  $  , the work function of ETL increases and the Fermi level decreases compared with pristine   $  SnO_{2}  $  . A reduced Fermi level could result in a reduction in   $  V_{oc}  $  . In order to gain further insight into   $  V_{oc}  $  , the capacitance-voltage (C-V) characteristics of devices based on different ETLs are recorded at room temperature without light. Generally, for Schottky diodes, the junction capacitance shows a dependence on the bias voltage according to the Mott-Schottky relationship:

$$
C ^ {- 2} = 2 (V _ {\mathrm{bi}} - V) / (A ^ {2} q \varepsilon \varepsilon_ {0} N)
$$

where   $  V_{bi}  $   is the built-in potential, V is the applied voltage, A is the active surface of the device, q is the basic charge,   $  \varepsilon  $   is the relative dielectric constant of perovskite,   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   is the dielectric constant of vacuum, and N is assumed to be the doping density of perovskite. The   $  V_{bi}  $   can be estimated by the voltage corresponding to the maximal of capacitance which equals to the flat-band condition. As shown in Fig. 5e, compared to   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL based device (0.93 V),   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   based device shows a slightly lower   $  V_{bi}  $   of 0.92 V. This is consistent with the   $  V_{oc}  $   value extracted from the J-V measurement. It can also be obtained from Fig. 5e that   $  SnO_{2}:InCl_{3}  $   ETL based devices show smaller capacitance under different bias voltages, which further proves that the accumulation of electrons at the interface is less. All these results demonstrate that interface trap states are reduced and interface charge extraction is more efficient.

Finally, by storing unencapsulated devices in an ambient environment with a temperature of  $ 22^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  and a humidity of about  $ 30\% $ , the stability of devices based on different ETLs was studied. From Fig. 5f, it can be seen that the PCE of the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $ -based device drops to  $ 49\% $  of its initial value after  $ 960\mathrm{h} $  storage, while the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2:\mathrm{InCl}_3 $ -based device still retains  $ 78\% $  of its initial value over the same time. Moreover, we also measured the light stability of PSCs, and the unencapsulated PSCs were

measured under continuous AM 1.5 G-light illumination without excluding UV light in ambient air with RH % ≈ 50%. As shown in Fig. S7, the PSC based on SnO₂:InCl₃ ETL maintained 75% of its initial PCE after 180 min illumination, which is higher than that based on SnO₂ ETL (54%). The increased long-term and light stability of SnO₂:InCl₃ based PSC is mainly due to the increase quality of perovskite film. The possible reason for enhanced stability is the better interface contact between the perovskite layer and ETL. What's more, the enhanced perovskite film quality also provides a barrier to water molecules in the air, hindering the penetration of water molecules into the perovskite.

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, the   $  InCl_{3}  $   is used in planar perovskite solar cells to doped ETL and passivate the defects at the ETL/perovskite interface simultaneously, and the PCE of planar PSCs increases to 20.8% with a remarkable   $  J_{sc}  $   of 24.12 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  . The significant performance enhancement of the planar PSCs is attributed to the superior advantages caused by the treatment of   $  SnO_{2}  $   with   $  InCl_{3}  $   and the subsequent perovskite growing on it, including lower film trap density, larger grain size, and higher electron mobility of the ETL. On the one hand, Cl has a good passivation effect on the defects of the   $  SnO_{2}  $   surface, and also forms Pb-Cl bonds with the Pb in the perovskite, which leads to efficient interface carrier transport. In addition, In is a good ETL dopant due to lower reactivity and better oxidation resistance, which can lead to improved carrier mobility of   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL. This work provides a promising direction toward better interface contact, and we believe that this work could facilitate the development of perovskite photovoltaics.
