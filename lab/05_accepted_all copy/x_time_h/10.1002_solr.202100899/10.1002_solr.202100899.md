# A Buried Functional Layer for Inorganic   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   Perovskite Solar Cells

Huibo Yan, Jincheng Huang, Xiaohui Zhang, Ming Wang, Jun Liu, Chunfeng Meng, Sunbin Deng, Lei Lu, Ping Xu,* Hoi-Sing Kwok, and Guijun Li*

The enlightening inorganic Sn-based metal halide perovskites hold promise for environment-friendly and efficient energy conversion. However, the undesired   $  Sn^{2+}  $   oxidation and uncontrollable crystallization of the perovskite absorber slow the development of highly efficient Sn-based inorganic perovskite solar cells. Herein, an ionic liquid layer of 1-butylpyridinium bromide (BPB) is employed as a buried functional template for the growth of the inorganic   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   perovskite absorber. The buried functional layer provides lone electron pairs from N atom to coordinate with the unsaturated metal ions (Pb and Sn) via the coupling effect. In addition, the electronegative atom from the hydrogen bond acceptor offers an electron-rich environment for the perovskite growth to suppress   $  Sn^{2+}  $   oxidation. More importantly, this positive effect transduces from the interface to the bulk perovskite growth, leading to enhanced crystallinity and thus reduced nonradiative trap defects. Consequently, the efficiency of the inorganic   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   PSCs is improved from 6.80% to 11.28%, and the unencapsulated device exhibits superior ambient stability, maintaining 62% of its initial power conversion efficiency in dried air for 200 h. The buried ionic liquid functional layer approach provides an avenue for the development of high-efficiency Sn-based optoelectronics.

# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been considered as promising photovoltaic (PV) technology for renewable energy generation.  $  ^{[1]}  $   In virtue of their impressive merits such as strong light absorption, long carrier diffusion length, and low exciton binding energy, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs has rocketed from 3.8% to 25.5% in decade.  $  ^{[2]}  $   Nevertheless, the devices with the highest PCEs are all based on Pb-based metal halide perovskite

efficiency perovskite/silicon tandem solar cells.  $  ^{[9]}  $

Similar to the Sn-based hybrid perovskite, the main limitations for the further development of high-quality   $  CsPb_{1-x}Sn_{x}I_{2}Br  $   are their high trap-defect density and poor crystallization, which mainly result from the facile oxidation of   $  Sn^{2+}  $   to   $  Sn^{4+}  $  .  $  ^{[10]}  $   Studies to date have focused on the modification of the bulk Sn-based perovskite by virtue of additive engineering, i.e., adding agent into the perovskite film to suppress the   $  Sn^{2+}  $   oxidation. In this scenario,   $  SnF_{2}  $   has been recognized as the widely used

materials.  $  ^{[3]}  $   Being one of the most toxic heavy metals, Pb has completely been proven to be a potential health hazard for plants and humans.  $  ^{[4]}  $   Thus, the exploration of less Pb-containing or Pb-free perovskite materials with excellent optoelectronic properties has been in great attention.  $  ^{[5]}  $   Compared with the organic-inorganic hybrid perovskites, the inorganic perovskites have been demonstrated as a promising alternative for light absorption because of their improved thermal stability.  $  ^{[6]}  $   Among inorganic PSCs,   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   is benefited from the largely improved structural stability, but at the cost of a relatively wide bandgap of 1.92 eV. In this theme, the usage of less harmful Sn to replace or partially replace Pb in the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite is an attractive approach to reduce its optical bandgap.  $  ^{[7]}  $   Practically, the bandgap of   $  CsPb_{1-x}Sn_{x}I_{2}Br  $   with   $  0 \leq x \leq 1  $   can be tuned between 1.38 and 1.92 eV.  $  ^{[8]}  $   The reduced bandgap of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $ -based perovskite also offers an opportunity in the development of high-

In conventional PSCs, it was suggested that deep-level trap states were accumulated at the growing buried interface, which induces undesirable nonradiative losses.  $  ^{[17]}  $   Indeed, for Sn-based perovskites, the competition between the Sn oxidization and bulk crystallization is also largely dependent on this growing interface. The control of this seeding environment for the perovskite growth is of crucial to developing high-efficiency   $  CsPb_{1-x}Sn_{x}I_{2}Br  $   PSCs.

In this study, the pyridine ionic liquid (IL), i.e., 1-butylpyridinium bromide (BPB), is introduced as the growing interlayer for the inorganic   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   perovskite. The buried BPB layer is found to suppress the   $  Sn^{2+}  $   oxidation due to the existence of hydrogen bond acceptor, which not only provides lone electron pairs to coordinate with the unsaturated Sn ions but also offers electron-rich environmental for perovskite growth. Though the antioxidization effect occurs at the initial growing interface, the bulk perovskite exhibited suppressed   $  Sn^{4+}  $   states and thus enhanced crystallinity. As a result, a PCE of 11.28% is obtained for inorganic   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   PSCs, along with a largely improved environmental and thermal stability.

# 2. Results and Discussion

The functional layer is introduced by spin coating the BPB solution onto the poly (3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS) hole transporting layer (HTL), as illustrated in Figure 1a. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurement was conducted to examine the existence of BPB on the surface of PEDOT:PSS. Figure S1, Supporting Information, shows the XPS spectra of the ITO/PEDOT:PSS (pristine HTL) and ITO/PEDOT:PSS/BPB (targeted HTL). As expected, obvious N 1s and Br 3 d peaks are detected for the targeted HTL substrate, indicating that BPB is successfully loaded on the surface of PEDOT:PSS. From the deconvolution of the C 1s spectrum (Figure S2, Supporting Information), it can be found that the BPB does not just layer on the PEDOT:PSS surface, but interact intimately with the PEDOT:PSS network through the C–N interaction. The interaction between PEDOT:PSS and BPB is interpreted by the obvious C–N peak located at   $  \approx  $  286.2 eV in C 1s spectrum (Figure S2b, Supporting Information).

For the inorganic Pb–Sn perovskite, a composition of   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   is targeted in this study. The properties of the inorganic   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   film grown on the PEDOT: PSS HTL with and without BPB buried layer were investigated by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The XRD patterns shown in Figure 1b reveal that no obvious peak shifts with BPB introduction, suggesting that the BPB molecules do not change the perovskite crystal structure. However, compared with the perovskite film grown on the pristine HTL, the film grown on the targeted HTL shows much strong XRD intensity in (100) and (200) planes, manifesting the improved bulk crystallinity. Furthermore, the full width at half-maximum (FWHM) of the peak (100) decreases from   $  0.307^{\circ}  $   on pristine HTL to   $  0.305^{\circ}  $   on targeted HTL, which is agree well with the improved   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   film quality that discussed below. Figure 1c shows the top-view SEM image of pristine HTL/  $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   film. The film shows a large number of discontinuities and pinholes at grain boundaries, which may cause the direct contact of charge transport layers to deteriorate recombination loss.  $  ^{[18]}  $   In contrast, the morphology of the   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   film grown on the targeted HTL substrate looks much dense and uniform with few pinholes (Figure 1d).

The photoelectric properties of HTL are also studied. The optical transmittance of the PEDOT:PSS was measured, as shown in Figure S3, Supporting Information. The introduction of the growing BPB layer causes little transmittance loss. Meanwhile, the space-charge-limited-current (SCLC) measurement is conducted to examine the hole mobility of the HTL (Figure S4, Supporting Information). The Mott–Gurney equation is applied to calculate the hole mobility. By fitting the current density–voltage   $  (J-V)  $   curves of a hole-only device (ITO/HTL/MoO  $  _{3}  $  /Ag), the hole mobility of the pristine and targeted HTLs are calculated to be   $  1.78 \times 10^{-6}  $   and   $  1.20 \times 10^{-5}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $   V  $  ^{-1}  $   s  $  ^{-1}  $  , respectively. Apparently, the loading of the BPB on PEDOT:PSS largely increases the hole ability of PEDOT:PSS by almost one order, which is favorable for the hole transport.

The light absorbance of   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   films fabricated on different HTLs is investigated (Figure 1e). The   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   film deposited on the targeted HTL displays slightly higher absorption from 500 to 750 nm compared with the film deposited on the pristine HTL. The optical bandgap (  $  E_{g}  $  ) of   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   is estimated to be 1.64 eV from the Tauc plot as indicated by the inset of Figure 1e. Figure 1f shows the steady-state photoluminescence (PL), the   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   film deposited on BPB/glass substrate exhibits higher PL intensity, indicating an enhanced bulk perovskite quality with reduced nonradiative recombination ceters. To investigate the carrier transport kinetics, time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) was measured. It is well known that the performance of Sn-contained PSCs is hindered by the facile oxidation of   $  Sn^{2+}  $   to   $  Sn^{4+}  $  . In this study, BPB is featured with hydrogen bond acceptor which contains lone electron pairs.  $  ^{[20]}  $   The lone electron pairs can coordinate with the unsaturated metal ions (  $  Pb^{2+}  $   and   $  Sn^{2+}  $  ) via coupling effect to form Lewis-bases adduct,  $  ^{[21]}  $   which is vital to reduce nonradiative recombination losses. The Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) measurement was conducted to verify the interaction between BPB and   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   film (Figure S5, Supporting Information). In the wavenumber range of 1000–2200 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  , additional peaks close to 1170 and 2060 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   of BPB-CsPb  $  _{0.75}  $  Sn  $  _{0.25}  $  I  $  _{2}  $  Br film are obviously distinguished. However,

the peaks are absent in the   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   film, indicating chemical interaction between BPB and   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $  . In addition to the passivation effect, the lone electron pairs from hydrogen bond acceptor provide an electron-rich environment for the growth of Sn-based perovskite. The electron-rich growing environment is believed to suppress the   $  Sn^{2+}  $   oxidation.

The expected antioxidation effect of BPB on   $  Sn^{2+}  $   was examined by adding BPB into the   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   precursor solution. The solution is exposed to humid air at room temperature. The evolution of the solution color is shown in Figure S6, Supporting Information. The color of the pristine   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   solution changes from yellow to brown after 24 h, which is indicative of the   $  Sn^{2+}  $   oxidation. And it turns into reddish-brown after 72 h, suggesting that most of   $  Sn^{2+}  $   ions are oxidized. In comparison, the color of the BPB-  $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   solution does not change within 48 h, manifesting that BPB can retard   $  Sn^{2+}  $   oxidation. Though the antioxidation effect of BPB on the inorganic   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   perovskite is obvious, it only occurs at the starting interface. The question is, does the bulk properties are also affected by the interfacial state? And how the positive antioxidation effect is transduced from the interface to the bulk. To shed light on this question, the depth-XPS measurement was conducted on the pristine and targeted   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   films. XPS measurements were detected at two positions, located at the buried interface and the top surface of the   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   film (Figure 2a). In comparison with the   $  Sn^{4+}/Sn^{2+}  $   ratio of the pristine   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   film (top surface: 77.37%, buried interface: 49.45%) (Figure 2b,c), the ratios of the targeted   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   film are largely reduced to 19.21% and 6.35% (Figure 2d,e), respectively. The simultaneous decrease of the   $  Sn^{4+}  $   fraction manifests that the BPB buried layer plays important roles not only for interface modification, but also for bulk crystallization. As schematically shown in Figure 2f, the suppression of the   $  Sn^{2+}  $   oxidation in the perpendicular direction along the growth of the perovskite is probably attributed to the easy electron diffusion from the bottom to the top.

And Figure S9b, Supporting Information, exhibits a smaller   $  \tau_{tr}  $   value (0.61   $  \mu  $  s) of the targeted device than that of the control device (0.97   $  \mu  $  s), suggesting the transport resistance is decreased by BPB introduction. The charge-collection efficiency   $  h_{cc}  $   can be extracted from the above time constants according to the equation:   $  h_{cc} = 1 - \tau_{tr}/\tau_{rec}  $  . To test the device reproducibility, 30 individual devices were fabricated for each type of   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   PSCs, and the statistical distribution of the efficiencies is displayed in Figure 5a. The environmental stability of control and targeted PSCs without encapsulation in inert atmosphere at room temperature was investigated (Figure 5b). An obvious decline to 46.73% of its initial PCE of the control PSCs is observed within 200 h, whereas the targeted PSC maintains 78.28% of its initial PCE. Moreover, the targeted PSCs also show enhanced thermal stability in inert atmosphere under continuous 65 °C annealing (Figure 5c). The stability of the unencapsulated devices under dried air is also tested, and the PCE of the control device drops to 3.70% of its initial value in 200 h. Impressively, the targeted device exhibits an improved ambient stability, remaining 61.65% of initial value under the same condition (Figure 5d). In addition, the light-stability testing is conducted by placing the device under stimulated AM1.5G 1-sun illumination for 130 h. As indicated by Figure S10, Supporting Information, the targeted device exhibits enhanced light-stability with 72.38% of its initial value maintained, while the control device only keeps 37.85% of its initial value. The good environmental stability further confirms the suppression of the   $  Sn^{2+}  $   oxidation with BPB buried functional layer. Apart from the above-discussed merits brought by BPB insertion, the enhanced stability can be ascribed to the robust interface contact between   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   and targeted HTL. As proven by the cross-sectional images of the device (Figure 5e-f), the   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $  /HTL interface of the targeted device maintains intact contact after being stored in inert atmosphere for 200 h. In contrast, the interface of the control device is seriously deteriorated, showing apparent discontinuities.

# 3. Conclusions

In summary, the buried interface engineering via constructing an ionic liquid growing environment for the growth of   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   perovskite is an effective strategy for the development of high-efficiency and stable Sn–Pb binary inorganic PSCs. The insertion of a buried ionic liquid functional layer is found to retard the   $  Sn^{2+}  $   oxidation. The positive effect transduces to the bulk growth of the perovskite absorber, leading to improved bulk crystallinity and thus reduced nonradiative recombination. Furthermore, the ionic liquid layer modifies the PEDOT:PSS/  $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   interface with more intact

and continuous, delivering faster charge extraction and robust contact against moisture and oxidization.

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials Preparation: Cesium iodide (CsI), lead iodide (PbI $ _2 $ ), lead bromide (PbBr $ _2 $ ), and bathocuproine (BCP) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. Tin iodide (SnI $ _2 $ ) and tin fluoride (SnF $ _2 $ ) were purchased from Greatcell Solar. Dimethylformamide (DMF), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and molybdenum trioxide (MoO $ _3 $ ) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) polystyrenesulfonate (PEDOT:PSS) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. [6,6]-Phenyl-C $ _{61} $ -butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) was purchased from Luminescence Technology Corp. 1-butylpyridinium bromide (BPB) was purchased from Damas-beta. A 0.8 M CsPb $ _{0.75} $ Sn $ _{0.25} $ I $ _2 $ Br precursor solution was obtained by adding CsI, PbI $ _2 $ , SnI $ _2 $ , PbBr $ _2 $  and SnF $ _2 $  in mixed solvent (DMSO/DMF = 1:1, v/v).

Device Fabrication: The ITO glasses were sonicated sequentially cleaned with deionized water, detergent, and IPA for 15 min, respectively. After drying with nitrogen and then put into the oven at   $  110^{\circ}  $  C overnight, the ITO substrates were treated with oxygen plasma for 20 min. A PEDOT:PSS solution was spin coated onto ITO substrates at 3000 rpm for 30 s, and then annealed in ambient air at   $  150^{\circ}  $  C for 20 min. The   $  CsPb_{0.75}Sn_{0.25}I_{2}Br  $   precursor solution was spin coated onto the ITO/HTL substrates at 800 rpm for 5 s and 3000 rpm for 30 s, successively. And then annealed at   $  140^{\circ}  $  C for 5 min. PCBM was spin coated onto the perovskite layer at 2000 rpm for 60 s. Following, the BCP was spin coated at 4000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, 100 nm Ag electrode on the top of the cell was deposited by thermal evaporation.

Characterization: The XRD patterns were measured from samples of perovskite deposited on the ITO/ETM substrates using an X-ray diffractometer (UltimalV), using Cu Kα radiation (  $  \lambda = 1.54050  $   Å). The top-view and cross-sectional SEM images were attained using an analytical field emission SEM (FEI Scios). XPS was conducted using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha X-ray photoelectron spectrometer with an Al Kα X-ray (hν = 1486.6 eV) as the excitation source. The photocurrent density–voltage (J-V) characteristics were measured under AM1.5G one sun illumination (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) with a solar simulator (Enlitech, SS-F5-3A) equipped with xenon lamp and a programmable Keithley 2400 digital source meter. The light intensity was calibrated using an NREL calibrated Si reference cell. The active area of the solar cell was 0.12 cm  $  ^{2}  $  , which was defined by the overlapping between the bottom ITO electrodes and top Ag. The absorption spectra were measured on a Shimadu SolidSpec-3700 UV–vis spectrophotometer. TRPL was carried out using a PicoQuant Fluo Time 300, and the wavelength of excitation light was 532 nm. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), capacitance–voltage (C-V) characteristics, and   $  V_{oc}  $   transient analysis were measured by an electrochemical workstation (Zahner, Germany).
