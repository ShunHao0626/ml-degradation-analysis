# Novel Surface Passivation for Stable FA $ _{0.85} $ MA $ _{0.15} $ PbI $ _3 $  Perovskite Solar Cells with 21.6% Efficiency

Fang Qian, Shihao Yuan, Yuan Cai, Yu Han, Huan Zhao, Jie Sun, Zhike Liu,* and Shengzhong (Frank) Liu*

In planar perovskite solar cells (PSCs), defect-induced recombination at the interface between the perovskite and hole transport layer (HTL) leads to a large potential loss and performance deterioration. Therefore, an effective method for improving interfacial properties is critical to boost the performance and stability of PSCs. Herein, a novel surface engineering technology is reported for passivating the perovskite surface with the polyfluoro organic compound tris(pentafluorophenyl)boron (TPFPB), which can yield large perovskite grains, reduced defect densities, and improved charge transport and phase stability for the perovskite film, and enhanced power conversion efficiency (PCE) and stability for PSCs. Using this strategy, a champion   $  FA_{0.85}MA_{0.15}PbI_{3}  $   perovskite cell achieves a high PCE of 21.6% as well as significantly improved air and light stabilities. This work demonstrates that TPFPB is a promising material for crystallization control and defect passivation and paves a new path for mitigating defects and further increasing the performance of planar PSCs.

To mitigate the surface/interface defect issue, passivation agents, such as polymers,  $  ^{[9-13]}  $   fullerenes and their derivatives,  $  ^{[14,15]}  $   inorganic acids,  $  ^{[16,17]}  $   metal halides,  $  ^{[18,19]}  $   and organic halide salts,  $  ^{[20-22]}  $   are either added into the antisolvent or blended with the perovskite precursor solution.  $  ^{[23]}  $   Some of them can also manipulate the morphology to form a uniform and pinhole-free perovskite film. Some can also

change the hydrophobic property to protect the perovskite from moisture, whereas others can also enhance the optical and electrical properties of the perovskite. For example, Bi et al.  $  ^{[24]}  $  introduced poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) into an antisolvent to prepare the perovskite film, and they found that PMMA can control the nucleation and growth of the perovskite film leading to a high-quality film with low defect concentration. Yu et al.  $  ^{[25]}  $  applied a new organic conjugated molecule (SA-1) as an additive into the perovskite precursor solution to passivate the surface and grain boundary defects, and it was found that the SA-1 treatment can enhance the hydrophobicity of perovskite to protect it well from moisture. Several works also introduced small organic molecules as a passivation agent to reduce the surface defects and improve the optoelectronic properties of perovskite.  $  ^{[12,13]}  $  However, it is currently still challenging to realize the multiple effects of suitable morphology, phase and hydrophobicity control, trap states passivation, and charge extraction enhancement for further enhancement of PCE and stability of planar PSCs.

# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted enormous attention in the past few years due to their exciting progress in power conversion efficiency (PCE) growth from 3.8% to a certified value of 23.7%.  $  ^{[1,2]}  $   The bulk perovskite itself has a long carrier diffusion length and low carrier recombination, and once high quality was achieved in the perovskite interior, the loss at the surface/interface became a main factor for further PCE improvement.  $  ^{[3,4]}  $   The uncontrollable decomposition on the surface/interface of the perovskite would cause large amounts of ionic defects, such as iodide vacancies, uncoordinated lead/halide ions, and clusters, which serve as nonradiative recombination centers.  $  ^{[5]}  $   These defects cause serious charge recombination on the surface/interface, which is detrimental to the carrier

In this work, we adopted a polyfluoro organic compound (tris(pentafluorophenyl)boron [TPFPB]) as a novel surface passivation agent for efficient and stable planar PSCs. TPFPB is a fluorine-containing hydrophobic Lewis acid, which has been applied as a dopant in hole transport layers (HTLs) to increase their conductivities and mobilities.  $  ^{[26,27]}  $   From our experience, TPFPB is more suitable for surface passivation due to its special structure. The impact of TPFPB passivation on the perovskite crystallinity and phase purity was investigated by X-ray diffraction (XRD). As shown in Figure 3a, Bragg peaks at 13.8°, 28.0°, 31.5°, and 37.7° can be assigned to the (110), (220), (310), and (330) planes of the perovskite crystal, respectively. The intensity of the (110) peak in the perovskite film w TPFPB is slightly increased compared with that of the perovskite film wo TPFPB, and the full width at half maximum of the (110) peak is reduced (Figure S3, Supporting Information), demonstrating improved crystallinity for the passivated film (Figure 2d). The large grain size and good crystalline quality are known to be important for achieving high-performance PSCs. Meanwhile, a typical peak for δ-phase perovskite at 11.65° was observed in the film wo TPFPB passivation. In contrast, no peak assigned to the δ-phase is found for the perovskite w passivation. This phenomenon suggests that the formation of the unwanted δ-phase can be inhibited by TPFPB passivation, which is consistent with the SEM and EDX results (Figure 2a,b and Figure S2, Supporting Information). Figure 3b shows the UV–vis absorption spectra of perovskite films wo/w TPFPB passivation. Notably, the perovskite film w TPFPB passivation exhibits enhanced absorption from 500 to 800 nm, which is probably due to the increased crystalline quality of the perovskite film after passivation.

To investigate the charge separation, recombination, and transport dynamics in perovskite films wo/w TPFPB passivation,

steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements were carried out.  $  ^{[31]}  $   Figure 3c shows the steady-state PL spectra of perovskite films wo/w TPFPB passivation. The PL intensity of the perovskite film w TPFPB passivation exhibits a stronger band emission at 804 nm relative to the control film at the same excitation, indicating lower nonradiative recombination loss compared with the control sample.  $  ^{[32,33]}  $   The most probable reason is that fluorine ions in the TPFPB can introduce excess halides onto the perovskite surface to compensate/fill any iodide vacancies, thereby passivating the nonradiative recombination pathways and leading to high luminescence efficiencies.  $  ^{[34-36]}  $   Figure 3d shows the TRPL spectra of the perovskite film wo/w TPFPB passivation. The curves were fitted with a biexponential decay function, and the fitting parameters are summarized in Table S1, Supporting Information. The fitted results indicate a longer average carrier lifetime (  $  \tau_{ave}  $  , 50.44 ns) for the perovskite film w TPFPB passivation than for the control film (  $  \tau_{ave}  $  , 44.55 ns), which is attributed to reduced trap-assisted recombination at defect sites.  $  ^{[37,38]}  $   In addition, PL and TRPL characterizations were also conducted to study the charge-extraction dynamics at the perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD interface. As shown in Figure S4 and Table S2, Supporting Information, the perovskite (TPFPB)/Spiro-OMeTAD films displayed a stronger PL quenching effect and shorter average carrier lifetime than the control perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD films, which suggests that the TPFPB passivation accelerates charge extraction from the perovskite to the HTL and is better for photovoltaic performance.  $  ^{[39]}  $   Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) was conducted to measure the detailed energy levels of the perovskites wo/w TPFPB passivation (Figure S5, Supporting Information). When compared with the perovskite wo TPFPB, as shown in Figure S6, Supporting Information, the perovskite w TPFPB shows a deeper Fermi level (  $  E_{F}  $  ), which is due to the p-type doping effect of TPFPB and is favorable for hole transport from the perovskite to the HTL.  $  ^{[20]}  $

To accurately assess the trap density in the perovskite materials, an electron-only device with the structure glass/FTO/TiO $ _2 $ /perovskite/PCBM/Ag was fabricated. Figure 3e,f shows the typical dark current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics of electron-only devices wo/w TPFPB treatment. The trap densities were estimated by the space charge-limited current (SCLC) technique. $ ^{[40,41]} $  The linear region at low bias corresponds to the ohmic response. A trap-filling process is identified by the marked increase in current injection in the intermediate region. Trap levels are continuously filled by increasing bias until the trap-filling limit voltage ( $ V_{\text{TFL}} $ ) is reached. The trap density can be calculated using the equation  $ n_{\text{trap}} = (2\varepsilon_0 V_{\text{TFL}})/(eL^2) $ , where  $ \varepsilon $  and  $ \varepsilon_0 $  are the relative dielectric constants for perovskite and vacuum permittivity, respectively;  $ V_{\text{TFL}} $  is the trap-filled limit voltage,  $ e $  is the elementary charge of an electron, and  $ L $  is the thickness of the perovskite film. For the perovskite film w TPFPB passivation,  $ n_{\text{trap}} $  was calculated to be  $ 1.49 \times 10^{16} \text{ cm}^{-3} $ , which is much lower than that of the control perovskite film wo TPFPB treatment ( $ 2.31 \times 10^{16} \text{ cm}^{-3} $ ). The reduced trap density is ascribed to the passivation effect of the fluorine ions in the TPFPB and the improved quality of the perovskite crystals, which is consistent with the PL and TRPL results (Figure 3c,d).

Finally, the air and light stabilities of PSCs wo/w TPFPB passivation were compared. The air-stability test of unencapsulated devices was carried out at 25 °C and 30% relative humidity. As shown in Figure 6a, PSCs w passivation maintained more than 70% of their initial PCE values after 750 h of storage, whereas the control devices maintained only 55% of their initial PCE values under the same conditions. The insets of Figure 6a show the backside photographs of devices after aging in air. The Au electrodes of the control sample can be observed clearly, indicating the decomposition of the perovskite film with the formation of δ-phase perovskite. By contrast, the PSC sample passivated w TPFPB maintains its black color. Therefore, PSCs w passivation showed better air stability than PSCs wo TPFPB passivation. The stronger water resistance of TPFPB is the main reason for the better air stability.  $  ^{[29,56,57]}  $   To evaluate the light stability of PSCs, we monitored the J-V characteristics of unsealed devices under simulated AM 1.5G illumination without using any ultraviolet filter, and the results are shown in Figure 6b. The PCEs of control devices wo TPFPB passivation rapidly degraded by 50% during 60 h, whereas the devices w passivation maintained 74% of their initial efficiencies during the same period, indicating enhanced light stability after TPFPB passivation. Our stability investigations clearly show that the use of TPFPB as a surface passivation agent is a promising strategy for enhancing the stability of the state-of-the-art PSCs. However, the light stability of passivated PSCs is not sufficient. # 3. Conclusion

In summary, we have demonstrated that TFPBP is a novel surface passivation agent for   $  FA_{0.85}MA_{0.15}PbI_{3}  $   PSCs. Systematic investigations have proven that TFPBP plays multiple roles in tuning the crystal quality, increasing the surface hydrophobicity, passivating the defects, and improving charge-carrier extraction. As a result,

we achieved a champion PCE of 21.6% for a planar PSC with a high FF of over 78%. PSCs w TPFPB passivation also showed superior air and light stabilities. This work demonstrates that the polyfluoro organic compound (TPFPB) is a promising interfacial passivation material for high-performance and stable PSCs.
