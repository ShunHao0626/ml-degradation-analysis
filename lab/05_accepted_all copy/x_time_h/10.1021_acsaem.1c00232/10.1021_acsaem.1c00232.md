# Improving the Interfacial Contact of Screen-Printed Carbon Electrodes for Perovskite Solar Cells

Shanshan Zhu,  $  ^{§}  $   Jiaqi Tian,  $  ^{§}  $   Jiejing Zhang, Chunxiao Gao, and Xizhe Liu  $  ^{*}  $

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Carbon electrodes were developed for perovskite solar cells due to their low cost, superior stability, and solution processability. However, perovskite solar cells with carbon electrodes usually have relatively low power conversion efficiency, which can be related to the recombination process and charge transfer process at the interfaces of carbon electrodes. For the carbon electrodes printed at low temperature, CuSCN hole conductors can decrease the recombination process of devices. By incorporating screen-printed carbon black layers, the contact of electrode interfaces was improved and the charge transfer resistance of devices was decreased. As a consequence, the PCEs of devices were increased from 7.3 to 15.5%. In addition, the stability of devices was improved simultaneously.

KEYWORDS: perovskite solar cells, interfacial contact, carbon electrodes, carrier recombination, stability

# 1. INTRODUCTION

In recent years, perovskite solar cells (PSCs) are intensively investigated because of their outstanding power conversion efficiency (PCE) and their great potential to compete with the conventional silicon technology.  $  ^{1-4}  $   However, PSCs usually employ precious metals as the material of electrodes, which are expensive and can degrade hole conductor layers by diffusion.  $  ^{5}  $   Owing to the high conductivity and superior chemical stability of graphite, PSCs with carbon electrodes are believed to be an ideal architecture for realizing low cost and good stability simultaneously.

Carbon electrodes can be produced by several solution processes without vacuum facilities. Han's group develops an architecture of carbon-based PSCs without hole conductor layers. $ ^{6} $  This kind of PSCs has a porous structure of TiO $ _{2} $ /ZrO $ _{2} $ /Carbon, which is obtained by a high-temperature sintering process to remove the organic components in the paste. $ ^{7-10} $  On the other hand, low-temperature printable carbon electrodes were also studied for saving energy and flexible application. Up to now, carbon electrodes can be prepared at low temperature by screen printing, $ ^{11,12} $  doctor blading, $ ^{13,14} $  rolling transfer, $ ^{15} $  and hot pressing. $ ^{16,17} $  Among these methods, screen printing is a suitable technique for industry application, but these devices usually have relatively low efficiency for the intensive recombination process at electrode interfaces. For further promoting PSCs with carbon electrodes, interfacial engineering between perovskite layers and carbon electrodes is necessary. $ ^{12} $

Hole conductor layers at electrode interfaces can remarkably suppress the recombination process by extracting holes and blocking electrons. As a consequence, carbon-based PSCs with hole conductor layers should have improved photovoltaic performance. Although organic hole conductors are widely employed in PSCs, inorganic ones are promising for their low cost and high stability.  $  ^{18-22}  $   Among various inorganic hole conductors, CuSCN is an abundant p-type semiconductor that exhibits high hole mobility, good thermal stability, and solution processability. CuSCN layers cannot be dissolved with common solvents, which can preserve their morphology in the screen printing process of carbon electrodes. As a consequence, CuSCN-based PSCs with carbon electrodes become a promising strategy, but the PCE of these devices is still lower than that of devices with Au electrodes.  $  ^{23,24}  $   Graphite is the major constituent of carbon electrodes for its superior conductivity and stability, and the large size of graphite flakes usually leads to the difficulty of interfacial contact. To further improve CuSCN-based PSCs with graphite electrodes (GE), the contact of CuSCN/graphite interfaces need to be studied.

# 2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

2.1. Materials. If not otherwise noted, reagents and solvents are listed in Table S1 and used without further purification. Methylammonium bromide (MABr) and formamidinium iodide (FAI) were prepared using the recipe proposed by ref 25.

2.2. CB Paste and Graphite Paste. A total of  $ 0.24\mathrm{g} $  of polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) was dissolved in  $ 2\mathrm{mL} $  of chlorobenzene. Then,  $ 0.8\mathrm{g} $  of graphite powder (1200 mesh) and  $ 0.107\mathrm{g} $  of CB (BP2000, Cabot) were added to the PMMA solution, and the mixture was milled for  $ 1\mathrm{h} $ . This paste was named as graphite paste. On the other hand, CB paste was prepared similar to graphite paste, but only  $ 0.2\mathrm{g} $  of CB was added in  $ 2\mathrm{mL} $  of PMMA solution.

2.3. Device Fabrication. FTO substrates were washed sequentially in deionized water, ethanol, and acetone in an ultrasonicator. After further cleaning by plasma treatment, compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   layers were prepared by spraying 1.2 mL of 0.05 M   $  \mathrm{Ti(OPr)_{2}(acetylacetonate)_{2}}  $   in ethanol solution on FTO substrates at 450 °C. Then, mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   layers were deposited on the compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   layers by spin coating of   $  TiO_{2}  $   paste (30NRD, Greatcell) diluted with ethanol (1:6 by weight) at 5000 rpm for 30 s. After drying at 80 °C, the samples were annealed at 500 °C for 30 min in a muffle furnace. The perovskite solution was composed of 1.1 M FAI, 1.2 M   $  PbI_{2}  $   (99.99%, TCI), 0.24 M MABr, and 0.24 M   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (99.9%) in a 4:1 (v/v) mixture of anhydrous DMF (99.9%) and DMSO (99.8%), and 1.5 M solution of CsI (99.9%) in DMSO was added to the abovementioned solution in 5:95 volume ratio to further improve the photovoltaic property of perovskite layers.

This perovskite precursor solution was cast onto the mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   layers and spin-coated in a two-step process at 2000 rpm for 10 s and 7000 rpm for 30 s. During the second step, 100   $  \mu  $  L of chlorobenzene was poured on the center of spinning substrates in the 20th second. After the spin coating process, samples were immediately annealed on a 100 °C hot plate for 60 min. For preparing CuSCN solution, 0.035 g of CuSCN powder (99%) was dissolved in 1 mL of diethyl sulfide (97%) by stirring for 0.5 h at room temperature. The CuSCN solution was dynamically spin-coated onto the perovskite layers at 5000 rpm for 30 s. After that, CB paste was screen-printed on the CuSCN layers, followed by the drying process at 100 °C for 10 min. GEs were fabricated by screen printing of graphite paste and dried at 100 °C for 10 min. Gold electrodes were used as the reference, which are deposited using a thermal evaporator.

2.4. Characterization. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained using a FEI-MAGELLAN 400 scanning electron microscope. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained using a Rigaku D/max-2550 X-ray diffractometer. Photocurrent density–photovoltage characteristics were recorded from 1.2 to 0 V using a CHI660 electrochemical workstation. The active area of PSCs was 0.09 cm $  ^{2}  $ , defined using a metal mask. AM1.5 illumination was provided using a 3A class solar simulator (UHE-16, ScienceTech Inc.), which was calibrated to one sun with a KG5 filtered Si reference solar cell (certificated by VLSI Standards Inc.). Impedance spectra were measured from 1 MHz to 1 Hz using a CHI660 electrochemical workstation under dark conditions with a bias potential of 0.8 V. IPCE spectra were measured in the DC mode using a controlled monochrometer (BOCIC Inc.) and a 500 W xenon lamp (NBet Inc.), and a calibrated Si cell was used as the reference. For the transient photovoltage (TPV) measurement, the device was perturbed using a 532 nm pulse laser (10 ns, LSR-PS-FA), and the resulting voltage transient was measured using a 1 GHz digital oscilloscope (DSO6104L, Agilent).

Graphite has low resistivity, but its structure of large flakes tends to the partial interfacial contact. As CuSCN has high resistivity, the partial interfacial contact limits the charge collection process. After inserting CB layers, carbon nanoparticles can be in a close contact with CuSCN layers, and holes can be extracted effectively (Figure 2c,d). As the CB layer has much lower resistivity than CuSCN layers (Figure S4), holes can easily transport to the nearest graphite flakes and then output through GEs (Figure 1b). Therefore, CB interfacial layers tend to improve the interfacial contact between CuSCN layers and GEs.

For investigating the function of interfacial layers, four kinds of electrode architectures were investigated, such as the bare GE, CuSCN/GE (CuSCN-GE), CuSCN/CB/GE (CuSCN-CB-GE), and CuSCN/Au electrode (CuSCN-Au). Figure 3a shows current density-voltage  $ (J - V) $  curves of PSCs with different architectures of electrodes, and their photovoltaic parameters are summarized in Table 1. The device with bare GEs shows a PCE of  $ 7.3\% $ , with an open-circuit voltage  $ (V_{\mathrm{oc}}) $  of  $ 0.859\mathrm{V} $ , a short-circuit current density  $ (J_{\mathrm{sc}}) $  of  $ 20.5\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2} $ , and a fill factor (FF) of  $ 39.9\% $ . After adding CuSCN hole conductor layers, the PCE is increased to  $ 12.3\% $  with obviously improved  $ J_{\mathrm{sc}}, V_{\mathrm{oc}} $ , and FF of  $ 20.8\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2} $ ,  $ 1.009\mathrm{V} $ , and  $ 58.6\% $  respectively. By inserting CB layers at the CuSCN/graphite interfaces, the FF of devices is remarkably increased to  $ 69.3\% $ , and the PCE of devices reaches  $ 15.5\% $ . It is noted that the performance of these devices with carbon electrodes is even

exceeding that of CuSCN-based devices with Au electrodes (14.2%). Moreover, the devices with CuSCN/CB/GEs have a relatively low hysteresis effect with a hysteresis index of 0.05, which is much lower than that of devices with CuSCN/Au electrodes (Figure S7). Figure 3b shows the PCE distributions of devices with different architectures of electrodes, and the average PCEs with standard deviations are summarized in Table 1. The average PCEs of these devices have the same tendency of J-V measurements, and CB layers improve the average PCE of devices from 10.9 to 14.4%.

In Figure 3c, the steady-state photocurrent density is measured at the maximum power points of devices with different architectures. All of the devices with GEs can stably output photocurrents in 200 s, which are 16.0, 16.3, and 19.3 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  for the devices with bare GEs, CuSCN/GEs, and CuSCN/CB/GEs, respectively. However, the photocurrent of CuSCN-based PSCs with Au electrodes gradually degrades to 13.78 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  in 200 s after reaching the maximum value of 17.9 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $ . It means that carbon electrodes tend to give a more stable performance than Au electrodes for CuSCN-based PSCs.

Figure 3d shows the IPCE spectra of PSCs with different architectures of electrodes. As these devices have similar light absorption layers, their IPCE spectra have a similar wavelength range of light response. For the devices with CuSCN/CB/GEs, the integral current density is 20.8 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , which is comparable to the short-circuit current density of J-V measurements (error is about 5%). As previous reports indicate the protecting function of PMMA for perovskite layers,  $  ^{31,32}  $   we think that the PMMA of carbon paste should have a similar function of protecting perovskite layers from humidity damage.

# 4. CONCLUSIONS

In summary, we prepare all-solution-processable PSCs with CuSCN hole conductors and GEs, and CB layers are developed for improving the CuSCN/graphite interfaces. It is found that CuSCN layers and CB layers can effectively decrease the charge transfer resistance at the electrode interfaces, and the lifetime of photogenerated carriers is increased simultaneously. As a consequence, the PCEs of devices with GEs increase from 7.3 to 15.5%, which is even superior to the case of the CuSCN-based PSCs with Au electrodes. This architecture of screen-printed carbon electrodes is a suitable technique for industrial applications.
