# ABSTRACT

Defect-induced charge carrier recombination at the interfaces between perovskite and adjacent charge transport layers restricts further improvements in the device performance of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Defect passivation at these interfaces can reduce trap states and inhibit the induced nonradiative recombination. Herein, we report a double-sided interfacial passivation via simply evaporating potassium chloride (DIP-KCl) at both the hole transport layer (HTL)/perovskite and

Journal Pre-proofs

perovskite/electron transport layer (ETL) interfaces in inverted planar PSCs. We demonstrate that the bottom KCl layer at the HTL/perovskite interface not only reduces the interfacial defects and improves the interfacial contact, but also leads to increased perovskite crystallinity, while the top KCl layer at the perovskite/ETL interface efficiently passivates the perovskite top surface defects and facilitates electron extraction at this interface. Thus, suppressed nonradiative recombination and faster charge extraction at both interfaces close to the perovskite layer can be achieved by using our DIP-KCl strategy. As a result, inverted PSCs based on DIP-KCl present an increased efficiency from  $ 17.1\% $  to  $ 19.2\% $  and enhanced stability, retaining over  $ 90\% $  of their initial efficiency after aging at maximum power point tracking for  $ 1000\mathrm{h} $ . This work provides a simple and efficient way for defect passivation to further increase the efficiency and stability of PSCs.

Keywords: Double-sided interfacial passivation; KCl; Defect passivation; Nonradiative recombination; Perovskite solar cells

# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted worldwide attention owing to their immense potential as a new-generation thin film photovoltaic (PV) technique. To date, the highest certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) in PSCs with sizes of approximately   $  0.1 \, cm^2  $   is over 25% [1]. Such encouraging performance is principally attributed to the excellent optoelectronic properties of perovskites, such as their high absorption coefficients, suitable direct band gaps, small exciton binding energies, high carrier mobilities, long diffusion lengths, and superior defect tolerance [2,3]. Nevertheless, the efficiencies of PSCs are still below the Shockley–Queisser theoretical limit because common solution-processed perovskite films exhibit

Journal Pre-proofs

numerous undesired defects, such as vacancies, interstitials, impurities, non-coordinated ions, and dangling bonds   $  [4–7]  $   both in the bulk and at the surface. Such defects can act as trap states that induce non-radiative recombination and as channels for ion migration, leading to the annihilation of photo-generated carriers and degradation of the perovskite device   $  [8–11]  $  .

The use of defect passivation strategies to reduce defects in the bulk and at the grain boundaries of perovskites and to reduce traps at the device interfaces has been recognized to be essential for the fabrication of PSCs with high PCEs and long-term stability   $  [12–22]  $  . Various passivation strategies have been developed to reduce defects and suppress charge recombination. For example, various additives such as polymers, small organic molecules, ionic liquids, inorganic salts, and nanoparticles have been directly added into the perovskite precursor solution and deliberately retained in the obtained perovskite films to passivate both bulk and surface defects   $  [12–15]  $  . Other kinds of interface modifications using organic or inorganic materials have also been explored to passivate interfacial traps, adjust energy level alignment, and improve the perovskite morphology. These modifications have led to the reduction of interfacial recombination, elimination of charge accumulation and hysteresis, and improvement of device stability   $  [16–22]  $  .

Low-cost alkaline halides have been demonstrated to be advantageous for defect passivation. Alkali cations and halide ions can passivate both positive- and negative-charged defects in perovskites   $  [23,24]  $  . They can be used either as additives in the perovskite precursors or as interlayers inserted between the perovskite and charge transport layers (CTLs)   $  [24–28]  $  .

Alkaline halides can be directly added in the precursors to restrict the formation of

Journal Pre-proofs

point defects or defects at the grain boundaries/surfaces of perovskites [14,24,25]. For example, Moon et al. reported that the addition of sodium chloride into perovskite precursor solution can retard the crystallization process during perovskite formation to form high-coverage and uniform perovskite films [26]. Park et al. compared a series of alkali metal iodide salts and investigated their effects on the device hysteresis, and found that excess  $ \mathrm{K}^+ $  ions can prevent the formation of Frenkel defects, leading to hysteresis-free PSCs [25]. Stranks et al. demonstrated that the additive KI can passivate defects at the surfaces and grain boundaries to substantially mitigate both photoinduced ion migration and non-radiative recombination in perovskite films, in which potassium can immobilize the halide by forming benign compounds at the grain boundaries [14].

The modification of bottom CTL/perovskite interfaces with alkaline halides has also been explored for improving energy alignment and perovskite morphology in addition to passivating interfacial defects [4,27,28]. In regular PSCs, Snaith et al. explored the use of cesium bromide as an interfacial modifier at the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  electron transport layer (ETL)/perovskite interface to reduce the electronic defect density at the ETL/perovskite heterojunction, resulting in improved UV stability [20]. Tan et al. used a  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ -KCl mixed film as the ETL to simultaneously passivate the ETL/perovskite contact and grain-boundary in perovskite film, leading to an enhancement of the open-circuit voltage ( $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $ ) by  $ 60~\mathrm{mV} $  and an increase in the PCE from  $ 20.2\% $  to  $ 22.2\% $  [27]. Hao et al. claimed that discrete passivation using KCl inserted between a  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  ETL and perovskite layer can simultaneously passivate several types of ionic defects, leading to the enhancement of  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  and PCE, elimination of hysteresis, and improvement of long-term stability [4]. Compared with the majority of the reported works on ETL/perovskite interface passivation using alkaline halides,

Journal Pre-proofs

only a few works have applied this strategy on the hole transport layer (HTL)/perovskite interface in inverted PSCs. He et al. performed a facile interfacial passivation on the   $  NiO_{x}  $   HTL of inverted PSCs using alkali halides including NaCl and KCl [28]. They found that alkali halide passivation improved the qualities of both the interface and the perovskite, leading to reduced interfacial recombination and suppressed ion diffusion. As a result, the KCl passivation delivered a higher PCE with the enhancement of   $  V_{oc}  $   from 1.07 eV to 1.15 eV, as well as a significantly improved stability [28]. From the above reports, it can be seen that inorganic alkali halides are effective for both bulk and interfacial defect passivation. Compared to the use of alkali halides at the bottom CTL/perovskite interface, there are few reports on the use of alkali halides to passivate the top perovskite/CTL interface. Considering the large numbers of traps such as dangling bonds at the perovskite surface and defects caused by the volatilization of organic compositions from the perovskite surface during thermal annealing [29–31], defect passivation at this interface could an efficient way of improving device performance [32–36]. You et al. used the organic halide phenethylammonium iodide for interfacial defect passivation to suppress non-radiative recombination, leading to a high   $  V_{oc}  $   of up to 1.18 V and a high certified efficiency of 23.3% in a regular PSC with a 1.53 eV bandgap perovskite [37]. Bawendi et al. performed perovskite/HTL interfacial passivation using linear alkyl ammonium bromides, and achieved reduced carrier quenching and improved PCE stabilization [38]. Huang et al. developed an inorganic lead oxysalt layer to passivate the undercoordinated surface lead centers on the perovskite surface, leading to increased carrier lifetime as well as enhanced PCE and stability [39]. Thus, defect passivation by using an inorganic halide layer at the top perovskite/CTL interface is attractive and should draw sufficient research attention compared to the use of

Journal Pre-proofs

complex and expensive organic materials [28]. Simple and facile passivation methods using low-cost alkali halides should be developed for further improving the PSC performance.

For interfacial passivation, most works have focused on passivating either only the bottom or the top contact. Simultaneous passivation at both the bottom and top interfaces of the perovskite layer, i.e. double-sided interfacial passivation (DIP), to improve the PSC performance has recently attracted increasing interest. For example, in regular PSCs, polymer poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) was used to passivate both the ETL/perovskite and perovskite/HTL interfaces, leading to a large  $  V_{\mathrm{oc}}  $  as high as 1.22 V for PSCs with perovskite having a bandgap of 1.60 eV [40]. Zhao et al. also reported the DIP of regular PSCs using excess  $  \mathrm{PbI}_2  $  induced by extended thermal annealing, and achieved a stabilized efficiency of 22% [41]. The above works using DIP treatment exhibit significantly improved device performance in regular PSCs. To the best of our knowledge, there are no reports on the use of simple, cheap, and upscalable inorganic alkali salts for DIP in inverted PSCs.

Herein, we report the significant suppression of interfacial recombination via a simple DIP strategy using low-cost potassium chloride (DIP-KCl) as interfacial passivation layers at both the  $ \mathrm{NiO_x} $ /perovskite and perovskite/PCBM interfaces in inverted planar PSCs. The DIP-KCl layers are prepared by simple thermal evaporation, which can be precisely controlled and is readily upscalable. This advantage is beneficial for future large-area perovskite solar module fabrication. In our DIP-KCl strategy, KCl is added to the hole transport layer (HTL), i.e.  $ \mathrm{NiO_x} $ , to simultaneously passivate the defects at the HTL/perovskite interface and at the grain boundaries of the perovskite bulk film. A further KCl modification of the perovskite top surface is performed to passivate the defects at the perovskite/PCBM interface.

Our DIP-KCl strategy is demonstrated to be effective, and leads to reduced defect density and suppressed recombination, as well as improved charge extraction. As a result, a large increase of the PSC efficiency from 17.1% to 19.2%, coupled with a long-term stability of 1000 h under continuous operation, has been achieved. Our double-sided passivation strategy provides an avenue to uncover the potential of further increasing the efficiencies of PSCs.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1.Materials

Formamidinium iodide  $ (\mathrm{CH}(\mathrm{NH}_2)_2\mathrm{I} $  (FAI),  $ 99.99\% $  ) was purchased from Hangzhou Perov Optoelectronic Technology Co., Ltd. Lead (II) iodide (99.99% metals basis), cesium bromide ( $ >99\% $ ), potassium chloride (99.5%), and bathocuproine ( $ >99\% $ ) were purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. All other chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All chemical reagents were used as received without further purification.

# 2.2. Sample preparation

Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glasses (TEC-15, Nippon Sheet Glass Co., Japan) were first laser-etched before preparation, then cleaned through ultrasonication for 30 min in detergent solution, distilled water, alcohol, and absolute ethanol sequentially. Before use, the substrates were dried with a high-pressure nitrogen flow. A p-type, compact  $ \mathrm{NiO}_x $  layer was then deposited on top of the FTO glass by spray pyrolysis. Afterwards, the substrates were transferred to a glovebox, following which a potassium chloride layer (approximately  $ 20~\mathrm{nm} $  thick) was thermal evaporated in a high vacuum ( $ <5\times 10^{-4}\mathrm{Pa} $ ). The perovskite was then fabricated by a one-step

Journal Pre-proofs

anti-solvent method: 70 μL 1.5 M precursor solution containing FAI (1.275 M), PbI $ _2 $  (1.5 M), and CsBr (0.225 M) dissolved in binary solvent (DMF: DMSO= 4:1 by volume ratio) was spin-coated at 6000 rpm for 45 s, while chlorobenzene (350 μL) was simultaneously drop-casted quickly as an anti-solvent within 15 s. The perovskite films were then thermally treated on a hotplate at 150 °C. Potassium chloride film with thickness of about 3 nm was next fabricated through thermal evaporation (vacuum was less than <5 × 10 $ ^{-4} $  Pa). Then, 70 μL of PCBM solution was spin-coated at a rotation speed of 5000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, 5 nm of bathocuproine (BCP) and 90 nm of Ag electrode were thermally evaporated under high vacuum (<5 × 10 $ ^{-4} $  Pa).

# 2.3. Characterization

SEM and AFM images were obtained by a Nova NanoSEM 450 scanning electron microscope (FEI Co., Netherlands) and an SPM-9700 atomic force microscope (Shimadzu Co., Japan). A Thermo ESCALAB 250XI system was used to acquire the XPS spectra. XRD was measured on an Empyrean X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (PANalytical B.V. Co., Netherlands). ToF-SIMS depth profiles were obtained using an IonToF ToF-SIMS 5 instrument (IONTOF Co., Germany). The PL spectra were measured by an Edinburgh FLS920 fluorescence spectrometer (Edinburgh Co., UK). The photovoltaic measurements applied a black mask with an aperture area of  $ 0.09\mathrm{cm}^2 $  under a standard irradiance of  $ 100\mathrm{mW}\cdot \mathrm{cm}^{-2} $  with a solar simulator (Oriel, model 9119), in which the intensity has been calibrated with a silicon photodiode. The incident photon-to-electron conversion efficiency (IPCE) was obtained using a Newport IPCE system (Newport, USA). The light-soaking stability was characterized by a CHI1000c multichannel electro-chemistry workstation (Chenhua, Co., China). The steady-state power output of the cells was

Journal Pre-proofs

measured using a Keithley 2400 Source Meter together with a 450 W Oriel Xenon lamp.

# 3. Results and discussion

For the DIP-KCl device, both KCl layers were prepared by thermal evaporation. Compared to the commonly used spin coating-based PSC passivation strategies reported in most works, thermal evaporation can be more precisely controlled even at large scales and shows great potential for future implementation in large-area perovskite solar module fabrication. Here, the thickness of the KCl layers on  $ \mathrm{NiO}_x $  and PCBM were controlled to be  $ 20~\mathrm{nm} $  and  $ 3\mathrm{nm} $ , respectively, as determined by the efficiency optimization experiments (Fig. S1). Fig. 1(a) shows the schematic diagram and the cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image of a DIP-KCl based PSC with a FTO/ $ \mathrm{NiO}_x/\mathrm{KCl}/\mathrm{FA}_{0.85}\mathrm{Cs}_{0.15}\mathrm{PbI}_{2.85}\mathrm{Br}_{0.15}/\mathrm{KCl}/\mathrm{PCBM}/\mathrm{BCP}/\mathrm{Ag} $  structure where both the HTL/perovskite and perovskite/ETL interfaces are passivated by the inserted KCl layers. Here, the perovskite  $ \mathrm{FA}_{0.85}\mathrm{Cs}_{0.15}\mathrm{PbI}_{2.85}\mathrm{Br}_{0.15} $  was chosen because of its superior thermal stability compared to MA-based perovskites [42,43]. Fig. 1(b) shows the element distribution detected by time-of-light secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) measurements of the DIP-KCl device. As can be seen, there is obvious accumulation of K and Cl ions at both the  $ \mathrm{NiO}_x/ $ perovskite interface and the perovskite/PCBM interface. The detected K concentration is slightly lower in the perovskite bulk than at the two adjacent interfaces. This indicates that K ions can also diffuse into the perovskite bulk film at the grain boundaries of the perovskite crystals to passivate the onsite defects [14,28]. The obvious K  $ 2p $  and Cl  $ 2p $  signals in

Journal Pre-proofs

the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) results shown in Fig. S2 confirm the existence of KCl at both interfaces [4]. The higher concentration of K and Cl at the interface regions is considered to play significant roles in passivating the interfacial defects and improving the interfacial contacts. Fig. 1(c) illustrates the predicted action of the DIP-KCl in the inverted PSC whereby defects including deep and shallow traps in the bulk perovskite film and at the surfaces/interfaces are reduced, resulting in suppressed carrier recombination and enhanced charge extraction. To verify this prediction, we explored the effects of DIP-KCl on the crystalline and photoelectric properties of the perovskite film as well as on the charge transport kinetics in the bulk perovskite and at the adjacent interfaces.

Some reports found that the bottom passivation layer can modify the growth of the perovskite and affect the defect distribution in the bulk perovskite film [27,28]. Thus, the morphology and crystallinity of the perovskite deposited on substrates with and without KCl were first carefully investigated. Fig. 2(a) shows the topography images of pristine  $ \mathrm{FTO / NiO_x} $  and  $ \mathrm{FTO / NiO_x / KCl} $  samples characterized by atomic force microscopy (AFM). Some small cubic particles were observed in the  $ \mathrm{FTO / NiO_x / KCl} $  sample, which can be attributed to KCl crystals formed on the top of the  $ \mathrm{NiO_x} $  layer after thermal evaporation. When the perovskite precursor solution is deposited on the  $ \mathrm{FTO / NiO_x / KCl} $  substrates, a part of KCl can be dissolved. This affects the perovskite formation process [27,44]. The  $ \mathrm{FTO / NiO_x} $  substrate shows a smoother morphology with a decrease in the roughness Ra from  $ 44.28\mathrm{nm} $  to  $ 39.76\mathrm{nm} $ . The flatter substrate together with the effect of dissolved KCl on perovskite formation may facilitate better

Journal Pre-proofs

crystallization of the deposited perovskite [45,46]. As seen in Fig. 2(b), although perovskite films with nearly similar topography on the  $ \mathrm{FTO / NiO_x / KCl} $  and  $ \mathrm{FTO / NiO_x} $  substrates were obtained, the former sample shows a smaller roughness of  $ 20.63\mathrm{nm} $  than the latter sample (19.95 nm), which may be beneficial for the formation of good upper interface contacts.

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) results of perovskite films deposited on  $ \mathrm{FTO / NiO_x} $  and  $ \mathrm{FTO / NiO_x / KCl} $  are exhibited in Fig. 2(c). The two samples show nearly identical crystal structures with nearly no shift of the perovskite peaks (Fig. S3), indicating that neither  $ \mathbf{K}^+ $  ions nor  $ \mathrm{Cl^-} $  ions have been incorporated into the perovskite lattice, in agreement with other reports [4,14]. The obtained perovskite shows a preferred orientation along the (012) plane, with an obviously increased intensity with respect to the (001) plane from 0.33 to 0.50 in the film deposited on  $ \mathrm{FTO / NiO_x / KCl} $ . Controlling the crystal orientation is an effective way to reduce the lattice distortion and crystal micro-strains, and subsequently retard the formation of corresponding defects/traps [42,47,48]. The higher film orientation along the (012) plane on the  $ \mathrm{FTO / NiO_x / KCl} $  substrate compared to the  $ \mathrm{FTO / NiO_x} $  substrate would lead to different defect conditions in the obtained perovskite films. In addition, a slight increase in the XRD intensity is observed for the perovskite on the  $ \mathrm{FTO / NiO_x / KCl} $  substrate compared to the reference sample. This indicates a better crystallinity in the former film. The grain size calculated using the Scherrer formula increases from  $ 269~\mathrm{nm} $  to  $ 298~\mathrm{nm} $  after depositing a KCl layer on top of  $ \mathrm{NiO_x} $ . Combined with the AFM results, we speculate that the improved crystallinity and increased grain size originate from

Journal Pre-proofs

the smoother substrate surface and the effect of the dissolved KCl on perovskite formation. The dissolved KCl can slow down the heterogeneous nucleation process at the HTL/perovskite interface and lead to large perovskite crystalline size [45,46,49]. The improved crystallinity and increased grain size achieved by using the bottom KCl passivation layer is likely to reduce the defect density and suppress recombination.

The morphology and crystallinity of perovskite films with the top KCl passivation layer were also investigated. As can be seen from the SEM and XRD results in Fig. S4, both the morphology and crystallinity of the perovskite did not change significantly after depositing the  $ 3\mathrm{nm} $  KCl layer, implying that the top KCl might be amorphous because it is too thin. Here, the ultrathin KCl is employed to further passivate the surface defects on the perovskite film and not to hinder the electron transport at the perovskite/PCBM interface, just like the tunnel-oxide passivation layers in silicon photovoltaics and the polymer or other insulting layers developed in some reported works on PSCs [50,51]. It is expected that the top KCl passivation layer can reduce traps and their induced recombination at the perovskite/ETL interface. Combining the top passivation with the bottom KCl layer, defects in both the bulk perovskite film and at the surfaces/interfaces of the absorber layer can be passivated.

In addition to the perovskite morphology and crystallography properties, the defect nature and photogenerated carrier behavior in perovskite films also play important roles in the device performance. To investigate the effects of DIP-KCl on the radiative

Journal Pre-proofs

and nonradiative recombination channels within the perovskite films and charge transfer across the interfaces, we further carried out steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements. An obvious increase in the luminescence intensity can be seen in Fig. 3(a) for the glass/KCl/perovskite sample compared to the reference glass/perovskite sample, indicating the large reduction in recombination in the perovskite bulk due to the improved crystalline quality. The PL intensity is further improved after depositing KCl on top of the glass/KCl/perovskite, indicating the suppressed recombination and reduced trap states at the top surface of the perovskite. Furthermore, a slight blue shift in the PL peak position can also be seen in the glass/KCl/perovskite sample versus the reference sample. This blue shift indicates a decreased trap density near the band edge and larger separation between the quasi Fermi levels, which is beneficial for achieving larger  $  V_{\mathrm{oc}}  $  [52-54]. The DIP-KCl passivation sample presented the PL with the shortest wavelength and the highest intensity, implying that DIP-KCl passivation leads to the lowest non-radiative recombination among all three samples. These observations suggest that the charge recombination in the bulk and at the surface of perovskites is successfully suppressed by KCl passivation, especially for the DIP-KCl sample. The PL decay lifetime derived from the TRPL curves can be used to evaluate both charge recombination and charge extraction. The corresponding TRPL decay curves are shown in Fig. 3(b). The TRPL curves are fitted with biexponential functions corresponding to two decay processes comprising a faster charge extraction process and a slower charge recombination process [44]. A series of average decay

To further estimate the effect of the DIP-KCl passivation on the nature of the defects in perovskite quantitatively, space charge limited current (SCLC) measurements were performed. The currents in devices with the FTO/perovskite/Ag (Pristine), FTO/KCl/perovskite/Ag (single-side passivation using KCl (SIP-KCl)), and FTO/KCl/perovskite/KCl/Ag (DIP-KCl) structures were measured at different voltage biases (Fig. 3(c)). To check the effect of DIP-KCl on device performance, the DIP-KCl was incorporated into devices with the device configuration FTO/NiO $ _x $ /FA $ _{0.85} $ Cs $ _{0.15} $ PbI $ _{2.85} $ Br $ _{0.15} $ /PCBM/BCP/Ag. The statistical photovoltaic parameters, including PCE,  $ V_{oc} $ , fill factor (FF), and short-circuit current density ( $ J_{sc} $ ), for different passivated devices are summarized in Fig. 4(a) and Table S2. All the photovoltaic parameters are improved by the KCl passivation. Besides, the DIP-KCl based PSCs present the most concentrated distribution of the photovoltaic parameters, which suggests good repeatability of the photovoltaic performance. The disappearance of the hysteresis may be caused by the improved charge transfer at the HTL/perovskite interface, leading to a balanced charge extraction at both CTL/perovskite interfaces [57]. Furthermore, the pristine devices presented an obvious non-steady state current/efficiency during the first 150 s, consistent with the hysteresis in the J-V curve. Once again, the DIP-KCl presented the highest SPO values within the shortest time, confirming the reduced recombination and improved charge transfer.

To further systematically investigate the charge recombination and charge transfer behaviors in the devices with and without DIP-KCl, we used ideality factors deduced from the curves of  $  V_{\mathrm{oc}}  $  versus light intensities to evaluate the trap-assisted interfacial recombination, defined as the Shockley-Read-Hall (SRH) recombination [58]. Smaller ideality factors indicate lower SRH recombination and trap density in the whole device [59,60]. As shown in Fig. 5(a), the ideality factor decreases from 1.95 for the pristine devices to 1.49 for the SIP-KCl devices and 1.44 for DIP-KCl devices, respectively. This confirms the suppression of the charge recombination by KCl passivation, which is attributed to the reduced defect density in the perovskite film

Journal Pre-proofs

and the passivated traps at the interfaces between the perovskite and CTLs as supported by the PL, TRPL and SCLC results. Additionally, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was also used to the characterize the interfacial recombination in the devices. The semicircle in the high frequency range of the Nyquist plots is attributed to the interfacial charge recombination behavior [61,62]. As can be seen from Fig. 5(b), the increase of the semicircle radii in the high frequency range in the SIP-KCl and DIP-KCl devices compared to the pristine devices suggests that reduced interfacial recombination occurred in the KCl-passivated PSCs. Furthermore, the transient photovoltage and photocurrent decays of the devices were measured. A slower photovoltage decay and faster photocurrent decay can be observed in Fig. 5(c) and (d) when KCl was inserted between the perovskite layer and CTLs. The photovoltage decay lifetime increases from  $ 17.3\mu s $  in the pristine PSC to  $ 57.0\mu s $  and  $ 68.9\mu s $  for the SIP-KCl and DIP-KCl devices respectively, indicating reduced recombination rates at the grain boundaries and interfaces in the KCl passivated devices [58]. On the other hand, the photocurrent decay lifetime decreases from  $ 2.95\mu s $  for the pristine PSC to  $ 3.39\mu s $  and  $ 3.76\mu s $  for the SIP-KCl and DIP-KCl devices respectively, confirming the faster charge extraction in PSCs with KCl passivation layers consistent with the above TRPL results [52,63,64]. The faster charge extraction can be attributed to the reduced shallow traps at the interfaces and improved interfacial contacts.

Stability is one of the major issues hampering the practical application of PSCs. It has been reported that weak chemical binding at grain boundaries with low

Journal Pre-proofs

crystallinity and the severe ion migration in low crystallinity regions, especially at grain boundaries, facilitate degradation. Improving the perovskite crystalline quality and reducing bulk and grain boundary/surface defects by employing passivation is thus beneficial for increasing stability [65]. Previous reports have indicated that alkali chloride passivation can improve device stability [4,20]. Interfacial passivation can also significantly enhance the durability of PSCs because interfacial degradation would induce dramatic charge accumulation and recombination at the interface, leading to the degradation of the whole device [66]. To investigate the effect of KCl passivation on the long-term stability of PSCs, aging tests including storage stability and continuous working stability were conducted on our PSCs with different interfacial passivation. The three kinds of PSCs were stored at 85 °C in a drying box and taken out into ambient air every 24 h to test their photovoltaic performance. Fig. 6(a) and Table S3 show that the DIP-KCl PSCs retained approximately 95% of their initial PCE values after thermal aging for 500 h, while the pristine PSCs lost >20% of their initial PCEs under the same aging condition. The stability under real operation conditions was also studied by aging the PSCs with different KCl passivation at maximum power point tracking in a N₂-filled glovebox with 1 sun illumination at 45 °C. Fig. 6(b) and Table S4 show that the DIP-KCl devices present the best long-term stability. The SIP-KCl and DIP-KCl devices maintained over 83% and 90% of their initial PCEs after 1000 h aging, while the pristine ones lost about 25% of their initial PCEs at the same aging condition. The above results confirm that KCl passivation effectively improves device stability, which is consistent with previous

Journal Pre-proofs

reports [4,20,66]. The longer stability can be attributed to the improved perovskite crystalline quality with reduced bulk and grain boundary/surface defects, and the passivated interface with suppressed interfacial recombination and charge accumulation.

# 4. Conclusions

In summary, we developed a DIP-KCl strategy for inverted planar PSCs by simply evaporating KCl at both the HTL/perovskite interface and perovskite/ETL interface.

Journal Pre-proofs

The DIP-KCl method was demonstrated to effectively passivate defects in the bulk perovskite and at the CTL/perovskite interfaces, as supported by our PL, TRPL, and SCLC results. The TRPL, EIS, transient photovoltage, and photocurrent decay results indicate improved charge extraction at both interfaces. The DIP-based inverted PSCs presented a remarkably increased PCE from 17.1% to 19.2% and an enhanced stability in which >90% of their initial efficiency was retained after 1000 h maximum power point tracking under continuous 1 sun light-soaking at 45 °C. This work provides a facile and efficient way of defect passivation and paves the way for further improving the PCE and stability of PSCs.
