# High-Performance Inverted Perovskite Solar Cells with Operational Stability via n-Type Small Molecule Additive-Assisted Defect Passivation

Donghwan Koo, Yongjoon Cho, Ungsoo Kim, Gyujeong Jeong, Junghyun Lee, Jihyung Seo, Changduk Yang,* and Hyesung Park*

Significant efforts have been devoted to modulating the grain size and improving the film quality of perovskite in perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Adding materials to the perovskite is especially promising for high-performance PSCs, because the additives effectively control the crystal structure. Although the additive engineering approach has substantially boosted the efficiency of PSCs, instability of the perovskite film has remained a primary bottleneck for the commercialization of PSCs. Herein, a newly conceived bithiophene-based n-type conjugated small molecule (Y-Th2) is introduced to PSCs, which simultaneously enhances the performance and stability of the cell. The Y-Th2 effectively passivates the defect states in perovskite through Lewis acid–base interactions, increasing the grain size and quality of the perovskite absorber. An inverted PSC containing the Y-Th2 additive achieves a power conversion efficiency of 21.5%, versus 18.3% in the reference device. The operational stability is also considerably enhanced by the improved hydrophobicity and intermolecular hydrogen bonds in the perovskite.

Perovskite is hydrophilic, meaning that moistures can readily infiltrate along the grain boundaries of its crystals, triggering

severe decomposition of the constituent elements and a consequent decline in device performance.  $  ^{[17,18]}  $   In addition, the constituent atomic species of perovskite materials are thermally decomposed at elevated temperatures, even under protective encapsulation layers. The decomposed organic and halide ions preferentially migrate along the grain boundaries of the perovskite crystals and into the adjacent charge transporting layers, or even into the metal electrodes of the solar cell. These behaviors readily lead to defect states (e.g., cations and halide vacancies) and metal halide formations, which severely degrade the device performance.  $  ^{[19,20]}  $   Moreover, the unfavorable volume expansion of the perovskite lattice under continuous thermal stresses accelerates the moisture diffusion, further deteriorating the quality of perovskite film.  $  ^{[21]}  $   Thus, improving the stability of PSCs in moist and thermally elevated environments is greatly demanded.

Perovskite is especially promising for its superior light absorption coefficient, long carrier diffusion length, high charge carrier mobility, and simple processability.[1-4] Progress in perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has been rapid and remarkable, accelerated by numerous studies. Herein, we introduce a simple rod-shaped n-type organic small molecule,   $  (5Z,5^{\prime}Z)-5,5^{\prime}-(7,7^{\prime}-(3,3^{\prime}-\text{dioctyl}-[2,2^{\prime}-\text{bithiophene}]-5,5^{\prime}-\text{diyl})\text{bis(benzo}[c][1,2,5]\text{thiadiazole-7,4-diyl}))\text{bis(methanylylidene)})  $   bis(3-ethyl-2-thioxothiazolidin-4-one (Y-Th2), which is tolerant to moisture and temperature. The Y-Th2 additive effectively mediates the perovskite grain and crystallinity. When incorporated in perovskite, Y-Th2 forms a Lewis acid–base interaction with the   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ions, thereby increasing the Gibbs free energy of nucleating perovskite crystals.  $  ^{[28-32]}  $   It also retards the crystallization rate of the perovskite crystals by forming hydrogen bonds with the A-site cations in perovskite. Owing to the multifaceted role of Y-Th2, the grain size and crystallinity of perovskite are significantly improved. Moreover, as the additive is semiconducting, it provides additional percolation pathways for the charge carriers. Consequently, an inverted structured PSC treated with Y-Th2 achieved significantly higher performance than the pristine device (PCE = 21.5% versus 18.3%). Meanwhile, the intermolecular hydrogen bonds immobilized the A-site cations, increasing the hydrophobicity and suppressing the ion migration in the perovskite layer, and thereby improving the operational moisture and thermal stability of the device.

We next investigated the moisture stability of the Y-Th2-treated PSCs. The defect sites and grain boundaries of perovskite crystals are permeable to moisture, which severely degrades the device performance. Therefore, the increased grain size and defect passivation of perovskite in the Y-Th2-incorporated device are expected to improve the moisture resistance. As shown in Figure 3a, the Y-Th2 treated device was significantly more stable to moisture than the pristine device

(25 °C, 40% RH). The performance degradation of the pristine device was notable. After 1700 h, the pristine device retained less than 30% of its initial PCE, whereas the Y-Th2-treated device maintained over 80% of its initial performance. The treated device demonstrated good operational stability even in harsher humidity environments (25 °C, 85% RH) (Figure S12, Supporting Information). The surface property of the perovskite film also influences its stability under external conditions, because adsorbate molecules with high reactivity and diffusivity will increase the moisture susceptibility of perovskite.[17,44] Figure 3b presents the contact angle measurements of a water droplet on the pristine and Y-Th2 added perovskite films. The intrinsically hydrophilic surface of the perovskite was hydrophobically modified by the long alkyl chains of the Y-Th2 molecules. Accordingly, the contact angle increased from 27.0° in the pristine sample to 100.6° in the treated sample, conferring stability against moistures.

The improved stability of the treated device in humid environments was further verified by absorption spectroscopy and 2D grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) analysis. Figure 3c,d shows the absorption spectra of the perovskite films exposed to moisture for different times (25 °C, 40% RH). As the moisture exposure time increased, significant absorption loss was observed in the pristine film, and an additional distinct absorption appeared at 500 nm, corresponding to the PbI $ _{2} $  peak originating from the decomposition of perovskite crystals. $ ^{[45]} $  In contrast, the absorption spectra of the perovskite film with the Y-Th2 additive showed negligible changes during the same measurement period. Furthermore, the decreased slope at the edge of the absorption spectra of the reference film, which is related to the Urbach energy, indicates augmentation of the defect state during the moisture exposure (Figure 3c, inset). $ ^{[46]} $  To further investigate the surface crystalline orientation of perovskite, and to confirm that Y-Th2 stabilizes the perovskite structure, the samples were characterized by GIWAXS measurements. Panels e and f of Figure 3 display the GIWAXS results of the pristine and Y-Th2-treated perovskite films, respectively, after exposure to 40% RH for 1200 h. Both films exhibited the tetragonal phase of perovskite at  $ q = 1.0 $  Å $ ^{-1} $  which corresponds to the (110) plane of perovskite. The intensity of the (110) peak was reduced in the pristine sample, and an additional peak of trigonal PbI $ _{2} $  phase appeared at  $ q = 0.9 $  Å $ ^{-1} $ ; in contrast, the (110) peak of the Y-Th2 treated film was strong with no distinct PbI $ _{2} $  phase. The GIWAXS patterns of Y-Th2 alone are provided in Figure S13, Supporting Information. These results confirm that the Y-Th2 additive confers the desirable stability of perovskite under humid conditions. The effect is achieved by defect passivation and increased hydrophobicity of the surface.

The ITO-coated glass substrate was cleaned with deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol by sonication, followed by O $ _{2} $  plasma treatment. The PTAA powder was dissolved in chlorobenzene (CB) with a concentration of 2 mg mL $ ^{-1} $ , then was spin-coated onto the ITO electrode at 4000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 120 °C for 10 min. The mixed cation and halide perovskite solution was prepared through three steps. i) dissolving 1.4 M of formamidinium iodide and 1.4 M of PbI $ _{2} $  powders in a co-solvent of dimethylformamide (DMF):dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (7:3, v/v), ii) dissolving 1.4 M of methylammonium iodide and 1.4 M of PbBr $ _{2} $  powders in a co-solvent of DMF:DMSO (7:3, v/v), and iii) mixing the as-prepared solution in an 8:2 volume ratio and stirring for 2 h. The perovskite solution was then spin-coated onto the PTAA layer at 1000 and 4000 rpm for 10 and 25 s, respectively. CB without or with Y-Th2 was dropped onto the substrate before the spin-coating process ended. The as-deposited perovskite film was annealed at 120 °C for 10 min. Next, PC $ _{61} $ BM solution in CB and ZnO nanoparticle dispersion in isopropanol were sequentially spin-coated onto the perovskite film at 3000 and 4000 rpm, respectively, forming the electron transport layer, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 10 min. Finally, the Ag electrode was thermally evaporated to a thickness of 100 nm under a base pressure of  $ 2 \times 10^{-6} $  Torr. The active area of devices was 0.13 cm $ ^{2} $ , and device efficiency was measured in an N $ _{2} $ -filled glove box at room temperature.

Measurement and Characterization: The   $  {}^{1}  $  H NMR spectra were recorded by a VNMRS 400 MHz spectrophotometer using deuterated chloroform (CDCl  $  _{3}  $  ) as the solvent and tetramethylsilane (TMS) as the internal standard. The XPS spectra were measured by an ESCALAB 250XI spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The FT-IR spectra were obtained by a 670-IR (Agilent) spectrophotometer. The TGA curve of Y-Th2 was evaluated with Q500 (TA Instruments). The surface morphologies of the perovskite films were analyzed using an SEM microscope (S-4800, Hitachi), and the XRD patterns were measured by MAX 2500 (Rigaku) using a Cu X-ray source. The UV–vis–NIR spectra were recorded by a Cary 5000 (Agilent) spectrophotometer. The steady-state and time-resolved PL spectra of the perovskites were measured by alpha300R (WITec PL setup) and a fluorescence lifetime spectrometer (Fluo Time 300, PicoQuant FmbH), respectively. The J-V characteristics of the PSCs were obtained by a Keithley 2635A source meter. The EQE spectra were measured by an IQE 200B (Oriel Instruments). The light-intensity dependent   $  J_{sc}  $   and   $  V_{oc}  $   were measured using neutral density filters, and the EIS measurements were carried out by an impedance analyzer (SI1260, Solartron). The contact angles were measured using a Phoenix 300Touch (SEO). The GIWAXS measurements were conducted at the PLS-II 6D and 9A U-SAXS beamlines in the Pohang Accelerator Laboratory (South Korea). The X-rays emitted from the in-vacuum undulator were monochromated at 11.05 keV (wavelength 1.12199 Å). At the selected incidence angle (0.12–0.14°), the X-ray beam completely penetrated the film, and the X-ray scattering was recorded by a 2D charge-coupled device detector. The ion migration in the completed devices under thermal stress was measured by a TOF-SIMS 5 (ION TOF GmbH), using pulsed primary ions from a Cs  $  ^{+}  $   (1 keV) liquid–metal ion gun for sputtering and a Bi  $  ^{+}  $   (25 keV) pulsed primary ion beam for detecting the negative ions in the analysis. The measurement of device stability including moisture and thermal stability was conducted under a non-continuous illumination condition without any encapsulation. The device stability under light soaking and damp heat conditions was measured using the encapsulation.
