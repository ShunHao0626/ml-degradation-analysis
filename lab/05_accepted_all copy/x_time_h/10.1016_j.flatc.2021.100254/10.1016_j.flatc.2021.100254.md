# Introduction

Organic–inorganic metal halide perovskite solar cells (PVSCs) are promising next-generation solar cells due to their excellent advantages of a broad absorption band from the visible to near-infrared region, easy solution processing technique, and high power conversion efficiency (PCE)   $  [1,2]  $  . The efficiency of PVSCs has markedly increased from 3.8% to more than 25% in the past 10 years   $  [3–7]  $  . At present, the efficiency of a single-junction PVSC can reach 25.5%. Furthermore, the performance of perovskite/Si tandem cells has already reached 29.5%, which surpasses the efficiency of crystalline Si cells   $  [8]  $  . PVSCs have two types of architectures: conventional mesoscopic and inverted planar heterojunction. In general, inverted planar PVSCs are fabricated in a sequence of transparent conducting oxide/hole transport layer (HTL)/perovskite absorber active layer/electron transport layer/Ag. In addition to the conventional architecture, inverted planar PVSCs have emerged due to their simple device structure, low-temperature solution processing, low hysteresis effect, and suitability as flexible substrates   $  [9–11]  $  . Recently, inverted PVSCs can reach an efficiency of over 22%, which means there is still a long way to improve their performance   $  [12]  $  .

# ABSTRACT

To enhance carrier transport, the internal charge transfer resistance of organic-inorganic metal halide perovskite solar cells (PVSCs) was reduced through the formation of well-distributed nanogold-graphene-based conductive networks in the matrix of the active layer. In this study, a reduced graphene oxide-cysteine/nanogold (rGO-CysAu) hybrid material was prepared and incorporated into PVSCs. Nanogold particles were modified with the amino acid cysteine and successfully deposited onto the surface of graphene oxide. To prepare inverted PVSCs, rGO-CysAu with a highly uniform distribution of gold nanoparticles on the rGO surface was added to the perovskite precursor solution. The incorporation of rGO-CysAu into PVSCs resulted in a  $ 12\% $  increase in power conversion efficiency, improving perovskite charge transport efficiency and crystallization. Consequently, PVSCs with rGO-CysAu had improved average efficiency of  $ 20.59\% $  compared to  $ 18.32\% $  for control cells.

Obtaining high-performance solar cells has become essential for the development of commercial PVSCs. Several fabrication methods have been reported so far, including additive engineering in solution processing to improve perovskite thin-film quality and device performance [7]. Various types of additives have been introduced into precursors, such as (i) Lewis acids/bases: The addition of a Lewis acid or base to a perovskite can form a Lewis adduct through coordinate bonding or ionic bonding. Son et al. doped a series of metal cations into pure and mixed cation/anion perovskite materials to reduce trap densities and diminish the hysteresis effect of the device [13]. Wang et al. introduced caffeine as a molecular lock to interact with lead ions in active layers to improve solar cell performance and thermal stability by increasing activation energy during film crystallization, which induced the preferred orientation with reduced defect density [14]. Zhang et al. used glucose as an additive to improve the performance of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -based perovskite solar cells with good quality of the perovskite films. Additionally, the charge recombination has reduced, and hysteresis has improved.[15] (ii) ammonium salts: Adding excessive ammonium salt derivatives to perovskite could improve PVSC performance and stability with defect passivation and the elongation of the carrier recombination lifetime

[16–18]. (iii) ionic liquids: Ionic liquid additives can control crystallization and passivate defects of perovskite films. Different types and combinations of anions and cations can produce a wide range of functionalized ionic liquids. Bai et al. incorporated 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate (BMIMBF $ _4 $ ) as an additive into triple-cation PVSCs; this enhanced device performance and long-term stability [19]. (iv) carbon-based materials: Carbon-based materials, such as graphene, carbon nanoparticles (NPs), and fullerene derivatives, have attracted increasing attention due to their remarkable properties in optoelectronics. Many research groups [20,21] have revealed the progress in and advantages of carbon-based materials as electrodes, charge transport layers, and additives in perovskite devices. Hadadian et al. investigated the influence of combining two novel materials, namely heteroatom-doped graphene and metal halide perovskite, in the active layer of solar cells, which enhanced the efficiency of PVSCs through crystal modification and surface passivation [22]. Yavari et al. employed carbon NPs containing functional groups in PVSCs to enlarge the perovskite film's grain size and enhance thermal stability [23]. Jiang et al. incorporated a two-dimensional polymeric material, namely graphitic carbon nitride (g-C $ _3 $ N $ _4 $ ), into the perovskite film. The addition of g-C $ _3 $ N $ _4 $  can reduce nucleation and increase carrier mobility in the perovskite layer, increasing efficiency from 16.2% to 19.4% and ensuring high crystal quality [24]. Li et al. used an ultrathin nanosheet of oxo-functionalized graphene/dodecylamine to passivate grain boundaries and inhibit ion migration in perovskites, thus enhancing the stability of perovskite in solar cells [25]. Moreover, nanometal-functionalized graphene, such as silver NP-anchored reduced graphene oxide (Ag-rGO) composites, has been introduced into the perovskite precursor solution. Ag-rGO composites in the active layer reduce ion migration, accelerate charge transport, and improve the thermal stability of PVSCs [26]. Fullerene derivatives have also been used as additives in perovskite; however, the solvents of 2,2',7,7'-Tetrakis[N,N-di(4-methoxyphenyl)amino]-9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD) and [6,6]-phenyl- $ \mathrm{C}_{61} $ -butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM), including chlorobenzene and dichlorobenzene, can adversely affect fullerene derivatives in the perovskite active layer. Therefore, developing a facile sequential process and preventing the washing out of the perovskite film by the solvent used in the upper layer are crucial for additive engineering of perovskite layers. Li et al. reported a novel approach for increasing the mechanical stability of perovskite films, in which a photo crosslinked fullerene network (C-PCBOD) is introduced into the perovskite layer. The incorporation of C-PCBOD resulted in the improved quality of the perovskite film with large grains and superior mechanical stability for fabricating flexible solar cells, which were attributed to passivation at grain boundaries through the formation of a fullerene network including perovskite grains [27,28]. In addition, Chen et al. introduced the element fluorine into CsPbI $ _2 $ Br perovskite solar cells, which in-situ passivated the defects and enlarged the grains of perovskite crystal, and thus a PCE of 15% has reached for all-inorganic perovskite solar cell [29].

Gold NPs (Au NPs) with plasmonic effects have been widely studied in organic photovoltaic research by embedding them in different parts of the device, including the hole transport active layer [30]. However, the use of nanogold in PVSCs has been less investigated. Zhang et al. reported that the addition of nanogold particles and insulating MgO to mesoporous  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  improved efficiency [31]. Bhosale et al. developed graphene oxide (GO) functionalized with Au NPs as a hole extraction layer (HEL) for inverted PVSCs. Au NPs on HEL enabled the rapid extraction of holes, controlling hole transport mobility into the indium tin oxide (ITO) substrate [32]. Xiao et al. demonstrated that the carrier concentration of nickel oxide could be increased by embedding a low concentration of Au NPs with the Ohmic contact nature; thus, the PCE of the solar cells was increased, with all critical parameters improved [33]. Jawad et al. reported that efficiency decreased when an additional layer of Au NPs was used between the HTL and perovskite; this could be related to the low-crystalline structure of perovskite, the layer of Au NPs,

and improper energy level leading to hole transfer competition [34]. Although the advantages of Au NPs on PVSCs have been investigated, Au NPs in the perovskite active layer have rarely been examined. Kim et al. synthesized the Au NP-embedded  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  film and studied how optical properties changed when the two materials were mixed. Their pioneer study verified that the introduction of Au NPs had a positive effect on the optical properties of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $ , with a  $ 4.15\% $  increase in absorbance and a  $ 75.2\% $  increase in photoluminescence (PL) intensity compared with the corresponding values of bare  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  film [35].

Au NPs tend to aggregate. The properties and applications of colloidal Au NPs strongly depend on their size, shape, and uniformity. Many approaches can be used to achieve satisfactory stability of Au NPs, including the adjustment of pH, concentration, temperature, and stabilizer. In our viewpoint, a functional graphene sheet would be an excellent stabilizer to localize Au NPs. In general, functionalized rGO can be produced by reducing GO, but the dispersibility of rGO is less than that of GO, limiting its application. We prepared a novel rGO using Au NP-capped cysteine as a mild reducing agent [36–38]. Cysteine played the vital role of an intercalating agent in graphene sheets, leading to higher exfoliation of rGO and the more favorable capture of Au NPs, resulting in size-controlled and well-distributed nanogold particles without aggregation [39]. Cysteine played the functions for chemical bonding grafting both with the Au NPs and the carboxyl and epoxide functional groups on the surface of GO, resulting in uniform distribution of the cystine modified nanogold particles without aggregation on the surface of rGO. Therefore, the conductive networks of rGO-CysAu could well-distributed in the active layer of perovskite and enhance the carrier transport efficiency, build a promising pathway for carrier movement, reduced the boundary impedance, and reduced recombination, thus increasing the charge collection efficiency and   $  J_{sc}  $  . Here, we fabricated inverted PVSCs based on an ITO/NiO  $  _{x}  $  /MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  :1%-urea:rGO-CysAu/PCBM/BCP/Ag structure system. The presence of rGO-CysAu in the active layer might contribute to uniformly distributed Au NPs/rGO conductive networks in the active layer matrix, enhancing charge transport, reducing internal charge transfer resistance, and increasing the PL quenching effect for enhancement the performance of the device.

# Synthesis of rGO-CysAu

Au NPs were synthesized through the seeding growth method reported in the literature   $  [41,42]  $  . GO was prepared through the oxidation of graphite powder in accordance with the improved Hummers' method   $  [43]  $   and our previous method   $  [44]  $  . To synthesize rGO-CysAu, 20 mL of nanogold particles were modified with 14 mL of 0.1 M cysteine aqueous solution through interactions between gold and thiol groups on cysteine. Next, 12.5 mL of GO (0.08 mg/mL) aqueous suspension was added to the solution of cysteine capped nanogold (CysAu). After stirring for 48 h, the as-obtained powders were suction filtered, washed with distilled water, and freeze-dried.

# Fabrication of PVSCs

# Hole transport layer

Precleaned ITO substrates were treated with   $  O_{2}  $   plasma for 15 min before coating. The   $  NiO_{x}  $   sol–gel precursor was prepared by dissolving the nickel acetylacetonate in ethanol with 1% HCl. Then,   $  NiO_{x}  $   precursor solution was dropped on the clean ITO substrate and spin-coated at 5000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, the   $  NiO_{x}  $   films were annealed at 250°C and 300°C for 30 min and 6 min, respectively.   $  NiO_{x}  $   sol–gel precursor was filtered through a 0.45-  $  \mu  $  m membrane filter before coating. After the HTLs were spin-coated, the substrates were moved to a glove box for perovskite film deposition.

# Preparation of perovskite precursor solution and film

The 1.4 M perovskite precursor with 1% urea was composed of   $  PbI_{2}  $   and   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $   at a molar ratio of 1:1 and was dissolved in a mixture of DMF: DMSO (4:1,v/v). rGO-CysAu (0.5 mg/mL in DMF) was added to the precursor by calculated its final desired concentration. The perovskite precursor solution and the solution containing rGO-CysAu composites were spin-coated on the ITO/HTL substrate at 2000 rpm for 10 s and 4000 rpm for 22 s. In the second spin-coating step, 300   $  \mu  $  L of antisolvent was dripped onto the spinning substrate, followed by thermal annealing at 100 °C for 10 min.

# Preparation of electron transport layer and top electrode

[6,6]-Phenyl-  $ \mathrm{C_{61}} $  -butyric acid methyl ester  $ (\mathrm{PC}_{61}\mathrm{BM}) $  solution (2 wt  $ \% $  in chlorobenzene [CB]) was spin-coated on the ITO/HTL/perovskite substrate at  $ 2000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 30~s $  ; following the deposition of BCP  $ (0.5\mathrm{mg}/ $  mL in isopropanol), spin-coating was performed at  $ 6000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 30~s. $

Finally, a 100-nm silver electrode was thermally evaporated in a vacuum chamber.

# Results and discussion

# Characteristics of rGO-CysAu

In this study, evenly distributed Au NPs without aggregation and rGO with cysteine capped Au NP was prepared, as shown in Fig. 1. During preparation, CTAB served as a surfactant to control NP morphology and prevent aggregation by forming micelles of nanogold particles. We also utilized cysteine binding with Au NPs for the uniform distribution of Au NPs on the graphene surface.

To examine the size of Au NPs and their distribution with or without cysteine, TEM images (Fig. 2) of Au NPs, GO-Au (nanogold particles directly mixed with GO), and rGO-CysAu under 200 K magnification were analyzed. Fig. 2(a) shows the images of CTAB-stabilized nanogold particles prepared through the seeding growth method and displays well-distributed spherical nanogold particles with an average diameter of less than 10 nm. Fig. 2(b) shows the TEM images of GO-Au and displays large clusters of NPs, indicating that severe aggregation occurred during stirring. By contrast, the TEM images of rGO-CysAu illustrated in Fig. 2(c) show the even distribution of nanogold particles with an average diameter of 8.26 nm (Fig. 2(d)), revealing the importance of cysteine for controlling the agglomeration behavior of Au NPs.

The SEM image of rGO-CysAu and EDS mapping results of sulfur, gold, and nitrogen elements are illustrated in Fig. 4(d) and Fig. S2, respectively, showing the deposition of nanogold particles on the surface of rGO with the homogeneous distribution. These SEM results were consistent with the TEM results and are attributed to the high specific surface area and abundant functional groups on GO, which provide numerous binding sites for CysAu.

# Conclusions

In this study, the rGO-CysAu nanocomposite with well-distributed nanogold particles on rGO was prepared through the covalent bonding approach, in which cysteine-modified nanogold was bonded to the functional groups of rGO. Graphene sheets were successfully deposited and exfoliated through amide bonding with Au NP-capped cysteine, as revealed by XPS, FTIR, and XRD. The SEM results, EDS mapping, and TEM results of rGO-CysAu revealed nanogold particles with an average diameter of 8.26 nm on rGO, demonstrating that the stratagem of using cysteine as the chemical bonding driving force could successfully control the deposition uniformity of Au NPs on rGO. Nanogold and rGO had synergistic effects; thus, the Au NPs/rGO composite was applied as conductive networks in the perovskite layer matrix. Inverted PVSCs with ITO/NiO  $  _{x}  $  /MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  :1%-urea:rGO-CysAu/PCBM/BCP/Ag were fabricated. The EDS mapping of sulfur and gold revealed that rGO-CysAu was homogeneously distributed in the entire perovskite layer. The additive of the rGO-CysAu nanocomposite improved charge transfer, which reduced charge recombination and boundary impedance in perovskite thin films. The decay times from the quenching and recombination of charge carriers were longer for PVSCs containing rGO-CysAu, suggesting that the recombination of carriers in the PVSC layer was reduced by rGO-CysAu. Moreover, the optimization of the incorporated rGO-CysAu concentration could increase the perovskite crystal size and grain size. The photovoltaic performance of devices was enhanced in V  $  _{oc}  $  , J  $  _{sc}  $  , EQE and PCE, with high reproducibility and stability. The highest device performance was obtained with the addition of 0.04 mg/mL rGO-CysAu, with a PCE of 20.59%, J  $  _{sc}  $   of 23.89 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  , V  $  _{oc}  $   of 1.09 V, and FF of 79.4%.
