# Synergistic Effect of Crystallization Control and Defect Passivation Induced by a Multifunctional Primidone Additive for High-Performance Perovskite Solar Cells

Nilesh G. Saykar, Muzahir Iqbal, Asim K. Ray, and Santosh K. Mahapatra*

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: The ionic nature of organic–inorganic metal halide perovskites endows intrinsic defects at the surface of the polycrystalline films. Simultaneous defect passivation during the growth of perovskite films could inhibit defect formation to a great extent. Herein, the anticonvulsant drug primidone (PRM) is demonstrated as a novel additive to control the crystallization and defect passivation of perovskites. The spectroscopic measurements support theoretical predictions showing the strong interaction between active functional groups and   $  PbI_{2}  $  . An amount of PRM is tuned to obtain the perfect perovskite films with improved grain size and crystallinity than their control counterparts. Efficient   $  Pb_{1}  $   antisite defect passivation suppresses the non-radiative recombinations, resulting in higher luminance intensity and significantly longer charge carrier lifetimes. The PRM-modified perovskite solar cells (PSCs) show a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 18.73%, much

higher than that of control PSCs (16.62%). The ambient stability of PRM-modified PSCs is meritoriously increased compared to control PSCs. The PRM-modified PSCs show stability retention of up to 85% of the initial PCE after 1000 h, while control PSCs retain only 25% of the initial PCE after 550 h. The multifunctional defect passivation approach with the PRM additive shows the effective way for the efficiency and stability improvement of PSCs.

# 1. INTRODUCTION

The excessive use of non-renewable energy resources to meet the growing energy demand will soon empty these resources. To secure the energy needs of future generations, limited use of non-renewable energy resources and the search for possible renewable energy resources are highly desirable. The combined efforts of the scientific community are essential to developing efficient techniques to produce energy from renewable sources, such as solar, geothermal, and wind.  $  ^{1,2}  $   Meantime, water splitting emerges as a promising technology to produce   $  H_{2}  $  , which can produce sustainable and clean energy.  $  ^{3,4}  $   Along with producing the energy, its efficient storage is also desired. In this view, storage devices, like supercapacitors and batteries, are being developed.  $  ^{5,6}  $

The abundance of solar energy on the Earth makes it a highly attractive and promising resource for energy production. The intriguing solar cells represent the efficient technology to convert solar light into electricity. An already-developed thin-film technology drives the rapid progress of solar cells. The enormous growth in the efficiency of the organic-inorganic lead halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has attracted the attention of the entire scientific research community.  $  ^{7}  $   However, lead toxicity poses environmental hazards, requiring

The prototypical perovskite, methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ), possesses 12 types of point defects, which are categorized as vacancies, interstitials, and antisite substitutions, out of which V  $  _{I}  $  , V  $  _{Pb}  $  , and Pb–I antisite (Pb  $  _{I}  $   and I  $  _{Pb}  $  ) point defects are the most vulnerable defects because the band edges of perovskites are mainly composed of Pb and I orbitals.  $  ^{23,25-29}  $   Perhaps, among these point defects, V  $  _{I}  $   and Pb  $  _{I}  $   antisite defects may readily form on the perovskite surface as a result of their low formation energy, viz., 0.51 and 0.57 eV, respectively, requiring a robust way for their mitigation.  $  ^{30}  $

The simple and cost-effective solution processing approach is widely utilized to prepare PSCs. However, perovskite thin films prepared by the solution process contain small grains accompanying huge GBs and pinholes as a result of the uncontrolled crystallization. It is also evident that most of the point defects are accumulated at the surface and GBs.  $  ^{22,26}  $   Recently, additive engineering is emerging as the potential method for defect passivation of perovskite as a result of the various advantages, such as ease of processing and unique interaction mechanism producing perovskite films with minimal defects.  $  ^{31-33}  $   Along with defect passivation, the perovskite nucleation and growth mechanism governed by Lewis acid–base chemistry of   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ions and the additive molecule were found to modulate the crystallization rate and, consequently, the quality of the perovskite films.  $  ^{34-37}  $   Because the dual functions of crystallization control and simultaneous defect passivation are possible only with additive engineering, the interaction between the rationally designed molecules and perovskite precursors needs to be evaluated for the successful implication.

A molecule like theophylline containing the carbonyl group and N–H functional moieties can form a coordinate bond with antisite Pb, while N–H forms a hydrogen bond with iodine, passivating the Pb–I antisite defects.  $  ^{30}  $   Because choline chloride contains positive and negative charges, it can effectively passivate the   $  I_{Pb}  $   antisite and under-coordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   defects.  $  ^{38}  $   Zhang et al. demonstrated a universal surface defect passivator N-phenylglycine for PSCs, which can passivate all typical surface defects, like cation vacancy, iodine vacancy, Pb vacancy, and Pb–I antisite defects with N-phenylglycine only.  $  ^{39}  $   Likewise, researchers are searching for a multifunctional molecule to mitigate typical Pb-related defects. Additionally, the synergistic effect of simultaneous crystallization control and defect passivation on the device performance is still not apparent, requiring more insights into the role of the multifunctional molecule.

Herein, we demonstrate primidone (PRM) as a novel additive molecule for   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   PSCs. The various chemical groups, like N–H, C=O, benzene ring, and C–H, present in PRM possess the ability to interact and passivate the typical surface defects of perovskites. The density functional theory (DFT) calculation reveals that the low interaction energy of PRM and   $  PbI_{2}  $   can form an intermediate product. During the perovskite annealing, an intermediate product may slow the nucleation of perovskite, which eventually retards the grain growth. Meanwhile, the multiple functional groups of PRM passivate the Pb–I antisite defects via coordinate and hydrogen bonding. The fine-tuned crystallization and grain size of PRM-modified perovskite enable highly efficient and stable PSCs.

The PRM-modified PSCs deliver a PCE of 18.73%, while the control PSCs only give a PCE of 16.62%. We also found that hysteresis in photocurrent density versus voltage   $  (J-V)  $   profiles is significantly reduced. The PRM-modified PSCs show 85% retention of initial PCE after 1000 h, whereas the control PSCs show a 25% retention of initial PCE after 550 h. This work presents PRM as an effective additive to modulate crystallization and simultaneous defect passivation, which may push the PSCs toward commercial applications.

Further, the interaction between PRM and   $  PbI_{2}  $   has been evaluated by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy measurement. For this purpose, we have prepared an adduct of MAI:  $  PbI_{2}  $ :PRM. The stretching vibration modes of the amide C=O band split into several peaks, as shown in the FTIR spectra of PRM (Figure 1f).  $  ^{42}  $   The peaks corresponding to the C=O function group of PRM observed at 1666 and 1710 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   shift to a lower wavenumber, i.e., 1641 and 1706 cm  $  ^{-1}  $ , in the case of the adduct. The substantial shift in vibration modes of both functional groups indicated that PRM strongly interacts with   $  PbI_{2}  $ , forming an intermediate product that can help to control the crystallization of perovskite.  $  ^{43,44}  $

The defect passivation of perovskite by the PRM molecule has been assessed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The core-level spectra of Pb 4f and I 3d are shown in panels a and b of Figure 2, while the XPS survey spectrum is shown in Figure SI 2 of the Supporting Information. The typical doublet of Pb 4f is observed in XPS spectra of both control perovskite and PRM–perovskite thin films. The peaks corresponding to Pb 4f $  _{7/2}  $  and Pb 4f $  _{5/2}  $  at binding energy (BE) values of 137.75 and 142.65 eV are observed in control perovskite, showing the shift to BE values of 137.65 and 142.55 eV in the case of PRM–perovskite thin films. The shift to the lower BE values reveals that C=O and uncoordinated Pb $  ^{2+}  $  may undergo a coordinate bonding. Further, two small peaks related to Pb $  ^{0}  $  defects are observed at BE values of 136.25 and 140.8 eV in XPS spectra of control perovskite. Upon the incorporation of PRM, peaks related to Pb $  ^{0}  $  are not observed, which may suggest that PRM prevents the formation of Pb $  ^{0}  $  cluster defects. Similarly, the peaks related to I 3d $  _{5/2}  $  and I 3d $  _{3/2}  $  show the shift in the peak position to lower BE values in PRM–perovskite thin films, predicting hydrogen bonding between the N–H functional group and iodine atom. Relatively higher intensity of the O 1s peak in XPS spectra of the PRM–perovskite thin films than in control perovskite thin films confirms the presence of PRM on the surface of PRM–perovskite thin films (Figure 2c).

To precisely quantify the effect of the PRM additive on the PSC performance, we need to measure the trap density. The space-charge-limited current measurement of the electron-only device prepared from the FTO/perovskite/Au configuration allows us the estimation of trap density. The current–voltage   $  (I-V)  $   plot of the electron-only devices containing control and 2 mg of PRM–perovskite is shown in Figure 5e. It is observed that, after a specific value of potential, the current suddenly starts to rise in both devices; that voltage is called the trap-filled limit voltage   $  (V_{\mathrm{TFL}})  $  . The resultant value of   $  V_{TFL}  $   with the relation between trap density and voltage   $  N_{t} = (2\varepsilon\varepsilon_{0}V_{\mathrm{TFL}})/(qL^{2})  $   is used to estimate trap density,  $  ^{51}  $   where   $  \varepsilon  $   and L are the relative dielectric constant and thickness of the perovskite, respectively,   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   and q are the vacuum permittivity of free space and elementary charge, respectively, and   $  V_{TFL}  $   is the trap-filled limit voltage. We found that control PSCs have a trap density of   $  7 \times 10^{13} cm^{-3}  $  , which is reduced to   $  4.12 \times 10^{15} cm^{-3}  $   in the case of 2 mg of PRM–PSCs. The reduction in trap density can be ascribed to the growth of perfect perovskite thin films and defect passivation introduced by the PRM additive.

We performed the EIS measurements of control and 2 mg of PRM–PSCs, and representative spectra are shown in Figure 5f. Furthermore, the equivalent circuit model (Figure SI 9 of the Supporting Information) is used to extract the parameters from EIS spectra. The detailed parameters are summarized in Table SI 2 of the Supporting Information. The charge transfer resistance (  $  R_{tr}  $  ) is found to be lower for 2 mg of PRM–PSC than for control PSC. Another critical parameter obtained from equivalent circuit fitting is the charge recombination resistance (  $  R_{rec}  $  ) for both PSCs. The PRM–PSC shows a higher   $  R_{rec}  $   than control PSCs. The additive PRM-induced growth of high-quality perovskite films bearing fewer defects strongly reduces the charge recombination compared to control films.

# 3. CONCLUSION

Along with the extraordinary properties, metal halide perovskites endow the defect states, which pull down perovskites from being a superior candidate for photovoltaics. At a glance, defect passivation is the only way to accomplish the desired performance of PSCs. This work demonstrates the anticonvulsant drug PRM as an efficient crystallization modulator and defect passivating agent. Balanced charge density distribution and higher interaction energy of PRM are behind the more vital interaction between PRM and   $  PbI_{2}  $  . Primarily, this interaction leads to intermediate product formation, which may reduce the crystallization rate of perovskite. The PRM-mediated slow crystallization forces perovskite to grow in bigger grains with fewer GBs. Subsequently, PRM molecules anchor at the site of the defect by forming coordinate and hydrogen bonding. The ultimate reduction in defects at GBs, the surface, and within perovskite forms the perfect perovskite thin films for photovoltaic applications. An appropriate amount of PRM is tuned to obtain the maximum output from PSCs. The PRM-modified PSC delivers 18.73% PCE, whereas the control PSC gives a PCE of 16.62%. PRM-induced crystallization control and defect passivation of perovskite thin films result in a dramatic rise in PCE and stability of PSCs.

# 4. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

4.1. Solar Cell Fabrication. The transparent conducting oxide, namely, fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) having a resistivity of   $  10 \Omega cm^{-1}  $  , was patterned with zinc powder and 2 M HCl solution. Patterned FTO was washed successively with soap solution, water, acetone, and 2-propanol, followed by blow drying with argon gas. Crystalline   $  TiO_{2}  $   (  $  cTiO_{2}  $  ) was spin-coated on patterned FTO from 0.15 and 0.3 M ethanolic solution of titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetylacetonate) at 2500 and 400 rpm for 20 s, respectively. After the coating, FTO substrates were heated in air at   $  125^{\circ}C  $   for 5 min and subsequently at   $  500^{\circ}C  $   for 30 min. Further, mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   (  $  mTiO_{2}  $  ) was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 20 s from the diluted titania paste in ethanol (150 mg/mL). The heating protocol for   $  mTiO_{2}  $   was the same as that for   $  cTiO_{2}  $  . Then,   $  mTiO_{2}  $  -coated FTO substrates were transferred to a   $  N_{2}  $   glovebox for perovskite deposition. The perovskite precursor solution comprising 159 mg of methylammonium iodide (MAI) and 461 mg of   $  PbI_{2}  $   was prepared in   $  700 \mu L  $   mixed solvents of N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and DMSO (9:1, v/v). The perovskite precursor solution was coated on the   $  mTiO_{2}  $   substrate at 4000 rpm for 25 s. The antisolvent toluene was dripped immediately after 12 s on the spinning substrate. After completion of the program, the substrate was transferred to the preheated hot plate at a temperature of   $  60^{\circ}C  $  . The substrate was heated at 60 and   $  100^{\circ}C  $   for 3 and 7 min, respectively. In the case of the PRM additive method, PRM amounts, viz., 1, 2, and 3 mg, were added directly to the   $  700 \mu L  $   mixed solvents of DMF and DMSO containing perovskite precursors and dissolved completely to form a clear solution. Subsequently, hole transport layer (HTL) solution was made in 1 mL of chlorobenzene using 72.3 mg of spiro-MeOTAD, 28.8 μL of 4-tert-butylpyridine, and 17.5 μL of LiTFSI (520 mg/mL acetonitrile). HTL solution (30 μL) was spin-coated on the perovskite film at 3500 rpm for 20 s. Finally, 80 nm Au contact was thermally deposited through a shadow mask on the spiro-MeOTAD HTL layer. The active area of the PSCs was set to be   $  0.08 cm^{2}  $  . To check the reproducibility of the proposed additive

system, we prepared a large number of samples, and the devices with high reproducibility are reported.
