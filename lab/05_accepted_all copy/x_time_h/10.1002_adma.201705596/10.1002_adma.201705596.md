# Efficient, Hysteresis-Free, and Stable Perovskite Solar Cells with ZnO as Electron-Transport Layer: Effect of Surface Passivation

Jing Cao, Binghui Wu, Ruihao Chen, Youyunqi Wu, Yong Hui, Bing-Wei Mao, and Nanfeng Zheng*

The power conversion efficiency of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has ascended from  $ 3.8\% $  to  $ 22.1\% $  in recent years. ZnO has been well-documented as an excellent electron-transport material. However, the poor chemical compatibility between ZnO and organo-metal halide perovskite makes it highly challenging to obtain highly efficient and stable PSCs using ZnO as the electron-transport layer. It is demonstrated in this work that the surface passivation of ZnO by a thin layer of MgO and protonated ethanolamine (EA) readily makes ZnO as a very promising electron-transporting material for creating hysteresis-free, efficient, and stable PSCs. Systematic studies in this work reveal several important roles of the modification: (i) MgO inhibits the interfacial charge recombination, and thus enhances cell performance and stability; (ii) the protonated EA promotes the effective electron transport from perovskite to ZnO, further fully eliminating PSCs hysteresis; (iii) the modification makes ZnO compatible with perovskite, nicely resolving the instability of ZnO/perovskite interface. With all these findings, PSCs with the best efficiency up to  $ 21.1\% $  and no hysteresis are successfully fabricated. PSCs stable in air for more than  $ 300\mathrm{h} $  are achieved when graphene is used to further encapsulate the cells.

ZnO has relatively broad bandgap and its electron mobility is several orders of magnitude higher than that of commonly used   $  TiO_{2}  $  .  $  ^{[22-28]}  $   The exceptional electronic mobility can accelerate the photogenerated electron transport and hence achieve the fabrication of hysteresis-less PSCs. However, the two following issues need to be addressed before ZnO can compete with   $  TiO_{2}  $   as an effective ETL for the fabrication of efficient and stable PSCs. The first issue is the severe charge recombination at the ZnO/perovskite interface, which is deleterious to the cells' efficiency. The second issue is the poor chemical stability of ZnO/perovskite interface. It has been reported that when the temperature is higher than 90 °C, the methylammonium (MA  $  ^{+}  $  ) cations in perovskite film is easily deprotonated by ZnO to release methylamine, decomposing the perovskite.  $  ^{[29]}  $   Hence, seeking for a suitable and simple method to passivate ZnO surface is paramount to improve both the efficiency and stability of PSCs with ZnO as ETL while eliminating the hysteresis.

Experimentally, in order to modify the surface of ZnO with MgO–EA  $  ^{+}  $  , the ZnO compact layer was first prepared by the reported method.  $  ^{[23]}  $   The as-obtained substrate was immersed in 2-methoxyethanol solution of magnesium acetate (MgAc  $  _{2}  $  ) and EA, and annealed at 450 °C for 30 min. The coexistence of Zn and Mg elements was confirmed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) mapping (Figure 1b) and spectrum (Figure S1, Supporting Information). As expected, the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns indicated that thermal annealing led to the formation of ZnO together with MgO (Figure 1c). The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) (Figure S2, Supporting Information) analyses suggested the existence of organic species when the annealing temperature was below 450 °C. The organic species were further identified to be EA by the temperature-programmed decomposition/mass spectrometry (TPD-MS) analysis based on

the release of the fragment peaks for EA at the temperature from 410 to 550 °C (Figure 1d). The actual electronic state of EA was then determined by IR spectra. As shown in Figure 1e,f, compared to the spectrum of neutral EA molecule, the disappearance of -OH absorption peaks at 3687 and 1382 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  for the modified sample suggested the removal of the proton on -OH. While the new forming absorption peak at 1509 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  suggested the transform of -NH  $  _{2}  $  to -NH  $  _{3}  $   $  ^{+}  $  after the modification process. The presence of protonated EA on the modified surface was also verified by the zeta-potential measurements, which revealed that the surface charge was changed from negative to positive (Table S1, Supporting Information). All these results confirmed the successful modification of ZnO by MgO and protonated EA.

As expected and illustrated in Figure 2b,c and Table S2 (Supporting Information), the overall performance of PSCs was significantly enhanced after the modification. The best cell efficiency of the modified device reached 18.3%, much higher than the best of ZnO-based devices, 15.1%. Especially, no hysteresis was observed on the modified devices. Measurements over 30 fabricated cells with ZnO–MgO–EA  $  ^{+}  $  gave average efficiencies of 17.2% ± 1.2% for the reverse scan (RS) and 17.1% ± 1.3% for the forward scan (FS). While the effectiveness of the modification was certified, it is essential to understand the mechanism behind the enhancement of efficiency and stability. There are two possible reasons for the enhanced performances for PSCs with ZnO–MgO–EA  $  ^{+}  $  : (i) The modification resulted in the change in morphologies of the perovskite films. (ii) The interfacial optoelectronic properties between ETL and perovskite were optimized by the modification. As shown in the scanning electron microscope (SEM) images (Figures S4 and S5, Supporting Information), the morphologies of the polycrystalline perovskite films before and after MgO–EA  $  ^{+}  $   modification were almost identical to each other. It is thus reasonable to anticipate that the improvements in device performances were mainly attributed to the enhanced interfacial optoelectronic properties. To validate the dependence of PSCs' performances on the interfacial changes, the work functions of ZnO with and without treatment were measured by scanning Kelvin probe microscopy (SKPM) to investigate the influence of work function variation caused by interfacial chemical interactions. As shown in Figures S6–S8 of the Supporting Information, after the surface modification, the surface work function decreased from its original value of 4.43 to 4.18 eV. According to the energy level alignment theory, the lowered work function after the modification matched the energy level of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   emissive layer better than the unmodified ZnO, thereby promoting efficient electron injection at the interface of ETL and perovskite. Moreover, the enhanced transmission of the treated film also makes it more suitable as an ETL (Figure S9a, Supporting Information).

To further understand how the modification altered the interfacial interaction between perovskite and ETL, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed on PSC devices with bare ZnO and ZnO–MgO–EA  $  ^{+}  $  under 1 sun light illumination conditions. In the measurements, the applied DC voltage (  $  V_{app}  $  ) was varied between 0 and 0.9 V and the frequency of the AC perturbation were ranged from 50 Hz to 1000 kHz. Figure S10 of the Supporting Information shows the Nyquist plots of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  -PSCs with and without MgO–EA  $  ^{+}  $  treatment. In the high frequency region, the response of the transport properties was observed. Two arcs can be identified at low forward bias. The arc at lower frequency is related to the charge recombination and the higher frequency one to the charge transfer at the interfaces of ETL/MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  /Spiro-OMeTAD configured devices.  $  ^{[42-44]}  $  For the device with ZnO–MgO–EA  $  ^{+}  $  as ETL, while the transfer resistance (  $  R_{tr}  $  ) decreases significantly, the recombination resistance (  $  R_{rec}  $  ) increases remarkably. On the other hand, no considerable difference was found in the electron mobility of ZnO and ZnO–MgO–EA  $  ^{+}  $  (Figure S9b, Supporting Information). Consequently, we conclude that such an interfacial treatment effectively modified the properties of the interface between ETL and perovskite interlayers with enhanced electron extraction and transfer, as well as reduced recombination of photogenerated

charges at the interface. Because of these improvements, PSC devices based on ZnO–MgO–EA  $  ^{+}  $  displayed no hysteresis in their current–voltage characteristics with much improved stability.

As demonstrated above, the application of ZnO layer treated by MgO and protonated EA enhanced electron extraction and transportation to the cathode, thereby facilitates the fabrication of highly efficient perovskite optoelectronic devices. It still needs to distinguish where the overall improved performance originated from, the MgO component, the protonated EA, or both. To address this question, we prepared ZnO–MgO layer (Figure 3a) to investigate the effect of MgO. In brief, the as-prepared ZnO layer was immersed into 2-methoxyethanol solution of   $  MgAc_{2}  $   and EA, followed by annealing at 550 °C for 30 min to completely remove all organic species. The XRD patterns (Figure 1c), IR spectra (Figure S11, Supporting Information), and TPD-MS spectra (Figure 1d) evidenced the presence of MgO-only on ZnO surface. After the MgO modification, the surface work function was found decreased from its original value of 4.43 to 4.28 eV (Figures S6, S8, and S12, Supporting Information), closer to the energy level of the   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   active layer than the original unmodified ZnO. The slightly improved transmission would somewhat improve the optical absorption efficiency (Figure S13a, Supporting Information). As expected, the performance measurement revealed that it was the MgO coating that improved the efficiency, from 15.1% to 17.4% (Figure 3c; Figure S14 and Table S2, Supporting Information), and stability for the best cell (Figure 3d). This improved stability indicated that the MgO coating provided a surface passivation on ZnO in MA-based PSCs. However, there still existed severe hysteresis similar to the devices without MgO coating (Figure 3c). The increased   $  R_{rec}  $   at the interface of ETL and perovskite for the MgO treated cells (Figure S15, Supporting Information) indicated that the insertion of an MgO layer strongly promoted the performance of PSCs by diminishing the interfacial charge recombination. Similar to the   $  MgO-EA^{+}  $  modified material, the morphologies of ZnO–MgO-modified perovskite films remained almost identical to the ZnO-based perovskite films (Figures S16 and S17, Supporting Information), while no considerable difference in electron mobility was found (Figure S13b, Supporting Information). Hence, we conclude that the strengthened interfacial interactions were the main reason for the performance improvements of PSC devices with ZnO–MgO–EA  $  ^{+}  $  .

In conclusion, we have demonstrated an effective surface passivation method to enhance the performance and stability of PSCs by modifying ZnO-based ETLs with MgO and protonated EA to tune the chemical interactions at the ZnO/perovskite interface. The introduction of MgO on the surface of ZnO reduced the electron recombination at the interface, thus improving device performance and stability. Delicate control over the interfacial chemical interactions by introducing protonated EA reduced the contact barrier, and improved electron injection and extraction. Therefore, the accumulated charges at the interface were easily extracted, thus fully eliminating the hysteresis. The conversion efficiency of modified ZnO–MgO–EA  $  ^{+}  $  PSCs was readily improved up to 21.1% by employing mesoscopic structure and doping the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  with Cs and FA. Furthermore, encapsulating the highly efficient PSCs with graphene remarkably improved the long-term stability of the PSCs against humidity and heat by creating a fully hydrophobic and heat conducting layer over the PSC devices. Such an integrated research on surface and interface engineering provides an effective approach to improve the performance and long-term stability of perovskite-based optoelectronic devices.

# Experimental Section

Materials: All chemicals and reagents were used as received from chemical companies without any further purification. The methylammonium iodide (MAI) was synthesized and purified according to the literature method.  $  ^{[2]}  $   To a stirring solution of methylamine in methanol (40 wt%, 24 mL) aqueous hydroiodic acid (57 wt%, 25 mL) was slowly added at 0 °C. After 2 h, the precipitate was collected by evaporation at 50 °C for 1 h. The as-obtained product was washed with diethyl ether three times and then finally dried at 60 °C in a vacuum oven for 24 h to afford the desired pure MAI as white crystals.

Solar Cell Fabrication: FTO glass substrates in the dimension of 2.0 cm × 2.0 cm were patterned by etching with zinc powder and 2 M hydrochloric acid. The substrates were then sequentially washed in ultrasonic baths of acetone, distilled water, and ethanol. The ZnO compact layer was first prepared by the reported method.  $  ^{[23]}  $   The precursor solution of magnesium acetate tetrahydrate (0.1 M) and ethanolamine (0.01 M) in 2-methoxyethanol was spin-coated on as-prepared ZnO substrate. The substrate was then annealed at 450 °C for 30 min to obtain the ZnO–MgO–EA  $  ^{+}  $  film, while the annealing temperature at 550 °C produced the ZnO–MgO film. For the ZnO–Mg–EA  $  ^{+}  $  film, the as-prepared ZnO substrate was then immersed in CHCl  $  _{3}  $  solution of [Mg(EA)  $  _{2}  $ ] at 60 °C for 10 min and then dried at room temperature. For the mesoporous PSCs with ZnO–MgO–EA  $  ^{+}  $  film as ETL, a ≈150 nm thick mesoporous TiO  $  _{2}  $  film was deposited on the pretreated FTO substrate by spin-coating of the TiO  $  _{2}  $  paste (Dyesol DSL 18NR-T) with ethanol (1:5, mass ratio), which was followed by heating at 450 °C for 30 min.

The  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite layers were fabricated by reacting mesoporous  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  with MAI, as reported in the previous work.[48] A mixture of  $ 461~\mathrm{mg} $  of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and  $ 78~\mathrm{mg} $  of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (molar ratio 1:1) dissolved in  $ 600~\mathrm{mg} $  of  $ N,N $ -dimethylformamide (DMF) was spin-coated on the prepared FTO substrate at  $ 3000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 25~s $ . The films were annealed at  $ 80^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 5\mathrm{min} $  to prepare the mesoporous  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  film. After cooling to room temperature, the prepared mesoporous  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  film was dipped into the hot solution ( $ 70^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ ) of MAI in 2-propanol ( $ 10~\mathrm{mg~mL^{-1}} $ ) for  $ 1\mathrm{min} $ . After that, the film was immersed into isopropanol solution about  $ 30~s $  to remove the excess I-rich ionic compound and then heated at  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 5\mathrm{min} $ . The (CsFAMA)  $ \mathrm{Pb(BrI)_3} $  perovskite precursor solution prepared by the reported ratio[45] was spin-coated on the prepared FTO substrate at  $ 4000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 25~s $ , when  $ 0.5~\mathrm{mL} $  of diethyl ether was slowly dripped on the rotating substrate in  $ 8~s $ . The obtained transparent film was then heated at  $ 120^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 5\mathrm{min} $  to form a dense  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  film.

After the preparation of perovskite layer, the hole trans-port layer solution was coated by solution process at 4000 rpm for 30 s, where Spiro-OMeTAD/chlorobenzene (72 mg/l mL) solution was employed with the additives containing 17.5   $  \mu  $  L Li-TFSI/acetonitrile (520 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) and 28.8   $  \mu  $  L 4-tert-butylpyridine (TBP). Finally, an 80 nm thick Au counter electrode was deposited by thermal evaporation under reduced pressure of   $  2 \times 10^{-7}  $   Torr. The active area was 0.11 cm  $  ^{2}  $  .

Device Characterization: The current–voltage characteristics were recorded at a solar simulator (with an aperture of   $  40 \times 40 \, cm^2  $  ) equipped with a Keithley 2400 source meter and 300 W collimated Xenon lamp (Newport) calibrated with the light intensity to 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $   under simulated AM 1.5G illumination by a certified silicon solar cell. Incident photon-to-electron conversion efficiency (IPCE) was measured on a computer-controlled IPCE system (Newport) containing a Xenon lamp, a monochromator, and a Keithley multimeter. The system was calibrated with the certified silicon solar cell and the IPCE data were collected at DC mode. The TPD-MS measurements were carried out in an in situ pyrolysis TPD-MS apparatus. The samples were heated from room temperature to 700 °C with a heating rate of 5 °C min  $  ^{-1}  $  , while the mass spectra and sample temperature were recorded. Each spectrum is an accumulation of 1000 spectra gathered at interval of 100 μs. The typical pressure of the pyrolytic furnace was kept at   $  2 \times 10^{-4}  $   Pa to prevent secondary reactions. Before the test, the sample was dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 24 h. XRD patterns were analyzed by an X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku, RINT-2500) with a Cu Kα radiation source. The surface morphologies were recorded on an SEM-4800 field-emission SEM. The IR spectra were

measured on an FTIR spectroscope (IR200, Thermo Nicolet, USA). The EIS measurements were carried out on a CHI 760E electrochemical workstation (Chenhua Instrument Company, Shanghai, China). Electron-transporting properties of the ETL materials were carried out using a space-charge-limited current model with electron-only devices (FTO/ETL/PC  $  _{71}  $  BM/Au). J-V characteristics were recorded in the dark by a Keithley 2440 source measurement unit. The electron mobilities were extracted by fitting the J-V curves according to the reported method.  $  ^{[49]}  $  Work function measurements were performed in air by SKPM with an atomic force microscope (AFM) (Agilent SPM 5500) equipped with a MAC III controller (three lock-in amplifiers) using a Pt/Ir-coated Si tip (FMG01/Pt, NT-MDT). A single-pass mode with amplitude modulation was employed and a Lock-in 1 amplifier was used to drive the cantilever to get topographic images. Lock-in 2 provided an AC tip bias, and a DC bias was also provided by an internal servo mechanism to counter the electrostatic force on the tip for SKPM measurement. Contact potential differences (CPDs) were recorded, which correlated with the work functions of the tip and sample by the formula  $  V_{\mathrm{CPD}} = (\Phi_{\mathrm{tip}} - \Phi_{\mathrm{sample}})/e  $ . Then the absolute work functions of the samples were estimated after calibration of the work function of the tip by using a highly oriented pyrolytic graphite sample in ambient conditions as a reference.
