# Abstract

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have become the front-running photovoltaic technology and have triggered enormous research interest worldwide owing to their ultra-high solar-to-electric power conversion efficiency and low fabrication costs, but their poor intrinsic stability issues still constitute the main obstacles that hinder their rapid commercialization. Herein, we demonstrate that, by carefully designing the modifier's molecular structure, a facile, highly reproducible and scalable surface modification strategy can effectively enhance both the stability and efficiency of the inverted PSCs. The most efficient modifier (2-Acetylpyridine) can not only passivate the surface traps of perovskite film but also enhance its stability against moisture by

forming a hydrophobic capping layer on top. Accordingly, the efficiency of the inverted PSC has been improved from 16.75% to 20.05% for the best-performing one, which is, to our knowledge, among the highest values for inverted MA-free PSCs. # Introduction

The state-of-the-art perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have achieved an impressive power conversion efficiency ( $ PCE $ ) of over 24% $  ^{1}  $ , rivaling other established photovoltaic technologies, such as multi-crystalline silicon solar cells, copper indium gallium selenide and cadmium telluride thin-film solar cells. Coupled with their low production costs and all-earth-abundant compositions $  ^{2-4}  $ , PSCs hold great promise for realizing industrialization in the future. However, it's still difficult to achieve high efficiency and high stability simultaneously $  ^{5-7}  $ , and that remains a crucial obstacle to rapid commercialization.

A critical factor in the durability of PSCs arises from the inherent structural and chemical instability of the perovskite absorbers  $  ^{8}  $  , which govern the light harvesting and charge generation. It has been suggested that perovskite absorbers with   $  MA^{+}  $  cations would lack thermal stability  $  ^{9}  $   because they would decompose to gaseous methylamine at temperatures as low as 85 °C. Replacing the   $  MA^{+}  $  by   $  FA^{+}  $  can enable a higher theoretical efficiency limit and decomposition temperature.  $  ^{10}  $   However, PSCs based on   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   absorbers normally exhibit abrupt suppression of photovoltaic

Page 3 of 37

Journal of Materials Chemistry A

performance as the FAPbI $ _3 $  undergoes a phase transition from the photo-active cubic phase to the photo-inactive hexagonal phase at operating temperature. $ ^{11-12} $  To combat this adverse phase transition in FAPbI $ _3 $ , partial substitution of FA $ ^+ $  with nonvolatile Cs $ ^+ $ /Rb $ ^+ $  and/or I $ ^- $  with Br $ ^- $  have proven effective. $ ^{13} $  In particular, the complex perovskite crystals of FA $ _{1-x} $ Cs $ _x $ PbI $ _y $ Br $ _{3-y} $  (hereafter, denoted as FACs) show excellent phase stability and desirable bandgaps. $ ^{14} $  However, these kinds of MA-free perovskite devices with high stability usually exhibit an inadequate efficiency, with few reports over 20%. $ ^{15} $  One of the main obstacles restricting the efficiency of the MA-free devices is their lower open-circuit photovoltage ( $ V_{oc} $ ) compared to that of the MA-contained PSCs.

The loss of   $  V_{oc}  $   can be induced by non-radiative recombination, which is closely related to the surface and grain boundary (GB) defects in perovskite.  $  ^{16-17}  $   Surface modification was proposed as one effective way to passivate these defects and thus improve the   $  V_{oc}  $   of the corresponding devices.  $  ^{18-20}  $   For example, phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM), as an outstanding electron transporting material (ETM), was reported to effectively passivate halide-induced deep traps such as the negatively-charged Pb-I anti-site defects   $  PbI_{3}^{-}  $  or under-coordinated halide ions in PSCs;  $  ^{21-23}  $   a combination of thiophene and pyridine was found capable of binding to positively charged under-coordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ions in perovskite films;  $  ^{24}  $   supramolecular halogen bond complexation was discovered to passivate the under-coordinated iodine ions in PSCs;  $  ^{25}  $   quaternary ammonium halide anions and cations were used to reduce the trap density and prolong the carrier lifetime for perovskite;  $  ^{26}  $   polymer-like Teflon

Journal of Materials Chemistry A

Page 4 of 37

or poly(3-hexylthiophene) (P3HT) was deposited as a hydrophobic passivation layer on top of perovskite film;  $  ^{27-29}  $   4-dimethylaminobenzoic acid (4-DMABA) was introduced to eliminate interfacial recombination within the perovskite layer.  $  ^{30}  $   Moreover, a proper surface modifier can form a compact thin layer to help enhance the corresponding PSCs' stability at the same time.  $  ^{31-32}  $   Therefore, among various routes implemented for enhancing the efficiency and/or stability of PSCs, the surface modification strategy was generally accepted as an ideal choice in that it can kill two birds with one stone. However, the influence of the surface modifier's structure on the perovskite layer has not been thoroughly classified hitherto.

Herein, we selected three kinds of pyridine derivatives, with ethanoyl substituents in different positions (o-substituted, meta-substituted, or para-substituted, hereafter denoted as 2-3- or 4-Acetylpyridine, respectively) as the surface modifiers to treat the FACs perovskites. The molecular-structure-dependent effects on the as-modified perovskite films, such as trap passivation and moisture resistance, have been demonstrated. As a result, the corresponding PSCs' efficiencies and stabilities have been simultaneously enhanced. In addition, the surface-modification strategy involved in this work features high technical scalability and reproducibility.

# Results and discussion

The processes of our surface modification strategy are illustrated in Figure 1a. The FACs perovskite films were prepared by the classic anti-solvent extraction method.  $  ^{33}  $   After annealing at 150 °C for 30 min, the perovskite films were immersed into a surface modification solution (different surface modifiers with controlled

concentrations in the perovskite antisolvent diethyl ether) bath for a 10 min. Lastly, the modified films were further annealed at 150 °C for 10 min.

Pyridine was reportedly used as a Lewis base to heal defects of perovskite film,  $  ^{34}  $  which could effectively prolong the carrier lifetime and promote the efficiency of the as-treated film and device. However, pyridine cannot be simply used as the surface modifier since its high polarity can quickly dissolve halide perovskites.  $  ^{35}  $  Based on these considerations, three kinds of pyridine derivatives with ethanoyl substituents in different positions (Figure 1b) were selected as surface modifiers in this work, with the aim of preventing their destructive dissolution effect and tuning their binding modes to perovskite films.

To verify the dissolution effect of pyridine derivatives on perovskites, one drop of each of their pure solutions was dripped on the surfaces of perovskite films. Conspicuous differences between these pyridine derivatives have been observed from the optical images shown in Figure S1 (Supporting Information). For the pure solutions of 3-Acetylpyridine and 4-Acetylpyridine, instantaneous dissolution of the perovskite films occurred; however, for the 2-Acetylpyridine pure solution, there is no obvious change of the perovskite film. To avoid the dissolution effect of the 3-Acetylpyridine and 4-Acetylpyridine, we diluted them in the perovskite anti-solvent diethyl ether at different volume ratios. As seen from the optical images in Figure S1, it is found that 1/1000-diluted 3-Acetylpyridine and 1/10000-diluted 4-Acetylpyridine could safely interact with the perovskites without destroying them. These should be ascribed to the steric hindrance provided by the abundant presence of diethyl ether

molecules in the solutions, which largely decreased the opportunity of the 3-Acetylpyridine and 4-Acetylpyridine molecules to attack the perovskites.

The significantly different impacts of 2-, 3-, and 4-Acetylpyridine on perovskites is caused by their differing molecular structures (Figure 1b), which may indicate different interaction modes between FACs perovskites and pyridine derivatives. Note that the common characteristic of these pyridine derivatives are the N atoms in their pyridine rings and O atoms in their ethanoyl substituents, which both possess lone pair electrons. According to the literature, they are Lewis base sites and tend to chelate with Pb atoms from the perovskite's surface according to Lewis acid-base theory.  $  ^{36}  $   Here, for simplicity, their interaction modes were predicated on calculations of the total structure energy of different chelating structures shown as the ball-and-stick diagrams depicted in Figure 1c. The calculation was conducted by DFT theory with the wb97xd calculation method under the LANL2DZ basis set followed by single-point energy calculations for correction of basis set superposition error (BSSE) to predict the stability of the total structure.  $  ^{37}  $   Other weaker interactions, such as hydrogen bonding between the FA from the perovskites and the N and O sites from the pyridine derivatives are ignored. Lower energy should imply a more stable structure. For 2-Acetylpyridine and 3-Acetylpyridine molecules, the structures with N and O on different sides are more stable than the structures with N and O on the same side, as reflected by their lower energies: -10904.17 eV, lower than -10903.84 eV for 2-Acetylpyridine, and -10904.08 eV, lower than -10904.03 eV for 3-Acetylpyridine (Table 1). But the presence of   $  Pb^{2+}  $   can change this situation. The chelating structures

Journal of Materials Chemistry A

of Pb and 2-Acetylpyridine/3-Acetylpyridine have lower energies when their N and O are on the same side than when on different sides (-10983.36 eV < -10981.77 eV for 2-Acetylpyridine-Pb; -10981.59 eV < -10981.51 eV for 2-Acetylpyridine-Pb). Notably, the 2-Acetylpyridine tends to form a stable five-membered ring structure of C-C-O-Pb-N, reflected by it having the lowest structure energy among the calculated five kinds of chelating structures shown in Figure 1c. We speculate that this special interaction mode of forming a stable five-membered ring structure may explain why there was no corrosion of the perovskite in the 2-Acetylpyridine pure solution.  $  ^{38}  $   In contrast, for 3-Acetylpyridine and 4-Acetylpyridine, this special five-membered ring chelating structure cannot form due to the steric hindrance of their molecular structures. On the basis of the above calculations, the speculated stable interaction modes between the pyridine derivatives and the   $  Pb^{2+}  $   of the perovskite film are depicted in Figure 1d. The dipole moment of each of the surface modifiers we used in this work has also been calculated, as it has been thought to have some relationship with the device performance after the surface modification treatment.  $  ^{39-40}  $   As shown in Figure S2 (Supporting Information), 2-Acetylpyridine with structure 2 possesses the highest electron density (negative area), thus it could provide more favorable conditions for coordination with the under-coordinated ionic defects existing in the perovskite crystal. Therefore, we deduce that the PSC modified with 2-Acetylpyridine may perform best.

The  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  of the PSCs after surface modification treatment  $ (1.06 \pm 0.02 \mathrm{~V} $  for 2-Acetylpyridine;  $ 1.02 \pm 0.04 \mathrm{~V} $  for 3-Acetylpyridine;  $ 1.00 \pm 0.05 \mathrm{~V} $  for 4-Acetylpyridine) are relatively higher than those of the pristine devices  $ (0.96 \pm 0.07 \mathrm{~V}) $ , as can be seen in Table 2. We further employed the Mott-Schottky analysis to elucidate the origin of the improved  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  (Figure 4h). As is well-known, the Mott-Schottky equation is as follows:  $ 1 / C^2 = -2 / (\mathrm{V} - \mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{bi}})\varepsilon \varepsilon_0qA^2N $ , where C is measured capacitance in the dark, A is the active area, V is the applied bias,  $ \varepsilon $  is the relative permittivity,  $ \varepsilon_0 $  is the vacuum permittivity, q is elementary charge and N is the concentration of free carriers.[56] From the X-axis of the Mott-Schottky plots, the built-in potentials  $ (V_{\mathrm{bi}}) $  can be calculated from the intercepts in the linear region. The  $ V_{\mathrm{bi}} $  values of  $ 0.76 \mathrm{~V} $ ,  $ 0.85 \mathrm{~V} $ ,  $ 0.82 \mathrm{~V} $ , and  $ 0.80 \mathrm{~V} $  were obtained for the pristine PSC and the PSCs treated with 2-Acetylpyridine, 3-Acetylpyridine, and 4-Acetylpyridine, respectively (Figure 4h). The results match well with the  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  values determined from the  $ J-V $  curves. It is of great importance to settle the tough issue of the long-term stability without sacrificing PSCs' outstanding efficiency. Owing to the intrinsic instability of organic-inorganic hybrid perovskites, the unencapsulated solar cells usually decompose quickly and completely in ambient environment;  $  ^{58}  $  therefore, more extensive investigations of storage stability of PSCs are needed. In this work, all the various kinds of unencapsulated PSCs were tested by keeping them in the dark in ambient air (without control, the relative humidity of local climate is normally 40-90%) at room temperature for 2000 hours, during which the J-V curves have been periodically evaluated on a standard solar simulator. The time evolutions of the PCEs are presented in Figure 5a and Table S2 (Supporting Information). A notable difference appeared for the PSCs based on different perovskite films: The modified PSCs exhibited only minor deterioration (95% of the initial PCE remained for 2-Acetylpyridine; 82% for 3-Acetylpyridine; 75% for 4-Acetylpyridine) after aging for 2000 h without encapsulation, while the pristine device's PCE dramatically decreased to 31% of its initial PCE. Such improved ambient storage stability should be ascribed to the surface modifier, which may help prevent access of moisture/oxygen to the perovskite film by forming an internal stable "hydrophobic capping layer."

The standardized test protocols for the long-term stability of PSCs were suggested to facilitate the standardization in degradation testing across the whole community and promote direct comparisons between laboratories and research institutions.  $  ^{59-60}  $

With the aim of approaching the stability test standards for commercialized solar cells, we compared the thermal stability of the unsealed devices by keeping them in the dark at 85 °C for 1000 hours in an   $  N_{2}  $  -filled glove box. According to the standard IEC 61215-1:2016 (Terrestrial Photovoltaic (PV) Modules-Design Qualification and Type Approval-Part 1: Test Requirements), 85 °C is a specified aging temperature in the thermal stability test. An inert atmosphere (  $  N_{2}  $  ) was advised for the thermal aging test to exclude the interference by other factors from the ambient, such as   $  H_{2}O  $   and   $  O_{2}  $  . The correlative J-V curves were recorded periodically, as shown in Figure 5b, and the parameters are summarized in Table S3 (Supporting Information). It can be seen that the modified PSCs showed superior thermal stability (maintaining over 90% of the initial PCE for 2-Acetylpyridine; 75% for 3-Acetylpyridine; and 69% for 4-Acetylpyridine) compared with the pristine devices (lost 45% of the initial efficiency). It is fair to note that an initial increase is detected in Figure 5b in the first several tens of hours during the stability test, which would be due to concomitant effects such as heat-induced defect healing in the perovskite active layer or field-induced ion movement with the associated structural rearrangement and so on.  $  ^{61}  $

# Conclusion

In summary, we have demonstrated an effective surface passivation approach with high reproducibility for preparing efficient and stable PSCs. A striking enhancement in  $ PCE $  from the original 16.75% to 20.05% has been observed, which could be ascribed to the passivation effect reducing surface traps and suppressing carrier recombination. To our knowledge, it is a fairly high  $ PCE $  for MA-free inverted planar PSCs. Simultaneously, significantly improved stability of the modified PSCs has been achieved. After dark storage at room temperature in ambient air for 2000 h, 85 °C thermal aging in the dark in an N $ _{2} $ -filled glove box for 500 h, and light-soaking at 45 °C in N $ _{2} $  for 500 h, the device modified using 2-Acetylpyridine maintained 95%, 90%,

Page 21 of 37

Journal of Materials Chemistry A

and 91% of its initial efficiency, respectively. Additionally, three kinds of modifiers with ethanoyl substituents in o-substituted, meta-substituted, para-substituted configurations have been compared. Their different effects on tuning the devices' efficiency and stability have been discussed with the aid of theoretical calculations. It is suggested that the special interaction mode that results in the formation of a stable five membered ring structure (C-C-O-Pb-N) may explain why no corrosion occurred between 2-Acetylpyridine and the perovskite film. The calculated dipole moments of these molecules manifest that 2-Acetylpyridine possesses the highest electron density (negative area), which provides more favorable conditions for coordination with the under-coordinated ionic defects present in the perovskite crystal. These calculation results indicate that 2-Acetylpyridine may be the best modifier among all those in this work, which is highly consistent with the experimental data. Furthermore, the surface modification route we used in this work is highly reproducible and easily scalable, and its application for producing high-performing perovskite modules is considered to be very feasible.

Device Fabrication: The fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) coated glass (Pilkington TEC 15,  $ 15\Omega/\mathrm{square} $ ) was first etched by a laser before being cleaned ultrasonically with detergent solution, rinsed sequentially with deionized water, ethanol, and acetone. The pristine perovskite film was annealing at 150°C for 30 min on a hotplate. For the preparation of the modified perovskite film, the as-fabricated layer was dipping into the modification solution (10  $  ^{-4}  $   vol%; in diethyl ether) for 10 min to complete the surface modification process, followed by thermal annealing at 150°C for 10 min to complete the procedure. After finishing the perovskite layer, a chlorobenzene solution of PCBM (20 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) as an electron-transporting layer was spin-coated on top of the perovskite film at the rotation speed of 1500 rpm for 30 seconds and then dried at 70°C for 10 min on a heating stage. Subsequently, a saturated methanol solution of BCP was spin-coated on top of the PCBM layer at a rotation speed of 6000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 70°C for 10 min on a hotplate. Finally, one batch of films was transferred to the evaporator chamber, and 80-nm-thick Ag electrodes were deposited by thermal evaporation under high vacuum (< 3×10  $  ^{-4}  $   Pa) to complete the whole device fabrication.
