# 1. Introduction

In recent years, metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have received a great deal of research attention, due to their excellent photoelectric properties, such as long charge carrier lifetime, tunable bandgap, and broad absorption range, etc.   $  [1-6]  $  . The achieved power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of PSCs are jumped from 3.8% in 2009 to a certified value of 25.5% in 2020   $  [7,8]  $  . Nonetheless, despite the rapid progress of PSCs, the nonradiative recombination caused by the interface and grain boundary defects during the crystallization process remains a significant negative impact   $  [9-12]  $  , leading to a decrease in PCE and stability   $  [13-17]  $  . In this regard, finding a practical approach to reduce these defects is crucial to enhance the efficiency and long-term stability of PSCs.

The primary defects on the perovskite surface include uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   or halide ions, Pb clusters, Pb–I antisite defects, and I or MA vacancies [18]. Researchers have tried to reduce the defects in different ways, such as solvent engineering, perovskite composition engineering, surface passivation engineering, additive engineering, etc [19–24]. Among these methods, perovskite surface passivation is considered as one of the most commonly used and efficient ways to minimize these defects. The concept of passivation was first proposed by Jinsong Huang and coworkers using fullerene to reduce the defects of the electron

Metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have made remarkable progress recently. However, the large number of defects in the polycrystalline perovskite film has an important impact on the carrier recombination and ion migration in the device. The non-radiative recombination is regarded as the main way of charge loss, which largely determines the performance of PSCs. Here, a small molecule, 2-mercaptopyrimidine (MPM), was selected as the surface passivator for  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite films. It is revealed that both S and N atoms in MPM can interact with uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  to passivation the surface defects. As a result, the devices based on MPM passivation achieve a higher power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 19.71\% $  than the control devices  $ (17.85\%) $  due to the reduced surface defect state density. Moreover, MPM-passivated PSCs also possess superior humidity stability, retaining  $ 94\% $  and  $ 82\% $  of the initial PCEs after 720 and  $ 480\mathrm{h} $  aging in air with a relative humidity of  $ 30\% $  and  $ 60\% $ , respectively.

Under-coordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ions are regarded to be the main source of charge traps, which can be coordinated effectively by this coordination binding passivation method [27–29]. For instance, Noel et al. proposed that pyridine and thiophene Lewis bases, including a lone pair of electrons on nitrogen and sulfur functional groups, can passivate uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   in perovskite materials [30]. In this work, we introduced an efficient and stable agent, 2-mercaptopyrimidine (MPM), to passivate the surface of the perovskites. # 2. Experimental

# 2.1.Materials

All chemicals and solvents were purchased and used directly. Methylammonium iodide (MAI, 99%) and Lead(II) iodide (PbI  $  _{2}  $  , 98%) were obtained from TCI. Spiro-OMeTAD and FK209 Co(III)TFSI were obtained from Xi'an Pol. Dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO,   $  \geq  $  99.9%), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF,   $  \geq  $  99.9%) and isopropanol were obtained from Aladdin. 2-Mercaptopyrimidine (98%) was obtained from Alfa Aesar.

# 2.2. Device fabrication

The fabrication of the   $  PbI_{2}  $  -(MPM)  $  _{x}  $   complexes was consisted of three sets: 1)   $  PbI_{2}  $   powders and MPM powders were mixed in DMF by stirring for 2 h. 2) The solution was filtered and the residual solid was washed with large amount of toluene. 3) The collected precipitates were vacuumized and dried at 60°C [31].

The PSC devices were prepared according to the method of our group previously   $  [32,33]  $  . The FTO conductive glass substrates were rinsed with abluent, ultrapure water, acetone, and ethanol by an ultrasonic cleaner for 20 min in turn. Later, the treated substrates were placed in plasma cleaner for 10 min to remove organic impurity before use. For the compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer, the solution made of ethanol and bis (2,4-pentanedionato) bis (2-propanolato) in a 9:1 volume ratio was thermal sprayed onto the prepared substrates. Then, they were transferred to a flat furnace to sinter at 500°C for 30 min. In order to prepare   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   precursor solution, MAI (190.8 mg) and   $  PbI_{2}  $   (553.2 mg) were added into 1 mL mixed DMSO: DMF solvent (1:4, volume ratio) and stirred for 2 h. For the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   perovskite layer, 50 μL precursor solution was dropped on the surface of   $  TiO_{2}  $  . Rotating the substrates at 1000 and 4000 rpm for 10 and 30 s in turn. In the middle of the second rotation process, 200 μL of chlorobenzene was added quickly onto the center of substrates. Next, the perovskite films were transferred to a 100°C heater to heat for 20 min. When the devices were cool, 100 μL passivation solution (0.2 mg 2-Mercaptopyrimidine/mL, isopropanol) was dropped on the surface of perovskite. Rotating the substrates at 1000 and 5000 rpm for 20 and 10 s in turn. For the hole transport layer, 72.3 mg Spiro-OMeTAD powers and additives (17.5 μL Li-TFSI, 30 μL FK209 Co(III)TFSI and 28.8 μL t-BP) were added into 1 mL chlorobenzene to prepare Spiro-OMeTAD precursor solution. Then, the solution was dropped on the passivated perovskite films. Rotating the devices at 3000 rpm for 30 s. At last, 80 nm thick Au film was evaporated on the surface of the hole transporter layer by a vacuum evaporator.

# 2.3. Characterizations

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) measurement was measured on a PerkinElmer FTIR (IS50) spectrometer. X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra were obtained by a 9KW diffractometer (Rigaku SmartLab). UV-Vis spectra were offered with an Agilent spectrophotometer. X-ray photoemission spectroscopy (XPS) were obtained by ESCALAB XI+ (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was measured by a NETZSCH TG209 thermal gravimetric analyzer under nitrogen atmosphere from 50°C to 400°C. The morphology SEM images of MAPbI₃ were illuminated with a scanning electron microscope (SEM, SU8220). The J-V curves were performed with a Source-Measure Unit (Keithley 2400). The irradiated area of devices was 0.1 cm² and the illumination was 100 mW/cm² (AM 1.5G) from the Oriel Sol3A solar simulator (Model: 94023A, Newport). For the IPCE curves, they were recorded by an IPCE measurement kit (TLS-300XR, Newport). The steady-state photocurrent output was performed at the maximum power point for 300 s under one sun irradiation from the Oriel Sol3A solar simulator (Model: 94023A, Newport). The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were measured by an electrochemical impedance meter

(Zennium/IM6 Zahner, Germany) in the dark at 0.8 V applied voltage. The AC perturbation was 10 mV and the rang was started from 100 mHz to 1 MHz. The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and the time-resolved PL (TRPL) spectra were performed using a fluorescence spectrometer (wavelength: 468 nm, Horiba FluorologFL-3). The contact angles of the perovskite surfer were obtained using a JC2000D Contact Angle Measuring Apparatus (CAA, made in Shanghai, PR China).

# 3. Results and discussion

As presented in Fig. 1a, there are two functional atoms in MPM molecular structure: nitrogen atoms in the pyrimidine ring and sulfur atoms in the sulfhydryl group. Both of the two functional atoms are expected to interact with Pb ions as illustrated in Fig. 1a. In order to confirm MPM and uncoordinated lead ions on the perovskite surface can interact through sulfur and nitrogen atoms, we first synthesized   $  PbI_{2}  $  -(MPM)  $  _{x}  $   complexes to investigate the coordination ability and strength of MPM and Pb ions. The Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR) spectra of the   $  PbI_{2}  $  -(MPM)  $  _{x}  $   complexes and pure MPM powders were characterized (Fig. 1b). For the pure MPM powders, the C=N and C-S stretching vibration frequency were appeared at 1608 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   and 1188 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  , respectively. While, for the   $  PbI_{2}  $  -(MPM)x complexes, the C=N and C-S stretching vibration frequency were red-shifted to 1592 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   and 1178 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  , indicating that both N and S atoms interact with   $  PbI_{2}  $   [34, 35]. XRD of the complexes and pure   $  PbI_{2}  $   powders were further performed to study the coordination of Pb ions in the   $  PbI_{2}  $  -(MPM)  $  _{x}  $   complexes (Fig. S1). The XRD spectra show that the typical peak of   $  PbI_{2}  $   at 2θ of 12.4° disappears in the complexes, indicative the coordination between MPM and   $  Pb^{2+}  $  . Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of   $  PbI_{2}  $  -(MPM)  $  _{x}  $   complexes was performed as shown in Fig. 1c. The elimination of   $  PbI_{2}  $  -(MPM)  $  _{x}  $   complexes was started at 220°C (weight loss by 5%). The decomposition temperature of   $  PbI_{2}  $  -(MPM)  $  _{x}  $   complexes is kept high due to the strong binding strength between MPM and   $  Pb^{2+}  $  . Therefore, we can conclude that MPM can coordinate effectively with   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ions to form the Pb–N and Pb–S interactions, which is expected to improve the quality of perovskite film.

Next, we fabricated control  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite films and MPM passivated samples by spin-coating MPM isopropanol solution onto the control perovskite layer to determine the passivation effect on the perovskite surface (Fig. 2a). The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was characterized to investigate the film morphology of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite before and after MPM passivation. As shown in Fig. S2, the perovskite grain size does not show noticeable changes. Especially, some small granules can be observed in the SEM image of MPM passivated samples. We can speculate that these small granules might be the  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ - (MPM)x complexes. The UV-visible absorption spectra of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite films before and after MPM passivation are shown in Fig. 2b. There is no significant difference in the absorption spectra between the control and the passivated perovskite films, indicating that the MPM passivation does not change the crystal structure of the perovskite. The XRD patterns of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite films are shown in Fig. 2c. Before passivation, there is a small diffraction peak at  $ 2\theta $  of  $ 12.6^{\circ} $ , which is the characteristic peak of lead iodide. After passivation treatment, this peak is disappeared, implying a good interaction between MPM and  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite surface. As shown in Fig. S3, we calculated the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the (110) perovskite peak at  $ 14.5^{\circ} $  to analyze the crystallinity of perovskite films. The FWHM decreased from 0.141 to 0.124 after passivation. The smaller value of FWHM reflects the higher crystallinity of perovskite film [36].

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra were further implemented for the perovskite films with and without MPM passivated. As shown in Fig. S4, we can observe a peak of S 2p at  $ 165\mathrm{eV} $  in the MPM passivated perovskite film, which is not present in the control film, indicating that MPM has been successfully introduced onto the perovskite surface [37]. Moreover, the  $ \mathrm{Pb}4\mathrm{f}_{5 / 2} $  and  $ 4\mathrm{f}_{7 / 2} $  peaks appear at 142.65 and  $ 137.75\mathrm{eV} $  for the control film, respectively (Fig. 2d). After

Photovoltaic performance of PSCs was investigated for the control and passivated perovskite films. Fig. 3a shows the schematic configuration of planar MAPbI $ _{3} $  PSCs consisting of a glass/FTO/c-TiO $ _{2} $ /perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD/Au structure, and the corresponding cross-sectional SEM image is shown in Fig. S5. We first investigated the effect of MPM concentrations on the device performance measured under 100 mW/cm $ ^{2} $  illumination (AM 1.5G). The current density-voltage (J-V) curves and corresponding parameters of MPM passivated devices based on different concentrations are displayed in Fig. S6 and Table S1. The champion J-V curves of the control and passivated devices with 0.2 mg/ml MPM are shown in Fig. 3b, and the corresponding photovoltaic parameters are listed in Table 1. The control devices showed a PCE of 17.85% with an open-circuit voltage (V $ _{oc} $ ) of 1.13 V, a short-circuit current density (J $ _{sc} $ ) of 22.39 mA/cm $ ^{2} $ , and a fill factor (FF) of 0.70 respectively. The enhanced performance could be largely attributed to the effective passivation of the MPM for the perovskite surface defects, which will be discussed in the following sections.

The incident photon-to-current conversion efficiency (IPCE) spectra of the control and MPM passivated PSCs were measured, and the integrated   $  J_{sc}  $   are shown in Fig. 3c. The champion device with MPM passivated shows a broad plateau over 90% between 350 and 750 nm together a higher integrated current density of 21.86 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  , which is in good agreement with the J-V results. To verify the J-V scanning PCE, the steady-state maximum PCE output of the MPM passivated device was measured at the maximum power point (MPP) for 300 s under continuous illumination, as shown in Fig. 3d. The passivated devices present a steady-state PCE of 19.31% at 0.86 V, which verifies the efficiencies obtained from the J-V measurements. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurement were carried out to gain insights into the recombination process of PSCs with and without MPM. The Nyquist plots are shown in Fig. 4f. The second semicircle represents the recombination resistance  $ (R_{\mathrm{rec}}) $  [40]. Obviously, the MPM passivated devices exhibit a higher  $ R_{\mathrm{rec}} $   $ (16.53\mathrm{k}\Omega) $  than that of the control devices  $ (11.52\mathrm{k}\Omega) $ , suggesting less charge recombination in the former devices [41,42].

In order to investigate the charge carrier recombination kinetics of the MPM passivated perovskite film, we measured the steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and the time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL), where the perovskite was directly coated on the glass substrates. As shown in Fig. 4a, the MPM passivated film shows stronger PL intensity, which indicates a higher film quality than the control film [43]. As illustrated in Fig. 4b, TRPL spectra were obtained to explore the carrier lifetimes. The decay curves were fitted with the biexponential decay kinetics function:  $  f(t) = A_{1}\exp (-t / \tau_{1}) + A_{2}\exp (-t / \tau_{2}) + b  $ , where  $  \tau_{1}  $  and  $  \tau_{2}  $  represent the fast and slow decay components,  $  A_{1}  $  and  $  A_{2}  $  represent the decay amplitudes, and  $  b  $  represents a constant quantity, respectively [44,45]. The average lifetime  $  (\tau_{\mathrm{avg}})  $  can be calculated by the equation:  $  \tau_{\mathrm{avg}} = (A_1\tau_1^2 +A_2\tau_2^2) / (A_1\tau_1 + A_2\tau_2)  $  [46]. The fitted parameters are listed in Table S2. The MPM passivated film displays a longer average carrier lifetime of 133.82 ns as compared to the control film (110.64 ns). These results suggest that MPM can effectively reduce non-radiation recombination due to the film surface defects passivation.

# 4. Conclusions

In summary, we have demonstrated an effective surface passivating agent for  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  PSCs. Detailed studies reveal that both S and N atoms in the passivation molecule can coordinate with uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  ions, which can passivate the surface defects of the perovskite film. Consequently, the PCE of the passivated devices deliver a much higher PCE (19.71%) than those of the control devices (17.85%) under identical conditions. Moreover, the PSCs with MPM passivated present enhanced humidity stability. This study provides a facile small molecule passivation strategy to improve the efficiency and stability of PSCs.
