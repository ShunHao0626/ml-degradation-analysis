# Abstract

Remarkable progress has been made in perovskite solar cells (PSCs) recently. However, the defects present in the perovskite layer act as non-radiative recombination centers to decrease the stability and restrict the further performance improvement of the device. We report herein a sulfur-rich two-dimensional small molecule, SMe-TATPyr, as a bifunctional layer to efficiently passivate the surface defects of perovskite and facilitate the hole transfer at the perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD interface. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy analyses show that the sulfur atoms of

SMe-TATPyr can passivate the uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  defects and suppress the  $ \mathrm{Pb}^0 $  defect formation as Lewis bases. As a result, the power conversion efficiency of PSCs is distinctly increased from  $ 20.4\% $  to  $ 22.3\% $ . Moreover, this simple interfacial modification could effectively enhance the stability of unencapsulated PSCs to retain  $ 95\% $  of the initial efficiency after storage for  $ 1500\mathrm{h} $  at ambient conditions, in contrast to  $ 70\% $  efficiency retention of the device without SMe-TATPyr under the same conditions.

# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted intense attention with a remarkable power conversion efficiency (PCE) of over  $ 25\% $  being achieved recently.[1-4] They have the advantages of low cost, light weight, flexibility, and roll-to-roll processing, making them competitive for future commercialization.[5-8] However, the best performing PSCs still lag behind the theoretical Shockley-Queisser efficiency limit.[9,10] Considerable efforts have recently been undertaken to reduce the number of defects of bulk perovskites and among various interfaces, which may act as non-radiative recombination centers to seriously affect the device stability and prevent the further improvement of photovoltaic performance.[11-14] In this context, various additives, such as organic ammonium salts,[15-17] polymers,[18-20] organic small molecules,[21-23] and semiconductor materials,[24,25] have been introduced at the perovskite interface to passivate defects and improve charge collection and transfer. Among them, organic molecules hold great promises because of their feasibility of structural modification for performance optimization and tunable hydrophobicity to protect perovskites against moisture and oxygen.[26-28]

To date, the most commonly used and effective small molecular hole transport material (HTM) for PSCs is 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-di-(p-methoxyphenyl)amino)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (spiro-OMeTAD).[29,30] One disadvantage of spiro-OMeTAD is that it has a

Journal Pre-proof

relatively destabilized level of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO, -5.2 eV) which lies too high above the valence band maximum (VBM) of perovskites. The resulting large energy level gap may severely influence the hole extraction efficiency of spiro-OMeTAD.[31] In order to address this issue, a buffer layer with a suitable HOMO level is introduced at the perovskite/HTM interface. For instance, Wang et al. reported an interfacial engineering method based on the incorporation of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbBr}_{0.9}\mathrm{I}_{2.1} $  quantum dots between the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  perovskite and HTM layers to enhance charge transfer and efficiency.[32] Recently, a pyrazine-cored small molecule PT-TPA has been employed at the perovskite/HTM interface to improve the performance (6.8% enhancement of PCE from 21.9% to 23.4%) and stability of PSCs.[33]

We report here a new small organic molecule 1,3,6,8-tetrakis[5-(N,N-di(p-(methylthio)phenyl)amino-p-phenyl)-thiophen-2-yl]pyrene (abbreviated as SMe-TATPyr, Figure 1a) as a bifunctional interfacial material to simultaneously address the above-discussed defect passivation and hole extraction issues. SMe-TATPyr is a two-dimensional molecule with a pyrene-core, to which four SMe-terminated triphenylamine side arms are connected through a phenyl-thiophene bridge. We believe that this sulfur-rich molecule will be an ideal candidate for defect passivation and the mediation of hole extraction and transport. On one hand, the presence of ample sulfur atoms can act as Lewis bases to effectively passivate the uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  defects, which is expected to enhance the device performance and stability.[34-37] On the other hand, the use of the less electron-rich SMe terminal

Journal Pre-proof

group, with respect to the commonly used OMe group (the Hammett constant of OMe and SMe is -0.27 and 0.0, respectively), would slightly lower the HOMO level of SMe-TATPyr for a better energy-level alignment and thus improve hole extraction. In consistence with these hypotheses, the best PCE  $ (\mathrm{PCE}_{\mathrm{best}}) $  of PSCs modified with an interlayer of SMe-TATPyr at the perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD interface has been significantly improved from  $ 20.4\% $  to an outstanding efficiency of  $ 22.3\% $ , accompanied by distinct improvement of the device stability (Figure 1b).

# 2. Experimental section

# 2.1 Preparation of SMe-TATPyr

SMe-TATPyr was readily synthesized via four-fold Suzuki coupling reactions of 1,3,6,8-tetrabromopyrene with 5-(N,N-di(p-(methylthio)phenyl)amino-p-phenyl)thiophene-2-boronic acid in  $ 40\% $  yield (Figure S1). Full structural characterizations of SMe-TATPyr have been undertaken using nuclear magnetic resonance and mass spectrometry.

# 2.2. Device fabrication and characterization

The SEM images were captured with Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (Hitachi, SU4800). The topography images were conducted with Bruke Multimpde 8. The absorption spectra were obtained with UV/vis spectrophotometer (Hitachi, UH-4150). The XRD diffraction patterns were measured by Rigaku DMAX-RB with a Cu-Kα X-ray radiation source. Steady-state and time-resolved photoluminescence spectra were performed at Edinburgh Instruments (FLS980) with

spiro-OMeTAD solution in chlorobenzene was spin coated dynamically on the rotating substrate at 3000 rpm for 30 s. In this fashion, the re-dissolution of the interlayer could be essentially avoided. Finally, 80 nm of Au was thermally evaporated to complete the device fabrication. The active area of   $  0.1 \, cm^{2}  $   was defined by the mask.

The photovoltaic curves were measured at ORIEL measurement system with Keithley 2420 source under simulated AM 1.5G irradiation (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ). The EQE spectra were recorded by QE-R3011 (Enlitch) measurement system. The contact angle measurements were carried out by KRUSS (DSA 100). For the long-term stability measurements, the unencapsulated perovskite solar cells were stored in ambient condition with a humidity below 35% RH at 25 °C. The perovskite solar cells without encapsulation were loaded on a hotplate at 85 °C in N  $  _{2}  $  atmosphere and measured in air condition periodically to assess the photovoltaic performance. To evaluate the humidity stability, the unencapsulated perovskite solar cells were aged in air condition with a high humidity of 60% RH at 25 °C.

# 3. Results and Discussion

The cyclic voltammograms (CVs) of SMe-TATPyr and spiro-OMeTAD are displayed in Figure 2a. The first anodic wave of SMe-TATPyr and spiro-OMeTAD is located at +0.76 and +0.47 V vs Ag/AgCl, respectively. The HOMO level of SMe-TATPyr was thus estimated to be -5.5 eV, contrasting to -5.2 eV of

Journal Pre-proof

spiro-OMeTAD.[34] This makes SMe-TATPyr a potential hole extraction/transfer mediator between the  $ \mathrm{FA}_{1 - x}\mathrm{MA}_x\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  perovskite (VBM = -5.9 eV) and spiro-OMeTAD owing to their well-matched energy-level alignment (Figure 2b). SMe-TATPyr displays an intense absorption peak at 460 nm and an emission band at 569 nm in  $ \mathrm{CH}_2\mathrm{Cl}_2 $  (Figure S2). The optical energy band gap ( $ E_g $ ) of SMe-TATPyr was calculated to be 2.3 eV from the absorption onset wavelength. The LUMO level of SMe-TATPyr was thus determined to be -3.2 eV based on the electrochemical and absorption data. The density functional theory (DFT) calculation shows that optimized structure of SMe-TATPyr presents a two-dimensional non-planar structure (Figure S3). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) indicates that SMe-TATPyr possesses good thermal stability (Figure 2c). The decomposition temperature ( $ T_d $ ) for 5% weight loss is around 430 °C. The hole mobility of SMe-TATPyr is estimated to be  $ 1.0 \times 10^{-3} $  cm² V⁻¹ s⁻¹ by the space charge limited current (SCLC) method (Figure 2d).

Prior to the device measurement, the influence of SMe-TATPyr on the perovskite morphology and the interaction between them were first examined. The thickness of the SMe-TATPyr layer is about  $ 2.80\mathrm{nm} $  as measured by AFM analysis (Figure S4). The top-view SEM and AFM images of the  $ \mathrm{FA}_{1 - x}\mathrm{MA}_x\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  perovskite with and without a covered thin layer of SMe-TATPyr are shown in Figure S5. In the bare perovskite film, two separated phases are observed. The dark perovskite grains are surrounded by the white  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  grains, consistent with reported results (Figure S5a and S5b).[38] After coating with a thin layer of SMe-TATPyr, the  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  grains are fully covered. Moreover, the film roughness decreases from  $ 42.3\mathrm{nm} $  to  $ 30.8\mathrm{nm} $  when

Journal Pre-proof

perovskite is spin-coated with SMe-TATPyr (Figure S5c and S5d). This suggests that the SMe-TATPyr molecules are more easily situated at grain boundaries. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis indicates that the presence of SMe-TATPyr does not distinctly affect the perovskite crystalline properties (Figure S6). The XRD spectra in Figure S6 show that the  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  diffraction peak is stronger with respect to the perovskite (100) peak, which is not uncommon in perovskite prepared by a two-step interdiffusion method [38]. Indeed, the presence of residual  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  is believed beneficial for the passivation of the perovskite defect at grain boundaries and film surface [38].

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) characterizations were further conducted to investigate the passivation effect at the perovskite/SMe-TATPyr interface. The Pb  $ 4\mathrm{f}_{5/2} $  and  $ 4\mathrm{f}_{7/2} $  peaks of the bare perovskite film locate at 143.5 and  $ 138.6\mathrm{eV} $ , respectively (Figure 2e). When modified with SMe-TATPyr, both peaks shift about  $ 0.2\mathrm{eV} $  toward the lower binding energy region (negative shift), corroborating the interaction between uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  and SMe-TATPyr.[39] In addition, the  $ \mathrm{Pb}^0 $  peaks of perovskite at 141.8 and  $ 136.9\mathrm{eV} $  disappear completely after modification with SMe-TATPyr, which is beneficial for suppressing the formation of deep-level defects.[40] On the other hand, the S  $ 2\mathrm{p}_{1/2} $  and  $ 2\mathrm{p}_{3/2} $  peaks of SMe-TATPyr exhibit a positive  $ 0.4\mathrm{eV} $  shift to 164.9 and  $ 163.9\mathrm{eV} $ , respectively, when it interacts with perovskite (Figure 2f). These data could be taken as strong evidences for the presence of Pb-S interactions between perovskite and SMe-TATPyr. However, we cannot judge at this stage whether the SMe or thiophene, or both, sulfur atoms are mainly involved in these interactions.

In order to verify the bifunctional effect of SMe-TATPyr, we fabricated n-i-p planar PSCs (active area:  $ 0.1~\mathrm{cm}^2 $ ) using  $ \mathrm{FA}_{1 - x}\mathrm{MA}_x\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  perovskite as the photoactive layer and  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  and spiro-OMeTAD as the n-type electron-transporting layer and p-type HTM, respectively. A thin interfacial layer of SMe-TATPyr (< 5 nm) was inserted between the perovskite film and spiro-OMeTAD as the interfacial modification layer (Figures 1b and 3a). In addition, normal PSCs without the SMe-TATPyr interfacial layer were fabricated as control experiments. The representative cross-sectional SEM image of the SMe-TATPyr-based device is displayed in Figure 3b. The thickness of  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ , perovskite, and spiro-OMeTAD is about 30, 730 and  $ 160~\mathrm{nm} $ , respectively. The presence of the SMe-TATPyr interlayer did not influence the interface contact between perovskite and spiro-OMeTAD. The typical  $ J $ -V curves and statistic device efficiency are depicted and summarized in Figures 3c, 3d, and S7 and Table 1. With the introduction of the SMe-TATPyr interlayer, the average device efficiency ( $ \mathrm{PCE}_{\mathrm{avg}} $ ) increases from  $ 19.81\% $  to  $ 21.71\% $ . The best PSC with the SMe-TATPyr interlayer exhibits a distinct increase of  $ \mathrm{PCE}_{\mathrm{best}} $  from  $ 20.40\% $  to  $ 22.34\% $  and open-circuit voltage ( $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $ ) from 1.10 to  $ 1.13~\mathrm{V} $ , respectively. The device hysteresis factor also decreases from  $ 9.8\% $  to  $ 5.9\% $  (Figure 3c). The remaining hysteresis might be related to the mismatched contact at the perovskite/ $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  interface. This improved  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  and weakened hysteresis behavior could be attributed to reduced defect density and faster interfacial carrier transfer of the interlayer-containing device. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra of the device with or without the SMe-TATPyr interlayer are presented in Figure 3e, in

which the integrated   $  J_{sc}  $   values match well with the J-V curves. In addition, the device of ITO/SnO  $  _{2}  $  /perovskite/SMe-TATPyr/Au, with a single layer of SMe-TATPyr as the HTM, was also fabricated and tested (Figure S8). This device yields a poor PCE of 5.3% with short-circuit current density (  $  J_{sc}  $  ) of 16.5 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ,   $  V_{oc}  $   of 804 mV, fill factor (FF) of 40%, respectively. This poor performance is partially caused by the inferior film quality of SMe-TATPyr as a result of its limited solubility (< 3 mg/mL in chlorobenzene, CB).

With the presence of the SMe-TATPyr interlayer, the PSCs exhibit an increased integrated  $  J_{\mathrm{sc}}  $  of  $  23.73\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2}  $  from  $  22.69\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2}  $  (Figure 3e). This increase mainly comes from the enhanced EQE values at the long-wavelength range, possibly as a result of the decreased non-radiative recombination by defect passivation.[41] We further investigated the time dependent PCE performance by monitoring the power output under the maximum power-point condition (Figure 3f). The control device with Spiro-OMeTAD gradually increases to the maximum power efficiency after light illumination, while that with the SMe-TATPyr interlayer increases to the maximum power efficiency immediately after light illumination. This quick response is likely due to the fewer traps and defects at the perovskite/SMe-TATPyr interface.[42] Moreover, both devices show a stable PCE response within  $  400~s  $  of exposure under the maximum power output.

In order to further study the effect of the SMe-TATPyr interlayer on the excited-state properties of perovskite films, steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved PL (TRPL) measurements were carried out on the neat perovskite film

and the perovskite film coated with SMe-TATPyr. Compared to the neat perovskite film in Figure 4a, the emission peak of perovskite/SMe-TATPyr presents an about 5 nm blue-shift. Besides, significant PL intensity quenching is observed when the perovskite (or perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD) is accompanied with SMe-TATPyr. These data suggest efficient hole transfer from perovskite to SMe-TATPyr, due to the well-matched energy level alignment of SMe-TATPyr with perovskite and strong Pb-S interactions on the interface. The TRPL results show that the bare perovskite film on glass exhibits a bi-exponential decay with  $ \tau_{1} = 63.49 $  ns,  $ \tau_{2} = 291.44 $  ns, and an averaged decay time ( $ \tau_{\mathrm{ave}} $ ) of 241.03 ns (Figure 4b and Table S1). When coated with SMe-TATPyr, the  $ \tau_{\mathrm{ave}} $  of perovskite dramatically decreases to 136.49 ns ( $ \tau_{1} = 32.69 $  ns,  $ \tau_{2} = 143.99 $  ns). Besides, the perovskite/SMe-TATPyr/spiro-OMeTAD sample presents a decreased carrier lifetime of 16.88 ns, in contrast to 21.17 ns of perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD. The PL and TRPL results indicate that SMe-TATPyr can facilitate the photo-generated carrier extraction and transfer.[43-45]

SCLC measurements have been conducted to compare the defect densities of perovskite films with or without the SMe-TATPyr interlayer. Figure 4c and 4d shows the dark current plots of the electron-only (ITO/SnO $ _2 $ /perovskite/interlayer/PCBM/Ag) and hole-only (ITO/NiOx/perovskite/interlayer/P3HT/Ag) device, respectively, in the presence or absence (control) of the SMe-TATPyr interlayer. The equation of  $ N_{\mathrm{t}} = 2\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}}V_{\mathrm{TFL}} / eL^2 $  was employed to calculate the defect density ( $ N_{\mathrm{t}} $ ), in which  $ \varepsilon_0 $  is the vacuum permittivity,  $ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}} $  is the relative dielectric constant,  $ V_{\mathrm{TFL}} $  is the defect-filled limited voltage,  $ e $  is the electron charge, and  $ L $  is the film thickness, respectively.[46]

The electron trap density is determined to be 4.06 and  $ 2.55 \times 10^{15} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3} $  for the control and SMe-TATPyr-included electron-only device, respectively. On the other hand, the hole defect density of the control and SMe-TATPyr-included hole-only device is determined to be 1.53 and  $ 0.32 \times 10^{15} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3} $ , respectively. These results suggest that SMe-TATPyr has an obvious effect for the defect passivation of perovskite. In particular, the hole defect density of perovskite can be reduced by five times with the inclusion of the SMe-TATPr interlayer.

In addition, the Nyquist plots of PSCs with or without the SMe-TATPyr interlayer have been measured (Figure 4e and Table S2). The PSC with SMe-TATPyr shows a considerably decreased series resistance  $ (\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{s}}) $  and increased recombination resistance  $ (\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{rec}}) $ , consistent with facilitated carrier transfer and reduced recombination losses. Figure 4f shows the Mott-Schottky plots of the device with or without SMe-TATPyr. The interlayer-containing device presents an increased build-in potential  $ (V_{\mathrm{bi}}) $  of  $ 0.89\mathrm{V} $  with respect to  $ 0.83\mathrm{V} $  of the control device, providing a higher driving force for carrier separation and transfer. These data consistently suggest that SMe-TATPyr acts as a bifunctional interlayer for both defect passivation and carrier extraction and transfer.

The long term stability was tested to compare the environmental tolerance of the control device and that with the SMe-TATPyr interlayer (Figure 5 and S9). After storage for  $ 1500\mathrm{h} $  at ambient conditions, the unencapsulated SMe-TATPyr-based PSC maintains over  $ 95\% $  of its initial efficiency, while the control device loses about  $ 30\% $  efficiency (Figure 5a). In addition, the device with the SMe-TATPyr interlayer also

shows enhanced stability toward harsh conditions. After testing for 168 h at 85 °C in   $  N_{2}  $   or in air with 60% relative humidity (RH) at rt, the device with SMe-TATPyr retains 77% and 87% of the original PCE, respectively. In comparison, only 47% and 76% efficiency is retained under these two conditions, respectively, in the case of interlayer-free device (Figure 5b and 5c). This improved stability of the SMe-TATPyr-based device may be attributed to the decreased uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   defects and increased moisture-resistance of the device by the interlayer. As shown in Figure S10, the water contact angle of bare perovskite increases distinctly from 69.3° to 84.6° when it is covered with a layer of SMe-TATPyr. This suggests that the insertion of the thin interlayer may help inhibit the moisture penetration and thus improve the long-term device stability. In the aging test under harsh conditions, the device efficiency decreased firstly and then increased slightly (Figure 5b and 5c). The reason for this phenomenon is not clear at this stage, though similar trend has been observed in literatures [47,48]. The initial abrupt decrease of PCE could be caused by the diffusion of   $  \Gamma  $   into spiro-OMeTAD and/or the degradation of   $  \Gamma  $   into   $  I_{2}  $  . When a thermodynamic equilibrium is reached, the ion diffusion and degradation will be alleviated, leading to a slight recovery of efficiency.

# 4. Conclusion

In conclusion, we present a sulfur-rich two-dimensional small molecule, SMe-TATPyr, as a bifunctional interfacial material to improve the efficiency and stability of PSCs. On one hand, the hole extraction and transfer is facilitated when a thin layer of SMe-TATPyr is applied at the perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD interface, as a result of their

Journal Pre-proof

well-matched energy level alignment. On the other hand, the presence of ample sulfur atoms in SMe-TATPyr leads to sufficient Pb-S interactions between SMe-TATPyr and perovskite and thus efficient interfacial defect passivation. As a result of this bifunctional effect, the PCE of the device is significantly enhanced from  $ 20.4\% $  to  $ 22.3\% $  with the inclusion of the interlayer, and  $ 95\% $  of the initial efficiency is retained after storage for  $ 1500\mathrm{h} $  at ambient conditions. The use of a sulfur-rich bifunctional interfacial layer is believed to become a powerful and general means to develop highly-efficient and stable perovskite solar cells.

# Highlights

1. A sulfur-rich small molecule is designed as a bifunctional reagent for interfacial defect passivation and hole transfer.

2. The use of the interfacial layer leads to the enhancement of the power conversion efficiency of perovskite solar cells from  $ 20.4\% $  to  $ 22.3\% $ , accompanied by the distinct enhancement of the device stability.

3. The effect of interfacial defect passivation is fully supported by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy analyses, defect density measurements, and resistance and built-in potential studies.
