# Crystal Orientation Modulation and Defect Passivation for Efficient and Stable Methylammonium-Free Dion-Jacobson Quasi-2D Perovskite Solar Cells

Pengyu Su,  $  ^{\nabla}  $   Le Bai,  $  ^{\nabla}  $   Huan Bi, Baibai Liu, Dongmei He, Wenqi Wang, Xiaobing Cao,  $  ^{*}  $   Shi Chen, Donghwa Lee, Hua Yang, Zhigang Zang, and Jiangzhao Chen  $  ^{*}  $

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Dion-Jacobson (DJ) quasi-2D perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have received increasing attention due to their greater potentials in realizing efficient and stable quasi-2D PSCs relative to their Ruddlesden–Popper counterpart. The substitution of methylammonium (MA  $  ^{+}  $  ) with formamidinium is expected to be able to further increase the stability and power conversion efficiency (PCE) of DJ quasi-2D PSCs. Herein, we report a multifunctional additive strategy for preparing high-quality MA-free DJ quasi-2D perovskite films, where 1,1'-carbonyldi(1,2,4-triazole) (CDTA) molecules are incorporated into the perovskite precursor solution. CDTA mod-

ification can control phase distribution, enlarge grain size, modulate crystallinity and crystal orientation, and passivate defects. After CDTA modification, more favorable gradient phase distribution and accordingly gradient band alignment are formed, which is conducive to carrier transport and extraction. The improved crystal orientation can facilitate carrier transport and collection. The enlarged grain size and effective defect passivation contribute to reduced defect density. As a result, the CDTA-modified device delivers a PCE of 16.07%, which is one of the highest PCEs ever reported for MA-free DJ quasi-2D PSCs. The unencapsulated device with CDTA maintains 92% of its initial PCE after aging under one sun illumination for 360 h and 86% after aging at 60 °C for 360 h.

KEYWORDS: perovskite solar cells, Dion-Jacobson perovskite, methylammonium-free, orientation modulation, defect passivation

# 1. INTRODUCTION

Organic–inorganic hybrid perovskite light-harvesting semiconductors have emerged as one of the most promising next-generation photovoltaic materials due to their advantages, such as high light-absorption coefficient, tunable band gaps, long exciton diffusion lengths, high carrier mobility, low exciton binding energy, low cost, solution processability, etc.  $  ^{1}  $   The perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have witnessed a great advancement in power conversion efficiency (PCE) from initial 3.8%  $  ^{2}  $   to presently certified 25.5%.  $  ^{3}  $   Although high PCE has been achieved, the poor environmental stability of three-dimensional (3D) perovskites, which are normally sensitive to moisture, temperature, and oxygen, still impedes the commercial application of 3D PSCs.  $  ^{4}  $   In the past several years, excellent environmental stability has been demonstrated in two-dimensional (2D) or quasi-2D PSCs, which was fabricated by incorporating bulky organic cations into 3D perovskite lattices.  $  ^{5,6}  $   The hydrophobic property of large-size organic cations in 2D perovskites should be responsible for the improved environmental stability. To date, over 19% PCE was reported based on RP 2D PSCs.  $  ^{7}  $   However, the obtained highest efficiency of RP 2D PSCs is still much lower than that of 3D counterparts. There are two interdigitated organic spacers between adjacent corner-sharing   $  [PbI_{6}]^{4-}  $   sheets in 2D perovskites, and van der Waals gaps are produced between two bulky organic insulating spacers.  $  ^{8,9}  $   The weak van der Waals interaction between neighboring inorganic corner-sharing   $  [PbI_{6}]^{4-}  $   sheets is not only detrimental to structural integrity and thus stability but also is not conducive to carrier transport and transfer between neighboring inorganic layers because of creating deep quantum wells.  $  ^{8,9}  $   Consequently, the van der Waals gap between neighboring inorganic layers poses a serious challenge for the further enhancement of PCE and stability of RP 2D PSCs. In contrast, there is no van der Waals gap between neighboring inorganic layers in DJ perovskites since only a single layer of insulating diammonium organic cation is inserted between inorganic layers.  $  ^{8-10}  $   Under the circumstances, the neighboring inorganic layers in DJ 2D perovskites are connected through hydrogen bond interaction instead of weak van der Waals interaction. Therefore, better structural stability due to strong hydrogen bond interaction and charge transfer ability due to the reduced insulating spacer distance are expected for the DJ 2D perovskites as compared to RP analogues. As we expected, DJ 2D PSCs have been tested to exhibit better stability and higher PCE in comparison with RP counterparts.  $  ^{11,12}  $   In recent several years, vast efforts have been devoted to improve the efficiency and stability of DJ 2D PSCs.  $  ^{8-14}  $   In spite of great advancements, there is still large room in PCE and stability enhancement for DJ 2D PSCs.

It is well known that poor thermal and light stability along with an enlarged band gap hinder the further increase of PCE and stability of 2D PSCs. Currently, most reported 2D PSCs are at the expense of sacrificing current density owing to the enlarged band gap resulting from the incorporation of large-size organic cations. Consequently, it is highly desirable to reduce the band gap via composition engineering. It was extensively demonstrated that the band gap can be effectively reduced through the replacement of MA $  ^{+}  $  by FA $  ^{+}  $ . $  ^{15,16}  $  Although hydrophobic nature of 2D perovskites endows 2D PSCs with outstanding moisture stability, most 2D perovskites reported up to now incorporate volatile MA cations into their lattices, which would result in poor thermal and light stability because MA $  ^{+}  $  was demonstrated to easily volatilize and escape from the perovskite crystal lattices. $  ^{17,18}  $  It was revealed that MAPbI $  _{3}  $  would easily decompose at the temperature as low as 80 °C. $  ^{17,19}  $  Furthermore, MAPbI $  _{3}  $  possesses a low phase change temperature of 55 °C from the tetragonal to cubic phase. $  ^{18}  $  The substitution of MA $  ^{+}  $  with FA $  ^{+}  $  was widely certified to be able to prominently increase the thermal and light stability of PSCs. $  ^{19-21}  $  In a word, the incorporation of FA $  ^{+}  $  into the perovskite composition can not only broaden the absorption spectrum range but also improve the thermal and light stability. Although FA $  ^{+}  $  has been widely incorporated into the 3D perovskite composition, the work on the application of FA $  ^{+}  $  in 2D perovskites is still highly limited up to now. $  ^{7,8,14}  $  Recently, Cheng et al. $  ^{8}  $  reported a DJ 2D perovskite PDAFA $  _{3}  $ Pb $  _{4}  $ I $  _{13}  $  for solar cells, which achieved a PCE of 13.8% together with superior thermal stability. This indicates that the fabrication of DJ FA-based 2D PSCs is critical to simultaneously realize efficient and stable 2D PSCs.

To date, it is still a huge challenge to prepare phase-pure 2D perovskite films. The phase of 2D perovskites in most works reported for 2D PSCs was not pure.  $  ^{22-24}  $   It was reported that

rational modulation of multiple perovskite phases was beneficial for promoting carrier transport/extraction and suppressing Shockley–Read–Hall (SRH) charge recombination due to favorable energy band alignment.  $  ^{22-24}  $   Therefore, it is imperative to rationally control the phase distribution of 2D or quasi-2D perovskite films for the purpose of improving carrier transport and extraction and accordingly inhibiting SRH nonradiative recombination. It needs to be noted that phase distribution requirement should be adverse for normal n-i-p PSCs and inverted p-i-n PSCs. Presently, almost all works related to phase distribution modulation were focused on inverted PSCs. In consequence, phase distribution control in normal PSCs could be more challenging. In addition to phase distribution, orientation of 2D perovskites is another important and key factor which affects carrier transport and collection.  $  ^{7}  $   The 2D perovskites would produce natural multiple-quantum-well structures because of the relatively low dielectric constant of the organic insulating spacers.  $  ^{7,10}  $   The organic layers act as potential “barriers” and the inorganic   $  [PbX_{6}]^{4-}  $   sheets act as potential “wells.” The multiple-quantum-well structures would hinder carrier transport and thereby lead to carrier recombination due to the quantum confinement effect. Since carriers transport via the inorganic “wells,” it is of great importance for 2D perovskite films to grow and orientate perpendicular to the substrate for the sake of facilitating carrier transport and collection. Apart from phase distribution and orientation, a large number of defects and/or traps, which would be generated inevitably in 2D perovskite films during high temperature annealing and fast crystallization, also have a great effect on carrier transport and collection by resulting in SRH nonradiative recombination losses.  $  ^{1,25}  $   In short, phase distribution, crystal orientation, and defects are three critical factors which affect carrier transport and collection and accordingly PCE as well as stability. It has been widely demonstrated that additive engineering is a feasible and effective strategy in controlling phase distribution, modulating crystal orientation, or passivating defects.  $  ^{7,10,26,27}  $   Presently, most reported additive molecules can either control phase distribution or modulate crystal orientation, such as   $  NH_{4}SCN  $  ,  $  ^{27,28}  $   dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO),  $  ^{29}  $     $  NH_{4}Cl  $  , and   $  H_{2}O  $  ,  $  ^{26}  $   etc. Nevertheless, these additive molecules cannot passivate film defects because they easily volatilize and leave the final 2D perovskite films. Consequently, it is a huge challenge but extremely important to develop a multifunctional additive molecule which can simultaneously control phase distribution, modulate crystal orientation, and passivate defects.

In this work, we reported a multifunctional additive molecule, namely, 1,1'-carbonyldi(1,2,4-triazole) (denoted as CDTA) for fabricating high-quality MA-free DJ quasi-2D perovskite films. Upon the incorporation of CDTA, phase distribution controlling, crystal orientation modulation, and defect passivation are achieved simultaneously, which translated to improved PCE and stability. The CDTA-modified device delivered a promising PCE of 16.07%, which is much higher than 13.77% of the control device. The unencapsulated device modified by CDTA maintains 94% of its initial PCE after aging under the relative humidity range of 15–20% for 1080 h, 92% after aging under one sun illumination for 360 h, and 86% after aging at 60 °C for 360 h.

# 2. MATERIALS AND METHODS

2.1. Materials. All chemical reagents were used as received without further purification. The   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloid precursor was purchased from Alfa Aesar (tin(IV) oxide, 15% in   $  H_{2}O  $   colloidal dispersion). Ammonium chloride (  $  NH_{4}Cl  $  , 99.99%) was purchased from Aladdin. Lead(II) iodide (  $  PbI_{2}  $  , 99.99%) and 1,4-diaminobutane dihydroiodide (BDADI, 99.5%) were obtained from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. Spiro-OMeTAD (99.86%) were bought from Advanced Election Technology Co., Ltd. Formamidine hydroiodide (FAI) was purchased from GreatCell Solar. N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.9%), and chlorobenzene (CB, 99.8%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

2.2. Device Fabrication. Indium tin oxide (ITO) conductive glass substrates were etched through laser. The etched ITO glass was cleaned ultrasonically using detergent, deionized water, and ethanol for 20 min, respectively. The   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloidal solutions were prepared by diluting   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloidal solution of 15 to 1.875 wt % by using deionized water. Subsequently, KCl was added into the above colloidal solution where the concentration of KCl was 1 mg/mL. After exposing ITO substrates to UV ozone (UVO) for 20 min, the   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloidal solution was spin-coated on the ITO substrates at 3000 rpm for 30 s and then annealed at 150 °C for 30 min. After the films cooled down to room temperature,   $  SnO_{2}  $   films were treated by UVO for 20 min. A 0.6 M perovskite precursor solution (nominal perovskite composition was (BDA)  $  FA_{4}Pb_{5}I_{16}  $  ) was prepared by dissolving BDADI of 41.28 mg, FAI of 82.54 mg,   $  NH_{4}Cl  $   of 10.70 mg, and   $  PbI_{2}  $   of 276.61 mg in the mixed solvents of DMF and DMSO (  $  V_{DMF}:V_{DMSO}=9:1  $  ). The perovskite films were fabricated by spin-coating the perovskite precursor solution on the   $  SnO_{2}  $   films preheated at 70 °C at 5000 rpm for 25 s where 100 μL CB was dripped on the perovskite films at 7 s before ending the program. The perovskite film was annealed at 150 °C for 15 min. Spiro-OMeTAD (72.3 mg) was dissolved in 1 mL CB to spiro-OMeTAD solution. Subsequently, 28.8 μL of 4-tert-butyl pyridine and 17.5 μL of lithium bis-(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (Li-TFSI) stock solution (520 mg Li-TSFI in 1 mL acetonitrile) were added into the above solution. Finally, a 80 nm thick gold counter electrode was deposited under a high vacuum of   $  3\times10^{-4}  $   Pa through a shadow mask.

2.3. Characterizations. J-V curves were obtained by a solar simulator equipped with a 150 W xenon lamp (150 W, SolarIV-150A) and a Keithley 2400 SourceMeter. Light intensity was calibrated to AM 1.5G one sun (100 mW/cm  $  ^{2}  $  ) with a NIM calibrated standard Si

solar cell (QE-B1). The devices were covered with a black nonreflective metal mask to give an active area of   $  0.07 \, cm^2  $  . J-V curves were recorded from -0.1 to 1.2 V (forward scan (FS)) or from 1.2 to -0.1 V (reverse scan (RS)) at a scan rate of 100 mV/s. The incident photon-to-electron conversion efficiency curves were collected on an incident photon-current conversion efficiency (IPCE) measurement system. Impedance spectroscopy measurement was carried out on an electrochemical workstation (China). UV–vis absorption spectrum measurement was carried out on a UV–vis spectrometer (Shimadzu UV-1800, Japan). The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were measured by using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (JSM-7800F, Japan). The Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra were measured using a Nicolet iS50 Infrared Fourier transform microscope by Thermo Fisher Scientific (America). Steady-state photoluminescence (SSPL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectra were collected through using Edinburgh FLS1000 (UK). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectrum measurement was performed on a Thermo Fisher ESCALAB 250Xi system with a monochromatized Al Kα (for XPS mode) under a pressure of   $  5.0 \times 10^{-7}  $   Pa. TPV and TPC measurements were carried out as follows: the devices were illuminated by laser pulses (532 nm, 100 mJ, 6 ns width from an Nd:YAG laser) and then the decay of signals was measured by a 1 GHz Agilent digital oscilloscope (DSO-X3102A) with an input impedance of 1 MΩ/50 Ω. X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra were obtained with a PANalytical Empyrean (Netherlands). Two-dimensional grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) images were collected on BL1W1A at the Beijing Synchrotron Radiation Facility (BSRF) (  $  \lambda = 1.54 \, \AA  $  ).

Subsequently, XPS and FTIR were carried out to investigate the chemical interaction between CDTA and perovskites. As shown in Figure 2a, the peaks of Pb 4f7/2 (137.5 eV) and Pb 4f5/2 (142.4 eV) in the control perovskite film were shifted to lower binding energies of 137.4 and 142.3 eV after CDTA modification, respectively, which suggested strong chemical interaction between the CDTA molecule and undercoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   in the perovskite composition as we expected. The XPS shift tendency is consistent with the previous reports.  $  ^{[31,32]}  $   The CDTA sample exhibited a vibration peak at 1764.6 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   assigned to -C=O, which was shifted to a higher wavenumber of 1770.4 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   for the CDTA-modified perovskite film (Figure 2b and Figure S4). By contrast, no peak of -C=O can be observed in the control perovskite film. This indicated that -C=O in the CDTA molecule coordinated with undercoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   in the perovskite film. The vibration peak (1638.7 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  ) of -C=N of the triazole ring in the CDTA molecule was shifted to a higher wavenumber of 1644.5 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   in the CDTA-modified perovskite film whereas no peak corresponding to -C=N was found in the control perovskite film as shown in (Figure 2c and Figure S4). It showed that -C=N in the CDTA molecule coordinated with undercoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   in the perovskite film.

Top-view SEM images of the control and target perovskite films deposited on the   $  SnO_{2}  $  -coated ITO substrates are illustrated in Figure S5a,b. It can be seen clearly that grain size was increased after CDTA modification, resulting in reduced GB density. From the cross-sectional SEM images of the control and target devices, a slightly improved cross-section was observed after CDTA modification, as exhibited in Figure S5c,d. XRD measurement was performed to study the effect of CDTA modification on the crystal structure and crystallinity of perovskite films. As illustrated in Figure 2d, the control and target perovskite films exhibited the same main characteristic diffraction peaks at   $  2\theta  $   values of 14.42 and   $  28.53^{\circ}  $  , corresponding to the (111) and (202) crystal planes of cubic perovskite, respectively. It was found that the intensity of diffraction peaks corresponding to the (111) and (202) crystal planes was slightly increased after CDTA modification. Full width at half maximum (FWHM) of (111) and (202) diffraction peaks was reduced from 0.225 and 0.171 of the control perovskite film to 0.115 and 0.111 of the target one, respectively (Table S5). The above results implied that crystallinity of the perovskite film was ameliorated upon the incorporation of the CDTA additive, which was conducive to carrier transport and collection. GIWAXS was performed to gain insights into the orientation of perovskite films without and with CDTA modification, as depicted in Figure 2e,f. The CDTA-modified perovskite film exhibited brighter scattering rings corresponding to the (111) and (202) planes of the perovskite and more discrete diffraction spots compared with the control perovskite film, which was indicative of improved orientation after CDTA modification. Improved crystallinity and crystal orientation should be favorable for carrier transport and collection.

It has been reported that this reverse-graded quantum well (QW) distribution can improve moisture resistance, form type-II band alignment between different phases, and expand the range of photon collection due to the natural structure of graded QWs.  $  ^{33}  $   In addition, the amount of small n value perovskite phases was increased while the amount of the 3D perovskite phase was reduced after CDTA modification when the perovskite films were excited from the top (Figure 3a). By contrast, the amount of small n value perovskite phases was similar while the amount of the 3D perovskite phase was increased after CDTA modification when the perovskite films were excited from the bottom (Figure 3b). The above results indicated that more favorable band alignment was formed after CDTA modification. The phase distribution reported in inverted PSCs was opposite to our work. This work suggested that regular and inverted PSCs had a different requirement for phase distribution from the perspective of carrier transport and extraction.

Improved light-harvesting efficiency should be partially responsible for the slightly increased   $  J_{SC}  $   and IPCE. # 4. CONCLUSIONS

In summary, we have demonstrated a multifunctional additive strategy for fabricating high-quality MA-free DJ quasi-2D perovskite films, where CDTA molecules simultaneously containing the carbanyl group and triazole ring are incorporated into the perovskite precursor solution. Upon the incorporation of CDTA, multiple functions have been achieved, including regulating phase distribution, increasing grain size, modulating crystallinity and crystal orientation, and passivating defects. After CDTA modification, more favorable gradient phase distribution and accordingly gradient band alignments are formed, which is conducive to carrier transport, transfer, and extraction. The improved crystallinity and crystal orientation can facilitate carrier transport and collection. The enlarged grain size and effective defect passivation contribute to reduced defect density. The above multiple functions enable the realization of simultaneous enhancement of PCE and stability. As a result, the CDTA-modified device delivers a PCE of as high as 16.07%, which is one of the highest PCEs ever reported for MA-free DJ quasi-2D PSCs. The unencapsulated device modified by CDTA maintains 94% of its initial PCE after aging under the relative humidity range of 15–20% for 1080 h, 92% after aging under one sun illumination for 360 h, and 86% after aging at 60 °C for 360 h. This work provides an

effective strategy for developing a multifunctional molecule modulator toward efficient and operationally stable PSCs.
