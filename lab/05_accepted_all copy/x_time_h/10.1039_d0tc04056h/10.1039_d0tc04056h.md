# Abstract

Scientific techniques on perovskite solar cells have been significantly developed since the emergence of organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite materials. An antisolvent strategy for preparing perovskite films has been widely used, which is important to improve the photovoltaic performance. In this work, an appropriate amount of diethyl ether (DE), which is usually used as an antisolvent, is added into the perovskite precursor to promote the crystallization of perovskite throughout the entire process. Due to the fast evaporation rate and Lewis base nature of DE, a quicker nuclei formation and retarded nuclei growth of the perovskite can be induced. Consequently, the quality of the perovskite film is improved with enlarged grain size and enhanced crystallinity. Based on this technique, the optimized perovskite solar cells exhibit an improved 19.2% power conversion efficiency (PCE), whereas the control devices only have a lower PCE of 16.8%. In addition, the environmental stability of the perovskite solar cells is also enhanced due to the decreased intrinsic defects of the perovskite film. After 500 h storage in ambient air (approximately 25 °C and 30% relative humidity) without encapsulation, the developed group maintains about 84% of their initial PCE, which is higher than that (77%) of control group.

# 1. Introduction

Nowadays, organic-inorganic halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been developed into a popular and promising candidate for new and clean energy solutions due to a series of desirable characteristics which include an easy fabrication process, facile solution-processability, strong light absorption, mechanical flexibility, etc.  $  ^{1-4}  $   Within several years of rapid development, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of perovskite photovoltaics has increased from 3.8% to a certified 25.2% under countless efforts of researchers all over the world.  $  ^{5-10}  $   At the same time, environmental stability and mass production efficiency have been significantly improved, which increases the prospects of future commercialization.

Nevertheless, perusing a better perovskite film quality remains an important topic of interest. Therefore, a variety of film formation technologies has been developed to achieve higher perovskite crystal qualities, such as solution method, chemical vapor deposition, atomic layer deposition, and thermal gas-assisted evaporation method. $ ^{11-17} $  In a laboratory condition, the solution method turns into the simplest and most time-saving fabrication process among the film formation techniques, achieving high perovskite crystal quality. $ ^{18-22} $  To obtain a pin-hole free and homogeneous perovskite film using this method, rapid nuclei formation and retarded crystal growth are critical factors which should be carefully controlled in the experimental operations. $ ^{23-25} $  Generally, the spin-coating process involves several stages as follows. $ ^{1} $  Firstly, the prepared perovskite precursor is dropped and spread over the substrate. Secondly, the spin-coater is accelerated and a major part of the dropped precursor is thrown off due to the centrifugal force. Thirdly, an antisolvent is dropped on the substrate to quickly remove the maintained solvent. Fourthly, all constituents that cannot be dissolved by antisolvent are left to form a dense and uniform layer. Finally, a heating process at approximately 100  $ ^{\circ} $ C converts the residual complex into a high-quality perovskite film. Generally, during the one-step spin-coating process, the use of antisolvent is important to generate a high-quality perovskite layer. $ ^{26} $  Several parameters related with antisolvent can obviously affect the final quality of perovskite films, such as the categories of antisolvents, $ ^{18} $  the time point of dropping antisolvent, $ ^{25} $  and the operating temperature of antisolvent. $ ^{24} $  This intrinsic principle can be understood by

analyzing the working mechanism of antisolvent which acts as a driving force to accelerate the supersaturated state during the crystal formation process.  $  ^{24}  $   Usually antisolvents are miscible with solvent, such as N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), and dimethylsulphoxide (DMSO), but insoluble for the raw materials for perovskite (such as lead iodide (PbI  $  _{2}  $  ) and Methylammonium iodide (MAI)). Hence, antisolvents can quickly remove the residual solvent from the samples, thus, forming a dense and homogenous perovskite film.  $  ^{27}  $   Diethyl ether (DE) is a widely used antisolvent for preparing perovskite films, which exhibits a quick volatilization during the spin-coating process.  $  ^{28-32}  $   It is reported that using DE as the antisolvent is effective to obtain high-quality perovskite with the optimized PCE of 19.7%.  $  ^{28}  $   However, many works only focused on the antisolvent casting step during the spin-coating process, which means the function of adding antisolvent is focused on removing residual solvent of perovskite precursor. Researches on adding the antisolvent into perovskite precursor and the related crystallization processes are rare, so far. The intrinsic influence on the perovskite precursor caused by adding antisolvent, such as nuclei formation ratio, has not been sufficiently studied. Especially, unlike other popular antisolvents such as chlorobenzene and toluene, DE behaves as a Lewis base, which can form Lewis acid-base adducts during crystallization of perovskite. Therefore, it is essential to study the influence of the antisolvent DE throughout all the crystal formation process of perovskite.

In this study, we introduced a method for adding DE antisolvent into perovskite precursor solution to prepare the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  based PSCs. This strategy allows an entire process of crystallization management by adding the antisolvent into a precursor which begins its influence at the initial step of perovskite crystallization. Furthermore, DE has a higher volatility property than DMF and DMSO, which can accelerate the evaporation rate of host solvent and promote the nucleation of perovskite.[33] The weak Lewis base nature of DE molecules can retard the growth of perovskite grains by forming coordination bonding with lead iodide. Consequently, we obtained an excellent device performance of PSCs with PCE of  $ 19.2\% $ , open-circuit voltage ( $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $ ) of  $ 1.09\mathrm{V} $ , short circuit current ( $ J_{\mathrm{sc}} $ ) of  $ 22.6\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $ , and fill factor (FF) of 0.78. Moreover, the antisolvent mixed precursor (AMP) based devices exhibited an enhanced

# 2.2 Device fabrication

Glass substrates coated with ITO bar as electrode were cleaned sequentially with detergent, deionized water, and isopropanol (IPA). The cleaned substrates were placed in ultraviolet-ozone (UVO) for 20 min. The   $  SnO_{2}  $   precursor was diluted with deionized water into a concentration of 2.67%. The prepared   $  SnO_{2}  $   precursor was dropped on ITO glass and spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 30 s in ambient air, followed with a thermal annealing at 150 °C for 25 min. Various volume of DE was mixed into DMF to form a mixed solvent with concentration of 0%, 5%, 10%, 20%, and 30% by volume. The   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   was prepared by mixing   $  PbI_{2}  $   and MAI in molar ratio of 1:1 and dissolved with corresponding mixed solvent to form various concentration, which was stirred for 8 h at 60 °C. # 3. Results and discussion

A regular device architecture was applied with   $  SnO_{2}  $   as the electron-transport layer and spiro-OMeTAD as the HTL (Fig. S1). As described above, we added DE into DMF to make a mixed solvent with concentrations of 0%, 5%, 10%, 20%, and 30% before preparing the perovskite precursor to study the optimized mixing ratio. As demonstrated in Fig. S2, the mixed solvent with DE concentration of 30% cannot fully dissolve the perovskite raw materials (complex of MAI and   $  PbI_{2}  $  ). This is because incorporating DE can decrease the solubility of solute and an excess ratio of DE leads to an insufficient dissolution of solute. Therefore, the decreased solubility can affect film thickness under the same spin-coating parameter. Note that adding an appropriate amount of DE, such as 10%, will not lead to the separation of small crystals out of the solution; as shown in Fig. S3, the perovskite precursor mixed with 10% DE is transparent without any precipitation after storing for 60 days. As shown in cross-sectional SEM images (Fig. 1a to d), the film thickness increases gradually from 400 nm to 550 nm with the DE concentration increasing from 0% to 20% in precursor. Obviously, when retaining the same parameters in the spin-coating process, the perovskite film thickness will increase with the raising of DE mixing ratio. This is due to the fact that adding DE makes it easier to reach the saturated condition because DE is an antisolvent with poor solubility for the precursor salt and extracts DMF with high evaporation rate. Furthermore, the accelerated evaporating rate for the residual precursor on substrate during the spin-coating process also contributes to the increased film thickness. However, the UV absorption results shown in Fig. S4 also confirmed this phenomenon (increased film thickness) by exhibiting an obviously enhanced absorption signal. Notably, the enhanced UV absorption can be summarized into two points: the increased film thickness and improved perovskite film quality, which will be discussed later. This could bring a new approach for low-cost fabrication in experiment and production. To obtain the same thickness (approximately 450 nm) of perovskite film, we carefully tuned the concentration for each DE assisted groups. Thus, 1.2 M, 1.15 M, 1.1 M, and 1.0 M were applied for the precursor with DE

The high volatility of DE is considered to impact the crystallization of perovskite during the spin-coating process (Table S1). Generally, the perovskite crystal formation process can be divided into two steps which involve nuclei formation (occurring at the early stage) and nuclei growth (occurring at the later stage).  $  ^{25}  $   A quicker nuclei formation is desirable for better crystal quality of perovskite.  $  ^{23-25}  $   To sufficiently understand this process, we proposed a model, as shown in Fig. 2. After dropping the precursor on the substrate, the spin-coater starts spinning and drops off the majority of solution. Before dropping antisolvent, the evaporation of residual solvent takes some time (usually several seconds). During this initial stage of spin-coating process, due to the fast evaporation rate of DE, the AMP presents a quicker evaporation rate, which leads to higher initial nuclei density which benefits the crystal formation of perovskite.  $  ^{24}  $

However, DE is found to be one category of Lewis base,  $  ^{34}  $  which could coordinate with Lewis acid to form the adducts. Due to the weak Lewis base nature of DE, it could have some weak acting force with the Lewis of  $  PbI_{2}^{28, 35, 36}  $ , forming an adduct (  $  PbI_{2}\cdot DE  $  ). FTIR spectra were measured to study the adduct of  $  PbI_{2}\cdot DE  $  (see Supporting Information for the details). As exhibited in Fig. S5, the peak intensity for the C–O bond might be weakened by the coordinate bonding between the O atom and  $  Pb^{2+}  $ , suggesting the existence of  $  PbI_{2}\cdot DE  $  adduct. To clearly understand this process, schematic models are shown in Fig. 3, where the  $  PbI_{2}  $  crystal is formed at the early stage. Next, the DE molecule is deduced to integrate with  $  PbI_{2}  $  crystal to form a  $  PbI_{2}  $ -based Lewis adducts which continuously form a MAI– $  PbI_{2}  $ –Sol complex, where “Sol” means the solvent molecules (DMF and DE), for the surrounding of MAI and DMF. Finally, with the residual solvent molecules removed by further annealing treatment, the MAI– $  PbI_{2}  $ –Sol complex is subsequently transformed into  $  MAPbI_{3}  $  crystal, which corresponds to the transition from step 3 to step

4 in Fig. 3. We inferred that the formation of   $  PbI_{2}\cdot DE  $  , at the early stage of spin coating, can retard the nuclei growth of perovskite, which can be proved by later experiment results. Note that there is a crucial moment, i.e., turbid point, when the precursor coated substrate turns from transparent to turbid during the spin-coating process. This turbid point can reflect the situation of crystal growth, from which the crystal growth rate can be estimated.  $  ^{[25]}  $   In the interval of the spin-coating process without antisolvent treatment, the substrate deposited with 10% DE contained precursor exhibited about 5 s delayed turbid point on average compared to the normal precursor (Fig. S6), which can be the proof for the retard crystal growth. The delay in turbid point remained unchanged when the mixing ratio of DE was increased to 20%, as listed in Table S2, while it was 3 s for the group with 5% DE. Therefore, adding DE into perovskite precursor could lead to a retard nuclei growth by forming an adduct of   $  PbI_{2}\cdot DE  $  , which contributed to the improvement of perovskite quality.

This environmental stability improvement is mainly attributed to the quality-improved perovskite film, which comprise a lower quantity of defects effectively suppressing the degradation rate.  $  ^{45}  $

# 4. Conclusions

In summary, we adopted DE as crystallization assisted solvent to tune the process of perovskite layer formation, including nuclei formation, and crystal growth. It is found that mixing DE into precursor can lead to a rapid nuclei formation and retarded crystal growth, which benefits a better perovskite crystal quality. Finally, we obtained a crystal quality enhanced perovskite film with the optimized mixing ratio of 10%. The SEM, UV–vis absorption and XRD results showed the enhanced crystallinity of perovskite film. The PL and EIS analysis indicate the suppressed traps inside the perovskite film. Consequently, the excellent PSC exhibited a PCE of 19.2%, a   $  J_{sc}  $   of 22.6 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , a   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.09 V, and a FF of 0.78. However, the PSCs deposited with original precursor presented a lower photovoltaic performance with PCE,   $  J_{sc}  $  ,   $  V_{oc}  $  , and FF of 16.8%, 21.4 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , 1.06 V, and 0.74, respectively. Moreover, the environmental stability is improved due to the incorporation of DE into precursor. After the 500 h of storage in ambient at 25 °C without encapsulation, the experimental group retained approximately 84% of the initial PCE which is 77% higher than control group.
