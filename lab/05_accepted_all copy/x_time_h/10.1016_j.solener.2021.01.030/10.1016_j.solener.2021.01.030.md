# 1. Introduction

In the last decade, organic/inorganic lead halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted great attention because of their facile fabrication and excellent photovoltaic performance. So far, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs has rapidly increased from  $ 3.8\% $  to  $ 25.5\% $  (Kojima et al., 2009; best-research-cell-efficiencies.20200925.pdf). Among the organic parts, methylammonium  $ (\mathrm{MA}^{+}) $  and formamidinium  $ (\mathrm{FA}^{+}) $  are popular for their best performance (Zhou et al., 2016). However,  $ \mathrm{MA}^{+} $ and  $ \mathrm{FA}^{+} $ molecules have low volatilization temperature, which limits the thermal stability of the device (Steele et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2018). In order to conquer this problem,  $ \mathrm{Cs}^{+} $ was used to replace the volatile organic ions (Zhang et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2018a, b; Li et al., 2018). A series of all-inorganic perovskite materials were proposed such as  $ \mathrm{CsPbBr_3} $ ,  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $ ,  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $ , and  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_3} $ , which all have high-temperature stability and superior optical properties (Yang et al., 2020a,b,c,d). Among them,  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  is one of the most potential absorption material for PSCs due to balance bandgap and phase stability than the others. All-inorganic perovskite materials, typically   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $  , have received widespread attention owing to their outstanding thermal stability than that of organic/inorganic hybrid counterparts. However, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite solar cell (PSC) is far lower than its theoretical value. In this study, to further improve the PCE, a sulfur-rich small molecule material (delta-2:2-bis(1,3-dithiazole)), is used to modify the interface between   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   and carbon electrode. Encouragingly, the carbon-based   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   PSCs achieve a high PCE of 13.78% than the control of 10.40%, which is the best performance of carbon-based   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   PSC among the literature report at present. The remarkable reduction of defect density and suppression recombination should be responsible for the PCE improvement. This work proposes a simple and effective strategy to enhance the efficiency of all-inorganic carbon-based   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   PSCs.

From perovskite materials itself view, partial replace lead ions by small metal ions such as   $  Ca^{2+}  $  ,   $  Mn^{2+}  $  ,   $  Sr^{2+}  $  ,   $  Sb^{3+}  $  ,   $  Bi^{3+}  $   and   $  Eu^{3+}  $   (Han et al., 2020; Ho-Baillie et al., 2019; Ke and Kanatzidis, 2019; Parida et al., 2020; Swarnkar et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2018) to obtain a more suitable tolerance factor value is a main method for improving the performance and stability of all-inorganic PSCs. Furthermore, interface engineering and additive engineering are also another efficient methods which can reduce trap density and suppress carrier recombination by interaction between representative materials and perovskite. Till now, the organic materials with functional components of sulfur atom, nitrogen atom, and ammonium such as thiols, phosphines, benzene-amine molecules, and delocalized organic cationic are reported to modify the surface of perovskites to effectively reduce the traps density and further suppress carrier recombination (Wang et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2019). To confirm this, the scanning electron microscope (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements were carried out to characterize the morphology of the perovskite layer without and with the TM modification. Fig. 1b and 1e show the SEM images of  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  films without and with TM modification, respectively. We can clearly observe that there are distinct grain boundaries in the pristine  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  film. However, after introducing TM, the surface of  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  become more compact and the grain boundaries become indistinguishable. We deduce that TM can effectively fill in grain boundaries of  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  surface by interaction between sulfur atom of TM and Pb ion for decreasing trap density just demonstrated in Fig. 1a. At the same time, the X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurement was applied to analyses the effect of surface modification on the crystallinity of perovskite films. In Fig. 2a, the peaks at 14.6, and  $ 29.5^{\circ} $  are assigned to the (100), and (200) crystal faces of cubic  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $ , respectively, which indicate that all the prepared samples are the pure cubic  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  phase without any impurity (Christodoulou et al., 2018; Duan et al., 2020). After TM modification, the peak intensity of  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  increase, and the FWHM values reduce (Table S1), which means the enhancement of  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  crystallinity. Therefore, the TM have a great effect on the crystal quality of the perovskite film which is consistent with the results of the SEM. Fig. 1c and 1f show the AFM height images ( $ 5\times 5\mu \mathrm{m} $ ) of two samples. The root-mean-square (RMS) roughness of  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  films without and with TM modification are  $ 14.0~\mathrm{nm} $  and  $ 12.2~\mathrm{nm} $ , respectively. The decrease of roughness should result from the healing of TM to defects of the grain boundaries and the surface of perovskite.

Fig. 2b-c shows the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements of  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  film with and without TM, the characteristic peak of S 2p was detected in  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  film with TM shown in Fig. 2b, indicating that TM located at grain boundary and surface of  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  film. Fig. 2c shows the changes of peak position of Pb 4f after TM modification. It clearly sees that the peaks of the elements represented by the red lines are all shifted to a higher binding energy since introducing TM. The peak position of  $ \mathrm{Pb}4\mathrm{f}_{5 / 2} $  shifts from  $ 143.5\mathrm{eV} $  to  $ 143.9\mathrm{eV} $  and the peak position of  $ \mathrm{Pb}4\mathrm{f}_{7 / 2} $  shifts from  $ 138.6\mathrm{eV} $  to  $ 138.9\mathrm{eV} $ . This fully proves that the TM successfully adheres to the surface of perovskite and interacts with the perovskite layer. The peaks of Cs 3d, I 3d, and Br 3d are in the Fig. S2.

Additionally, to understand the carrier dynamic process of PSCs with and without TM, we performed electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements in the dark at a  $ 0.6\mathrm{V} $  bias. Fig. 4a shows a typical Nyquist plots and the embedded part is an equivalent circuit diagram for fitting data. The intercept point with zero imaginary part at the high-frequency region is called series resistance  $ (R_{s}) $ , which mainly comes from external circuits and substrates resistance. The arc in the low-frequency region represents recombination resistance  $ (R_{rec}) $  and the chemical capacitance  $ (C_{rec}) $ , which mainly result from the charge transporting between the layers (Yang et al., 2019a,b; Almora et al., 2018; Hou et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2017). From Fig. 4a and its fitting data, it is clearly seen that the device with TM has a smaller  $ R_{s} $  and a larger  $ R_{rec} $  than that of counterpart. This proves that the existence of TM can effectively improve the charge transfer and suppress the charge recombination. Fig. 4b shows the dark  $ J - V $  curves of the devices with and without TM. We can clearly observe that the current leakage of the device reduced after adding TM, which is agreement with the EIS measurement. Therefore, modification of TM is the reason for improving PCE.

For further verification the improvement of defect density by TM, we have performed trap density and charge mobility tests of perovskite with and without TM. Space charge limiting current (SCLC) measurement method was used for single carrier devices (Ge et al., 2012; Nikolka et al., 2019; Singh and Shukla, 2019). Fig. 5 shows the dark current–voltage curves of hole-only device with a structure of ITO/NiO  $  _{x}  $  /CsPbI  $  _{2}  $  Br/Carbon. The curve is divided into three parts (Peng et al., 2017). The first part of the low bias position is used to calculate the conductivity in linear ohmic state, the middle part is used to estimate the trap density using the trap filling mechanism, and the third part of the high bias position is used to calculate the charge mobility (Fei and Wang,

2019; Ye et al., 2019). Linearly fit the line on both sides of the first kink point, and the voltage value at the intersection is trap-filled limit voltage   $  V_{TFL}  $  . Trap state density (  $  N_{t}  $  ) is calculated by   $  V_{TFL}  $  , the Eq. (2) is as follows,

$$
N _ {t} = \left(2 \varepsilon_ {0} \varepsilon_ {r} V _ {T F L}\right) / \left(q L ^ {2}\right)
$$

(2)

where   $  \varepsilon_{r}  $   is the relative dielectric constant (about 8.6) (Yang et al., 2017),   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   refers to the vacuum permittivity (  $  8.85 \times 10^{12}F m^{-1}  $  ), q is the electron charge, and L refers to the thickness of the perovskite layer (Fei and Wang, 2019; Yang et al., 2020a,b,c,d; Yang et al., 2019a,b).

The   $  V_{TFL}  $   of the perovskites with and without TM are 1.0 and 1.1 V, with calculated   $  N_{t}  $   of   $  3.81 \times 10^{15}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $   and   $  4.19 \times 10^{15}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  , respectively. This means that the TM can effectively reduce the trap density of perovskite, further suppressing the carrier recombination.

Long-term stability is one of the important parameter for evaluating device performance. Fig. 6a shows the stability data of device with and without TM. It is clear seen that the devices with TM can maintain  $ 80\% $  of the initial PCE after  $ 150\mathrm{h} $  storage under air condition at  $ 80^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  with a relative humidity (RH) of  $ 50\% $ , whereas the devices without TM have no photoelectric performance. Even though storage in  $ \mathbf{N}_2 $  condition (Fig. 6b) for two months, the devices with TM still demonstrate excellent stability, which can maintain the initial efficiency of  $ 80\% $ . However, the efficiency of the devices without TM just remains about  $ 50\% $  of its initial PCE.

# 4.1. Materials

The transparent conductive substrate ITO was purchased from Yingkou OPV Tech Co. Ltd.  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  colloid (tin(IV) oxide) precursor was purchased from Alfa Aesar. Ultra dry  $ \mathrm{SnCl}_2 $  (99.9%) was purchased from Wako. Co. We purchased Lead iodide (PbI2, 99.999%) and Cesium Bromide (CsBr, 99.0%) were obtained from TCI and used as received. Delta-2:2-bis (1,3-dithiazole) (97%) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and used as received. The remaining test-related reagents and materials were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

# 4.2. Device fabrication

The substrate of ITO/glass was sonicated with detergent and then was washed by deionized water, ethanol, acetone, isopropanol, and deionized water in an ultrasonic cleaner. And then treat the dried ITO/glass substrate in UV-ozone for 20 min. The   $  SnO_{2}  $   precursor was obtained by diluting the original   $  SnO_{2}  $   aqueous solution of 15% to 2.67%. The uniform   $  SnO_{2}  $   precursor solution was spin-coated on ITO/glass at 2000 rpm for 30 s. And then annealed at 150 °C for 30 min. 0.01 M   $  SnCl_{2}  $   solution was spin-coated on the   $  SnO_{2}  $   layer at 6000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 100 °C for 10 min and 180 °C for 1 h. The   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   inorganic perovskite absorbers were fabricated in glove box. First, the 1.0 M   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite precursor was prepared by dissolving CsBr and   $  PbI_{2}  $   with mole ratio of 1:1 in DMSO/DMF with volume ratio of 9:1 and then stirred until the clear solution was obtained. Then the perovskite precursor was spin-coated on the   $  SnO_{2}  $   layer by a two-step spin-coating

# 4.3. Device characterization

The  $ J - V $  curves were measured by Keithley 2450 interfaced with a xenon lamp (Bunko Keiki BSOX150LC) under AM 1.5 G conditions, 100 mW cm $ ^{-2} $  with a simulated sunlight instrument (SAN-EI ELECTRIC XES-40S1). The incident light intensity had been calibrated with a standard silicon crystal solar cell (Bunko keiki BS-520 S/N 353) and double check with data logger illuminometer (HD450, As One). The EIS were measured in the dark by using The Solartron Analytical 1255B at 0.6 V applied bias. The XRD patterns were tested from 10 to 50 at a scanning speed of 10 °s $ ^{-1} $  using an XRD (Rigaku Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) with Cu-Kα radiation ( $ \lambda\frac{1}{4} $  1. 54 056 Å). The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra (KRATOS, Japan) was used to determine the surface elemental composition of the hole-selective layer. The morphology of the devices were characterized by the field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) (JSM-6701, JEOL). The EQE was measured by monochromatic illumination (A 300 W Xenon arc lamp through Nikon G25 monochromatic equipped). All of the devices were measures using a black metal mask with area of 0.09 cm $ ^{2} $  to control the photovoltaic performance of the solar cells.
