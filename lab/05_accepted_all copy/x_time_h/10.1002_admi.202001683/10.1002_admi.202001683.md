# Toward Efficient and Stable Perovskite Solar Cells by 2D Interface Energy Band Alignment

Weiwei Wang, Zhenhuang Su, Bo Sun, Lei Tao, Hao Gu, Wei Hui, Qi Wei, Wei Shi, Xingyu Gao, Yingdong Xia,* and Yonghua Chen

Interfacial engineering is essential for facilitating carrier separation, charge extraction, and enhancing the stability in organic-inorganic perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Herein, a facile and effective method is demonstrated not only to tune the electronic performance of electron transporting layer (ETL) but also to passivate the defects at the interface between the ETL and perovskite. On the top of the tin(IV) oxide  $ (\mathrm{SnO}_2) $  ETL, butylammonium chloride (BACl) and lead(II) iodide  $ (\mathrm{PbI}_2) $  are introduced as interface to modify the ETL/perovskite interface. The PSCs with interface modified exhibit a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 21.15\% $ , compared to  $ 18.33\% $  for the device without interface modified. Such enhancement in efficiency is mainly attributed to a better energy band alignment, and the quality of perovskite films is improved through the interface modification, thus enhancing photogenerated charge extraction and leading to low charge carrier recombination at the interface of ETL/perovskite. Furthermore, the device with interface modified exhibits significant stability. This work provides an alternative strategy on the ETL/perovskite interface to obtain highly stable and efficient PSCs.

# 1. Introduction

Recently, researchers are committed to interfacial engineering to explore and address the stability of PSCs; several methods of efficient surface passivation have been adopted, for instance, using small molecule additives to cap the large grain boundaries (GBs), such as pyridine,  $  ^{[19,20]}  $   alkylphosphonic acid,   $  \omega  $  -ammonium chlorides,  $  ^{[21]}  $   ammonium chloride,  $  ^{[22]}  $   organic halide salt, phenethylammonium iodide (PEAI),  $  ^{[23]}  $   and phenylalkylamine.  $  ^{[24,25]}  $   Besides, a self-assembled monolayer (SAM) was introduced to modify the interface between electron transporting layer (ETL) and perovskite on the top of the ETL, such as dopamine,  $  ^{[26]}  $   4-pyridinecarboxylic acid (PA),  $  ^{[27]}  $   newly synthesized molecules with high dipole moments,  $  ^{[28]}  $   and protonated ethanolamine (EA).  $  ^{[29]}  $   Furthermore, lead(II) iodide (PbI  $  _{2}  $  ) has been used to passivate defects not only at the grain boundary, but also in the perovskite layer and on the surface.  $  ^{[21,30-33]}  $   Inspired by these improvements, we believe that a better understanding of the defect performance of perovskites can help improve passivation strategies, which improve the performance of PSCs.

Here, we develop an interface, butylammonium chloride (BACl) with   $  PbI_{2}  $  , for the ETL/perovskite interface to modify the perovskite polycrystalline films and suppress the surface defects of perovskite for efficient solar cells. Planar heterojunction structured PSCs based on   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{2.8}Br_{0.2}  $   achieve a high PCE of   $  >21\%  $  , as well as an excellent long-term stability which retains over 80% of its initial PCE after 3000 h in a   $  N_{2}  $   atmosphere. # 2. Results and Discussion

The planar heterojunction PSCs in this study are structured as indium tin oxide (ITO) /tin(IV) oxide (SnO $ _2 $ , 30 nm)/BACL:PbI $ _2 $  (10 nm)/perovskite (500 nm)/2,2',7,7'-tetrakis[N,N-di(4-methoxyphenyl)amino]-9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD) (100 nm)/MoO $ _3 $  (1 nm)/Ag (100 nm). Here the BACL:PbI $ _2 $  (0.9:0.02 mole ratio), dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), serves as an interface. The active layer MAPbI $ _{2.8} $ Br $ _{0.2} $  was

formed by a one-step spin-coating process from our previously reported methylammonium acetate (MAAc)-solvent-based perovskite precursor solutions under ambient air conditions,  $  ^{[34]}  $  and then followed by a solvent annealing process. Schematic illustration of the perovskite crystal growth is depicted in Figure 1a. The BACl:PbI  $  _{2}  $  film was investigated by means of X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements. As shown in Figure 1b, the pristine BACl film shows only ITO characteristic peaks at 21.31° and 30.32°, and the pristine PbI  $  _{2}  $  film shows the peak at 9.8°, which is associated with intermediate phase of the Pb halide–DMSO complex.  $  ^{[35]}  $  However, for the BACl:PbI  $  _{2}  $  film, the intermediate phase disappears, and the characteristic peaks of PbI  $  _{2}  $  appear at 12.7° and 25.4° as a result of a small amount of unreacted PbI  $  _{2}  $ . Significantly, a dominant peak appears at 2θ < 10° which is a characteristic feature of 2D perovskites. The UV–vis absorption spectrum and fluorescence spectrum show the peak at about 510 and 520 nm, respectively, in Figure S1a,b (Supporting Information). These results indicate the formation of 2D perovskites.

The surface scanning electron microscope (SEM) images are shown in Figure 1c. The perovskite film without (w/o) 2D perovskite interface shows a large number of pinholes, which would cause increased nonradiative recombination losses.  $  ^{[31]}  $   A relatively bright contrast is also observed, possibly because the film is less conductive and accumulates more charges.  $  ^{[29]}  $   In contrast, the pinholes of the perovskite film vanish after introducing the 2D perovskite interface, leading to a smooth morphology with large grains. The atomic force microscopy (AFM) images of the perovskite films are shown in Figure S2 (Supporting Information) to investigate the changed surface morphology. After interface modification, the perovskite film showed more uniform characteristics than that without interface (root-mean-square (rms) roughness = 31.64 nm), and the rms surface roughness was 11.39 nm. The improved perovskite morphology demonstrates that the 2D perovskite interface layer can be used as a template to promote the growth of perovskite crystals, thereby improving the perovskite morphology and increasing the grain size.

Further crystallization of these films was demonstrated by 2D-grazing incidence X-ray diffraction (GIXRD) measurements based on synchrotron. In Figure 1d, the perovskite films with and without interfaces both show diffraction rings similar to the reported textured highly crystalline perovskite film.  $  ^{[37]}  $   As you can see from the figure, the intensity of the interface-modified perovskite at the 110 diffraction peak is weakened and the diffraction ring is more uniform compared with the perovskite without interface modification. These results indicate that the perovskite sacrifices part of the vertical orientation and makes a more uniform crystallographic orientation, which improved the perovskite film quality and reduced the density of trapped states; this lays a foundation for the high stability of perovskite film.  $  ^{[38-40]}  $   Furthermore, by comparing the q-dependent 1D-GIXRD spectrum around the perovskite (110) peak in Figure 1d, two (110) diffraction peaks at   $  q \approx 10.0 \, nm^{-1}  $   showed similar symmetrical intensity distributions (shown in Figure 1e), which proves that a highly crystalline film with high phase purity was formed in both cases. Besides, as shown in Figure S3 (Supporting Information), the contact angles of bare and BACl:PbI  $  _{2}  $  -modified SnO  $  _{2}  $   films are measured to be 59.6° and 20.7°, respectively. The reduced contact angle demonstrates that the perovskite precursor solution has better wettability, which makes it easier to form the perovskite film and obtain a uniform film.  $  ^{[41]}  $   On the other hand, the interface layer also assists the growth of the perovskite crystal faster, resulting in a more uniform and dense film.  $  ^{[42]}  $   The XRD patterns of the perovskite films in Figure 1f, respectively, present the crystallization of perovskite grown on bare and BACl:PbI  $  _{2}  $  -modified SnO  $  _{2}  $  -coated substrates. Perovskite crystals grow almost in the same direction on different substrates. According to previous reports, the diffraction peaks at 14.2° and 28.4° are assigned to the (110) and (220) planes of tetragonal crystal structure.  $  ^{[43]}  $   After introducing the interfacial layer, the diffraction peaks of the perovskite are significantly enhanced, indicating that the crystallinity of perovskite films is improved, which is consistent with the increase in crystal grains

in the SEM image. Higher crystallinity also means a fewer surface defects, which will benefit the performance of PSC.

As we all know, the electron mobility and defect density are obviously the key quality factors of PSCs. As shown in Figure S10a,b (Supporting Information), the space-charge-limited-current (SCLC) measurements with electron-only devices (ITO/SnO $ _2 $  or SnO $ _2 $  interface/perovskite/PCBM/LiF/Al) in the dark were employed to demonstrate the superior transport properties and low trap density. We calculated the electron mobility with interface film to be  $ 1.08 \times 10^{-3} $  cm $ ^2 $  V $ ^{-1} $  s $ ^{-1} $ , which is more than double of the film without interface ( $ 2.97 \times 10^{-4} $  cm $ ^2 $  V $ ^{-1} $  s $ ^{-1} $ ), by using the Mott-Gurney law described as

$$
J _ {\mathrm{D}} = \frac {9 \mu_ {\mathrm{e}} \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{r}} \varepsilon_ {0} V _ {\mathrm{b}} ^ {2}}{8 L ^ {3}}
$$

(3)

where   $  V_{b}  $   is the applied voltage,  $  ^{[55]}  $   L is the thickness of the perovskite layer (L = 514 nm),  $  ^{[56]}  $   J is the current density,   $  \varepsilon_{r}  $   is the dielectric constant (here   $  \varepsilon_{r} = 25.5  $  ),   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   is the vacuum permittivity (8.8542 × 10  $  ^{-11}  $   F cm  $  ^{-1}  $  ), and   $  \mu_{e}  $   is the electron mobility. The increase of   $  \mu_{e}  $   indicates that the thin film with interface does facilitate the electron transport properties of the ETL, which facilitates the charge transfer in solar cells. We suspect that the main reason for the increase in electron mobility is that the Fermi level of the charge transfer layer and the certificate layer is reduced after the interface layer is added. There is an energetic barrier at the ETL/perovskite interface, where charge carriers are confined. However, the band edge matching between ETL, interface, and the perovskite facilitates the charge transport in PSCs.

To exhibit the excellent optoelectronics of the perovskite with interface, PSCs with a planar heterojunction structure of ITO/SnO $ _2 $  (30 nm)/BACl:PbI $ _2 $  (10 nm)/perovskite (500 nm)/Spiro-OMeTAD (100 nm)/MoO $ _3 $  (1 nm)/Ag (100 nm) were fabricated. Equipment performance is estimated by recording

the current density–voltage (J-V) curve measured under simulated air quality 1.5 global (AM1.5G) solar radiation (Figure 4a). The PSCs with interface exhibit the highest PCE = 21.15%,   $  V_{OC} = 1.15  $   V,   $  J_{SC} = 23.08  $   mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and FF = 79.84%, which are much higher than the device without interface modified (18.33%). It is apparent due to the fact that 2D interface layer enables the energy band alignment, which facilitates the charge transfer and decreases charge accumulation at the interface of ETL/perovskite.  $  ^{[39,59]}  $   Apart from that, the device structures of ITO/SnO  $  _{2}  $  /interface/perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD/MoO  $  _{3}  $  /Ag with the BACl film, the PbI  $  _{2}  $   film, or the BACl:PbI  $  _{2}  $   film as the interface were evaluated to prove that an excellent result was obtained by using BACl:PbI  $  _{2}  $   as the interface. The efficiency of the obtained devices is listed in Table S2 (Supporting Information), making at least ten devices for per condition. Figure 4b presents the statistics and summary of device parameters of 100 independent PSC devices. The devices with interface exist with reproducible high performance, and an average   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.15 ± 0.02 V, an FF of 81.0 ± 2.4%, and a   $  J_{SC}  $   of 23.01 ± 0.32 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   are obtained, leading to an average efficiency of 20.72 ± 0.43%. The superior FF of 83.5% was obtained because of the larger uniform crystal grains and quite dense perovskite film. For control devices without interface, the average efficiency is 18.82 ± 0.53%, with a   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.05 ± 0.03 V, an FF of 78.2 ± 1.7%, and a   $  J_{SC}  $   of 22.5 ± 0.69 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , respectively.

Apart from improving device performance, the stability of the devices with interface is also improved. Figure 4c demonstrates the evolution in PCE of the unpackaged devices with interface modified with 82% of the initial device efficiency, and PSCs without interface modified with 54% of the original device after 2000 h. We also conducted air stability tests on the films with and without interfacial modification. After 6 months, the perovskite film without interface modification seriously decomposed compared with the perovskite film with interface modification (Figure 4d). This is because the interface modification allows the perovskite film to be uniform and dense, which further blocks the invasion of water and oxygen, and makes the film more stable. These results demonstrate that using BACl:PbI  $  _{2}  $  as an interface-modified film can largely enhance the stability of the device.

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, our work proposes a unique scheme to improve the stability and efficiency of PSCs via 2D interface consisted of BACl:PbI  $  _{2}  $  . The defect in the interface between the ETL and perovskite was modified and passivated simultaneously by 2D interface, which improves the electronic characteristics of ETL. Due to the favorable energy alignment of the interface layer, facilitating charge separation/collection, the interface recombination has been suppressed and improves the perovskite film quality with crystallinity. More importantly, the stability of the device can obviously be improved. This work confirmed the significant role of the interface between ETL and perovskite films for high-performance photovoltaic and provides a feasible guideline for interfacial engineering to obtain the PSCs with high performance and excellent stability.

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials' Preparation:   $  SnO_{2}  $   was purchased from Alfa Aesar. Methylammonium iodide (MAI), methylammonium bromide (MABr), Spiro-OMeTAD, and BACl (99.99%) was purchased from Youxuan Tech. Chlorobenzene (CB; 99.8%) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich). PbI  $  _{2}  $   (>98.0%), bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide lithium salt (Li-TFSI), 4-tert-butylpyridine (TBP), and Spiro-OMeTAD were purchased from TCI, Aldrich, Aladdin, and Shanghai Mater Win New Materials Co., Ltd, respectively.

Synthesis of MAAc: In a 250 mL round-bottom flask in an ice bath, glacial acetic acid (15.3 mL, 0.327 mol, Fisher Scientific) and methylamine (27.8 mL, 0.491 mol, 40% in ethanol, Aldrich) were stirred for 2 h. The resulting solution was then subjected to rotary evaporated for 1 h at 80 °C, and the resulting recovered solution was the synthetic liquid chemical, MAAc. The liquid product was put into a refrigerator for 2 h to crystallize. Then, the obtained solid product was washed three

times with ethyl ether and then dissolved in ethanol, and was recovered by rotating evaporation at   $  80\ °C  $   for 1 h. Finally, the resulting liquid was cooled to room temperature before use.

Precursor Solutions' Preparation: For  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_{2.8}\mathrm{Br}_{0.2} $ , MAI (83.32 mg), MABr (14.66 mg), and  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  (302.02 mg) were mixed in MAAc (1 mL) via stirring at  $ 60^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for 6 h. The molar ratio for MAI:MABr: $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  was 0.8:0.2:1.

For BACl:PbI $ _2 $ , BACl (20 mg) and PbI $ _2 $  (20 mg) were mixed in DMSO (1 mL) via stirring at 60 °C for 6 h. The molar ratio for BACl:PbI $ _2 $  was 0.9:0.02.

Device Fabrication: ITO substrates were cleaned using ultrasound with ethanol, detergent, deionized water, and acetone sequentially, followed by drying with   $  N_{2}  $   flow and then set aside in an oven. The   $  SnO_{2}  $   dilute in the ratio of 1:5 by ultrapure water was deposited onto ITO electrodes under 4000 rpm for 30 s, followed by heating for 30 min at 150 °C and UV–ozone treatment for 10 min. BACl (25 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) or BACl:PbI  $  _{2}  $   in DMSO solution was deposited on   $  SnO_{2}  $   by spin-coating (4000 rpm, 20 s). Perovskite was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 20 s under
