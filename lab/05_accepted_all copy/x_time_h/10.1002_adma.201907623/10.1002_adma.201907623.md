# Self-Repairing Tin-Based Perovskite Solar Cells with a Breakthrough Efficiency Over 11%

Chengbo Wang, Feidan Gu, Ziran Zhao, Haixia Rao, Yaming Qiu, Zelun Cai, Ge Zhan, Xiaoyue Li, Boxun Sun, Xiao Yu, Boqin Zhao, Zhiwei Liu,* Zuqiang Bian, and Chunhui Huang

The development of tin (Sn)-based perovskite solar cells (PSCs) is hindered by their lower power conversion efficiency and poorer stability compared to the lead-based ones, which arise from the easy oxidation of   $  Sn^{2+}  $   to   $  Sn^{4+}  $  . Herein, phenylhydrazine hydrochloride (PHCl) is introduced into   $  FASnI_{3}  $   (  $  FA = NH_{2}CH = NH_{2}^{+}  $  ) perovskite films to reduce the existing   $  Sn^{4+}  $   and prevent the further degradation of   $  FASnI_{3}  $  , since PHCl has a reductive hydrazino group and a hydrophobic phenyl group. Consequently, the device achieves a record power conversion efficiency of 11.4% for lead-free PSCs. Besides, the unencapsulated device displays almost no efficiency reduction in a glove box over 110 days and shows efficiency recovery after being exposed to air, due to a proposed self-repairing trap state passivation process.

Organic–inorganic hybrid perovskite materials have attracted considerable attention in the field of solar cells due to their excellent optical and electrical properties.  $  ^{[1-5]}  $   With the optimization of device structure and light absorbing layer, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of lead (Pb)-based perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has improved from the initial 3.8% to 25.2%.  $  ^{[6,7]}  $   However, the toxicity of lead limits their large-scale commercialization. Additionally, the optical bandgaps of Pb-based perovskites deviate from the optimal bandgap corresponding to the Shockley–Queisser limit.  $  ^{[8]}  $   Therefore, it is essential to replace lead with other elements, such as tin (Sn),  $  ^{[9]}  $   germanium (Ge),  $  ^{[10]}  $   indium (In),  $  ^{[11]}  $   copper (Cu),  $  ^{[12]}  $   antimony (Sb),  $  ^{[13]}  $   and bismuth (Bi).  $  ^{[14]}  $   Among the reported lead-free perovskites, the Sn-based ones have shown great potential owing to their appropriate optical bandgaps, small exciton binding energy, and superior charge carrier mobility.  $  ^{[15,16]}  $   However, Sn-based PSCs suffer from low PCE and poor stability due to the easy oxidation of   $  Sn^{2+}  $   to   $  Sn^{4+}  $ , which results in heavy p-doping in Sn-based perovskite films,  $  ^{[17-19]}  $   elevating the defect concentration and exacerbating the carrier recombination in the devices.  $  ^{[20]}  $

Therefore, the concept of 2D/3D mixed and quasi-2D perovskite has been proposed to combine the excellent optoelectric properties of 3D perovskites and the good stability of 2D perovskites.  $  ^{[31]}  $   Large ammonium cations, such as ethylenediammonium (EDA  $  ^{2+}  $  ) and phenylethylammonium (PEA  $  ^{+}  $  ) have been introduced,  $  ^{[32-34]}  $   and have shown great potential in Sn-based perovskite.  $  ^{[35,36]}  $

Herein, we introduce an additive with large reductive cation, phenylhydrazine hydrochloride (PHCl), into   $  FASnI_{3}  $   films in order to passivate trap states and improve stability. It is proven by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) that phenylhydrazine ions (  $  PH^{+}  $  ) are successfully incorporated into the crystal lattice without forming a 2D structure. The champion device with an optimal amount (5.0%) of PHCl showed a PCE of 11.4% and an open circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ) of 0.76 V. Time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) and Mott–Schottky (M–S) analysis showed that the   $  FASnI_{3}  $   film with 5.0% of PHCl has a carrier lifetime   $  \approx  $  3 times longer and a built-in voltage   $  \approx  $  3.5 times higher than those of the control film without PHCl. Moreover, the unencapsulated device possessed the best stability among Sn-based PSCs, maintaining its initial performance for over 110 days in a glove box. Interestingly, after being exposed to an oxygen-containing environment for 10 days, the device exhibited astounding efficiency recovery over a short period. In a word, PHCl shows both the effect of trap state passivation and self-repairing and can be classified as a self-repairing trap state passivator (SRTSP).

Regulating the composition and structure of Sn-based perovskites are two effective ways to suppress the oxidation of   $  Sn^{2+}  $  . The optical properties of the aforementioned films have also been measured. UV–vis absorption spectra revealed that the absorption band edge slightly blue-shifts as the doping concentration of PHCl increases (Figure 3a). The result corresponds to the steady-state PL spectra shown in Figure 3b, where the emission peak gradually blue-shifts with PHCl doping concentration increasing. With the incorporation of PHCl, the charge carrier lifetime is significantly prolonged (Figure 3c), which is consistent with the enhanced steady-state PL intensity in Figure S3, Supporting Information. The lifetime of the carrier increases from 7.6 to 25.6 ns with 5.0% of PHCl added into the precursor solution, while the excessive addition of PHCl decreases the lifetime to 10.5 ns. The results indicate that the addition of PHCl could effectively suppress the recombination of carriers.

Considering that the energy level matching between each layer is a crucial factor affecting device performance, an inverted cell architecture is adopted and the device structure is ITO/poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) polystyrene sulfonate (PEDOT:PSS)/FASnI $ _{3} $ /C $ _{60} $ /bathocuproine (BCP)/Ag. For these perovskite films, the valence band maximum (VBM) is obtained from the ultraviolet photoelectron spectra (UPS), and the conduction band minimum (CBM) is calculated by adding the optical bandgap and VBM value (Figure S4, Supporting Information). It is found that the incorporation of PHCl deepens both the VBM and CBM of the perovskites, resulting in better alignment with the work function of PEDOT:PSS and the CBM of C $ _{60} $ , and the well-matched energy level could facilitate carrier transport and thus significantly improve  $ V_{oc} $ .[38,39]

The photovoltaic parameters of all PSCs are listed in Table 1. As can be seen, the addition of PHCl has a significant improvement on device performance. With a PHCl concentration of 5.0%, the cell showed the best performance, with an average PCE increase from 5.3% to 10.1%. As the PHCl concentration further increases, the PCE decreases because excessive PHCl incorporation results in pinholes as demonstrated in morphology study, which leads to the presence of shunts. The J-V curves of the devices with different PHCl concentrations are shown in Figure S5, Supporting Information. The device with 5.0% of PHCl exhibits a maximum PCE of 11.4% under forward scan along with a   $  V_{oc}  $   of 0.76 V, a short-circuit current density (  $  J_{sc}  $  ) of 23.5 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and a fill factor (FF) of 64%, which are almost identical to the values measured by the reverse scan, suggesting negligible J-V hysteresis (Figure 4a). Furthermore, the integrated   $  J_{sc}  $   obtained from the incident photon to current conversion efficiency (IPCE) spectrum of the champion device is consistent with the   $  J_{sc}  $   obtained by J-V curve scan (Figure S6, Supporting Information). The device with 5.0% of PHCl also showed excellent reproducibility, as depicted in the PCE histogram (Figure 4b). A total of 48 devices were prepared in different batches under the same experimental conditions, and all devices have PCE exceeding 9%. To verify the accuracy of our measurement, a device with 5.0% of PHCl has been tested by a professional institution, giving a certificated PCE of 10.8% (Figure S7, Supporting Information).

The outstanding efficiency and stability of our devices call for an explanation for the role of PHCl. It is confirmed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) that PHCl can reduce   $  Sn^{4+}  $   to   $  Sn^{2+}  $   during the preparation process. As shown in Figure 6a–d, the two peaks at 487.0 and 486.0 eV can be assigned to the   $  3d_{5/2}  $   energy level of   $  Sn^{4+}  $   and   $  Sn^{2+}  $ , respectively. The addition of 2.5% PHCl significantly reduced the content of   $  Sn^{4+}  $ , while excessive PHCl does not have much effect on   $  Sn^{4+}  $   content (Table S1, Supporting Information). Besides, the reductive property of PHCl may also result in self-repairing capability of the device. To verify this point, two films with 0.0% and 5.0% of PHCl were exposed to air for 20 min, transferred back to the glove box, and irradiated with continuous AM 1.5G for a period of time, during which PL spectra were measured (Figure S10, Supporting Information). It is found that the PL intensities of both films decrease with the increasing exposure time, and

red-shift in spectrum was observed for the film with 5% of PHCl. When the two films were put back into the glove box and irradiated with light, the PHCl-doped film recovered its emission intensity and maximum peak, while the film without PHCl showed unchanged PL intensity and peak. We inferred that the blue-shift of the film with 5% PHCl under illumination could be explained by the combination of two mechanisms. First, when the film was exposed to air, the peak was red-shifted, indicating that the oxidation of   $  Sn^{2+}  $   produced some shallow defect levels. When the film was exposed to light subsequently, PHCl passivated the trap states, and the PL of the film gradually blue-shifted with recovering intensity. Second, it might cause the Burstein–Moss effect under continuous light, resulting in the lifting of the quasi-Fermi level,  $  ^{[40]}  $   and the self-repairing process was also simulated through PHCl in situ reduction of   $  Sn^{4+}  $   in the precursor solution. As shown in Figure S11, Supporting Information, with the introduction of PHCl, the dark brown   $  SnI_{4}  $   precursor solution gradually turned to light yellow   $  SnI_{2}  $   solution, demonstrating that the presence of PHCl could slowly reduce   $  Sn^{4+}  $   to   $  Sn^{2+}  $ . Hence, we assign the self-repairing capability of PHCl to its slowly reducing   $  Sn^{4+}  $   to   $  Sn^{2+}  $   ability in the film.

In addition, we experimentally demonstrated that the addition of PHCl passivates the trap state of perovskites. As obtained from the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements (Figure S12, Supporting Information), the device with 5.0% of PHCl has the largest charge carrier recombination resistance (  $  R_{rec}  $  ) at any bias voltage, and   $  R_{rec}  $   decline is not obvious as the bias voltage increases (Figure 6e and Table S2, Supporting Information). The large   $  R_{rec}  $   indicates that the incorporation of PHCl can reduce carrier recombination in the device, which is consistent with its higher   $  V_{oc}  $  . To clearly show the effect of PHCl in reducing trap state densities in the device, Mott–Schottky (M-S) analysis through capacitance–voltage (C-V) measurements was implemented. According to the M-S equation:   $  A^{2}/C^{2}=2\left(V_{bi}-V\right)/q\varepsilon\varepsilon_{0}N  $  , where A, C, V, q,   $  \varepsilon  $  , and   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   are the active area, capacitance, applied bias, unit charge, static permittivity of perovskite, and permittivity of free space, respectively. The   $  V_{bi}  $   (built-in potential) and N (the charge carrier density) can be sequentially calculated from the X-axis intercept and slope of the linear portion of the M-S curve (Figure 6f). It can be inferred that all the slopes are negative, revealing the

p-type semiconductor characteristics of the perovskite film. The device with 5.0% of PHCl showed significantly improved   $  V_{bi}  $   of 0.39 V as compared with the device without PHCl (0.11 V), meaning that the addition of PHCl enhanced the driving force of photo-generated carrier separation and extended depletion region, thereby increasing   $  V_{oc}  $  . [41] Compared to   $  V_{oc}  $  , the lower   $  V_{bi}  $   should be attributed to the presence of charged ions and/or high doping at the interface. [42]

However, when PHCl is incorporated,   $  Sn^{4+}  $   can be well reduced in the precursor solution, making perovskite grains larger and smoother. # Experimental Section

Device Fabrication: The precursor solution of FASnI $ _3 $  was prepared by dissolving SnI $ _2 $  (298.4 mg, 99.999%, Alfa Aesar), SnF $ _2 $  (12.6 mg, 99%, Sigma), and formamidinium iodide (FAI, 137.6 mg, previously reported) in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 1 mL) and stirring for 3 h. $ ^{[43]} $  The precursor solutions of FASnI $ _3 $  with 2.5%, 5.0%, and 10.0% of PHCl were prepared by adding phenylhydrazine hydrochloride (2.9, 5.8, and 11.6 mg; 99%; Sigma) to 1 mL precursor solution of FASnI $ _3 $ , respectively. The ITO-coated glass substrates (with sheet resistance about 10 Ω sq $ ^{-1} $  and thickness of 180 nm) were sequentially ultrasonically washed with sodium hydroxide solution, abstergent aqueous solution, deionized water, acetone, and ethanol for 20 min, respectively. Then, the ITO-coated glass was treated with oxygen plasma for 10 min, spin-coated with PEDOT:PSS (Clevios P VP AI 4083) at 500 rpm for 9 s and 4000 rpm for 60 s. Then, the PEDOT:PSS-coated ITO glass was transferred to a glove box and annealed at 150 °C for 20 min. The precursor solution was filtered through a 0.25 μm filter and spin-coated on the substrate at 5000 rpm for 90 s, and chlorobenzene was dropped on the substrate at 55–65 s. Finally, 50 nm C $ _{60} $  (99.9%; Puyang Yongxin Fullerene Technology Co., Ltd.), 8 nm BCP (99.9%; Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp.), and 100 nm Ag (99.99%; China New Metal Materials Technology Co., Ltd.) layers were evaporated sequentially on top of the perovskite layer in a Kurt J. Lesker vacuum chamber.

Characterization: The XRD patterns were measured by a D/MAX-2000 X-ray diffractometer with monochromatic Cu Kα irradiation (  $  \lambda_{4}^{1.5418}  $   Å). The steady-state PL and time-resolved PL spectra were measured by a FLS980 fluorescence spectrometer (Edinburgh Instruments Ltd.) and a Deltaflex TCSPC system (Horiba), respectively. XPS and UPS were carried out by an AXIS Ultra instrument (Kratos, UK). UV–vis absorption spectra were measured by a Shimadzu UV–vis–NIR spectrometer (UV-3600). The J–V curves were measured by a Keithley 4200 semiconductor characterization system under an Oriel 300W solar simulator (Thermo Oriel 91160-1000). The EIS and Mott-Schottky plot measurements were tested with a CHI660 electrochemical workstation (CH Instrument Inc.) The IPCE spectra were recorded on a Keithley 2400 source meter under an irradiation of a 150 W tungsten lamp with a 1/4 m monochromator (Spectral Product DK 240). SEM was tested with a Hitachi S-4800 field emission SEM. GIWAXS was carried out on a Xeuss WAXS/SAXS system, Xenocs, France.
