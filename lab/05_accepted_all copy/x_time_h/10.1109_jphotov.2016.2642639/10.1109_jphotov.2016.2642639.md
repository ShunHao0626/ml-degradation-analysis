# Highly Stable Perovskite Solar Cells Fabricated Under Humid Ambient Conditions

Bárbara Andrade de Carvalho, Shalinee Kavadiya, Su Huang, Dariusz M. Niedzwiedzki, and Pratim Biswas

Abstract—Organometallic perovskite solar cells have gained immense attention due to their rapid increase in efficiency and compatibility with low-cost fabrication methods. However, the material's instability in humid ambient conditions has remained a key challenge for the large-scale fabrication and application of such cells. In this paper, we present devices fabricated under 50% humidity with significantly improved long-term stability through three parallel approaches. First, the small molecule hole transport material, 2,2',7,7'-Tetrakis[N,N-di(4-methoxyphenyl)amino]-9,9'-spirobifluorene (spiro-MeOTAD) is replaced by a polymeric material Poly(3-hexylthiophene) (P3HT). Second, the device stability is further enhanced by increasing the thickness of the mesoporous titania scaffold. Finally, tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) is used as a processing additive in the perovskite precursor solution to form an in situ protective layer. On our optimized device, a remarkable long-term device stability of more than 1200 h is achieved. X-ray diffraction patterns suggest more than 2500 h of material stability.

Index Terms—Methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ), perovskite solar cells, photovoltaic (PV), stability, tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS).

# I. INTRODUCTION

O VER the past few years, organometallic perovskites, such as  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbX}_3 $  ( $ X = I $ , Br, Cl), have rapidly emerged as one of the most promising candidates for next-generation cost-effective photovoltaic (PV) materials due to their phenomenal increase in power conversion efficiency (PCE) and the

compatibility with low-cost roll-to-roll fabrication techniques [1]–[9]. Perovskites were first used as sensitizers in liquid electrolyte solar cells in 2009 with a PCE of 3.8% [1]. In 2012, the first solid-state perovskite solar cell was developed by replacing the liquid electrolyte with spiro-MeOTAD as the hole transport material (HTM), accomplishing a PCE of 9.7% [2]. A significant efficiency improvement has been achieved since then. To date, a record PCE of 22.1% has been certified by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory [3].

However, the commercial application of this material has been hindered by the notorious material instability under humidity [4], [5]. Although the detailed degradation mechanism of methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI $ _3 $ ) is not yet fully understood [6], it has been associated with a series of water catalyzed decomposition reactions. Therefore, the material properties deteriorate with increased humidity [7]. Currently, most perovskite PVs have to be fabricated under strictly controlled inert atmosphere with sub-ppm H $ _2 $ O content [8]–[10]. Such restriction drives up the production cost, limits the fabrication scalability, and has only produced devices with limited stability. Recently, advances toward enhancing the stability of perovskite solar cells have been made. The two-step deposition process to form perovskite layer has been modified to prepare devices under 50% relative humidity [11]. In this study, the substrate was preheated to different temperatures prior to the PbI $ _2 $  deposition, confirming the importance of PbI $ _2 $  morphology to perovskite solar cell performance. The fabrication of fully printable perovskite solar cells using a blade-coating technique under controlled humidity environment has also been demonstrated [12]. A total of 10.44% average PCE was achieved on those devices fabricated under 15–25% humidity. However, under very high ambient humidity (60–70%), the PCE significantly dropped to an average of only ∼0.35% PCE. Furthermore, long-term stability enhancement has also been a subject of study. Through perovskite grain boundaries modification using alkylphosphonic acid ω-ammonium chloride as crystal crosslinkers, improved long-term stability for 1000 h with device retaining 60% the initial efficiency under ∼55% humidity was demonstrated [13]. The effect of the mesoporous titania layer thickness on the long-term stability of perovskite solar cells has also been investigated. After the optimization of mesoporous (mp) TiO $ _2 $  thickness, PV devices were reported to maintain more than 85% of initial PCE after 100 days in air contact [14]. Few studies have focused on enhancing stability by improved charge collection, either using heavily doped inorganic charge extraction layer [15] or forming graded heterojunction [16], to achieve high efficiency and stability.

Additionally, other methods such as, encapsulation of the device, use of less permeable HTM and use of an interlayer to protect the perovskite from degradation, have also been proved as effective means to extend the lifetime of perovskite PV devices [17], [18]. However, most of the existing methods, although claiming enhanced long-term stability, need a controlled atmosphere during device fabrication [13], [19]–[22]. Therefore, it is very desirable to develop a method that can combine both the fabrication of perovskite PVs under humid ambient conditions while also offering high long-term stability.

In this paper, we report a modified two-step method that can produce stable perovskite PVs under 50% relative humidity. Three approaches were used in parallel to reduce the exposure of the perovskite core layer to moisture. First, the conventional small molecule HTM 2, 2', 7, -7'-tetrakis(N, N-di-p-methoxyphenylamine)-9, 9'-spirobifluorene (spiro-MeOTAD) was substituted with less permeable, hydrophobic polymeric HTM P3HT. Second, the thickness of the mesoporous titania scaffold was increased to reduce the exposure of the pure perovskite capping layer. Finally, perovskite grain boundary passivation was performed by adding tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) as a dopant to the methylammonium iodide (MAI) precursor solution. TEOS is a common silica (SiO  $  _{2}  $  ) precursor [23], [24], which has been previously used as a binder for other perovskite materials such as BaSnO  $  _{3}  $   and BiFeO  $  _{3}  $   [25], [26]. TEOS was added to the perovskite layer, which enhances the device stability by absorbing the moisture at the grain boundaries and generating SiO  $  _{2}  $   protective layer. Detail characterization of the presence of Si in the perovskite layer was performed. Finally, the device performance and stability with and without the addition of TEOS was measured.

# II. EXPERIMENTAL

# A. Materials Synthesis

MAI  $ (\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{I}) $  was synthesized following a previous report [27]. A total of  $ 24~\mathrm{mL} $  of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_2 $  (33% in absolute ethanol, Sigma-Aldrich) was reacted with  $ 10~\mathrm{mL} $  of Hydrogen iodide (HI) (57% in water, Sigma-Aldrich) in a round-bottom flask at  $ 0^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 2\mathrm{h} $  under stirring with nitrogen protection. A rotary evaporator was used to remove the solvents and precipitate the MAI. The product was redissolved in absolute ethanol and precipitated by the addition of diethyl ether, which was repeated twice. The final product was collected and dried at  $ 60^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  in a vacuum oven overnight.

# B. Solar Cell Fabrication

The solar cells were fabricated under indoor ambient conditions with relative humidity under 50%. Fluorine doped tin oxide (FTO)-coated glass (Pilkington, TEC-15, 12–14   $  \Omega/sq.  $  ) was cleaned with detergent and water followed by sonication in acetone and isopropanol for 10 min each. The substrates were then treated with UV–ozone cleaner for 10 min. A compact layer of   $  TiO_{2}  $   was prepared by spin-coating 0.15 M solution of titanium diisopropoxidebis(acetyl-acetonate) (75 wt% in isopropanol, Sigma-Aldrich) in n-butanol (99.8%, Sigma-Aldrich) onto FTO substrates. The wet film was then dried at 125 °C for 5 min [11].

A diluted   $  TiO_{2}  $   paste (Dyesol 18 NR-T: ethanol = 1:3 by weight) was prepared and spin-coated on the as-prepared compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer and annealed at 500 °C for 30 min. The thickness of mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $   was varied by changing the spin coating rate (3000, 5000, and 7000 r/min). For the perovskite layer deposition, we followed the two-step spin coating process as previously reported [11]. Specifically, 60 °C   $  PbI_{2}  $   1 M solution in N,N-dimethylformamide (anhydrous 99.8%, Sigma-Aldrich) was spin-coated on 50 °C mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $   at 7000 r/min for 20 s. The film was dried at 50 °C for 3 min and annealed at 100 °C for 5 min. Cold solution of 7 mg/mL MAI in 2-propanol was then loaded to the   $  PbI_{2}  $   film for 45 s and spin-coated at 4000 r/min for 20 s. The film was dried and annealed at 100 °C for 5 min. For samples with TEOS additive, 2.5 mol% of TEOS was added to the MAI solution before coating. For the hole transporting layer, 72.3 mg of spiro-MeOTAD was dissolved in 1 mL of chlorobenzene, to which 28.8 μL of 4-tert-butylpyridine (TBP, Sigma-Aldrich) and 17.5 μL of Li-bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (Li-TFSI, 520 mg/mL in acetonitrile) were added. This solution was then spin-coated at 4000 r/min for 20 s. The P3HT HTM was prepared by spin-coating a chlorobenzene solution of P3HT (Sigma-Aldrich, 36 and 15 mg/mL for different thickness of P3HT) doped with Li-TFSI (28.3 mg/mL solution in acetonitrile) and TBP using normalized amount from literature [28]–[30]. Finally, 80 nm thick gold electrode was deposited by thermal evaporation to complete the device.

# C. Characterization

The photocurrent density–voltage (J-V) characteristics were measured using a Keithley 2400 source-meter under   $  100\ mW/cm^{2}  $   AM 1.5 illumination generated with a Newport 69911 solar simulator. The light intensity was calibrated using a spectroradiometer (International Light Technologies, ILT900). The J-V curves were measured over an active area of   $  0.1\ cm^{2}  $  . For the stability test, devices were stored in a drawer and were tested periodically. Devices were only illuminated during the testing. The crystal structure of the materials fabricated on the devices was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD, D8 Advance, Bruker Corporation) with Cu kα radiation at 40 kV and 40 mA. The morphology and the thickness of each layer of the device were investigated using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM; Nova Nano SEM 230 Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope). Elemental analysis to detect the presence of Si in the perovskite was performed by SEM-energy dispersive spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) using FEI Nova Nano SEM 230—EDAX Genesis with 4.5 spot size and 20 kV accelerating voltage.

The time-resolved photoluminescence of perovskite solar cells was recorded upon weak excitation condition. The experimental setup comprised Hamamatsu universal streak camera attached to a Spectra-Physics ultrafast laser system as described in detail previously [31]. The frequency of the excitation pulses was set at  $ 2\mathrm{MHz} $  (500 ns between subsequent laser pulses). The excitation beam set to  $ 710~\mathrm{nm} $  was depolarized, adjusted to power of  $ \sim 60~\mu \mathrm{W} $ , and focused on the solar cell in a circular spot of  $ \sim 1\mathrm{mm} $  diameter. This corresponds to a very low photon intensity of  $ \sim 1\times 10^{10} $  photons/ $ \mathrm{cm}^2 $  per pulse.

# III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The performance of a reference device fabricated under ambient conditions (under  $ 50\% $  relative humidity) using spiro-MeOTAD as the HTM is shown in Fig. 1(a). An average PCE of  $ 12.3 \pm 0.2\% $  was obtained on these reference cells. The open-circuit voltage  $ (V_{\mathrm{OC}}) $  is  $ 1.00 \pm 0.02\mathrm{V} $ , short-circuit current density  $ (J_{\mathrm{sc}}) $  is  $ 24.3 \pm 0.2\mathrm{mA/cm}^2 $ , and fill factor (FF) is  $ 0.51 \pm 0.01 $ . All the results were obtained from forward scan with  $ 10~\mathrm{ms} $  delay time. The current density of these devices seems to be little higher than previous report, which may be because of small error present in the device area. However, the measurement of the current is accurate. Fig. 1(b) shows the cross-sectional SEM image of the same device, revealing the layer configuration. As reported in literature, the two-step spin-coating process led to  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  cuboid formation on top of  $ \mathrm{mp - TiO_2} $  (see Fig. S1 in the supporting information) [10].

In the rest of the study, spiro-MeOTAD HTM was replaced by P3HT. The devices with 60 and 100 nm thick P3HT HTM were fabricated by spin-coating the solutions of P3HT of different concentrations 15 and 36 mg/ml, respectively. Simultaneously, the thickness of the mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $  was modified for both the P3HT thicknesses. The PV cells performance were tested and summarized in Table I. We noticed that by changing spiro-MeOTAD to P3HT, we ended up with a compromised  $  V_{OC}  $  of 0.75–0.9 V. After the initial characterization, these devices were stored in a drawer in ambient air and were repeatedly tested over time to study their long-term performance. Fig. 2(a) shows the stability comparison of the reference cell and the (stability-wise) optimized cell with 100 nm P3HT and 790 nm mp-TiO  $  _{2}  $  . Fig. 2(b) shows the comparison between P3HT cells with different thicknesses of mp-TiO  $  _{2}  $   as well as HTM layer. We found that devices with P3HT HTM and thicker mp-TiO  $  _{2}  $   showed increased stability compared to reference cells with spiro-MeOTAD HTM. Unlike the spiro-MeOTAD reference, which sharply lost 75% of efficiency in the first 300 h, the decay rate of P3HT devices slowed down over time. In addition, it was shown that the stability increased with increased thickness of P3HT as well as the mp-TiO  $  _{2}  $   layer. With 100 nm P3HT and 790 nm mp-TiO  $  _{2}  $  , 90% of initial PCE can be preserved even after 1224 h. We attribute the effect from P3HT to its polymeric nature that makes the HTM hydrophobic and less permeable than spiro-MeOTAD, which enhanced the perovskite protection to moisture. This was confirmed by the contact angle measurement of water on P3HT and spiro-MeOTAD layer individually (see the results in Fig. S2 of the supporting information). The contact angle for P3HT layer is 99.02° and that for spiro-MeOTAD layer is 47.56°, showing the hydrophobicity of P3HT. It is conceivable that the sealing is more effective for thicker P3HT coverage because the water present in the ambience has to cross a longer pathway before reaching the perovskite material compared to the devices with thinner P3HT layers. The effect from mp-TiO  $  _{2}  $   is cross-referenced with the morphology data obtained from the cross-sectional SEM images (as shown in Fig. 3). We found that as we increased the mp-TiO  $  _{2}  $   thickness, the exposure of the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   cuboid capping layer was reduced. The capping layer was almost invisible in the device with 790 nm mp-TiO  $  _{2}  $  . As a result, the most stable devices were those with 790 nm mp-TiO  $  _{2}  $   and 100 nm P3HT, which showed 92% (PCE  $  _{(t)}  $   = 6.1%) of its original PCE (PCE  $  _{(0h)}  $   = 6.6%) after 1224 h. However, it is not very meaningful to use mp-TiO  $  _{2}  $   or P3HT layers thicker than these, due to the negative impacts on the PCE. It is also worth noting that on the devices with 790 nm mp-TiO  $  _{2}  $   and 100 nm P3HT, a ~30% initial increase in PCE was observed in the first 72 h, which enhanced the low starting PCE of 6.6–8.5%.

Similar initial increase was reported before [33]–[35]. A possible explanation is the oxidation of the   $  Li^{+}  $  on doped HTM [36]. However, since we did not see the same trend on devices with the same HTM but thinner mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $   layer, it is more likely that this effect is due to the slow diffusion of unreacted precursor in the mesoporous scaffold.

In order to further increase the stability of these perovskite solar cells in humid ambient air, we hypothesize that an in situ generated inert protective layer at the grain boundaries should be able to help preventing moisture attack. We consider TEOS as a good candidate for this purpose because it is a colorless liquid that can absorb water and generate insulating, inert, and optically nonabsorbing  $ \mathrm{SiO}_2 $ . More importantly, it can be dissolved in 2-propanol, which we use to prepare the MAI solution. We started the test by adding TEOS to the MAI solution using the most stable device configuration. During the sequential deposition, MAI quickly reacted with the  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  but TEOS remained unreacted. After the solvent was evaporated, liquid TEOS was expelled from the growing  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  lattice and only remained at the grain boundaries. XRD pattern of the as-prepared device (see Fig. 4 bottom line) shows the characteristic peaks of perovskite  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  as well as a hump around  $ 2\theta = 20^{\circ} $ , which corresponds to the existence of amorphous  $ \mathrm{TEOS / SiO_2} $  [8], [37]-[39]. This is an indication that the addition of TEOS did not change the perovskite crystal structure and did not cause any reaction that could alter the formation of crystallite perovskite. After a few days of exposure to moisture, the amorphous hump of TEOS/ $ \mathrm{SiO}_2 $  disappeared and two new peaks were seen in the XRD, which are probably correlated to a new phase of  $ \mathrm{SiO}_2 $  formed in the device (see Fig. 4). However, the absolute intensity of the perovskite peaks was not affected. Even after being stored for 107 days in ambient air, all the perovskite peaks remained the same. The only change in the XRD pattern came from the two emerged peaks at  $ 2\theta = 38.7^{\circ} $  and  $ 44.7^{\circ} $ . This two peak matches with the Cristobalite phase of  $ \mathrm{SiO}_2 $  (JCPDS file PDF # 00-004-0379). Surface morphology of the perovskite with and without TEOS doping was also characterized under a scanning electron microscope (see Fig. S1 in the supporting information), which also shows the cuboidal perovskite crystals.

Based on the observed high stability of TEOS-doped devices, we propose a mechanism to describe the TEOS activity in the perovskite solar cells (see Fig. 5). In the as-prepared devices with TEOS doping, water molecules from air that penetrate the P3HT covering layer cannot easily degrade the perovskite material because the favored reaction is with TEOS, forming  $ \mathrm{SiO}_2 $  and ethanol. Ethanol is volatile and readily escapes from the device by evaporation, leaving the  $ \mathrm{SiO}_2 $  at the grain boundaries. After the first few days, TEOS was completely converted into  $ \mathrm{SiO}_2 $ , which physically passivated the grain boundaries, thus forming an in situ protection layer to prevent further infiltration of water though the most vulnerable grain boundaries. Furthermore, the presence of  $ \mathrm{SiO}_2 $ , which is an inert material, should not interfere negatively with the device performance. In fact, the use of insulating  $ \mathrm{Al_2O_3} $  mesoporous scaffold has been previously reported in highly efficient perovskite solar cells and due to the high carrier mobility in perovskites, the charge transfer is not interrupted [40]. Therefore, similar to  $ \mathrm{Al_2O_3} $ , the use of  $ \mathrm{SiO}_2 $  in the device is not likely to interfere in the charge transfer in perovskite PVs. The device performance and long-term stability were studied on the TEOS-doped devices. The stability result is compared with both the spiro-MeOTAD reference and the undoped best device in Fig. 7(a). It can be seen that the TEOS-doped device showed exceptional high stability. Similar to the original undoped device with the same configuration (790 nm mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $  and 100 nm P3HT), the PCE of this TEOS-doped sample showed an initial increase, but the amplitude reached 52%, which is probably due to the settling of the TEOS liquid at the first few days. The decay curve became very flat after the first 500 h, and after 1224 h, 118% of its initial efficiency (or  $  \sim80\%  $  of its peak PCE) was observed, which was 28% better than its undoped counterpart.

a moderate PCE of 6.5% and gradually climbed to its maximum PCE of 9.8% after 72 h. Both   $  V_{OC}  $   and FF were much higher than the original values. After 1224 h, the PCE dropped to 7.7%, which is still considerably higher than the initial PCE of 6.5%. The time-resolved photoluminescence decay measurement allowed us to measure the trap-mediated charge-carrier recombination rate in the perovskite solar cell with and without TEOS addition. Fig. 8 shows the time-resolved photoluminescence results. In general, charge carrier recombination kinetics can be modeled by the following differential equation:

$$
- \frac {d n}{d t} = n R _ {T} (n)
$$

(1)

where   $  R_{T}(n) = k_{1} + nk_{2} + n^{2}k_{3}, k_{1}  $   is monomolecular recombination rate,   $  k_{2}  $   is the bimolecular electron–hole recombination rate constant, and   $  k_{3}  $   is the Auger recombination rate constant. The equation demonstrates that kinetic behavior of carrier recombination strongly depends from carrier densities. # IV. CONCLUSION

Highly stable perovskite solar cells were fabricated under 50% relative humidity by passivation of perovskite surface and crystal grain boundaries, which are the two most susceptible areas to moisture degradation. Significantly enhanced long-term stability is demonstrated by using an optimized P3HT hole transport layer, thick mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $  scaffold, and a small amount of TEOS as a processing additive to modify the grain boundaries. As a result of all the three optimizations combined, the most stable device can hold 118% of its initial PCE (  $  \sim80\%  $  of its maximum PCE) by more than 1200 h with no encapsulation. We found that the doping of a small amount of TEOS does not compromise the device performance significantly but can enhance the stability.

XRD results suggest better device stability, even after  $ 2500\mathrm{h} $ , can be achieved with more durable electrodes. This method is very suitable for its large-scale fabrication in ambient air. Incorporation with an encapsulation method can potentially generate perovskite solar cells with industry level long-term stability.
