# Abstract

Hole transport materials and their processing occupy at least one third of the cost of perovskite solar cells (PSCs), which leaves us plenty room to improve the process of device fabrication. Herein, we report on a facile immersing and washing strategy (I-method) to prepare effective organic monomolecular layers (MLs) of poly[N,N'-bis(4-butylphenyl)-N,N'-bis(phenyl) benzidine] (polyTPD) as hole transport layers (ML-HTLs) to construct cost-effective planar inverted PSCs. The ML enables an enhanced wettability to perovskite precursor and thus results in the growth of compact and uniform perovskite films with reduced trap density. In addition, the ML exhibits better energy level alignment with perovskite to largely increase the hole transport property. Consequently, the ML-polyTPD based PSCs deliver significantly enhanced performance, about 10% increase in power conversion efficiency (PCE), as compared to that of pristine polyTPD based devices, where the HTL was prepared by conventional spin coating method (S-method) with thickness of tens of nanometers. The practical consumption of polyTPD during the I-method is cut to the bone, with the cost of \$0.8 for 1 m $  ^{2}  $  substrate being achieved, which is 0.15% of that by S-method. The developed I-method is facile, time- and cost-saving with low requirement for facilities as well as with low temperature and solution processability.

# Introduction

Organic-inorganic hybrid perovskites are promising photovoltaic materials due to their appealing properties such as large absorption coefficient, small exciton binding energy and solution processibility.  $  ^{[1-3]}  $   The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has been boosted over 25% within ten years.  $  ^{[4-6]}  $   In addition, encouraging progress on long-term stability under harsh ambient conditions has also been achieved recently.  $  ^{[7, 8]}  $   These achievements accelerate the commercialization of PSCs in the near future. Although lots of fruitful efforts have been made for cost-effective PSCs, hole transport materials and their processing are still reported to cover 33.9% of the entire device cost,  $  ^{[9]}  $   which leaves us plenty room for optimization.

Planar inverted PSCs that using organic hole transport layers (HTLs) have been receiving extensive attention due to their low temperature (<150 °C) and solution processibility, and thus is suitable to large scale and flexible assembling.  $  ^{[10-14]}  $   The selection of appropriate organic hole transport materials and their preparation strategy are crucial to construct low cost and highly efficient inverted PSCs.  $  ^{[13, 15, 16]}  $   In general, HTLs are with their thickness of tens of nanometer prepared by conventional spin coating method (S-method). However, most reported organic hole transport materials are rather expensive. In the conventional S-method, excess precursors of these materials are used during the preparation of HTLs, thus the actual materials usage is always beyond dozens of times of the amount contained in devices due to the unavoidable waste. In addition, special facilities such as spin coater, blade coater, spray, and heating stage, as well as corresponding professional operations are required to prepare these organic HTLs with desired thickness, which would further increase the cost of PSC production.

On the other hand, the widely reported organic HTLs with hydrophobic nature such

as poly[bis(4-phenyl)(2,4,6-trimethylphenyl)amine] (PTAA) and 4,4'-cyclohexylidenebis[N,N-bis(4-methylphenyl) benzenamine] (TAPC), always suffer from the common problem of poor wettability to perovskite precursors, which would easily cause pinholes and large area exposures of perovskite films.  $  ^{[17,18]}  $   As a result, the severe non-wetting issue would easily cause large shunt losses and poor charge transport in PSCs.  $  ^{[19-21]}  $   Several methods have been applied to address the non-wetting issue. It has been reported that by doping the perovskite precursor or pre-wetting the surface of hydrophobic HTL, the adhesion of perovskite precursor to the underlying surface can be improved effectively.  $  ^{[18]}  $   In general, these treatments need precise control of either the doping content or the pre-wetting process, otherwise the properties/morphologies of achieved target films are normally different from batch to batch. In addition, ultraviolet (UV) ozone treatment have been used to improve the surface properties of organic HTLs.  $  ^{[17]}  $   However, the intense energy of UV light (648 kJ/mol for 184.9 nm) can break the bonds of C-N (305 kJ/mol), C-C (347 kJ/mol) and C=C (611 kJ/mol) of organic materials, which is detrimental to the stability of HTLs and PSCs.  $  ^{[22]}  $   Accordingly, it is necessary to improve the property of HTL surfaces by a facile, effective but non-destructive strategy. In our previous work,  $  ^{[23]}  $   we have reported a washing process to prepare organic monomolecular layers (MLs) as effective HTLs (ML-HTLs). Interestingly, it is found that good coverage of perovskite films was observed on the MLs to deliver enhanced performance. The results indicate that the non-wetting issue of hydrophobic organic materials could be probably addressed by this ML-HTL strategy, although the actual consumption of materials is still similar to that of conventional preparation process for thicker HTLs. Therefore, it is still challenging to develop appropriate strategy to facilely and cost-effectively prepare HTLs for the construction of economic and highly efficient inverted PSCs.

In this work, we report on an immersing method (I-method) to prepare poly[N,N'-bis(4-butylphenyl)-N,N'-bis(phenyl) benzidine] (polyTPD) ML (ML-polyTPD). Note that this method for ML preparation is much more facile and economical than that reported in our previous paper.  $  ^{[23]}  $   To demonstrate the advantages of ML-polyTPD, the control polyTPD layers with thickness of tens of nanometer were

This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved

also prepared by conventional spin coating method (S-method) with or without UV-ozone treatment to improve surface wetting. The pristine polyTPD (P-polyTPD) treated by UV light is termed   $  P_{UV}  $  -polyTPD. It is found that, apart from enhanced wettability, the energy level of ML-polyTPD matches better with that of perovskite by completely eliminating the energy level offset for hole transfer. In addition, the increased film conductivity and largely reduced thickness of ML-polyTPD benefit the charge collection efficiency. As a result, the fill factor (FF) is increased significantly from 78.1% of   $  P_{UV}  $  -polyTPD based device to a promising 83.2% of ML-polyTPD based device, which is one of top FF values in PSCs.  $  ^{[16, 24]}  $   The corresponding PCE is enhanced from 17.2% to 18.8% with improved stability and hysteresis. More importantly, the actual consumption of polyTPD by the I-method is cut to the bone and the cost of 0.8 for 1 m  $  ^{2}  $   substrate is achieved, which is 0.15% of that by S-method. Compared with the conventional S-method, the I-method is facile, time- and cost-saving with low requirement for facilities and operations, as well as with low temperature and solution processability. Therefore, it is more suitable to commercialized batch preparation of ML-HTLs either on large scale or flexible substrates.

The preparation process for  $ \mathrm{P_{UV}} $ -polyTPD and ML-polyTPD is shown in Figure 1a and 1b, respectively. For typical conventional process (Figure 1a), the indium tin oxide (ITO) substrates need to be taken out for UV-ozone treatment to improve surface wetting after ultrasonic cleaning, then polyTPD solution is spin coated onto the substrates piece by piece, followed by thermal annealing to remove residual solvent. While for ML-polyTPD (Figure 1b), the ultrasonically cleaned ITO substrates from ethanol are placed on the airlaid paper for absorption, then they are transferred directly into the polyTPD solution in batches and then soaked for  $ 1\mathrm{min} $ , following that the substrates are rinsed in chlorobenzene (CB) for  $ 5\mathrm{s} $  to obtain ML-polyTPD on ITO substrates. After  $ \mathbf{N}_2 $  drying, these ML-covered ITO substrates can be used for perovskite deposition. Clearly, the entire process is free of thermal annealing and is more time/material-saving for batch preparation.

Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) has been widely applied to detect the local contact potential difference between tip and samples, as well as to investigate the homogeneity of electrical properties of films.  $  ^{[27]}  $   As shown in Figure 2a-c, for   $  P_{UV}  $  -polyTPD and ML-polyTPD films, the surface potential is evenly distributed without large fluctuations, suggesting full coverage of polyTPD molecules on the ITO substrates without exposure of naked ITO surface on the basis of photocurrent density-voltage (J-V) results (shown in the following study). Therefore, the polyTPD ML grown by the I-method is reasonably regarded as compact in spite of the fact that the layer thickness is only 1.3 nm.

Figure 2d-f show the contact angle measurement by dripping N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF) on different films. The P-polyTPD film exhibits a large contact angle of  $ 52.9^{\circ} $ , while it decreases a bit to  $ 47.4^{\circ} $  after UV-ozone treatment  $ (\mathrm{P_{UV}} $ -polyTPD). Notably, the contact angle is decreased to  $ 24.1^{\circ} $  for the ML-polyTPD film. According to classic nucleation theory, a lower contact angle indicates smaller potential barrier for heterogeneous nucleation on the surface, which leads to a higher

This strategy is effective, and more importantly, it is nondestructive to the chemical structure, thus it is expected to benefit the device performance.

To investigate energy level structures between perovskite and polyTPD, ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) was carried out. It is found that all the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy level, the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy level, and the Fermi level ( $ E_{\mathrm{f}} $ ) are changed by reducing the polyTPD film thickness, as measured from the UPS spectra (Figure S2 in Supporting Information). The work function (WF), valence band (VB), and conduction band (CB) of the perovskite  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_{3 - x}\mathrm{Cl}_x $  are obtained from our previous report.[23] Based on these, the energy level structures being referenced to a common Fermi level are illustrated in Figure 3. Compared with  $ \mathrm{P_{UV}} $ -polyTPD, the lower  $ E_{\mathrm{f}} $  of ML-polyTPD would benefit the accumulation of holes on the superficial space of perovskite, leading to a region with higher conductivity. Moreover, the larger band bending at

$ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_{3 - x}\mathrm{Cl}_x / \mathrm{ML} $  -polyTPD interface would cause lower hole concentration in the superficial space charge region of polyTPD, due to larger difference of  $ E_{\mathrm{f}} $  between perovskite and ML-polyTPD in comparison with that between perovskite and  $ \mathrm{P_{UV}} $  -polyTPD. As a result, both these factors are favorable to the hole transfer from perovskite to HTL under illumination. Another issue that affects charge collection efficiency is the energy level offset  $ (\Delta E_{\mathrm{h}}) $  for hole transfer between VB of perovskite and HOMO of polyTPD.[28] As can be seen,  $ \Delta E_{\mathrm{h}} $  is completely eliminated, that is,  $ \Delta E_{\mathrm{h}} = 0 $  eV at the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_{3 - x}\mathrm{Cl}_x / \mathrm{ML} $  -polyTPD interface, indicating suppressed thermionic losses. Apart from energy level alignment, the enhanced film conductivity of ML (Figure 1c) can expedite the injected holes to be collected by the ITO electrode.[29] Consequently, hole transfer is more efficient between perovskite and ML-polyTPD.

To prove the enhanced charge transfer properties, steady-state and time-resolved photoluminescence (PL and TRPL) spectra were conducted (Figure 4a-b). Compared with the ITO substrate, PL intensity is reduced notably for perovskite grown on  $ \mathrm{P_{UV}} $ -polyTPD. By comparing the maximum emission peak intensity of perovskite grown on HTLs to that on ITO substrates, quenching efficiency can be calculated. The quenching efficiency is  $ 85\% $  for  $ \mathrm{P_{UV}} $ -polyTPD, and it is increased to  $ 94\% $  for perovskite grown on ML-polyTPD, indicating more efficient charge transfer between perovskite and the ML. In addition, a slight blue shift of the PL peak is observed for ML-polyTPD. This is probably attributed to reduced trap states of perovskite film grown on the ML.[30] Similar results are obtained from the TRPL spectra, from which the fitted charge carrier decay lifetime is 65.0 ns, 16.8 ns, and 7.3 ns for perovskite grown on the ITO,  $ \mathrm{P_{UV}} $ -polyTPD, and ML-polyTPD substrates, respectively. The improved charge transfer and collection efficiency are to the advantage of the device short circuit current ( $ J_{\mathrm{sc}} $ ) and FF.[21]

The trap densities of perovskite can be quantitatively analyzed by space-charge limited current (SCLC) measurement on hole-only devices, as shown in Figure 4c-d. By increasing the bias voltage, the device current rises sharply once the voltage reaches the trap-filled limit (TFL) region, where the onset voltage  $ (V_{\mathrm{TFL}}) $  is proportional to the trap density  $ (N_{\mathrm{trap}}) $  in perovskite film by,

$$
N _ {t r a p} = \frac {2 \varepsilon_ {0} \varepsilon_ {r}}{q L ^ {2}} V _ {T F L}
$$

where   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $  ,   $  \varepsilon_{r}  $  , q and L is vacuum permittivity, relative dielectric constant, elementary charge, and film thickness of perovskite, respectively. Clearly,   $  V_{TFL}  $   is smaller for ML-polyTPD based device, indicating fewer traps of the perovskite grown on the ML. From the SCLC region (  $  J^{\infty}V^{n}  $  , n = 2), hole mobility (  $  \mu  $  ) of perovskite can be calculated (Figure S3 in Supporting Information). For ML-polyTPD based device,   $  \mu = 6 \times 10^{-3}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $  V  $  ^{-1}  $  s  $  ^{-1}  $  , which is three times larger than that of P  $  _{UV}  $  -polyTPD based one. The lower trap density and higher hole mobility suggests improved quality of perovskite film grown on the ML, which is conducive to reducing charge carrier losses for efficient charge collection.  $  ^{[21, 31]}  $

Typical inverted PSCs were fabricated to investigate the effect of MLs on the device performance (Figure 5a and Table 1). Compared to the devices with perovskites grown on bare ITO substrates, the devices with HTLs show largely increased open circuit voltage  $ (V_{\mathrm{oc}}) $ ,  $ J_{\mathrm{sc}} $  and FF, indicating that HTL plays an important role to improve the diode characteristics even though its thickness is only several nanometers for the ML-polyTPD HTL. The ML-polyTPD based devices perform better than the  $ \mathrm{P_{UV}} $ -polyTPD based ones, mainly due to significant enhancement in FF (Table 1 and Figure 5b). Note that no voltage loss is observed for ML-polyTPD based devices in comparison with  $ \mathrm{P_{UV}} $ -polyTPD based ones, indicating full coverage of ML-polyTPD on the ITO substrates combined with the results from KPFM measurement (Figure 2). In addition, the performance deviation of the former devices is smaller than the latter,

The I-method is thus a quite promising and general approach owing to the possibility to extend it for the preparation of ML-HTLs with most of organic hole transport materials.

# Conclusions

In summary, we report on an exploration of the most facile and economic approach to prepare ML-HTLs for the construction of cost-effective and efficient inverted PSCs. Compared with conventional thick HTL, the ML-polyTPD enables improved wettability to perovskite precursor and thus result in the growth of compact and fully covered perovskite film with reduced trap density; the ML-polyTPD aligns better with the energy level of perovskite, leading to enhanced charge extraction.
