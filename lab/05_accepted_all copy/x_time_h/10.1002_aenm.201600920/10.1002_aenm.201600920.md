# Few-Layer MoS  $  _{2}  $  Flakes as Active Buffer Layer for Stable Perovskite Solar Cells

Andrea Capasso, Fabio Matteocci, Leyla Najafi, Mirko Prato, Joka Buha, Lucio Cinà, Vittorio Pellegrini, Aldo Di Carlo,* and Francesco Bonaccorso*

Solution-processed few-layer MoS $ _2 $  flakes are exploited as an active buffer layer in hybrid lead-halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Glass/FTO/compact-TiO $ _2 $ /mesoporous-TiO $ _2 $ /CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ PbI $ _3 $ /MoS $ _2 $ /Spiro-OMeTAD/Au solar cells are realized with the MoS $ _2 $  flakes having a twofold function, acting both as a protective layer, by preventing the formation of shunt contacts between the perovskite and the Au electrode, and as a hole transport layer from the perovskite to the Spiro-OMeTAD. As prepared PSC demonstrates a power conversion efficiency ( $ \eta $ ) of 13.3%, along with a higher lifetime stability over 550 h with respect to reference PSC without MoS $ _2 $  ( $ \Delta\eta/\eta = -7\% $  vs.  $ \Delta\eta/\eta = -34\% $ ). Large-area PSCs (1.05 cm $ ^2 $  active area) are also fabricated to demonstrate the scalability of this approach, achieving  $ \eta $  of 11.5%. Our results pave the way toward the implementation of MoS $ _2 $  as a material able to boost the shelf life of large-area perovskite solar cells in view of their commercialization.

Perovskites have the general formula ABX $ _3 $ , where X is an anion while A and B are cations. $ ^{[5]} $  In the organometal halide perovskites, the anion X is a halogen, generally I, although Br and Cl are also used. $ ^{[5]} $  The A cation is organic, usually methylammonium (CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3^+ $ ), or less frequently ethylammonium (CH $ _3 $ CH $ _2 $ NH $ _3^+ $ ) and formamidinium

$  (\mathrm{NH}_{2}\mathrm{CH}=\mathrm{NH}_{2}^{+})  $ .  $  ^{[5]}  $  The B cation is predominantly Pb in the PSCs with the highest reported  $  \eta = 22.1\%  $ .  $  ^{[8,9]}  $  Sn was also used  $  ^{[10]}  $  to increase photon absorption.  $  ^{[11]}  $  However, Sn suffers oxidation issues strongly undermining the cell stability.  $  ^{[10]}  $  Currently, the standard compound for PSCs is methylammonium lead triiodide (  $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $  ).  $  ^{[5]}  $  For the realization of PSCs, two different approaches have been proposed: the so-called mesostructured  $  ^{[1,2,7]}  $  and planar-heterojunction  $  ^{[3,12,13]}  $  PSCs. Mesostructured PSCs rely on a mesoporous layer (typically made of nanocrystalline  $  TiO_{2}^{(1,2,7)}  $ ) acting as scaffold for the perovskite growth.  $  ^{[14]}  $  Planar-heterojunction PSCs are instead made of a compact perovskite layer, which is grown without any supporting structure.  $  ^{[13,15]}  $

# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) are emerging as the most promising solution-processed photovoltaic technology.  $  ^{[1-3]}  $   The interest stems from several key features shown by the hybrid organometal halide perovskites used as photoactive materials, such as intense light absorption coefficient   $  (1.5 \times 10^{4} \text{ cm}^{-1} \text{ at } 550 \text{ nm})  $  ,  $  ^{[4,5]}  $   long electron and hole diffusion length (from 100 nm to 1 mm),  $  ^{[4]}  $   high carrier mobility   $  (1-10 \text{ cm}^{2} \text{ V}^{-1} \text{ s}^{-1})  $  ,  $  ^{[6]}  $

Electron (ETL) and hole (HTL) transporting layers are added in PSCs between the photoactive layer and the electrodes in order to separate the generated charges and collect them (holes at the photoanode and electrons at the back electrode) efficiently.  $  ^{[16]}  $   For what regards the ETL, compact layers of   $  TiO_{2}  $   (50–80 nm)  $  ^{[17]}  $   or ZnO (25 nm)  $  ^{[18]}  $   are usually chosen to prevent holes recombination at the photo-anode. Concerning the HTL, the standard de facto material for high performance (i.e., high   $  \eta  $  ) PSCs is 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis-(N,N-di-4-methoxyphenylamino)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD).  $  ^{[1,3,7,19,20]}  $   Spiro-OMeTAD can be easily integrated in PSCs since it can be solution-processed in water-based solvents, having also a high glass-transition temperature (120 °C).  $  ^{[19,21]}  $   Thanks to the highest-occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy level of 5.03 eV,  $  ^{[22]}  $   which is a value approaching the valence band maximum (VBM) of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   (5.4 eV)  $  ^{[23]}  $   and   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3-x}Cl_{x}  $   (5.3 eV),  $  ^{[24]}  $   Spiro-OMeTAD effectively transfers holes from the

and the possibility of both vacuum  $  ^{[3]}  $  and solution-processing.  $  ^{[7]}  $  Hybrid organometal halide perovskites were proposed in 2009 as light absorbers in dye-sensitized solar cells (DSSCs) with a power conversion efficiency (  $  \eta  $  ) ≈4%.  $  ^{[4]}  $  Several reports followed this seminal work, demonstrating that perovskite materials can be used to engineer highly efficient solar cells, with  $  \eta  $  up to 22.1%.  $  ^{[8]}  $

Nevertheless, despite these recent results,  $  ^{[9,37]}  $  Spiro-OMeTAD still represents the reference HTL material used for the fabrication of PSCs.

A more significant limitation that poses severe concerns in view of the possible commercialization of PSCs is their lifetime stability,  $  ^{[38]}  $   which is adversely affected by the presence of the Spiro-OMeTAD.  $  ^{[39]}  $   This is due to the fact that Spiro-OMeTAD, in its pristine form (amorphous), has low electrical conductivity (  $  \sigma  $  ),  $  ^{[40]}  $   i.e.,   $  \approx10^{-8}  $   S cm  $  ^{-1}  $  .  $  ^{[41]}  $   In order to increase the   $  \sigma  $   with the target to reach high device   $  \eta  $  ,  $  ^{[8,9]}  $   Spiro-OMeTAD is usually doped with a combination of tert-butylpyridine (TBP)  $  ^{[42,43]}  $   and lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (Li-TFSI).  $  ^{[44]}  $   Such doping process successfully increases its hole mobility (from   $  10^{-5}  $   to   $  10^{-3}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $   V  $  ^{-1}  $   s  $  ^{-1}  $  )  $  ^{[45]}  $   and hence the   $  \sigma  $   (from   $  10^{-8}  $   to   $  10^{-3}  $   S cm  $  ^{-1}  $  ).  $  ^{[41]}  $   The doping process has also an effect on the HOMO level, which increases from 5.03 to 5.22 eV.  $  ^{[46]}  $   Unfortunately, the downside of such doping process is the reduced stability of the PSCs, which is currently the main drawback of such technology.  $  ^{[38,39]}  $   The Li-TFSI tends to desorb during cell operation, reducing the cell   $  \eta  $   (a 20% decrease in the first 120 h at room temperature with a humidity of   $  \approx40\%  $  );  $  ^{[47]}  $   TBP instead causes the corrosion of the perovskite layer strongly affecting the   $  \eta  $   of the cells over time.  $  ^{[43,48]}  $

Another factor limiting the device stability regards the interface created by the Spiro-OMeTAD and the perovskite layer.  $  ^{[49,50]}  $   Recent analysis using in-situ TEM characterization of PSCs operating under illumination reported evidence of iodine migration from the perovskite layer to the Spiro-OMeTAD, with consequent degradation of the cell   $  \eta  $  .  $  ^{[49]}  $   Furthermore, it is known  $  ^{[51]}  $   that a direct contact between the perovskite and the metal electrode results in high recombination losses, which results in a decrease of   $  V_{OC}  $   and FF.  $  ^{[50]}  $   Such losses can occur in solution-processed PSCs due to the incomplete coverage of the perovskite by the HTL.  $  ^{[52]}  $   Another source of loss is the metal electrode that can also get in contact with the perovskite by migrating through the HTL, as demonstrated in organic solar cells (OSCs) (i.e., metal migration through the photoactive organic layer during device operation).  $  ^{[53]}  $   One strategy to minimize such detrimental effect could be the increase of the HTL thickness, which is however associated with an increase of the PSCs series resistance, determining a reduction of the solar cell   $  \eta  $  .  $  ^{[54]}  $   Guarnera et al. proposed the addition of an insulating mesoporous   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   layer between the perovskite and the

Spiro-OMeTAD layer to prevent shunting pathways, reporting an increase in the lifetime stability of the devices over 350 h of operation (starting   $  \eta  $   of 13% that decreased by 5% after 350 h, for the best cell).  $  ^{[55]}  $   Such   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   layer has a “passive” protective function, because it physically prevents the contact between the Au electrode and the perovskite layer.  $  ^{[50,52,55]}  $

Here we successfully implement liquid-phase exfoliated few-layer   $  MoS_{2}  $   flakes in solution-processed PSCs with glass/FTO/ compact-  $  TiO_{2}  $  /mesoporous-  $  TiO_{2}/CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}/MoS_{2}/Spiro-OMeTAD/Au  $   structure to create an “active” buffer layer (ABL) between the Spiro-OMeTAD and the perovskite layer with a dual function: (i) barrier toward the metal electrode migration, and (ii) additional energy-matching layer to further ease the hole collection of the Spiro-OMeTAD. The presented approach allows us to build metal–organic PSCs with   $  \eta  $   matching that of the Spiro-OMeTAD reference PSCs, but with superior lifetime stability over a period of 550 h. In fact,   $  MoS_{2}  $  -based PSCs (0.1 cm  $  ^{2}  $   active area), show   $  \eta = 13.3\%  $   and higher lifetime stability after 550 h with respect to reference cells (  $  \Delta\eta/\eta = -7\%  $   vs.   $  \Delta\eta/\eta = -34\%  $  ). We have also fabricated large-area cells (1.05 cm  $  ^{2}  $   active area) obtaining a   $  \eta = 11.5\%  $  , demonstrating the versatility of the ABL toward the scaling up of the cell size.

# 2. Results and Discussion

The interest in   $  MoS_{2}  $   flakes for photovoltaic applications  $  ^{[56]}  $   stems originally from a high charge carriers mobility (up to   $  \approx470\ cm^{2}\ V^{-1}\ s^{-1}  $   for electrons, and   $  \approx480\ cm^{2}\ V^{-1}\ s^{-1}  $   for holes)  $  ^{[57]}  $   and chemical inertness of the basal-plane flake surface.  $  ^{[58]}  $   The solution-processed   $  MoS_{2}  $   flakes were already exploited as HTL in organic solar cells (OSCs).  $  ^{[59,60]}  $   achieving   $  \eta  $   comparable with the ones based on standard HTLs (i.e.,   $  MoO_{3}  $    $  ^{[61]}  $   and PEDOT:PSS  $  ^{[62]}  $  ). In particular, Gu et al. fabricated OSCs based on PTB7:  $  PB_{71}BM  $   using   $  MoS_{2}  $   HTL obtaining   $  \eta = 8.1\%  $ , which is higher than the reference cell with thermally evaporated   $  MoO_{3}  $   (  $  \eta = 7.5\%  $  ).  $  ^{[60]}  $   Kelvin Probe microscopy analysis indicated that   $  MoS_{2}  $   flakes have a surface potential analogous to that of   $  MoO_{3}  $   (the energy offset between the two materials is only 0.02 eV), thus working efficiently as hole collector in OSCs.  $  ^{[60]}  $

We carried out the liquid-phase exfoliation (LPE) of   $  MoS_{2}  $  , see Experimental Section for details, in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) solvent, since it has a surface tension matching the surface energy of the exfoliated flakes, which thus tend to be stabilized against re-aggregation.  $  ^{[63,64]}  $   NMP was found to provide higher concentration   $  [0.023\ g\ L^{-1}]  $   of exfoliated flakes (single or few layer) in the processed dispersions than low boiling point solvents such as isopropyl alcohol (IPA,   $  0.009\ g\ L^{-1}  $  ) and acetone (0.007).  $  ^{[65]}  $   Although NMP is the most effective solvent for the   $  MoS_{2}  $   exfoliation, in the case of PSCs its use is problematic because it dissolves the perovskite crystals during the HTL processing.  $  ^{[37,52]}  $   Moreover, NMP has a high boiling point (  $  \approx202\ ^{\circ}C  $  ) and post-processing treatments are then required to remove its residues on the flakes after the process.  $  ^{[66]}  $   Additionally, the exfoliation of   $  MoS_{2}  $   in NMP results in the presence of superficial oxidized Mo species,  $  ^{[67]}  $   which have to be carefully quantified and removed before device fabrication. To address these limitations and implement   $  MoS_{2}  $   few-layer flakes in the fabrication of a buffer layer between Spiro-OMeTAD and perovskite layer.

# 3. Conclusion

We demonstrated the use of   $  MoS_{2}  $   flakes as an active buffer layer in hybrid lead halide perovskite solar cells with glass/FTO/compact-  $  TiO_{2}  $  /mesoporous-  $  TiO_{2}/CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}/MoS_{2}/Spiro-OMeTAD/Au  $   structure. The   $  MoS_{2}  $   flakes are first exfoliated in NMP and successively re-dispersed in IPA by using a solvent-exchange process. The   $  MoS_{2}  $   flakes are then spin-coated onto the perovskite layer before the deposition of the Spiro-OMeTAD HTL and the Au electrode. Reference PSCs without   $  MoS_{2}  $   have shown a   $  \eta  $   of 14.2% diminishing to   $  \eta = 9.3\%  $   after an endurance test of 550 h.   $  MoS_{2}  $  -based PSCs instead reached a   $  \eta  $   of 13.3% after device fabrication which decreased to   $  \eta = 12.4\%  $   after 550 h, proving the enhanced stability of the cell due to the presence of   $  MoS_{2}  $   layer acting as an active buffer layer. We have also demonstrated the scalability of our approach by fabricating large-area cells (1.05 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ), obtaining   $  \eta = 11.5\%  $  . Our results pave the way towards the implementation of   $  MoS_{2}  $   to boost the shelf life of PSCs in view of their commercialization.

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials: MoS $ _2 $  powder, NMP (99% purity), and IPA (absolute alcohol, without additive,  $ \geq $ 99.8%) were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich and used without further purification.

Liquid-Phase Exfoliation of   $  MoS_{2}  $  : Bulk   $  MoS_{2}  $   was exfoliated in NMP, obtaining dispersions of   $  MoS_{2}  $   flakes. 100 mg of   $  MoS_{2}  $   was dispersed in 10 mL of NMP and ultrasonicated (Branson 5800) for 6 h. The obtained dispersion was ultracentrifuged at 3000 g (Beckman Coulter Optima XE-90 with a SW41Ti rotor) for 20 min at 15 °C, to remove thick and un-exfoliated   $  MoS_{2}  $   flakes. After the ultracentrifugation process, 90% of the supernatant was collected by pipetting.

Solvent-Exchange Process: After the   $  MoS_{2}  $   exfoliation in NMP, we used a solvent-exchange process to re-disperse the   $  MoS_{2}  $   flakes in IPA. The NMP solvent was successively removed from the supernatant dispersion with a rotary evaporator (70 °C at 1 mPa). The precipitate was collected and washed with IPA using an ultracentrifuge at 77 000 g for 10 min for three times to optimize the NMP removal. After the last washing step, the IPA solvent was evaporated at 60 °C to collect the   $  MoS_{2}  $   powder. The flakes (27 mg) were dried in vacuum at 200 °C for 2 h to further purify the material, which was then re-dispersed in 1 mL of IPA. This solvent-exchange process has a dual function: i) it removes the NMP, detrimental for the perovskite layer; ii) it reduces the presence of Mo oxide. Finally, the purified   $  MoS_{2}  $   flakes in IPA are deposited on the perovskite layer of the cell by spin coating.

Characterization: The   $  MoS_{2}  $   dispersions were characterized by optical absorption spectroscopy in the range 300–900 nm with a Cary Varian 6000i UV–vis–NIR spectrometer. The absorption spectra were acquired using a 1 mL quartz glass cuvette. The solvent baseline was subtracted to the recorded spectrum. The concentration of   $  MoS_{2}  $   flakes in the dispersions can be estimated from the optical absorption coefficient at 672 nm, using A =   $  \alpha lc  $   where I [m] is the light path length, c [g L  $  ^{-1}  $  ] is the concentration of the dispersed flakes, and   $  \alpha  $   [L g  $  ^{-1}  $   m  $  ^{-1}  $  ] is the absorption coefficient, with   $  \alpha \approx 3400  $   L g  $  ^{-1}  $   m  $  ^{-1}  $   at 672 nm,  $  ^{[65]}  $   Raman spectroscopy (Renishaw inVia confocal Raman microscope) was performed on the   $  MoS_{2}  $   flakes collected from the dispersions. The samples were drop-cast on a Si/SiO  $  _{2}  $   (300 nm SiO  $  _{2}  $  ) substrate and dried under vacuum. An excitation wavelength of 532 nm was used with a 50× objective (incident power of ≈1 mW on the sample). The   $  MoS_{2}  $   flakes, drop-cast on Si/SiO  $  _{2}  $   substrate, were characterized morphologically by AFM with an Innova AFM (Bruker, Santa Barbara, CA). The measurements were taken in tapping mode with a NTESPA 3.75 mm cantilever (Bruker, 300 kHz k: 40 N m  $  ^{-1}  $  ), in air at room temperature, with a relative humidity less than 30%.

The crystal structure, composition, and chemical bonding of the samples in NMP and IPA were analyzed by means of TEM and XPS. The samples for the TEM analysis were prepared by drop-casting the   $  MoS_{2}  $   dispersion samples onto ultrathin carbon on holey carbon-coated copper TEM grids. A low-resolution TEM characterization was carried out using a JOEL JEM 1011 TEM operated at 100 kV. The HRTEM, STEM, EFTEM, and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) mapping were performed using a JEOL JEM 2200FS microscope operated at 200 kV and equipped with a field emission gun, in-column Omega energy filter and CEOS image Cs corrector and Bruker Quantax EDS system with a XFlash 6T-60 silicon drift detector. A FEI Tecnai F20 operated at 200 kV and equipped with Gatan Enfinum SE spectrometer was used for the compositional analysis by means of electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS). The EELS data were collected in the TEM mode with a collection semi-angle of 100 mrad.

The XPS analysis was carried out using a Kratos Axis Ultra spectrometer on samples drop cast onto silicon wafers. The XPS spectra were acquired using a monochromatic Al Kα source operated at 20 mA and 15 kV. The analyses were carried out on a 300 μm × 700 μm area. Wide scans were collected with pass energy of 160 eV and energy step of 1 eV; high-resolution spectra were instead acquired at pass energy of 10 eV and energy step of 0.1 eV. The Kratos charge neutralizer system was used on all specimens. Spectra have been charge corrected to the main line of the C 1s spectrum set to 284.8 eV, and analyzed with CasaXPS software (version 2.3.17). Region 1 allows us to identify possible residues of NMP in the samples after each preparation step, by analyzing the N 1s peak. The analysis of Region 2 is instead used to identify the evolution of the oxidation state of Mo after exfoliation in NMP and after the solvent exchange in IPA. In particular, fitting of the data in Region 2 is used to determine the ratio between  $ \mathrm{MoS}_2 $  and Mo oxides. The fitting has been performed considering that, while N 1s and S 2s signals can be fitted with single peaks, both Mo 3p and Mo 3d signals are actually doublets, due to spin-orbit coupling.[79] Mo 3p doublets were fitted to pairs of peaks with the constraints of having the same full width at half maximum (FWHM), a spin-orbit splitting of  $ 17.4\mathrm{eV} $  and a branching ratio of  $ 1/2 $ .[78] Similarly, the Mo 3d doublets were fitted to pairs of peaks with the constraints of having the same FWHM, a spin-orbit splitting of  $ 3.14\mathrm{eV} $  and a branching ratio of  $ 2/3 $ .[78] The position of each doublet has been identified by the position of the component centered at lower binding energy, i.e., Mo  $ 3p_{3/2} $  for Mo 3p and Mo  $ 3d_{5/2} $  for Mo 3d. The best fit of the data over Region 1 has been obtained considering, together with the N 1s peak, also two Mo 3p doublets, having their Mo  $ 3p_{3/2} $  components centered at  $ (394.7\pm 0.3) $  eV and at  $ (397.9\pm 0.3) $  eV. The observed values are in good agreement with those reported in ref. [80] for  $ \mathrm{MoS}_2 $  and  $ \mathrm{MoO}_3 $ , respectively. Region 2 is characterized by the presence of four peaks at  $ \approx 226\mathrm{eV} $  (peak A),  $ \approx 229\mathrm{eV} $  (peak B),  $ \approx 232 $  (peak C), and  $ \approx 236\mathrm{eV} $  (peak D). By fitting the data, we were able to assign peak A to S 2s (peak centered at  $ (226.0\pm 0.3) $  eV), peak B to the Mo  $ 3d_{5/2} $  component for Mo(IV) as observed in  $ \mathrm{MoS}_2 $  (peak centered at  $ (228.8\pm 0.3) $  eV), and peak D to the Mo  $ 3d_{3/2} $  component for Mo(VI) in  $ \mathrm{MoO}_3 $  (peak centered at  $ (235.9\pm 0.3) $  eV). The broad peak C is the results of the overlapping of the Mo  $ 3d_{3/2} $  component for Mo(IV) in  $ \mathrm{MoS}_2 $  (peak centered at  $ (231.9\pm 0.3) $  eV) with the Mo  $ 3d_{5/2} $  component for Mo(VI) in  $ \mathrm{MoO}_3 $  (peak centered at  $ (232.7\pm 0.3) $  eV).[67] A further Mo 3d doublet was needed for the best fitting, having its Mo  $ 3d_{5/2} $  component centered at  $ (231.3\pm 0.3) $  eV. This value is in good agreement with the presence of  $ \mathrm{Mo}_2\mathrm{O}_5 $  species,[81] which could appear only in presence of  $ \mathrm{MoO}_3 $  as the result of partial de-oxidation. The positions of the aforementioned peaks closely match with the data recently reported in ref. [67].

Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) analysis was performed on drop-cast films of   $  MoS_{2}  $   flakes on Si, to estimate the position of the valence band maximum (VBM) of the materials under investigation and the effect of illumination on it. The measurements were carried out with a Kratos Axis UltraDLD spectrometer using an He I (21.22 eV) discharge lamp. The analyses were carried out on an area of 55   $  \mu  $  m in diameter, at pass energy of 5 eV and with a dwell time of 100 ms. The work function (i.e., the position of the Fermi level with respect to vacuum level) was measured from the threshold energy for the emission of secondary electrons during He I excitation. A -9.0 V bias was applied to the sample in order to precisely determine the low kinetic energy cut-off, as discussed in ref. [82]. Then, the position of the VBM vs. vacuum level was estimated by measuring their distance from the Fermi level, according to the graphical method used in refs. [73,83].

Solar Cells Fabrication and Morphological Characterization: A raster scanning laser (Nd:YVO  $  _{4}  $   pulsed at 30 kHz average output power P = 10 W) was used to etch the FTO/glass substrates (Pilkington, 8   $  \Omega  $   cm  $  ^{-1}  $  , 25 mm × 25 mm), used as transparent conductive window. Small area cell (0.1 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ) and large area cell (1.05 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ) were realized using a specific pattern of the FTO substrates (the cell active area was calculated from the overlap area between FTO and gold electrodes). The patterned substrates were cleaned in an ultrasonic bath, using detergent with de-ionized water, acetone and IPA (10 min for each cleaning step). A 50 nm thick blocking TiO  $  _{2}  $   (BL-TiO  $  _{2}  $  ) layer was deposited onto the patterned FTO by spray pyrolysis deposition.  $  ^{[17,77]}  $   A 150 nm layer nanocrystalline mesoporous TiO  $  _{2}  $   (18NR-Tpaste Dyesol diluted with terpineol and ethylcellulose) was screen-printed onto the BL-TiO  $  _{2}  $   surface and sintered at 480 °C for 30 min. The mesoporous-TiO  $  _{2}  $   thickness was measured using the profilometer Dektak Veeco 150. The perovskite layer was deposited by solvent-engineering method using spin-coating technique, which permits a complete perovskite coverage of the mesoporous TiO  $  _{2}  $  , avoiding recombination of the charge carriers at the HTM/TiO  $  _{2}  $   interface.  $  ^{[32]}  $   To obtain the perovskite dispersion,

1.1 M PbI  $  _{2}  $  powder (Sigma Aldrich) and 1.1 M of CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}  $  I (Dyesol) were dispersed in dimethyl sulfoxide and stirred at 70 °C overnight. 110 μL of this dispersion was spin coated on the TiO  $  _{2}  $  substrate at 1000 rpm for 10 s and 5000 rpm 30 s. 200 μL of toluene was poured on the substrate 10 s prior the end of the second ramp. Then, the perovskite layer growth was obtained after annealing step at 100 °C for 1 h. After the perovskite growth, a layer of (1) doped Spiro-OMeTAD or (2) doped MoS  $  _{2}  $  exfoliated flakes+Spiro-OMeTAD was deposited, as follows: (1) Spiro-OMeTAD was spin coated at 2000 rpm for 20 s on the perovskite layer from a dispersion in chlorobenzene (74 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $ ) doped by the addition of 27 μL of TBP, 16 μL of Li-TFSI dispersion (520 mg in 1 mL of acetonitrile solvent) and 7 μL of cobalt additive (FK209, 0.25 M in acetonitrile). (2) MoS  $  _{2}  $  exfoliated flakes in IPA were spin-coated onto the perovskite layer at 1700 rpm for 45 s. Then, the samples were annealed for one minute at 70 °C. After the MoS  $  _{2}  $  deposition, the perovskite surface of test samples was analyzed by SEM to evaluate the uniformity of the deposition. Spiro-OMeTAD was then deposited on the MoS  $  _{2}  $  flakes as reported in (1). The Spiro-OMeTAD and MoS  $  _{2}  $  + Spiro-OMeTAD PSCs were at last introduced into a high vacuum chamber (10  $  ^{-6}  $  mbar) to thermally evaporate Au back contacts (thickness 100 nm). No sealing was applied to the fabricated cells.

Solar Cells Photovoltaic Characterization: The PSCs were tested under a solar simulator (ABET Sun 2000, class A) at AM1.5G and  $ 100\mathrm{mWcm}^{-2} $  illumination conditions (calibrated with a certified reference Si Cell, RERA Solutions RR-1002). Incident light power was measured with a Skye SKS 1110 sensor. The endurance test was carried out monitoring the devices for the period of time specified in the text (when not under measurement, the devices were kept unsealed into a desiccator in dark conditions). Incident photon-to-current conversion efficiency was measured using an apparatus made of an amperometer (Keithley 2612) and a monochromator (Newport Mod. 74000). The cells were masked with a aperture of  $ 0.25\mathrm{cm}^2 $ . Dark I/V and transient measurements were performed with a measurement system based on a high speed source meter  $ (0.6\mathrm{MS}\mathrm{s}^{-1}) $  and a white LED (Arkeo—Cicci research srl).
