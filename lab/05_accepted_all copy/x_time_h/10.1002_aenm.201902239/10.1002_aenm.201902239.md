# Halogen Engineering for Operationally Stable Perovskite Solar Cells via Sequential Deposition

Qi Li, Yao Zhao, Wenke Zhou, Zhengyuan Han, Rui Fu, Fang Lin, Dapeng Yu, and Qing Zhao*

The performance of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) relies on the synthesis method and chemical composition of the perovskite materials. So far, PSCs that have adopted two-step sequential deposited perovskite with the state-of-art composition   $  (\mathrm{FAPbI}_{3})_{1-x}(\mathrm{MAPbBr}_{3})_{x}  $   (x < 0.05) have achieved record power conversion efficiency (PCE), while their one-step antisolvent dripping counterparts with typical composition   $  Cs_{0.05}FA_{0.81}MA_{0.14}Pb(I_{0.85}Br_{0.15})_{3}  $   with more bromine have exhibited much better long-term operational stability. Thus, halogen engineering that aims to elevate bromine content in sequential deposited perovskite film would push operational stability of PSCs toward that of antisolvent dripping deposited perovskite materials. Here, a Br-rich seeding growth method is devised and perovskite seed solution with high bromine content is introduced into a   $  PbI_{2}  $   precursor, leading to bromine incorporation in the resulting perovskite film. Photovoltaic devices fabricated by Br-rich seeding growth method exhibit a PCE of 21.5%, similar to 21.6% for PSCs having lower bromine content. Whereas, the operational stability of PSCs with higher bromine content is significantly enhanced, with over 80% of initial PCE retained after 500 h tracking at maximum power point under 1-sun illumination. This work highlights the vital importance of halogen composition for the operational stability of PSCs, and introduces an effective way to incorporate bromine into mixed-cation-halide perovskite film via sequential deposition method.

# 1. Introduction

Hybrid organic–inorganic halide perovskite materials have drawn intensive research attention recently, due to its extraordinary photoelectric properties, such as large optical absorption coefficient,  $  ^{[1-3]}  $   tunable bandgap,  $  ^{[4]}  $   long and balance carrier diffusion length,  $  ^{[5]}  $   low exciton binding energy,  $  ^{[6]}  $   and simple preparation methods,  $  ^{[7-9]}  $   etc. These advantages make the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) boom from 3.8%,  $  ^{[1]}  $   to today's record 25.2%.  $  ^{[10]}  $   Nowadays, one-step antisolvent dripping deposition  $  ^{[3,9,11,12]}  $   and two-step sequential deposition  $  ^{[7,13-16]}  $   are widely used to fabricate high-quality perovskite film. Compared to one-step antisolvent dripping method, two-step sequential deposition could lead to vertical-columnar perovskite grains due to the top-down penetration during the second step.  $  ^{[13-16]}  $   Thus, a higher PCE could be achieved by sequential deposition, especially for the formamidinium-lead-iodide-based (FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  -based) perovskite material with minimal bromide anions and close-to-ideal bandgap below

Notably, perovskite materials deposited by one-step antisolvent dripping method usually contain much higher content of bromine (  $  \approx  $  17%) than the two-step sequential deposition counterpart (  $  \approx  $  3%) and better operational stability compared to PSCs based on sequential deposition method. These results indicated the vital importance of bromine anions for stabilizing mixed-cation perovskite materials during the long-term operation. The incorporation of higher content of bromine would stabilize the perovskite phase of FAPbI  $  _{3}  $    $  ^{[28]}  $   and make the perovskite phase a thermodynamically favored phase,  $  ^{[23]}  $   resulting in a better thermal stability. In addition, because the iodine anions have lower ionic migration activation energy, especially under light illumination, it would migrate easily under operational stability test and cause performance degradation of the PSC devices.  $  ^{[29,30]}  $   As a result, the replacement of iodine by bromine would improve the operational stability of PSC devices.

However, introducing bromine anions efficiently in two-step sequential deposition is a tough mission. In traditional two-step sequential deposition method, there are two possible ways to introduce bromine anions into perovksite film. One is adding  $ \mathrm{PbBr}_2 $  powder into  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  solution and forming  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 / \mathrm{PbBr}_2 $  film, then spin coating FAI solution on it (Figure 1a). Unlike the layer structure of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ ,[9,15,20,31]  $ \mathrm{PbBr}_2 $  possess a bulk structure,

which would impede the intercalation of organic amine halide during the second step and cause incomplete formation of perovskite materials (Figure 1a).  $  ^{[32]}  $   Another way is resolving FABr and MABr into FAI solution, then depositing on the PbI  $  _{2}  $   film (Figure 1b). Compared with I  $  ^{-}  $  Br  $  ^{-}  $  anion has higher ionic migration activation energy, thus the transportation of Br  $  ^{-}  $  in PbI  $  _{2}  $   layer or perovskite film is much harder and slower, which would impede the formation of final perovskite materials as well (Figure 1b).  $  ^{[25,29,33]}  $

Recently, perovskite seeding growth method  $  ^{[20]}  $  and CsCl-enhanced  $  PbI_{2}  $  precursor method were developed for better perovskite film formation with larger grains.  $  ^{[17]}  $  Zhao et al. reported the usage of perovskite precursor as seeds for the first time, which was referred as perovskite seeding growth method. In this method, submicron-sized crystalline perovskite seeds were added in  $  PbI_{2}  $  solution during the first step in sequential deposition. The perovskite seeds could facilitate perovskite crystallization during the reaction between organic amine halide and  $  PbI_{2}  $  in the second step. At the same time, perovskite seeds could provide element source, such as Cs, to the final film for better operational stability of PSCs.  $  ^{[20]}  $  On the basis of perovskite seeding growth method, Li et al. developed CsCl-enhanced  $  PbI_{2}  $  precursor method, in which CsCl was introduced into  $  PbI_{2}  $  precursor alongside the perovskite seeds in the first step in sequential deposition. CsCl could incorporate more cesium into final perovskite film, resulting in better operational stability of devices. Meanwhile, chlorine could reduce the number of nucleation sites and retard the perovskite crystallization process, leading to larger grain size and better device performance.  $  ^{[17]}  $  Based on both methods, here, we devised a Br-rich seeding growth method via halogen engineering by altering the perovskite seeding composition and seeding concentration. # 2. Results and Discussion

The schematic of Br-rich seeding growth method was shown in Figure 1c and the experimental details could be found in experimental section.  $ \mathrm{PbBr_2} $ , FABr, and MABr were preresolved with  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ , FAI, and CsI in DMF/DMSO mixed solvent at stoichiometric proportion to obtain Br-rich perovskite seed, which could be written as  $ \mathrm{Cs}_{0.2}\mathrm{MA}_{0.2}\mathrm{FA}_{0.6}\mathrm{Pb(I_{0.22}Br_{0.78})_3} $ . And then Br-rich perovskite seed was added into  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2/\mathrm{CsCl} $  solution, forming  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2/\mathrm{CsCl}/\mathrm{Br} $ -rich seed solution, followed by spin coating on  $ \mathrm{TiO}_2 $  substrate. After annealing, the organic amine salts solution containing FAI, MABr, and MACl was deposited atop the  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2/\mathrm{CsCl}/\mathrm{Br} $ -rich seed layer. After another annealing process, a perovskite layer with high bromine content was formed. By using this method, we could preintercalate bromine into  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  layer via Br-rich perovskite seed during the first step, no need to increase bromine content of FAI solution in the second step. At the same time, the bromine would not impede the intercalation of organic amine halide during the second step. There are three main parameters to better control the Br-rich perovskite seed solution: seed composition, seed concentration, and the seed adding amount to  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2/\mathrm{CsCl} $  solution. To introduce bromine anions effectively, the concentration of Br-rich perovskite seed solution should be as high as possible. Thus, the best choice

was seed solution with pure bromine. However, too much bromine would intensively decrease the solubility of the seed solution with DMF as main solvent, especially for pure bromine composition.[20,25,26] For another aspect, as different mixed solvent was used for the perovskite seed solution and  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 / \mathrm{CsCl} $  solution (see solution preparation part), the adding amount of Br-rich perovskite seed solution to  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 / \mathrm{CsCl} $  solution should be limited to minimize the mutual interference of the solvent, which required high solubility. Therefore, a little iodine should be added to compromise the contradiction between bromine content and solubility. Here, we chose I/Br proportion around 1:4, under which a Br-rich perovskite seed solution with about  $ 1\mathrm{M} $  concentration could be acquired (see solution preparation part), not too far lower than that Br-low perovskite seed solution used in CsCl-enhanced  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  precursor method (about  $ 1.2\mathrm{M} $ , see solution preparation part). At the same time, the bromine content was high enough to introduce bromine into final perovskite film. The adding amount of Br-rich perovskite seed solution to  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 / \mathrm{CsCl} $  solution would influence bromine content in final perovskite film. To obtain the target bromine content around  $ 6\% $  (this target number would be discussed later), the adding amount as  $ 70~\mu \mathrm{L} $  per  $ 1\mathrm{mL} $   $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 / \mathrm{CsCl} $  mixed solution was determined. Finally, to keep the other cations content in final perovskite film unchanged, we got the Br-rich seed composition as  $ \mathrm{Cs}_{0.2}\mathrm{MA}_{0.2}\mathrm{FA}_{0.6}\mathrm{Pb(I_{0.22}Br_{0.78})_3} $ .

Then optical properties of the perovskite film were investigated. The photoluminescence (PL) peak of "Br-rich" sample shifted from 805 to 800 nm (Figure 3e), which is originated from a shrunken perovskite unit cell parameter,  $  ^{[23,37]}  $  suggesting that bromine anions were integrated into perovskite lattice, in accordance with XRD results. Also, time-resolved PL (TRPL) decay measurement was carried out to study carrier lifetime (Figure 3f). Single exponential function was applied to fit measurement results. A carrier lifetime increase from 1049.4 ns ("control" sample) to 1527.4 ns ("Br-rich" sample) was observed, indicating reduced density of defect states by using Br-rich seeding growth method, which could be allocated to the enlarged grain size and lessened grain boundaries. To confirm the carrier lifetime results, we also used biexponential function to fit TRPL results. The fitting curve and fitting result parameters were given in Figure S2 in the Supporting Information. It is worthy noting that when biexponential decay model was assigned, carrier lifetime for the relatively fast decay component (t1) shown a very similar value compared to that for the slower component (t2), which means the TRPL results exhibit a monoexponential behavior in fact. In other words, no fast decay could be detected. Because the fast decay component usually related to nonradiative recombination induced by trap states,  $  ^{[16,38]}  $  this monoexponential behavior indicated a high perovskite film quality with rare defect states at the same time.

External quantum efficiency (EQE) measurement was also performed to confirm the   $  J_{sc}  $   value in the J-V curves (Figure 4b). And the integrated   $  J_{sc}  $   values are 23.7 and 23.4 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   for the “control” devices and “Br-rich” devices, respectively, consisting well with the   $  J_{sc}  $   values determined by J-V curves. The zoomed onset part of EQE spectra around 800 nm (Figure S4, Supporting Information) shows a clear shift to shorter wavelength in “Br-rich” sample, indicating a larger bandgap of perovskite film, in accordance with PL and XRD results. The EQE results also pointed out that the short-circuit current loss mainly originated from inferior light absorption at long wavelength region near the bandgap, in agreement with the higher bandgap of “Br-rich” sample. Besides J-V curve, a similar performance is observed in steady-state output measurement (Figure 4c), both “control” devices and “Br-rich” devices exhibited stabilized output efficiency around 20.55% within 500 s.

Then the thermal stability and long-term operational stability of the PSCs were studied. Thermal stress test was applied to bare perovskite film considering that Spiro-OMeTAD cannot sustain high temperature.[3] Both "control" and "Br-rich" samples were heated under  $ 150^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 30\mathrm{min} $  in a  $ \mathbf{N}_2 $  -filled glove box. XRD pattern of "control" and "Br-rich" samples (Figure 5a,b) showed an intensity growth for  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  peak (denoted as solid triangles) after heating, but the "Br-rich" sample exhibited a less profound  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  after heating, implying an enhanced thermal stability. The operational stability was performed under AM 1.5G irradiation and MPP tracking condition, and the normalized PCE versus time was shown in Figure 5c. PCE of "control" device decayed below  $ 80\% $  of the initial value after  $ 150\mathrm{h} $ . Surprisingly, with only  $ 3\% $  higher bromine content (from  $ \approx 3\% $  to  $ \approx 6\% $ ), the "Br-rich" device could maintain  $ 90\% $  of its initial PCE after  $ 200\mathrm{h} $  of continuous working. Even after  $ 500\mathrm{h} $  of continuous working, the "Br-rich" device still retains over  $ 80\% $  of its initial PCE. This result stands for an operational stability among the best class of PSCs fabricated via sequential deposition method, even comparable with that fabricated by one-step antisolvent dripping method (Table 1). At the same time, it highlights the significance of Br content in improving operational stability of PSCs.

To confirm that the operational stability originated from higher bromine anion content, we performed further control experiments by substituting CsCl using in   $  PbI_{2}  $   solution by the same molar weight CsBr to further increase Br content in the final perovskite film. XPS spectra of perovskite film samples fabricated by using   $  PbI_{2}/CsCl/Br  $  -rich seed mixed solution (denoted as “CsCl” here) and by using   $  PbI_{2}/CsBr/Br  $  -rich seed mixed solution (denoted as “CsBr” here) were shown in Figure S5 in the Supporting Information. It is obvious that the bromine content was elevated again (Figure S5c, Supporting Information). Also, qualitative investigation was applied to extract relative content of different elements from XPS spectrum (Figure S5, Supporting Information). The entire calculation process was similar to that mentioned above and could be found in Note S2 in the Supporting Information. XPS result showed that after substituting CsCl with CsBr, cesium content remained unchanged while bromine concentration in final perovskite film was further promoted from   $  \approx6\%  $   to   $  \approx8\%  $  , indicating a perovskite composition of   $  Cs_{0.05}MA_{0.03}FA_{0.92}Pb(I_{0.92}Br_{0.08})_{3}  $  . The excess bromine anion further widened bandgap of perovskite material, leading to a higher   $  V_{OC}  $   and lower   $  J_{SC}  $  . The   $  V_{OC}  $   increase could not compensate the   $  J_{SC}  $   decrease this time, resulting in a slight decay on final PCE from 21.5% to 20.8% (Figure S6, Supporting Information). Thus, in Br-rich seeding growth method, bromine content should not exceed 6% to maintain a basic PCE. To determine the target bromine content of Br-rich seeding growth method as 6%, which was used throughout this work, we fabricated solar cell devices with perovskite films having different bromine content ranging from 3% to 6%. The different bromine content was reached

by adding different amount of Br-rich seeding precursor into the same amount   $  PbI_{2}  $   solution. The statistical results showed that when bromine content elevated from 3% to 6%, PCE value decreased slightly while operational stability was obviously enhanced (Figures S8 and S9, Supporting Information). However, further elevating bromine content to 8% would lead to a fast drop of PCE, although the device performed even better operational stability (Figures S8 and S9, Supporting Information). Finally, we chose 6% as target bromine content to balance the PCE and operational stability of devices. As depicted in Figure S7 in the Supporting Information, the operational stability of PSC devices based on perovskite layer with 8% bromine content was further enhanced. After continuous testing under AM 1.5G irradiation and MPP tracking for 200 h, the device with 8% bromine content (that is “CsBr” here) could maintain 95% of its initial PCE, better than 90% for device fabricated by Br-rich seeding growth method (that is “CsCl” here). These results further confirm that higher bromine anion content is beneficial to the operational stability of final photovoltaic devices.

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, we reported a Br-rich seeding growth method to produce perovskite film with higher bromine content based on state-of-art perovskite material composition   $  Cs_{0.05}MA_{0.03}FA_{0.92}Pb(I_{0.97}Br_{0.03})_{3}  $   via halogen engineering. The Br-rich seeding growth method led to larger perovskite grain size, less grain boundaries and defect states, and longer carrier lifetime, leading to a best PCE of 21.5% for PSC devices. More importantly, the introducing of more bromine anions from 3% to 6% significantly promoted the operational stability of PSC device. The “Br-rich” devices could sustain over 80% of its initial PCE after 500 h of continuous working at MPP bias under AM 1.5G illumination, rather than just 100 h for “control” samples. This work offers an approach to effectively incorporate bromine anions into the state-of-art mixed cation perovskite via sequential deposition, and at the same time of sustaining high PCE, achieving much better operational stability.

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials: N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.9%), chlorobenzene (99.8%), acetonitrile (ACN, 99.9%), isopropanol (IPA, 99.7%), lead iodide (PbI  $  _{2}  $  , beads, 99.999%), cesium chloride (CsCl, beads, 99.999%), cesium bromide (CsBr, 99.999%) and cesium iodide (CsI, 99.999%), were bought from Sigma-Aldrich. Lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (Li-TFSI, 98%) and lead bromide (PbBr  $  _{2}  $  , 99.999%) were purchased from Alfa Aesar. 4-tert-butylpyridine (4-TBP, 96%) was got from TCI. Methylammonium bromide (MABr), methylammonium chloride (MACI, 99.5%), formamidinium bromide (FABr), formamidinium iodide (FAI, 99.5%) and 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-p-dimethoxy-phenylamino)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD, 99.8%) were obtained from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corporation. No further purification was conducted to all the chemicals and were used as received.

Solution Preparation: 625 mg PbI  $  _{2}  $  beads and 10 mg CsCl beads were dissolved in 850  $  \mu  $  L DMF and 115  $  \mu  $  L DMSO and stirred at 80 °C overnight to prepare the PbI  $  _{2}  $ /CsCl solution. The control perovskite seed solution was prepared by adding 600 mg PbI  $  _{2}  $  beads, 36.75 mg PbBr  $  _{2}  $  powder, 189.2 mg FAI powder, 16.8 mg MABr powder, and 39 mg CsI powder into 1 mL 4:1 volume ratio DMF/DMSO mixed solvent and stirring at 60 °C for 30 min. Similarly, the Br-rich perovskite seed solution was prepared by solving 34.6 mg PbI  $  _{2}  $  beads, 357.8 mg PbBr  $  _{2}  $  powder, 57.25 mg FAI powder, 37.15 mg FABr powder, 23.5 mg MABr

powder, and 54.5 mg CsI powder into 1 mL 4:1 volume ratio DMF/DMSO mixed solvent and stirring at room temperature (  $  \approx  $  25 °C) for 60 min. The PbI  $  _{2}  $  /CsCl/control-seed and PbI  $  _{2}  $  /CsCl/Br-rich seed mixed solution were obtained by adding 130 μL control perovskite seed solution and 70 μL Br-rich perovskite seed solution into 1 mL PbI  $  _{2}  $  /CsCl solution, respectively. The PbI  $  _{2}  $  /CsBr solution was prepared by dissolving 3750 mg PbI  $  _{2}  $  beads and 70 mg CsBr in 5.1 mL DMF and 700 μL DMSO, and the solution was stirred at 80 °C overnight. The PbI  $  _{2}  $  /CsBr/Br-rich seed mixed solution were obtained by adding 70 μL Br-rich perovskite seed solution into 1 mL PbI  $  _{2}  $  /CsBr solution. For the preparation of FAI/MABr/MACl solution, 733.3 mg FAI, 73.3 mg MABr, and 76.7 mg MACl powder were dissolved in 10 mL IPA and stirred for about 90 min under room temperature. For the preparation of Spiro-OMeTAD solution, 73.4 mg Spiro-OMeTAD, 17.6 μL Li-TFSI solution (520 mg Li-TFSI in 1 mL ACN), and 28.8 μL 4-TBP were mixed in 1 mL chlorobenzene and stirred overnight. The water-based TiO  $  _{2}  $  colloid precursor was prepared according to previous work.  $  ^{[42]}  $  Briefly, the TiO  $  _{2}  $  -Cl nanoparticles were synthesized following modified nonhydrolytic sol-gel method and the nanocrystals sediment were dispersed in ultrapure water to make a suspension.

Perovskite Solar Cells Fabrication: Indium tin oxide (ITO) coated glasses (  $  \approx15\ \Omega\ square^{-1}  $  ) were prepatterned by retailer. The ITO-coated glasses were cleaned by ultrasonic cleaner using acetone and isopropanol sequentially before use. After cleaning, water-based   $  TiO_{2}  $   colloid precursor was spin-coated on the ITO at 4000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, the substrates were annealed on a hot plate at 150 °C for 30 min in ambient air. After annealing and cooling to room temperature, the substrates were transferred into   $  N_{2}  $  -filled glovebox. The perovskite films were fabricated via a two-step CsCl-enhanced   $  PbI_{2}  $   precursor method introduced in a previous work.  $  ^{[17]}  $   Firstly, the   $  PbI_{2}/CsCl/control-seed  $  ,   $  PbI_{2}/CsCl/Br-rich seed  $   or   $  PbI_{2}/CsBr/Br-rich seed mixed  $   solution was spin-coated on the substrates for 20 s at 1500 rpm and 60 s at 5000 rpm, a 2 min annealing at 80 °C was followed up. The FAI/MABr/MACl solution was subsequently spin-coated on the   $  PbI_{2}/CsCl/control-seed  $   or   $  PbI_{2}/CsCl/Br-rich seed layer  $   after it cooling down to room temperature. The spin-coated program was 1700 rpm for 30 s. Then, the film was annealed at 40 °C for 2 min in glovebox and 140 °C for 35 min in air sequentially. Finally, the perovskite film was washed by isopropanol using drop-casting method at 4500 rpm for 30 s after cooling to room temperature, a 5 min annealing at 100 °C was followed up to remove residual isopropanol. For the hole-transport layer, it was obtained by spin coating the Spiro-OMeTAD solution on the top of perovskite film at 3000 rpm for 30 s. At last, on top of the Spiro-OMeTAD layer, a 80 nm Au electrode was thermally evaporated.

Solar Cells Characterization: An Agilent B2900 Series precision source/measure unit was used to perform current density-voltage (J-V) characterization. During the test, the cells were illuminated under AM 1.5G irradiation (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) by a Xeon lamp solar simulator (Solar IV-150A, Zolix). A Newport calibrated KG5-filtered Si reference cell was used to calibrate the light intensity. The scanning rate of the J-V curves was 50 mV s  $  ^{-1}  $   (voltage step of 10 mV and delay time of 200 ms), and the scanning scope and direction was from 1.15 to -0.01 V (reverse) or from -0.01 to 1.15 V (forward). For the EQE measurement, a solar cell QE/IPCE measurement system (Solar Cell Scan100, Zolix) was used, and it was also calibrated by a certified silicon solar cells. Meanwhile, the scan interval was 2 nm and no bias light was used during the measurement. The stability test was performed in a N  $  _{2}  $  -filled glovebox under room-temperature and measured under AM 1.5G solar simulator (XES-50S1, AAA class, SAN-EI electric co., LTD). A 420 nm cutoff UV filter was applied. The stability test was performed by setting the bias voltage to the initial max power point and tracing the current density. Under all measurement above, the perovskite solar cells' active area was fixed at 4.9 mm  $  ^{2}  $   by a mask.

Perovskite Film Characterization: The top-view SEM images of the perovskite films and the cross-sectional SEM images of the PSC devices were recorded by a scanning electron microscope (Quanta FEG 450) under  $ 5\mathrm{kV} $  electron beam accelerate voltage.
