# Abstract:

Metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have made a great progress in recent years, however, charge recombination and losses at interfaces between the different layers restrict their performance. In the present work, we devised an efficient method that not only passivates the interfacial traps but also enhances charge extraction of the PSC. The chlorobenzene with the addition of Poly(3-hexylthiophene) (P3HT) is used as antisolvent during the spin coating process of the wet perovskite film. In this way, P3HT molecules permeate along perovskite grain boundaries to fill the pinholes and repair interfacial defects of the perovskite film. In addition, the valence band (VB) of the surface of the perovskite layer is thus tuned to form an energy level better aligned with the hole transport layer (HTL). Such a gradient heterojunction structure (GHJ) layer containing P3HT and perovskite provides a rapid channel for hole migration and improves carrier extraction efficiency. The power conversion efficiency of the champion solar cell is up to  $ 20.0\% $  with an open-circuit voltage of 1.18V, a short-circuit current density of  $ 22.2\mathrm{mA/cm^2} $  and a fill factor of  $ 76.2\% $ . Due to the hydrophobicity of the mixture layer, the long-term stability of the devices has been improved as well.

Key words: perovskite solar cell; surface passivation; hole extraction; P3HT

# 1 Introduction

Metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have shown rapid development since 2009 and their record power conversion efficiency (PCE) rockets to  $ 25.2\%^{1,2} $  These are attributed to the unique combination of excellent electrical and electro-optical properties of the perovskite, such as remarkably high absorption over the visible spectrum, $ ^{3} $  long charge carrier diffusion length, $ ^{4} $  high carrier mobility, $ ^{5} $  and low exciton binding energy. $ ^{3,6} $  As light-absorbing layer, the perovskite could harvest photons to produce excitons that are separated and extracted by the electron transport layer (ETL) and hole transport layer (HTL), respectively, thus generating electric current. Therefore, the quality of the perovskite layer plays a vital role in the performance of PSCs. During the crystallization process of the perovskite prepared by solution methods, pinholes and cracks around grain boundaries are easy to appear due to heterogeneous nucleation and growth, deteriorating the quality of the film. $ ^{7-9} $  Meanwhile, due to the presence of volatile organic components, the uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} $  generated in the perovskite film during the thermal annealing process would increase the trap density of the perovskite film. $ ^{10} $  The above pinholes, cracks and uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} $  are usually served as the recombination sites, which are detrimental to the performance of PSCs. Also, the matched interface energy level alignment between the light-absorption layer and HTL could assure a high hole extraction efficiency, which may lead to a high efficiency. $ ^{11-19} $  The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of the commonly used hole transport material, Spiro-OMeTAD is around  $ -5.2\mathrm{eV},^{20} $  while the valence band (VB) of the perovskite is about  $ -5.7\mathrm{eV} $ . The gap of  $ 0.5\mathrm{eV} $  between the two layers would reduce the efficiency of hole extraction and cause a loss of open-circuit voltage ( $ \mathrm{V_{OC}} $ ) of PSCs.

Constructing a thin layer between the perovskite and HTL is commonly used to passivate the surface defects of the perovskite film. Two kinds of materials are commonly used. The insulating layer,  $  ^{21-23}  $  which is beneficial for the charge recombination, but not helpful for the charge extraction. And its passivation effect will be restricted by the film thickness. Another one is semiconducting material,  $  ^{22-26}  $  a

relatively thicker semiconducting layer between the perovskite layer and HTL, can not only passivate those defects on the perovskite film, but also increase the hole extraction efficiency and reduce the energy loss of hole extraction. However, the perovskite layer is usually destroyed by solvent during fabrication process of passivation layer.  $  ^{27}  $

In the present study, we devise an idea to introduce an additive with function of hole extraction into antisolvent. The additive may penetrate the perovskite layer during the process of antisolvent extraction to form a gradient heterojunction in the upper layer of perovskite.  $  ^{28}  $   The interface energy level alignment between the light-absorption layer and HTL may be tuned by composition engineering to change the VB position of the upper perovskite layer, thus reduce the recombination loss at the interface. Meanwhile, the additive could fill the pinholes and repair the cracks in the perovskite grain boundaries to reduce the surface traps of the perovskite film, thereby decreasing charge recombination. Thus, we select Poly(3-hexylthiophene) (P3HT) as an additive, which is dissolved in antisolvent chlorobenzene (CB). During the process of antisolvent extraction, P3HT molecules penetrate along the perovskite grain boundaries. In addition, the slight residual P3HT molecules are deposited on the perovskite layer surface to form an ultrathin film. P3HT molecules not only fill up pinholes and penetrate grain boundary to efficiently reduce recombination sites, but also tune the VB position of perovskite layer to pursue a more matched energy level alignment with HTL. And the sulfur element of the P3HT may bond with the uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $  , thus reducing the non-radiative recombination and improving the stability of the overall devices. As a result, the solar cells achieve PCE of 20.0% and still maintain over 80% of initial PCE after 350h storing in drying vessel without encapsulation.

# 2.2 Device fabrication

FTO glasses with sheet resistance of 7 Ω had been cleaned using deionized water, acetone, and ethyl alcohol in sequence. After that, the FTO glasses were treated by ultraviolet ozone for 15min. Then, a thin layer of SnO₂ nanoparticle was obtained by spin coating SnO₂ solution (1:3, volume ratio, diluted by water) at 3000r.p.m. for 30s. Then the substrates were annealed in ambient air at 150°C for 30min. For perovskite precursor, 1M of FAI, 1.1M of PbI₂, 0.2M of MABr, 0.2M of PbBr₂ dissolved in DMF/DMSO (4:1; volume ratio) mixed solvent, and add 45μL of CsI solution (1.5M, dissolved in DMSO), 20μL of KI solution (2M, dissolved in DMF/DMSO). For optimized antisolvent, 1, 2, 4mg P3HT was dissolved in CB, respectively. The following steps were all operated in a nitrogen-filled glove box. Next, the perovskite solution was spinning coated by one-step method via a two steps program at 1000r.p.m. and 6000r.p.m. for 10s and 20s, respectively. During the second step, 200μL of antisolvent (CB and CB solutions with different concentrations of P3HT) was poured on the spinning substrate 5s prior to the end of the program, and then annealed at 105°C for 50min. After cooling down to room temperature, a 20μL Spiro-OMeTAD solution which by dissolved 72.3mg Spiro-OMeTAD into 1mL chlorobenzene followed by the addition of 17.5μL Li-TFSI (pre-dissolved as a 520mg/mL stock solution in acetonitrile) and 29μL 4-TBP was spun on the annealed perovskite films at 4000 rpm for 30s. Finally, a 60nm of Ag was evaporated on the top of HTL as the back electrode. So does the whole device was completed with an active area of 0.1008cm².

The crystal structure of the perovskite films were studied by X-ray diffraction using the diffractometer (PW3040/60, PANalytical, Holland) with Cu K  $ \alpha $  radiation.

# 3 Results and Discussion

P3HT will exist on both the surface and grain boundaries of the perovskite layer. As shown in top-view scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of perovskite layer (Figure 1b, c), the surface morphology of the pristine perovskite film (fabrication using antisolvent without P3HT) is clearer than that of the perovskite passivated by P3HT, suggesting a thin layer of P3HT on the perovskite film. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra have been carried out to demonstrate the existing P3HT within the perovskite layer (Figure 2a). The perovskite passivated with P3HT presents two characteristic peaks at  $ 2916\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $  and  $ 2856\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $ , which correspond with the  $ -\mathrm{CH}_2 $ -stretching vibrational modes of pure P3HT. These are not observed in the curve of the pristine perovskite. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is used to investigate the binding energy (BE) changes. Figure 2b, c present XPS spectra of Pb 4f and S 2p of the perovskite films prepared with and without P3HT passivation. A slight shift to higher BE observed in XPS spectra of Pb 4f in the passivated film indicates that sulfur in P3HT structure may interact with the uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  which are usually served as carrier recombination sites. This would decrease the defects density of the perovskite layer. Meanwhile, the XPS spectra of S 2p occurred in the passivated film further confirms the existence of P3HT again.

Ultraviolet photoelectron emission spectra (UPS) is used to study the energy levels of the perovskite layer with and without passivation as shown in Figure 2d, e. The Fermi edge (  $  E_{Fermi}  $  ) and cutoff edge (  $  E_{Cutoff}  $  ) in the curves are used to calculate the corresponding VB position. The results show the VB of the passivated perovskite is -5.53 eV, which locates at between the VB of perovskite (-5.72 eV) and HOMO of P3HT (-5.28 eV as shown in Figure S3). This indicates that the surface of the passivated layer is the mixture of perovskite and P3HT (GHJ layer), which is attributed to the interaction between perovskite and P3HT that causes the shift of VB position. Since P3HT molecules exist on the upper surface of perovskite, it affects only the energy level of the perovskite near the crystal surface.  $  ^{28}  $   On the basis of the above results, we draw a schematic diagram of the energy level of the passivated device (Figure 2f), showing the better match energy band alignment. As we can see, the existence of the GHJ structure (containing perovskite and P3HT) provides a green channel for holes transfer and extraction, thereby improving the charge collection efficiency.

However, both the perovskite layers covered and passivated by P3HT show much lower PL intensity than that of the pristine perovskite. We know the passivation of defects could reduce nonradiative recombination, which has not contribution on the decrease of PL intensity. The defect density of the film has been evaluated by space-charge limited current (SCLC) measurements as show in Figure 3c, d. The typical dark J-V curve can be classified into three stages based on exponent of n value. J is proportional to   $  V^{n=1}  $  ,   $  V^{n=2}  $   and   $  V^{n\geq3}  $   corresponding to the Ohmic, Child and trap-filled limit regions, respectively. The all the trap states can be filled by the injected carriers at the trap-filled stage. The voltage located at the cross point of the Ohmic and trap-filled limit stages is named as trap-filled limit voltage (  $  V_{TFL}  $  ). Then, we can calculate the defect density of the perovskite film using the following equation:  $  ^{34-36}  $

$$
\mathrm{N} _ {\text { traps }} = 2 \varepsilon_ {0} \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{r}} \mathrm{V} _ {\mathrm{TFL}} / \mathrm{eL} ^ {2}
$$

Where   $  \varepsilon_{r}  $   and   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   are the dielectric constants of perovskite and the vacuum permittivity, respectively.   $  V_{TFL}  $   is trap-filled limit voltage that is obtained from Figure 3c, d. L is the thickness of the perovskite films, and e is the elementary charge. A hole-only device with the configuration of FTO/PEDOT:PSS/Perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD/Ag is used to study the hole trap density. The results reveal that the   $  V_{TFL}  $   values for the pristine perovskite and passivated perovskite devices were 0.526V and 0.344V, respectively. The corresponding trap densities are   $  1.84\times10^{16}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $   and   $  1.12\times10^{16}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  , respectively. This demonstrates the passivated perovskite film has a lower trap density, thus leading to a higher   $  V_{OC}  $   value.  $  ^{37}  $

# 4 Conclusion

We demonstrated an effective method for constructing a GHJ layer on the surface of perovskite layer using additive of P3HT. As an additive in antisolvent solution, P3HT molecules penetrated into perovskite grain boundaries with a gradient distribution to

Journal Pre-proofs

form a GHJ structure. The sulfur in P3HT interacted with the uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   of perovskite to reduce the trap density of the perovskite layer, thereby reducing the charge recombination at the interface of perovskite layer and HTL. In addition, the VB of the P3HT passivated perovskite locates at the between perovskite and Spiro-OMeTAD, which improve the energy band alignment of PSC for assisting holes migration and extraction. The PSC based on passivated layer exhibited a PCE of 20.0% with a   $  V_{OC}  $   as high as 1.18 V. Due to hydrophobic performance of the P3HT passivated layer, the devices without encapsulation showed excellent stability and maintained over 80% of the initial PCE for 350h in drying vessel at room temperature.
