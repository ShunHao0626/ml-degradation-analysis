# Effect of the incorporation of poly(ethylene oxide) copolymer on the stability of perovskite solar cells

Jeann Carlos da Silva  $  ^{a}  $  , Francineide Lopes de Araújo  $  ^{a}  $  , Rodrigo Szostak  $  ^{a,b}  $  , Paulo Ernesto Marchezia  $  ^{a}  $  , Raphael Fernando Moral  $  ^{a}  $  , Jilian Nei de Freitas  $  ^{c}  $   and Ana Flávia Nogueira  $  ^{*a}  $

The incorporation of a well-established copolymer based on poly (ethylene oxide) was investigated towards maximizing the stability of perovskite solar cells based on methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI $ _3 $ ) Poly (ethylene oxide-co-epichlorohydrin) P(EO/EP) was chosen as an additive to the precursor solution to minimize the degradation process under ambient conditions. A power conversion efficiency of 17.04% was obtained for the standard solar cell, and the efficiency was gradually decreased as the concentration of P(EO/EP) is increased. In spite of the efficiency loss, the stability of the films against aggressive humidity and illumination conditions was investigated and the perovskite films containing the copolymer showed enhanced stability. Through H-NMR spectroscopy, it was possible to verify the existence of hydrogen bonding between the polymer and methylammonium cation. This interaction is responsible for retaining the cation in the structure, thus conferring stability to the film. It was also observed that the polymer incorporation delays the perovskite crystallization, which was accompanied by in situ Grazing Incidence Wide Angle X-ray Scattering. The incorporation of P(EO/EP) has also decreased the average grain size and passivated the surface defects of the perovskite layer. The device without P(EO/EP) and the device containing 1.5 mg mL $ ^{-1} $  P(EO/EP) retained 47% and 68% of the starting PCE values, respectively, after 480 hours (20 days) exposed to environmental conditions (relative humidity ~ 53%), indicating that the incorporation of P(EO/EP) in the perovskite active layer provided greater stability to the perovskite film.

# Introduction

Hybrid organic-inorganic perovskites (general formula ABX $ _3 $ , where A represents an organic cation, B an inorganic cation and X a halide anion) $ ^1 $  have attracted great interest in the field of photovoltaics due to their excellent optoelectronic properties, such as high optical absorption coefficient, low exciton binding energy, long diffusion length, high carrier mobility, and ease of fabrication. $ ^{2-5} $  In just a few years, the perovskite solar cells (PCSs) in lab-scale reached a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of over 25.2%, which exceeds the efficiencies of polycrystalline silicon and second generation thin-film photovoltaic devices. $ ^{6,7} $  This advance occurred as a result of intense research in the area, including the synthesis of new materials, device fabrication optimization including new architectures, interface engineering, among other strategies. $ ^{8-14} $  However, despite the rapid progress in terms of efficiency, there is still room to improve all photovoltaic parameters. Besides, stability of the

PSCs under environmental conditions is not less important and both are key challenges for their commercialization.  $  ^{[15,16]}  $   Environmental factors such as oxygen, humidity, heat and UV irradiation are responsible for the degradation of these devices, mainly affecting the integrity of the active layer (perovskite film) and the interfaces of the solar cells.  $  ^{[17-20]}  $   Thus, investigating the causes and finding solutions to improve the stability of these devices in environmental conditions has been a matter of utmost importance.

The stability of the perovskite active layer is directly linked to its composition, so that changing the perovskite chemical composition is a way to obtain enhanced stability. The difficulty of this strategy is that the replacement of the cations and anions within the structure must be done in compliance with the structural parameters of the material. Besides that, the optoelectronic properties of the perovskites come precisely from their particular band structure, a direct result of their composition.  $  ^{21}  $   Li et al.  $  ^{22}  $   studied the tolerance factor (t) of the solid-state alloying of FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   (large t) and CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   (small t) and showed that, with proper tuning of the tolerance factor, the alloyed material displays a more stable   $  \alpha  $  -phase, leading to devices with better stability. FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   solar cells had a loss of 50% of the initial PCE after 15 days (at 15% relative humidity), while FA  $  _{0.85}  $  Cs  $  _{0.15}  $  PbI  $  _{3}  $   solar cells had an increase of the initial PCE during the first three days and then maintained its performance almost unchanged. Saliba et al.  $  ^{8}  $   produced perovskites with general formula Cs  $  _{x}  $  (MA  $  _{0.17}  $  FA  $  _{0.83}  $  )  $  _{(100-x)}  $  Pb(I  $  _{0.83}  $  Br  $  _{0.17}  $  )  $  _{3}  $   containing a mixture of cesium (Cs  $  ^{+}  $  ), formamidinium (FA  $  ^{+}  $  ) and

Another point of discussion is that, in their work, the control device performed poorly, and this led us to conclude whether the impact of the polymer would not only be on the stability gain, but on the performance.

P(EO/EP) is a copolymer that has been successfully used by our group in solid-state dye-sensitized solar cells (DSSCs). $ ^{33-38} $  We employed in situ grazing incidence wide angle X-ray scattering analysis to investigate the formation of the perovskite layer in the presence of P(EO/EP). The incorporation of P(EO/EP) has also decreased the average grain size and passivated the surface defects of the perovskite layer. respectively, after 480 hours (20 days) exposed to environmental conditions (relative humidity  $ 53\% $ ), indicating that the incorporation of P(EO/EP) in the perovskite active layer provided greater stability to the perovskite film.

# Experimental details

# Perovskite precursor solutions

Methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI $ _3 $ ) perovskite stock solution was prepared by dissolving 645 mg of PbI $ _2 $  (TCI America) and 222 mg of methylammonium iodide (MAI) (GreatCell Solar) in 1 mL of a mixture of N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) 4:1 v/v. Copolymer P(EO/EP) with average molar mass $ ^{39} $  1.3×10 $ ^6 $  g mol $ ^{-1} $  was supplied by Daiso Co., Ltd (Japan). Stock solution was prepared by dissolving 18.75 mg of P(EO/EP) in 1 mL of a mixture of N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) 4:1 v/v. The final solutions were prepared from mixtures between the MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite stock solution and the P(EO/EP) stock solution, as displayed in Table S1.

# Measurement and Characterization

The current-voltage characteristics (J-V curves) of the PSCs were measured under 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  AM 1.5 G illumination of a class AAA Solar Simulator (HAL-320, Asahi Spectra Co., Ltd.), using a source-meter (Keithley 2400 SourceMeter), in a voltage range of -0.1 to 1.1 V (forward scan) and +1.1 to -0.1 V (reverse scan) with steps of 10 mV and delay time of 0.25 s. Solar Simulator was calibrated with a silicon reference solar cell with a KG5 filter. Stability tests were performed in ambient conditions at room temperature (25.3 ± 1.2) °C, relative humidity (53.5 ± 11.1) % and in dark, without any encapsulation. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were acquired with a Shimadzu diffractometer model XRD-6000) with X-ray wavelength of CuKα = 0.154 nm. The morphology of the perovskite films without and

with the addition of P(EO/EP) was obtained by an NX10 atomic force microscope (AFM) from Park Systems using a   $  \beta  $   tip NSC14/Cr-Au, and by scanning electron microscopy using a Quanta 250 microscope coupled with a field emission-scanning (FEG-SEM). The hydrogen nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectra were obtained using a Bruker spectrometer, model Avance 500 MHz and the data were processed using the software Topspin 3.5.

The regeneration test of the perovskite films was carried out with the sample exposed at a distance of  $ 3\mathrm{cm} $  from a commercial humidifier outlet for 30 seconds and, after that time, photographs of the films were captured 20 to  $ 30\mathrm{s} $  after the end of the exposure. X-Ray diffractograms were collected before and after exposure.

Experiments of in-situ GIWAXS (grazing incidence wide angle X-Ray scattering) during the spin coating stage were conducted on the XRD2 beam line at the National Synchrotron Light Laboratory (LNLS), Campinas, Brazil. The X-ray energy was 7 kV and the scattering signal was collected by a Dectris Pilatus 300K detector with an integration time of 1 s. The angle of incidence of the X-ray beam was set to 3°. The detector was placed at 290.7 mm from the sample. The antisolvent (150 μL of ethyl acetate) injection was carried out using an automatic syringe coupled to a hose. Data were collected during spin coating deposition using the same parameters used for film and solar cell preparation. After, the sample was kept for more 600 seconds (10 minutes) after the end of the rotation process. Each GIWAXS image was calibrated using AgBE (silver behenate) standard and azimuthally integrated to obtain 1D X-ray diffraction patterns using pyFAI python libraries. Figure S1 displays the photographs of the experimental setup at XRD2/LNLS beamline used for in situ GIWAXS measurements. Figures S2-S5 show the GIWAXS reciprocal lattice maps.

Looking closely to Figure 7e there is a change in the slope of the intensity versus time curve for the standard sample, what is not observed for the samples with PEO. This result differs from those reported by Zhao and collaborators,  $  ^{32}  $   where the authors obtained denser films with better substrate coverage after PEO addition. The authors attributed this improvement to the polymer's ability to form a three-dimensional scaffold that would support the growth of perovskite crystals, in addition to decreasing the crystallization speed. In that work, the crystallization of perovskite was achieved by spin coating a solution containing MAI and PbCl  $  _{2}  $   in DMF (3:1 volume ratio) without the use of anti-solvent. Besides, the molecular weight of the polymer was much lower in their work. As the crystallinity and morphology of the film are closely linked to the deposition methodology, there is further evidence that the interaction of the polymer with the DMSO and, consequently, with the intermediate species, is responsible for the different morphologies of the samples with different concentrations of polymer,

A visual stability test of films was performed with a polymer concentration of 0 to  $ 2\mathrm{mg}\mathrm{mL}^{-1} $  (Figure 8). The films were kept under ambient lighting and humidity conditions for 9 days. The images show that, with the increase in the amount of P(EO/EP) copolymer, the appearance of yellowish regions, characteristic of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ , is delayed in the films with higher concentration of the copolymer. This stability is justified by the MAI retention in the structure, caused by the establishment of hydrogen bonds between the polymer and the  $ -\mathrm{NH}_3 $  group, a previously discussed. However, we cannot discard an additional protective effect by the insulating polymer.

# Conclusions

The preparation of perovskite-based photovoltaic devices in the absence of a controlled environment was possible using ethyl acetate as an anti-solvent during the deposition of the perovskite layer by spin-coating. The choice for ethyl acetate as anti-solvent allowed us the assembly of devices with 17.04% efficiency based on MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  in ambient conditions. This solvent had a good ability to solubilize water, being capable of protecting the intermediate species from moisture, simplifying the deposition process. It was observed that the incorporation of P(EO/EP) copolymer modifies the crystallization dynamics and the morphology of the black phase perovskite films. Results from in situ GIWAXS indicated that this copolymer is capable of stabilizing the MA  $  _{2}  $  Pb  $  _{3}  $  I  $  _{8}  $ .2DMSO intermediate phase in the film through interaction with DMSO. The photovoltaic performance decreased upon polymer addition, with all parameters being affected, but with more influence on FF and Jsc values. The apparent buffer effect on the V  $  _{oc}  $  values might be explained by the strong donor character of PEO and its ability to passivate defects. The morphology is also drastically changed and we believe that the decrease in the PCE values is a sum of the insulating character of the polymer and decrease in the grain size. On the other hand, the addition of P(EO/EP) provided greater stability to the perovskite film in the face of moisture, either under continuous or aggressive exposure conditions, mainly due to the hydrogen bond formed between the -NH  $  _{3}^{+}  $  group of perovskite and the oxygen of the polymer; an interaction that impairs the loss of the organic cation to from the structure. This effect also assisted in the protection of the perovskite from light-induced degradation processes. Therefore, incorporation of P(EO/EP) copolymer to the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  perovskite was found to be beneficial for the stability of the cells, even though some loss of efficiency was observed. The best trade-off was achieved for the device containing 1.5 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  of P(EO/EP).
