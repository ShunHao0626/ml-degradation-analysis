# Grain Boundary Passivation with Dion–Jacobson Phase Perovskites for High-Performance Pb–Sn Mixed Narrow-Bandgap Perovskite Solar Cells

Lei Zhang, Qiao Kang, Yanping Song, Dan Chi,* Shihua Huang,* and Gang He*

The mixed Pb–Sn perovskites have the ideal bandgap of   $  \approx1.2  $   eV for photovoltaic application. However, the undesirable p-doping introduced by   $  Sn^{2+}  $   oxidation restrains the device's power conversion efficiency (PCE) and stability. Herein, an additive strategy with p-phenyl dimethylammonium iodide (PhDMADI) is proposed, which has a bulky divalent organic cation and facilitates the formation of Dion–Jacobson phase-based quasi-2D perovskites at the grain boundaries. It is found that this unique 2D/3D bulk heterojunction structure is beneficial to suppress the oxidation of   $  Sn^{2+}  $   and isolate the moisture and oxygen, resulting in a good stability of the solar cell. Moreover, the quasi-2D perovskites can passivate defects effectively. The trap density of the perovskite film has decreased by one order of magnitude, thus the carrier lifetime is increased more than twice. These enhanced properties enable us to fabricate a device of 20.5% PCE with great stability.

Organic–inorganic hybrid metal halide materials recently have attracted extensive research interest, attributing to their unique physical and chemical properties such as long exciton diffusion length,  $  ^{[1]}  $   robust optical absorption,  $  ^{[2]}  $   tunable bandgap,  $  ^{[3]}  $   and high tolerance to defects.  $  ^{[4]}  $   Benefiting from the development of perovskite film fabrication technology  $  ^{[4]}  $   and device structure design,  $  ^{[5]}  $   the single-junction perovskite solar cells (PVSCs) have a rapid progress of power conversion efficiency (PCE),  $  ^{[6-12]}  $   achieving a record PCE of 25.5%. However, the light absorbers of pure lead-based perovskite with bandgap in the range of 1.5–1.6 eV seem imperfect, by considering the Shockley–Queisser limit.  $  ^{[13]}  $   An ideal bandgap should be 1.4 eV, to harvest all the visible and near-infrared (NIR) light. Grain boundaries assemble large amounts of defects and act a critical role in the perovskite films degradation processes.  $  ^{[23]}  $   Therefore, passivating grain boundaries of perovskite films is an effective way to improve the PCE and stability of device. Lee and co-workers used phenethylammonium to passivate grain boundaries, slowing down the degradation of unencapsulated devices in the relative humidity = 70% humidity circumstance.  $  ^{[24]}  $   Wang and co-workers reported that the grain boundaries passivation by gallic acid could effectively suppress   $  Sn^{2+}  $   oxidation, leading to highly stable and efficient   $  FASnI_{3}  $   PVSCs.  $  ^{[25]}  $

It has been proven that 2D hybrid halide perovskites include Ruddlesden–Popper (RP) phase perovskites and Dion–Jacobson (DJ) phase perovskites can improve the PCE and stability of Pb-based PVSCs because the hydrophobic organic spacer cations in 2D perovskite can separate perovskite films from moisture and oxygen.  $  ^{[26-34]}  $   Moreover, the oxidation of   $  Sn^{2+}  $   to   $  Sn^{4+}  $   is suppressed because of the higher formation energy of the 2D tin perovskites, resulting in reduced trap density in perovskite films.  $  ^{[21,35,36]}  $

Here, we introduce p-phenyl dimethylammonium iodide (PhDMADI) as the additive in Pb–Sn perovskite films. We found that a small additive amount of PhDMADI significantly improved the PCE and stability of PVSCs based on   $  FA_{0.7}MA_{0.3}Pb_{0.5}Sn_{0.5}I_{3}  $   films. This strategy 1) had no blue-shift of bandgap, avoiding   $  J_{SC}  $   decay; 2) improved film crystallinity and morphology; and 3) enabled one order of magnitude reduction in defect density. These improvements of narrow-bandgap light absorber are contributed to the 2D/3D bulk heterojunction structure form at the grain boundaries of perovskite films, passivate the grain boundaries, and suppress the oxidation of   $  Sn^{2+}  $   to   $  Sn^{4+}  $  . Finally, we have demonstrated that the PVSCs based on 2D/3D bulk heterojunction Pb–Sn perovskites deliver an upgraded PCE of 20.5% with much enhanced   $  V_{OC}  $   of 0.83 V and ultrahigh   $  J_{SC}  $   of 33.3 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  .

Compared with RP phase perovskites (A'2A $  _{n-1}  $ M $  _{n}  $ X $  _{3n+1}  $ , A' represents a monovalence organic cation, such as BA $  ^{+}  $ , PEA $  ^{+}  $ ), DJ phase perovskites (A'A $  _{n-1}  $ M $  _{n}  $ X $  _{3n+1}  $ , A' represents a divalent organic cation, such as 1,4-butanediamine, ethylenediamine cations) were considered as more promising photovoltaic (PV) material materials because diamine compounds can bridge the neighboring inorganic slabs without any van der Waals gap. $ ^{[37]} $  Meanwhile, the inorganic slabs' interlayer distance of DJ phase perovskites is short with the value of only 3.25 Å. $ ^{[35]} $  These characteristics of DJ phase perovskites lead to reduced quantum confinement which facilitates carrier transport and increases device stability. $ ^{[38]} $  Therefore, we selected p-phenyl dimethylammonium (PhDMA) as the organic spacer cation and added it into the FA $  _{0.7}  $ MA $  _{0.3}  $ Pb $  _{0.5}  $ Sn $  _{0.5}  $ I $  _{3}  $  precursor solution. PhDMADI was chosen by the following three main reasons. First, due to the delocalized  $  \pi  $ -electrons of benzene ring, the PhDMA shows a large dielectric constant that reduces the dielectric mismatch between organic cation and [PbI $  _{6}  $ ] $  ^{4-}  $  octahedron. $ ^{[38]} $  Second, on account of the hydrophobic property of PhDMA, the perovskite thin films are protected from moisture. $ ^{[39]} $  Third, the generated V $  _{I}  $  defects owing to the interaction of perovskite and fullerene can be passivated by the iodide ions of PhDMADI. $ ^{[40]} $

$  FA_{0.7}MA_{0.3}Pb_{0.5}Sn_{0.5}I_{3}  $   were used as the base composition for the narrow-bandgap perovskite.  $  ^{[19]}  $   Perovskite thin films

were prepared by spin-coating the dimethyl sulfoxide/N, N-dimethylformamide mixed solution of formamidinium iodide (FAI), methylammonium iodide (MAI),   $  PbI_{2}  $  ,   $  SnI_{2}  $  , and   $  SnF_{2}  $   with the antisolvent one-step method. Various amounts of PhDMADI (0–4 mol%) were added into the precursor solution as an improvement strategy. For demonstrating the good effect of additive on the device property, we prepared the mixed Pb–Sn narrow-bandgap PVSCs with a series of PhDMADI concentration. We found that 2 mol% of PhDMADI was the best recipe for optimizing the performance of solar cells (Figure S1 and S2, Supporting Information). As a consequence, the perovskite films without PhDMADI and with the best recipe of PhDMADI are explored and analyzed in the following.

Figure 1a,b shows the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of control and target films, respectively. It is shown that both the films deposited from perovskite precursor solutions with 0 mol% (control) and 2 mol% (target) contain micron-sized dense perovskite crystals with distinct grain boundaries; the difference in grain size is negligible. In comparison with the control film, a serious of bright crystals was observed at the grain boundaries of target perovskite film. As previously reported, the bright area in the SEM is relating to the electrical insulating phase which might be quasi-2D perovskites.  $  ^{[41]}  $   The quasi-2D phase form at grain boundaries can shield the perovskite crystals from moisture and retard the oxidation of the perovskite because the oxidation of   $  Sn^{2+}  $   starts at the naked surfaces and grain boundaries.  $  ^{[42]}  $   Furthermore, we applied the atomic microscope (AFM) to obtain surface roughness of perovskite thin films (Figure S3, Supporting Information). It is exhibited that target thin film owns a smoother surface with a calculated root mean square (RMS) roughness of   $  \approx52  $   nm as compared with   $  \approx56  $   nm for the control thin film, indicating a better contact with the carrier transport layers.  $  ^{[15]}  $   Unlike pure Pb perovskites, the Pb–Sn perovskites commonly tend to have larger roughness, owing to higher film thickness.  $  ^{[43]}  $

Figure 1c shows the UV–vis absorption spectra of control and target thin films, demonstrating the addition of 2 mol% PhDMADI does not change the band gap (  $  \approx  $  1.25 eV). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of both control and target thin films are shown in Figure 1d. It is obvious that both films have single peaks between 22° and 25°, which could be indexed to (113) planes in the pseudocubic P4mm space group.  $  ^{[14]}  $   Comparing the XRD patterns of control and target films, XRD diffraction peaks have no shift, but the target film exhibits stronger diffraction intensity, indicating enhanced crystallinity of target film. With the addition of PhDMADI increased from 1 to 3 mol%, the diffraction peaks become stronger (Figure S4, Supporting Information). However, there is no evidence to confirm that the 2D perovskites have formed with a little addition of PhDMADI from 1 to 4 mol%. This is perhaps attributable to the fact that 2D perovskite has formed in the perovskite film at low PhDMADI concentration, but too few to be detected. When the concentration of PhDMADI is increased to 20 mol%, two peaks are observed under 10° (Figure S5, Supporting Information), which correspond to the quasi-2D DJ phase perovskites.  $  ^{[44-46]}  $   Based on this result, we can infer that PhDMADI can cooperate with   $  FA_{0.7}MA_{0.3}Pb_{0.5}Sn_{0.5}I_{3}  $   to form DJ phase perovskites in terms of chemical thermodynamics; even the concentration of PhDMADI is very low.

The proton nuclear magnetic resonance (  $  ^{1}  $  H NMR) spectroscopy was conducted by dissolving the films in DMSO-d6 solution to verify the content of PhDMADI in the target film with 2 mol% additive. The solution prepared by dissolving the target film has signals at 4.15 and 7.80 ppm, which are corresponding to —CH—proton of FA and —CH  $  _{2}  $  —proton of PhDMADI, respectively (Figure S6, Supporting Information). The actual content of PhDMADI in the target film was calculated to be ≈1.75 mol%, which was in accord with the addition of PhDMADI. According to the additive amount of PhDMADI, quasi-2D perovskite (n=80) was predicted to form. However, there is no exact evidence to confirm the existence of such high-dimensional quasi-2D perovskite. It is certified previously that if we use quasi-2D perovskite precursor solution with large n value (n>8) to prepare films, 2D/3D bulk heterojunction structure will form.  $  ^{[36]}  $   This phenomenon was observed in n-butylamine iodide- and phenylethylammonium iodide-modified perovskite process.  $  ^{[49,50]}  $

On the basis of the aforementioned results, the 2D/3D bulk heterojunction structure of target film is demonstrated and the possible schematic diagram is shown in Figure 2. It is shown that the quasi-2D perovskites (n = 4) formed at grain boundaries are benefit to passivate defects, suppress Sn  $  ^{4+}  $  formation, and isolate the moisture and oxygen, resulting in the lessened carrier recombination, enhanced PCE, and stability of Pb–Sn PVSCs.

The fine Sn XPS spectra of control and target films are shown in Figure 3a,b, respectively. The plots of control and target films at the surface exhibit peaks at 487.4 and 487.2 eV, which indicates   $  Sn^{2+}  $   at the surface is almost oxidized.

The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements were conducted for perovskite films deposited on bare glass to confirm the effects of 2D/3D bulk heterojunction structure on passivating defects. Figure 4a shows the steady-state PL spectra of control and target perovskite films, which shows the target perovskite film has a stronger PL intensity than that of the control film, indicating the high excitonic quality of the target film.  $  ^{[51]}  $   PL-mapping measurements were performed to investigate the PL intensity distribution of perovskite film in a relative broad area. However, the control device drops to 75% after 300 h, and retains only 65% of initial efficiency after 700 h. It is obvious that the target device has much better stability than control device.

In this article, we have demonstrated that the addition of PhDMADI with bulky divalent organic cation in Pb–Sn perovskites is an effective strategy to improve the performance and stability of the resulting device. The formation of DJ phase-based quasi-2D perovskites at the grain boundaries plays an important role to passivate defects, suppress   $  Sn^{2+}  $   oxidation, and hamper the moisture and oxygen passing through the grain boundaries. # Experimental Section

Materials: Unless otherwise noted, all materials were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and without further purification. MAI, FAI, PhDMADI, PEDOT:PSS (AI 4083), BCP, and C60 were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp.

Perovskite Precursor Solution: 1.4 M   $  FA_{0.7}MA_{0.3}Pb_{0.5}Sn_{0.5}I_{3}  $   precursor was prepared by mixing FAI (168.5 mg), MAI (66.8 mg),   $  PbI_{2}  $   (322.7 mg),   $  SnI_{2}  $   (260.8 mg), and   $  SnF_{2}  $   (11.0 mg) in 750   $  \mu  $  L DMF and 250   $  \mu  $  L DMSO. Then PhDMADI with different molar ratios was added to the solution. The perovskite precursor solution was stirring at 60°C for 2 h and then filtered with 0.20   $  \mu  $  m polytetrafluoroethylene filter.

Device Fabrication: The prepatterned ITO substrates (15 × 15 mm $  ^{2}  $ ) were cleaned by ultrasonication in detergent, deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol for 15 min sequentially. A 20 nm-thick PEDOT:PSS film was obtained by spin-coating the PEDOT:PSS aqueous solution on ITO substrates at 4000 rpm for 30 s and then dried on a hotplate at 140 °C for 15 min. The FA $  _{0.7}  $ MA $  _{0.3}  $ Pb $  _{0.5}  $ Sn $  _{0.5}  $ I $  _{3}  $  perovskites without and with PhDMADI were spin-coated onto the PEDOT:PSS-coated ITO substrate

at 4000 rpm for 60 s, and chlorobenzene (CB) was dropped onto the substrate at 30 s. The perovskite wet film was annealed in 100 °C for 10 min. Then electrotransporting layer C60 (30 nm), interfacial layer BCP (5 nm), and Ag (100 nm) were deposited by thermal evaporation sequentially.

Characterization: The morphology and the cross-sectional structure of the prepared perovskite films were investigated by the field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; Zeiss Merlin). HRTEM images were obtained by JEOL JEM-2100F with an acceleration voltage of 300 kV.  $ ^{1} $ H NMR spectra were measured by Bruker AV400 Spectrometer. Steady-state PL was measured using Renishaw in Via-Reflex with excitation at 780 nm. TRPL spectra were measured by FLS980 (Edinburgh Instruments Ltd.) with a 785 nm pulsed laser. Absorption spectra were conducted with a Cary 5000 UV–vis–NIR spectrophotometer. The roughness of perovskite films was measured with Scanning probe microscope (BIO22T, NT-MDTCO.Russia). The XRD patterns were measured by the Rigaku MiniFlex/600 instrument with Cu Kα ( $ \lambda = 1.5406 $  Å) operating at 45 kV and 15 mA. The water contact angles were obtained by the DSA100 contact angle analyzer. The XPS spectra were measured by PHI-5000C ESCA system with an Ar ion gun. The current–voltage characteristics were recorded by the Keithley 2400 digital source meter under AM1.5 G (Newport Oriel Sol3A solar simulator) in N $ _{2} $  glove box. Light intensity was calibrated to 100 mW cm $ ^{-2} $  by a crystalline silicon reference solar cell. The J–V curves were measured both in reverse scan (1 V→0 V) and in forward scan (0 V→1 V) with a 200 mV s $ ^{-1} $  scan rate. The active area was defined as 0.0864 cm $ ^{2} $  by a metal mask.
