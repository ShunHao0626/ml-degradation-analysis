Abstract: Metal-halide perovskite solar cells have shown great success in achieving high efficiencies but less satisfaction in achieving long term stability. Perovskites are prone to form perovskite hydrates in humid environment, which would lead to decomposition of the perovskite materials. Herein, we introduce a common and cheap dye molecule, called cis-di(thiocyanato)bis(2,2-bipyridyl4,4-dicarboxylate)ruthenium(II), denoted as N719, into mixed-cation mixed-halide perovskites for better moisture stability. We discover that N719 molecules would form perovskite-dye complexes in the precursor solution, leading to larger grains and better film crystallinity by slowing down crystallization process. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction characterizations suggest that the N719 molecules exist in the crystallized perovskite films but are not incorporated into the perovskite crystal lattice. The presence of N719 molecules in perovskite films would greatly retard the formation of perovskite hydrates due to 3 times increased water migration barrier. Owing to these improvements, non-encapsulated N719-perovskite solar cells retain over  $ 80\% $  of their original efficiencies after aging under a high relative humidity of  $ 60\% $  for 250 hours, which is 3 times longer than pristine cells. Our work represents a cheap and effective route for controlling perovskite crystallization process and improving the stability of perovskite solar cells without sacrificing device efficiency.

# 1. Introduction

Metal halide perovskite solar cells have experienced an unprecedented rise in power conversion efficiencies (PCE) over the past decade from 3.9% to a certified value of 24.2% for small-area cells and 28% for a perovskite/Si tandem cell.  $  ^{[1-3]}  $   Towards real world applications, long term stability of perovskite solar cells is still a big hurdle to overcome. Perovskite materials are prone to degradation under light, oxygen, moisture, temperature and electric bias.  $  ^{[4-8]}  $   It has been found that enhanced stability can be obtained by stabilizing   $  PbI_{6}^{4-}  $   octahedra via a stronger interaction between A-site cations and X-site halides in   $  ABX_{3}  $   perovskites, and various replacements at A-site including partial substitution of   $  Cs^{+}  $ ,   $  Rb^{+}  $ ,   $  CH_{6}N_{3}^{+}  $  have been developed

Besides, interfacial layers, such as PCBM (that is, [6,6]-phenyl-  $  C_{61}  $  -butyric acid methyl ester),   $  TiO_{2}  $   (that is, titanium di-oxide) and other modified interlayers, are found to be important to improve moisture-resistance by preventing the incursion of water vapor.  $  ^{[5, 12, 13]}  $   Understanding moisture-induced instability and developing effective methods to enhance durability of perovskite films against humid environment would be crucial for advancing commercialization of perovskite solar cells.

Different mechanisms have been proposed to explain the extreme humidity sensitivity of perovskites.  $  ^{[8, 14, 15]}  $   It is generally accepted that the formation of monohydrate (e.g.   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}\cdot H_{2}O  $  ) and dihydrate [e.g.   $  (\mathrm{CH}_{3}\mathrm{NH}_{3})_{4}\mathrm{PbI}_{6}\cdot2\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O}]  $   intermediates both containing widely predicted   $  PbI_{6}^{4-}  $   octahedra are the two important steps during the degradation reactions. The monohydrate, as an intermediate product, is able to convert back to   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   while the dihydrate irreversibly degrades the perovskite structure under prolonged exposure to moisture.  $  ^{[8]}  $   With the continuous penetration of water molecules into bulk structure, perovskite films degrade gradually with the formation of different hydrate species at different reaction stages. Owing to the hygroscopicity of methylamino, the natural sensitivity to moisture of organic perovskites is challenging to be fundamentally overcome. In this regard, with clearly elucidated degradation mechanisms, studies on slowing the hydration process are severely required in order to improve the operation time in humid atmosphere.

Morphology and crystal quality of the films are two important factors that need to be carefully optimized for high efficiency and exceptional stability. Various functional additives, including polymers  $  ^{[16, 17]}  $  , halide ionic additives  $  ^{[18, 19]}  $  , metal ionic additives  $  ^{[20, 21]}  $  , and organic molecules  $  ^{[22]}  $  , have been adopted in perovskite precursor formulations to improve crystal growth of perovskite films by different means, such as alleviating surface tension, stimulating homogeneous nucleation and altering crystallization speed, etc.  $  ^{[23-25]}  $   These methods can be commercially viable alternatives to demanding processing optimization at the expense of simplicity. Plus, they barely provide special demands for the original one-step procedures. In spite of advantages of such a satisfying strategy, the study of mechanistic origins of improved film quality

Herein, we report an effective method for improving both moisture stability and crystal quality of perovskite films by introducing a dye molecule N719 (cis-diisothiocyanato-bis(2,2'-bipyridyl-4,4'-dicaboxylato) ruthenium(II) bis-(tetrabutylammonium)) (Figure S1). N719 is one of the most efficient and conventional sensitizers used in dye-sensitized solar cells. Although recent organic dye molecules have been developed to have higher molar extinction coefficients without using expensive metals, the state-of-art ruthenium-based N719 dye molecules still display fairly broad absorption spectra ( $ \Delta\lambda\approx350\mathrm{nm} $ ) and photoexcitation processes due to the swift electron transfer between electron-rich and electron-poor sections.[26, 27] In addition, considering its bandgap of  $ 1.8\mathrm{eV} $ , being well matched with perovskite,[28] N719 dye molecules can potentially be a promising additive for perovskite absorbers. By adding the N719 molecules into perovskite precursor solutions, we are able to tune the crystal growth of the perovskite films gaining better crystallinity, larger grain sizes. Via monitoring film compositions at different crystallization and phase transition stages, we identify that the addition of the dye molecules largely mitigates the perovskite hydration process. Density functional calculations indicate that the introduction of the dye molecules creates diffusion barriers of water molecules into the perovskite layer. By carefully tuning the amount of dye added into perovskite, we obtain a record efficiency of  $ 19.6\% $  and 3-times

We firstly performed X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra as we show the data in the Figure S3 to provide proof for the successful incorporation of N719 in the crystallized films. We clearly see the peak of Ru in the XPS spectra indicating the presence of N719 in the films. To further examine if the N719 would affect morphology and crystallinity of perovskite films, we performed scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements, as shown in Figure 1a-d. We observe that perovskite film shows larger grains and better surface coverage when incorporating  $ 1\mathrm{mg / ml} $  N719 compared with pristine perovskite film. We give the grain size distribution extracted from the SEM images in Figure S4. At  $ 2\mathrm{mg / ml} $ , we see the formation of pinholes which may cause shunting paths in the final devices. This can be ascribed to the serious steric effect of large-sized N719 molecules in the crystallization process due to the excessively high concentration.[31] In Figure 1d, we observe three XRD peaks at  $ 14.2^{\circ} $ ,  $ 28.5^{\circ} $ , and  $ 31.9^{\circ} $  in all the films which are attributed to typical perovskite diffractions.[21] With  $ 1\mathrm{mg / ml} $  N719, we see increased peak intensities for all the three peaks. This suggests that the addition of N719 would enhance the crystal growth of the perovskite films. This is also in line with the observed larger grain sizes in the SEM images. Further increasing the dye concentration to  $ 2\mathrm{mg / ml} $  causes overall decreased peak intensity suggesting a deteriorated crystal growth. To understand the improved crystal growth of perovskite films by adding the dye molecules, we performed dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) measurement, as shown in Figure 1e and f. As reported before, most perovskite precursors are actually colloidal dispersions.[32] Consequently, we determined colloidal particle radius of original precursor samples which follow a Gaussian distribution with a peak around  $ 1000\mathrm{nm} $ .[33] With N719 additions, the colloidal radius reveals a wider distribution and

This suggests that the dye molecules would lead to the formation of perovskite-dye complex and bigger colloidal particles by stronger coordinating effects in the precursor. FTIR spectra of resultant films provide further proof for such complex and insights in the interaction between perovskite and dye molecules. We observe peaks at  $ 1369~\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $  which can be assigned to the pure C-N stretching vibration of pristine perovskite film.[34] The deviation from normal wavenumber of  $ 1369~\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $  to  $ 1357~\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $  indicates weakened C-N bond in  $ \mathrm{MA^{+} / FA^{+}} $  as a consequence of the  $ \mathrm{MA^{+} \cdot N719} $  complex formation (Figure 1g), which has been further testified by the DFT calculations. It is noteworthy that the FTIR measured peak shift was smaller than the values estimated from the DFT calculations which can be explained by the very little amount of N719 incorporated. Subsequently, based on cationic charge analysis in Figure S5, a slight shift of peak position to a higher binding energy is observed in the hybrid perovskite-dye film according to the XPS spectra for Pb 4f electron. The increase in cationic charge of lead ions could be originated from the formation of perovskite/N719 complex by forming stronger Pb-X bonds with counteracted  $ \mathrm{MA^{+} / FA^{+}} $ . Accordingly, N719 molecules are able to form a perovskite-dye complex to tune the reaction process by hindering the intercalation process, the insertion of MAI/FAI into the interlayer  $ \mathrm{PbI_2} $  network, due to a chemical interaction between bare carboxyl groups and  $ \mathrm{MA^{+}} $ . The migration barrier of  $ \mathrm{MA^{+}} $  intercalation process thus got enhanced and the perovskite formation process is slowed down. Such a modulated reaction process contributes to a tune-up of the crystallinity of perovskite-dye hybrid films.[24, 35, 36]

We then take a macrocosmic and accelerated test on the degradation of perovskite with and without dye molecules to externalize the retarded hydration. Perovskite films are sensitive to various polar solvents possibly caused by the dipole moment on the crystal surface and many small-molecule polar solvents including water, methanol and formic acid can directly lead to the decomposition. $ ^{[40]} $  With a smaller surface tension, methanol was found to be easier to spread out on the perovskite film as compared to water. Besides, the invasion of H $ _2 $ O molecules into perovskite films is done by intercalating the pristine MAI/FAI structure and filling in the void of formed structure. With a similar volume, polarity and hydrogen bond with the water molecules, methanol is believed to be able to have a comparable interaction effect with perovskite. # 3. Conclusion

In conclusion, we report an effective method to prepare solar cells based on a

This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved

perovskite-dye hybrid film with improved moisture stability and relatively high efficiency. We find that the addition of dye molecules enables a better crystallinity, stronger light absorption and better moisture stability for the perovskite films. Solar cells with the optimized perovskite-dye photo absorber shows a champion PCE of 19.6%. Importantly, the perovskite-dye cells show much improved moisture stability which we identify to be due to the delayed formation of hydrates and a 3-times higher diffusion barrier for water penetration with the N719 molecule. Devices based on our perovskite-dye hybrid films show clearly improved moisture stability, remaining over 80% after exposition to a humid air with controlled RH of 60% for 250 hours without encapsulation. This exhibits a breakthrough of the stability improvement of PSCs without sacrificing the PCE and also provides insightful ideas of the internal mechanism. Our work reveals a new route for improving moisture stability of perovskite solar cells and potentially effective for other perovskite-based optoelectronic devices.

Materials: All chemicals and solvents unless specified were purchased from either Sigma-Aldrich or Alfa-Aesar, and used as received without further purification.  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  paste (30 NR), methylammonium iodide  $ (\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{I}) $  and N719 dye were purchased from Dyesol (Australia). 2,2',7,7'-Tetrakis-(N,N-di-4-methoxyphenylamino)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD) was received from Borun Chemicals (Ningbo, China).

Fabrication of Perovskite Solar Cells: Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)-coated glass substrate (sheet resistance   $  \approx7\ \Omega\ sq^{-1}  $  , Nippon, Tokyo, Japan) was patterned by etching with activated hydrogen which is generated from the mixture of zinc (powder) and dilute hydrochloric acid (2M). Then they were ultrasonically washed for 15 minutes with sequential solvents including detergent, ultra-pure water, acetone and

isopropanol. Before the  $ \mathrm{cp - TiO_2} $  deposition, they were treated with oxygen plasma for 10 minutes to further clean the surface of substrate. With an methanol solution of titanium isopropoxide, the  $ \mathrm{cp - TiO_2} $  film was spin-coated on the substrate at  $ 2000~\mathrm{rpm} $  and annealed at  $ 500^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for half an hour. Again, after oxygen plasma treatment for 10 minutes, mesoporous  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  layer was coated onto the  $ \mathrm{cp - TiO_2} $  layer by spin-coating an methanol solution of  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  paste (120 mg/ml) at 2000 rpm for 30s and subsequently dried at  $ 150^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for half an hour.

The perovskite solution containing 512 mg PbI $ _2 $ , 187 mg FAI, 16.2 mg MABr, 73.4 mg PbBr $ _2 $ , and 17.6mg CsI in 0.8 ml DMF and 0.2 ml DMSO was prepared in a N2-filled glove box and the following steps were also conducted in such a glove box except gold deposition. The precursor of perovskite is filtered before used. Then 80 μl perovskite solution was spin-coated at 2000 rpm for 10 s and 5000 rpm for 30 s. The substrates were washed with 100 μL of chlorobenzene at the end of 10 s and they were subsequently annealed at 120 °C for 30min. Then a Spiro-OMeTAD solution was spin-coated on the substrates at 4 000 rpm for 30 s, which was prepared by adding 72.3mg Spiro-OMeTAD, 28.8mL TBP and 17.5mL 520mg/mL LiTFSI (Bis[trifluoromethane]sulfonimide lithium salt) in acetonitrile in 1mL chlorobenzene. Finally, an 80-nm-thick gold electrode was deposited using thermal evaporation.

Characterization: In order to observe the morphology and cross-sectional SEM images, a field emission scanning electron microscope at 5kV(Zeiss LEO-1530, Germany) is used in this study. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded by a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer (40 kV, Cu Kα1). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra were obtained on an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) with Al Kα radiation (hv = 1486.6 eV) as the source. The Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy measurements were performed in diffuse reflectance mode with a BRUKER Vertex 70 spectrometer. Steady-state and time-resolved PL spectra were acquired by a FLS920 transient optical spectrometer. Steady-state PL spectra were obtained by using a monochromatic xenon lamp source (central wavelength  $ \lambda_{\mathrm{exc}} = 460 $  nm) to photoexcite

IPCE spectra were characterized by a QEX10 quantum efficiency system (calibrated by a standard crystalline silicon solar cell) in DC mode.

Computational Methods: First-principles calculation based on density functional theory (DFT) are performed through using Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) code.  $  ^{[41, 42]}  $   Projector augmented wave (PAW) was adopted to construct potentials to describe pseudopotential   $  {}^{[43, 44]}  $   while exchange-correlation function is described by generalized gradient approximation (GGA).  $  ^{[45]}  $   The criterion for force convergence was set to -0.01 eV/  $  \AA  $   to fully relax atomic positions. The kinetic energy cutoff for plane wave expansion is set to 450 eV and gamma cantered Monkhorst-Pack grids  $  ^{[46]}  $   for each slab models is   $  3\times3\times1  $  . The calculation of the density of states (DOS) was smeared by the Gaussian smearing method with a smearing width of 0.05 eV. # SUMMARY

In this work, we present a facile method to improve long-term stability of PSCs by using N719 dye molecules as additive. Perovskite-dye hybrid films show better crystallinity, enhanced light absorption and boosted moisture stability. Due to greatly retarded hydration process, degradation process of PDSCs is 3 times longer than pristine devices.
