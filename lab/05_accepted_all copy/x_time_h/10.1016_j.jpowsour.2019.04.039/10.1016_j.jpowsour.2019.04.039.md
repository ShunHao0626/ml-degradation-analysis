# 1. Introduction

Organometallic halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) as a new generation of solar cells have elicited intensive research and development due to their advantageous features such as minor environmental impact and high conversion efficiency   $  [1-4]  $  . The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSC has reached a certified value of 23.3% from 3.8% nine years ago   $  [5]  $  , which is attributed to the perovskite prominent characteristics such as a long charge carrier diffusion length   $  [6,7]  $  , high absorption coefficient   $  [8]  $   and facile solution processability   $  [9]  $  . In this regard, there are two kinds to widen the application of solar irradiation. One is up-conversion luminescence that lower-energy NIR photons are convert to higher-energy visible light photons; the other is down-conversion luminescence that higher-energy UV photons are convert to lower-energy visible light photons. Fortunately, rare earth (RE) ions with a specific 4f electronic structure have this function.

The integration of upconversion (UC) materials into PSCs as a spectral converter to harvest NIR solar photons and convert them to visible light photons that can thus be absorbed by the lead amine halide perovskite should be an optimal strategy   $  [29]  $  . Among numerous kinds of UC materials, lanthanide-doped beta-phase sodium yttrium fluoride (  $  \beta-NaYF_{4}  $  ) is well-known as the most efficient UC material because of its long luminescence lifetime and optical stability. Recently, several groups have reported the PSC performance improvement via upconversion luminescence by the rare-earth ion co-doped   $  NaYF_{4}  $   (  $  \beta-NaYF_{4}  $  )   $  [30–32]  $  . In addition, the upconversion nanoparticles (UCNPs) acting as scattering center can increase the light path   $  [31]  $  , and facilitate the formation of large perovskite grains with fewer defects   $  [33]  $  , which are all beneficial for the enhancement of photovoltaic performance. Despite these merits, the practical applications of UCNPs in photovoltaic device are still limited due to low UC intensity.

Based on the above considerations, we synthesized  $ \beta $ -NaYF $ _4 $ :Yb $ ^{3+} $ /Er $ ^{3+} $ /Sc $ ^{3+} $ @NaYF $ _4 $  upconversion nanoparticles (UCNPs) with core-shell (CS) structure, in which  $ \beta $ -NaYF $ _4 $  is host, rare-earth ions Yb $ ^{3+} $  and Er $ ^{3+} $  converts NIR light to red and green light, and Sc $ ^{3+} $  ion is doped for improving the UC luminescence intensity. The core-shell structure is designed for surface passivation. The total UC intensities for green and red emissions are enhanced by 10 and 16 times compared the sample without Sc $ ^{3+} $  doping. The optimal perovskite solar cell based on the mixed  $ \beta $ -NaYF $ _4 $ :Yb $ ^{3+} $ /Er $ ^{3+} $ /Sc $ ^{3+} $ @NaYF $ _4 $  mesoporous layer exhibited a high PCE of 20.19%, superior to those reported previously for UCNPs-incorporated PSCs.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1.Materials

Most of the chemicals were purchased from TCI or Sigma Aldrich and used as received, unless specifically mentioned. Methylammonium bromide (MABr) and formamidinium iodide (FAI) salts were obtained from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp, China. Spiro-OMeTAD was supplied by Luminescence Technology Corp, Taiwan, China.

# 2.2. Synthesis of  $ \beta $ -NaYF $ _4 $ :Yb $ ^{3+} $ /Er $ ^{3+} $ /Sc $ ^{3+} $  @NaYF $ _4 $  UCNPs

Oleate-capped and monodispersed  $ \beta $ -NaYF $ _{4} $ :Yb $ ^{3+} $ /Er $ ^{3+} $ /Sc $ ^{3+} $  nanoparticles were synthesized according to the literature [34]. Relevant solution of lanthanide complexes (1 M) were prepared by dissolving the particular lanthanide hydrochloride in deionized water with an ultrasonic cleaning bath. In a typical procedure, 5 mL of aqueous solution containing YCl $ _{3} $ , YbCl $ _{3} $ , ErCl $ _{3} $  and ScCl $ _{3} $  (lanthanide ion molar ratio, Y $ ^{3+} $ /Yb $ ^{3+} $ /Er $ ^{3+} $ /Sc $ ^{3+} $  = (78% - x)/20%/2%/x, x = 0%, 2%, 4%, 8% and 16%) mixed with 30 mL of oleic acid (OA, AR, Aladdin) and 75 mL of 1-octadecene (ODE, AR, Aladdin) were added into a 250 mL three-necked flask under stirring. The mixture was heated to 160 °C for 60 min under an Ar atmosphere to form a bright yellow transparent solution, and then cooled down gradually to 50 °C. Afterwards, 40 mL of methanol (purity ≥ 99.9%, Aladdin) solution containing 0.5 g of NaOH (purity 98%, Aladdin) and 0.74 g of NH $ _{4} $ F (purity 99.99%, Aladdin) was added drop by drop in the flask and keep the reaction at 50 °C for 30 min. After that, the solution was heated at 70 °C for 30 min to remove the methanol solvent. Then the system is heated up to 310 °C at a heating rate of 15 °C·min $ ^{-1} $  and kept the temperature for 60 min in the

flowing of the argon. Finally, the mixture was cooled down rapidly to room temperature and the product was collected by centrifugation and washed with ethanol/cyclohexane several times to remove possible remnants. Thus the core of  $ \beta $ -NaYF $ _4 $ :Yb $ ^{3+} $ /Er $ ^{3+} $ /Sc $ ^{3+} $  nanoparticles was synthesized.

For the synthesis of  $ \mathrm{NaYF_4} $  shell, the steps are the same as those for  $ \beta $ - $ \mathrm{NaYF_4:Yb^{3+}/Er^{3+}/Sc^{3+}} $  core, except replacing  $ 5\mathrm{mL} $  of the mixture of lanthanide hydrochlorides by  $ \mathrm{YCl}_3 $  aqueous solution alone and slowly dropping  $ 25\mathrm{mL} $  of cyclohexane solution of the obtained core particles  $ (0.1\mathrm{molL}^{-1}) $  into the reaction flask before  $ \mathrm{NaOH} $  and  $ \mathrm{NH_4F} $  methanol solution was added.

The  $ \beta $ -NaYF $ _4 $ :Yb $ ^{3+} $ /Er $ ^{3+} $ /Sc $ ^{3+} $ @NaYF $ _4 $  nanoparticles were obtained by acid treatment [35]. The resulting oleate-capped  $ \beta $ -NaYF $ _4 $ :Yb $ ^{3+} $ /Er $ ^{3+} $ /Sc $ ^{3+} $  and  $ \beta $ -NaYF $ _4 $  product (1 mmol) was dispersed in 40 mL of acidic ethanol solution (pH = 1) adjusted with concentrated hydrochloric acid and ultrasonicated for 1 h to remove the surface ligands. Afterwards the UCNPs were collected by centrifugation at 13000 rpm and washed with distilled water several times, and then re-dispersed in absolute ethanol.

# 2.3. Preparation of mesoporous layer

$  \beta  $ -NaYF $ _{4} $ :Yb $ ^{3+} $ /Er $ ^{3+} $ /Sc $ ^{3+} $ @NaYF $ _{4} $  UCNPs were mixed with the commercial TiO $ _{2} $  paste (Dyesol 30NRT, Dyesol) for preparing the mesoporous layer of PSCs. Briefly, a concentration of 1 mol L $ ^{-1} $  UCNPs ethanol solution was prepared by dispersing 1 mmol of  $  \beta  $ -NaYF $ _{4} $ :Yb $ ^{3+} $ /Er $ ^{3+} $ /Sc $ ^{3+} $ @NaYF $ _{4} $  UCNPs into 1 mL of absolute ethanol. Then the solution was mixed with the commercial TiO $ _{2} $  paste corresponding different mass-fractions respect to TiO $ _{2} $  varying from Y = 15%–100%. The mixtures were diluted in different amount of absolute ethanol to keep the concentration of 0.1 g mL $ ^{-1} $ , and then ultrasonicated and stirred overnight. For clarity, PSC devices based on different mass ratio of UCNPs/TiO $ _{2} $  mesoporous layer are denoted as the pristine cell and NYES-Y cells (where Y is the mass-fraction of UCNPs ranging from 15 to 100), respectively.

# 2.4. Device fabrication

The laser-patterned FTO (15 Ohm·sq  $  ^{-1}  $  , NPG, Japan) glass with size of   $  1.5 \times 1.5  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $   were consecutively cleaned by detergent and sonicated in isopropanol, acetone, distilled water and ethanol and finally treated with UV-ozone for 30 min. A compact TiO  $  _{2}  $   layer with thickness about 40 nm was prepared by spin-coating 0.15 M titanium diisopropoxide bis (acetylacetonate) (75 wt% in isopropanol, Aldrich) in a 1-butanol (99.8%, Aldrich) solution onto the substrate at 2500 rpm for 30 s, and then annealed at 500 °C for 30 min. A scaffold layer about 150–200 nm with different UCNPs/TiO  $  _{2}  $   weight ratios was prepared by spin coating mixed paste at 2000 rpm for 30 s and then annealed at 500 °C for 30 min.

Cesium-containing triple cation perovskite   $  \left(\mathrm{FA}_{0.83}\mathrm{MA}_{0.17}\right)_{0.95}\mathrm{Cs}_{0.05}\mathrm{Pb}\left(\mathrm{I}_{0.9}\mathrm{Br}_{0.1}\right)_{3}  $   was synthesized by using one-step spin-coating procedure [20,36,37]. Briefly, a perovskite precursor solution containing 171.9 mg of FAI, 507.1 mg of   $  PbI_{2}  $  , 21.32 mg of MABr and 76.67 mg of   $  PbBr_{2}  $   was prepared in a mixed solvent of DMF and DMSO totally 950   $  \mu  $  L with a volume ratio of 4:1. Then 50   $  \mu  $  L of CsI solution in DMSO (1.5 M) was added into the mixed perovskite precursor solution to achieve the desired triple cation composition. The precursor solution was spin-coated at 1000 rpm for 10 s and then at 6000 rpm for 20 s. During the second step, 130   $  \mu  $  L of chlorobenzene was poured on the spinning substrate 5 s prior to the end of the program to rinse out residual DMSO and DMF in the precursor films. The substrate was then annealed at 100 °C for 60 min on a hotplate to form crystalline triple cation perovskite layers.

Afterwards, the spiro-OMeTAD layers were subsequently deposited on top of the as-prepared triple cation perovskite layers by spin-coating 20   $  \mu  $  L of chlorobenzene solution contained 72.3 mg of spiro-OMeTAD,

# 2.5. Characterization

The crystal structures of samples were determined by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD, Smart Lab, Rigaku) using graphite monochromatic copper radiation ( $ \lambda = 1.5418\AA $ ). The morphologies characterizations were performed on the field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, SU8000, Hitachi) and a JEOL JEM-2100 transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The UC luminescence and was measured via the FLS 980E spectrometer (Edinburgh Instruments Ltd., UK), in which the excitation light source is a  $ 980~\mathrm{nm} $  laser. The transmittance, reflection and absorption spectra of the samples were recorded on a UV-Vis-NIR spectrophotometer (Lambda 950, PerkinElmer). The current density-voltage (J-V) characteristic curves of PSCs were recorded with a Keithley 2400 source-measure unit under  $ 100\mathrm{mWcm}^{-2} $  (AM 1.5 G) illumination provided by a solar light simulator (Newport Oriel Sol 3A class, USA, calibrated by a Newport reference cell) with pre sweep delay of  $ 0.04\mathrm{s} $ , max reverse bias of  $ 0.1\mathrm{V} $ , max forward bias of  $ 2.0\mathrm{V} $ , dwell time of  $ 30\mathrm{ms} $  in ambient environment. Average photovoltaic parameters of the each proposed PSC sample were obtained from 20 devices to check the reproducibility. The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra was acquired using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (Lumina, ThermoFisher) equipped with a Xenon lamp at an excitation wavelength of  $ 507\mathrm{nm} $ . The incident-photo-to-current conversion efficiency (IPCE) curves were measured as a function of wavelength from  $ 300\mathrm{nm} $  to  $ 1100\mathrm{nm} $  using the Newport incident photo-to-electron conversion efficiency (IPCE) system (Newport, USA). The NIR response of the PSC devices was tested using a  $ 980\mathrm{nm} $  laser with a power of  $ 200\mathrm{mW} $  (MDL-III-980L, CNI). The stabilized current density and power output were recorded close to the maximum power point, which was extracted from the J-V curves. The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were conducted on a Zennium electrochemical workstation (IM6) under AM 1.5G with the frequencies from  $ 100\mathrm{mHz} $  to  $ 1\mathrm{MHz} $ , the bias of  $ 0\mathrm{V} $  and the amplitude of  $ 20\mathrm{mV} $ . Long-term stability under persistent moisture was tested by XRD measurement for 7 days and record of time-dependent photovoltaic performances for  $ 400\mathrm{h} $  under ambient condition with  $ 40\% $  RH and  $ 25^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  without encapsulation. All the average values of sample were obtained from 8 devices.

# 3.1. Crystal structure

Fig. 1 (a) shows the X-ray diffraction pattern of  $ \beta $ -NaYF $ _4 $ :Yb $ ^{3+} $ /Er $ ^{3+} $ /Sc $ ^{3+} $  UCNPs doped with different amounts of Sc $ ^{3+} $  ions. Detailed comparing some selected regions of diffraction peaks, marked by dotted lines in Fig. 1 (a), were magnified as shown in Fig. 1 (b), which demonstrates the positions of the diffraction peaks shift slightly towards higher angles as the Sc $ ^{3+} $  molar ratio increases from 0 to 8% compared to the Sc $ ^{3+} $  free sample and then shift toward smaller angles as the Sc $ ^{3+} $  concentration increases to 16%. The relevant calculated cell parameters and unit cell volume for the Sc $ ^{3+} $  tridoped  $ \beta $ -NaYF $ _4 $ :Yb $ ^{3+} $ /Er $ ^{3+} $ /Sc $ ^{3+} $  are listed in Table S1. As the smaller ionic radius of Yb $ ^{3+} $  ( $ r = 0.0985 $  nm) and Er $ ^{3+} $  ( $ r = 0.1004 $  nm) than Y $ ^{3+} $  ( $ r = 0.1019 $  nm) for 8-fold coordination [38], the doping of Yb $ ^{3+} $  and Er $ ^{3+} $  ions will cause the NaYF $ _4 $  host lattice to shrink by substituting the Y $ ^{3+} $  site due to the same charge valence (+3). Simultaneously, in the presence of the low amount of Sc $ ^{3+} $  ( $ r = 0.087 $  nm), because of the similar physicochemical properties the same valence state of the rare-earth ions, it is logical that Sc $ ^{3+} $  can partially be substituted for Y $ ^{3+} $ , which leads to gradual lattice shrinkage. While the molar ratio of Sc $ ^{3+} $  increased up to 16%, Sc $ ^{3+} $  ion will act as an interstitial atom in NaYF $ _4 $  rather than a substitutional atom educed from smaller angles of XRD peaks migration shown in Fig. 1 (b) and corresponding calculated unit cell volume listed in Table S1.

# 3.2. Upconversion luminescence

It is reported that the decrease of cationic size or cell volume in the host can cause an increase in the crystal field strength and an enhancement of UC efficiency [39]. Based on the quantum selection rules, the intra-4f electronic-dipole transitions of   $  Ln^{3+}  $   ions strongly depend on their local crystal field and site symmetry. Many literature have illustrated theoretically that tailoring the local crystal field of host materials via non-luminescent ions can become a general route for increasing the UC radiation [40–42]. Fig. 2 (a) shows the UC emission spectra of   $  \beta-NaYF_{4}:20\%Yb^{3+}/2\%Er^{3+}/xSc^{3+}  $   (x = 0%, 2%, 4%, 8% and 16%). The dominant emission peaks are located in the range of 500–700 nm, consist of two emission bands in green region and one

Continuous improving of   $  Sc^{3+}  $   doping concentration will cause the lattice content to decrease (Fig. 1 (b)), change of   $  Er^{3+}  $   surrounding environment and local crystal field symmetry. Moreover, cross-relaxation of energy between   $  Er^{3+}  $   and   $  Sc^{3+}  $   ions also strengthen the population of each emission level (Fig. 2 (d)) [43]. While the   $  Sc^{3+}  $   ion doping concentration exceeds its optimal value,   $  Sc^{3+}  $   ion will act as an interstitial atom in   $  NaYF_{4}  $   instead of a substitutional atom, which will enlarge the distances between   $  Yb^{3+}  $   and   $  Er^{3+}  $   ions and make it difficult to obtain their energy transfer, superadd more lattice defects arising from an excess   $  Sc^{3+}  $   ion doping content, may also be harmful to UC emission due to increased luminescence quenching [44]. Hence, the β-  $  NaYF_{4}:20\%Yb^{3+}/2\%Er^{3+}/8\%Sc^{3+}  $   core was used to fabricate the core-shell nanostructure.

# 3.3. Morphology observation

TEM provides further insight into the morphology and microstructural details of  $ \beta $ - $ \mathrm{NaYF_4} $ : $ \mathrm{Yb}^{3+}/\mathrm{Er}^{3+}/\mathrm{Sc}^{3+} $  nanostructures. As can be seen in Fig. 3 (a)  $ \sim $  (e), almost no difference in the size and morphology can be observed from the nanoparticles doping with different  $ \mathrm{Sc}^{3+} $  concentrations, which are all spherical in shape and very uniform modified by oleic acid with the average grain sizes about  $ 20.18 \pm 1.39 \mathrm{~nm} $ ,  $ 20.40 \pm 1.19 \mathrm{~nm} $ ,  $ 20.08 \pm 1.48 \mathrm{~nm} $ ,  $ 20.33 \pm 1.29 \mathrm{~nm} $ , and  $ 20.03 \pm 1.27 \mathrm{~nm} $ , respectively. Fig. 3 (g) shows the hexagonal  $ \beta $ - $ \mathrm{NaYF_4} $  phase {101(—)0} lattice planes of  $ \beta $ - $ \mathrm{NaYF_4} $ : $ 20\% $   $ \mathrm{Yb}^{3+}/2\% \mathrm{Er}^{3+}/8\% \mathrm{Sc}^{3+} $  with the interplanar spacing of  $ 0.52 \mathrm{~nm} $ , indicating that the  $ \mathrm{Sc}^{3+} $  doping has no influence on the crystalline phase of the nanocrystals, which is in good agreement with the XRD patterns (Fig. 1 (a)). Compared with the cubic  $ \alpha $ - $ \mathrm{NaYF_4} $  phase, hexagonal  $ \beta $ - $ \mathrm{NaYF_4} $  is a more efficient host lattice of various lanthanide ions for UC luminescence due to its high UC quantum yield [48]. Meanwhile, the final particle size of the core-shell is favorable to mixed with  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  nanoparticles randomly thus forming a homogeneous paste for mesoporous layer.

# 3.4. Optical property

To assess the optical properties of the configurations as designed, the light response of mesoporous layers corresponding to NYES-Y (Y = 0, 15, 30, 60, 100) are conducted. As seen in Fig. 5 (a) and (b), the reflectance value increases monotonously with the increase of the amount of CS UCNPs, preliminarily demonstrating that the CS UCNPs can act as effective scattering centers in the UV–Vis region. The sharp absorption edges starting at 400–500 nm are ascribed to the band gap absorption of anatase TiO  $  _{2}  $  (  $  \sim  $  3.2 eV) [49,50]. The light absorption around 980 nm gradually becomes apparent with the addition amount of CS UCNPs going up, corresponding to the absorption of Yb  $  ^{3+}  $  ion. Notably, accompanied by the light scattering function, the addition of CS UCNPs leads to a slight increase in the light absorption from 500 to 700 nm, which unequivocally signifies that the CS UCNPs can not only capture more photons from NIR region, but also extend the light path length to promote photon harvesting. Besides, the CS UCNPs have strong emission at 541 nm, close to the excitation wavelength of the perovskite film (507 nm) and overlapped with the UV–Vis absorption spectra of the perovskite film over a broad wavelength range (Fig. 2(c)), which directly embodies an efficient utilization of light. The CS UCNPs are incorporated into the mesoporous TiO  $  _{2}  $  layer for constructing the improved upconversion mesoporous scaffold, and the schematic configuration of the fabricated PSC is shown in Fig. 2 (e). When incident light is projected to the PSCs, the visible photons can be absorbed by perovskite sensitizer and converted into a photocurrent directly. Then the NIR photons are effectively converted into visible photons by CS UCNPs to generate additional photocurrent.

# 3.5. Photovoltaic performance

To determine the optimum mixing ratio, the best photocurrent density-voltage curves (J-V) of the as-fabricated devices with different weight ratios of CS UCNPs/TiO  $  _{2}  $  in the mesoporous layer measured under one sun illumination at AM 1.5 G are plotted in Fig. 6 (a), and relative photovoltaic parameters are summarized in Table 1. Compared to the control device, the optimal mass-fraction is 30% (NYES-30) and both higher and lower mixing amount would result in lower short circuit current density (  $  J_{SC}  $  ) and power conversion efficiency (PCE), while with a further increase of the mass-fraction of CS UCNPs to 100% (NYES-100), the device performance decreases, which is even inferior to the pristine cell. NYES-30 device demonstrates the  $  J_{SC}  $  and PCE values of 22.91 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  and 20.19%, respectively, which indicates a 7.06% enhancement in  $  J_{SC}  $  (22.91 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  vs 21.4 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $ ) and a 15.77% enhancement in PCE (20.19% vs 17.44%) compared with the pristine device, while  $  V_{OC}  $  remains unchanged. The enhancement can be associated with the UC luminescence properties of CS UCNPs and enhanced scattering effect.

To ensure accuracy and reproducibility, the average photovoltaic parameters of each sample were further investigated by statistically analyzing 20 of the fabricated devices. The box-plots of the results are demonstrated in Fig. 6 (b) and (c), and summarized in Table S2. Apparently, the NYES-30 devices exhibit the highest average value of PCE  $ (19.15 \pm 0.55\%) $ , which is about  $ 11.01\% $  higher than that of the pristine devices  $ (17.25 \pm 0.12\%) $ , and other three related average photovoltaic parameters are also the highest. All the statistical data of photovoltaic parameters have an identical trend of rise first then fall, distinctly demonstrating an outstanding reproducibility of the NYES-30 devices with champion performance. The above decreasing tendency is probably due to following three factors: (1) although the light scattering effect contributes to light harvesting, while excessive back-scattering will lead to large part of incident light reflected out of the cell and absorption can be weakened after all [51], which can be observed from the wide distribution of  $ \mathrm{J}_{\mathrm{SC}} $  for NYES-60 devices; (2) excess addition of CS UCNPs will impede the electron extraction from the perovskite film and the conductivity of the whole electron transport layer decreases owing to the poor conductivity property of CS UCNPs, resulting in a decrease of FF observed in NYES-60 and NYES-100 devices; (3) the CS UCNPs aggregation in the mesoporous layer induced by sintering process may also exert a poor contact with perovskite layer and thus the  $ \mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{OC}} $  decreases.

Charge carrier transportation in PSC devices were investigated by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurement. Fig. 7 (c) demonstrates the Nyquist plots of the PSCs for NYES-15, NYES-30, NYES-60, NYES-100 and pristine cell. Typically, the semicircle in the high-frequency range is attributed to the charge-transfer resistance  $ (\mathrm{R_{ct}}) $  between the hole transport layer/Au electrode. Another semicircle at low-frequency range is related to the charge recombination resistance  $ (\mathrm{R_{rec}}) $  at the mesoporous/perovskite layer interface.  $ \mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{s}} $  is regarded as the series resistance of the electrodes and the external circuit. All devices are found to possess comparable  $ \mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{s}} $  and  $ \mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{ct}} $ . Moreover, the change tendency of  $ \mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{rec}} $  is in good coincidence with the J-V measurements (as shown in Fig. 6 (a)). The NYES-30 device shows the maximum  $ \mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{rec}} $  as compared to the pristine cell (Table 1), indicating a suppressed charge recombination and an enhanced charge migration for the NYES-30 perovskite film.

The electron extraction mechanism and electron-hole recombination properties in the devices were further investigated by performing the steady-state photoluminescence (PL) measurement. As shown in Fig. 7 (d), when coated on  $ \mathrm{SiO}_2 $  substrate, the PL peak intensity of the perovskite film with the NYES-30 based film is higher than that of the pristine film, suggesting a decrease in the non-radiative recombination and the defect trap states [52,53], greatly related to smooth surface and the reduced grain boundaries, which is cross-checked by the SEM images shown in Fig. 4 (g)  $ \sim $  (i). Notably, when deposited on the FTO substrate to quench the electron emission, even more significantly decrease of the PL intensity than that of the pristine film can be observed,

implying efficient electron extraction and decreased carried recombination for the NYES-30 configuration, which indirectly suggests the NYES-30 configuration can exhibit the improved   $  J_{SC}  $   and PCE.

Long-term stability is an important parameter for the application of PSCs. To shed light on the humidity tolerance of CS UCNPs fabricated PSCs, the degradation experiment of NYES-30 and pristine perovskite films were monitored by XRD measurement (Fig. 8 (a)) for 7 days under ambient condition with 40% RH and 25 °C without encapsulation and their time-dependent photovoltaic performances (e.g. PCE, Fig. 8 (b)) were tested in the same situation for 400 h. According to the previous studies, moisture sensitive and UV-induced catalytic reaction of   $  TiO_{2}  $   both lead to the decomposition of perovskite film [54,55]. After exposure of the pristine perovskite to ambient air for one week, a new sharp diffraction peak at 12.7° corresponding to the   $  PbI_{2}  $   (001) lattice plane is observed in the XRD pattern, ascribed to the decomposition of the aged perovskite film. The   $  PbI_{2}  $   would block the charge transport, resulting in a decrease of PCE. In comparison, the intensity of   $  PbI_{2}  $   diffraction peak for the NYES-30 film aged in the same situation is weaken due to the smooth surface and UV light stability brought by the integration of CS UCNPs, implying a much lower degree of decomposition. As shown in Fig. 8 (b), after exposing in ambient air for 40 h, the normalized PCE reached the highest value due to the oxidation of spiro-OMeTAD [56], then a dramatic degradation to 56% in the first 160 h followed by a slowed degradation to 48% of the highest PCE for pristine devices, while NYES-30 devices decreased slowly at nearly 72% of the highest PCE, exhibiting significantly improved stability under the same testing conditions. In detail, narrower PCE distribution for NYES-30 devices performed at each data point than that of pristine devices, which ensure a high reliability (Table S4).

# 4. Conclusion

In conclusion,  $ \beta $ -NaYF $ _{4} $ :Yb $ ^{3+} $ /Er $ ^{3+} $ /Sc $ ^{3+} $ @NaYF $ _{4} $  CS UCNPs were successfully prepared and incorporated into TiO $ _{2} $  mesoporous layer as an energy conversion and light scattering bifunctional layer. The UC emission intensities of 541 nm and 654 nm of the UCNPs tri-doped with 8 mol% Sc $ ^{3+} $  ions were increased by about 10 and 16 times. The radiationless deactivation at the particle surface was eliminated to a great extent due to core-shell structure. By optimizing the amount of CS UCNPs in the TiO $ _{2} $  mesoporous layer, the NYES-30 device exhibited the high PCE of 20.19%, an overall enhancement of 15.77% compared with that of the pristine device. UV–Vis, IPCE, and PCE results all verify the feasibility of the efficient NIR utilization via the UC effect of the CS UCNPs, resulting in enhanced J $ _{SC} $  and PCE. Our work provides a viable strategy to render the minimization of non-absorption loss of solar photons with improved energy alignment for PSCs or other types of solar cells. The present results demonstrate a low-cost, efficient and novel strategy for enhancing the performance of PSCs.
