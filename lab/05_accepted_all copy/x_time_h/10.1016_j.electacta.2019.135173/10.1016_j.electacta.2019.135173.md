# ABSTRACT

Methylammonium bismuth iodide  $ \left(\mathrm{(CH_3NH_3)_3Bi_2I_9}\right. $ , MBI) is a promising alternative to perovskite solar cells (PSCs) due to its air stability and low-toxicity. Herein, a two-stage electric-field-assisted reactive deposition method has been introduced to prepare MBI films for the first time. PSCs based on MBI films were fabricated and characterized. The influences of electric parameters (applied voltage mode, pulse and time, etc.) on the morphology and crystal structure of MBI film correlated with the photovoltaic performance of PSCs have also been investigated systematically in detail. Through adjusting the voltage mode, crystal growth can be completely controlled, leading to a compact MBI film with large grain size. Moreover, the MBI film prepared by electrochemical deposition required no annealing. This work determined that the optimal performance of PSCs is based on the MBI film deposited by square-wave (AC) voltage mode (-7.3 V/+1 V, 5 Hz, 5 min). All devices exhibited long-term stability in ambient air (humidity of  $ >50\% $ ) for more than 300 h. # 1. Introduction

Recently, organic–inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted considerable attention owing to their excellent and unique photoelectric properties   $  [1-3]  $  . Efficiencies of nearly 25% have been achieved based on organic–inorganic halide perovskite   $  [4-6]  $  . However, systematic research on the heavy metal element lead   $  \left(\mathrm{Pb}^{2+}\right)  $   and its decomposability in humid environment have aroused vigilance   $  [7-9]  $  . Therefore, studies on stable organic–inorganic hybrid alternatives with low toxicity are important for commercialization of PSCs   $  [10-13]  $  . Reports on various lead-free perovskites with initial efficiencies of up to 9.6%, such as tin-based halide perovskite, have emerged   $  [14-16]  $  . However, the oxidation of   $  Sn^{2+}  $   to   $  Sn^{4+}  $   in the air presents a challenge that needs to be resolved by control test conditions. Replacing Sn with other stable and low-toxic elements is probably an optimum choice   $  [17]  $  . Bismuth (Bi)-based hybrids, which exhibit structural diversity, low toxicity, strong light absorption, similar   $  6s^{2}  $   electron orbital structure with Pb   $  [17-22]  $  , and long charge–carrier lifetimes   $  [23]  $  , have been reported as substitutes in settling the issues of toxicity associated with lead-based perovskites and instability of tin-based ones, although, Bi-based PSCs feature low power conversion efficiency (PCE).

At present, perovskite films of PSCs mainly prepared by one-step method   $  [24]  $  , gaseous-phase reactive deposition   $  [25]  $  , and two-step spin-coating method and so on   $  [26]  $  , promoting the development of highly efficient and stable perovskite solar devices   $  [27-36]  $  . These methods led to the significant development of PSCs, but they feature high requirements for experimental environment and conditions and are likely affected by the environment. According to pioneering works, such methods need annealing at certain temperatures for a certain period or additional processing steps, making the process complicated and unsatisfyingly repeatable. Given the existence of ionic   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}^{+}  $   (  $  MA^{+}  $  ),   $  I^{-}  $  , and   $  Pb^{2+}  $  , a new strategy that introduces an external electrical field has been considered

Electrochemical deposition is a suitable technique for film deposition, due to its simple operation, fast deposition rate, and ambient temperature operation   $  [37-41]  $  . To date, the application of electrochemical deposition in the fabrication of perovskite films is rarely reported. Related studies reported lead oxides prepared by electrochemical method before the formation of methylamine compounds, which are converted into   $  PbI_{2}  $   and then reacted with methylammonium iodide (MAI) solution to form   $  MAPbI_{3}  $  , and PbO prepared by electrochemical method followed by reacting with MAI to form   $  MAPbI_{3}  $     $  [42]  $  . Recently, Hong Liu and co-authors reported the high efficiency (up to 15.65%) of electrochemically deposited   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   system   $  [25]  $  . Furthermore, a novel study has indicated that the applied electric field can accelerate the crystallization process, which implies the feasibility of introduction of an applied electric field   $  [43,44]  $  .

In this study, we report a strategy for fabricating methylammonium bismuth iodide   $  ((CH_{3}NH_{3})_{3}Bi_{2}I_{9}, MA_{3}Bi_{2}I_{9}, MBI)  $   films using a two-stage electric-field-assisted reactive deposition method, which is a fast and controllable process and requires no annealing. Directed by theory and detailed investigation, the mechanism of MBI films prepared by electrochemical deposition is systematically described in detail. For the first time, PSCs were fabricated based on MBI films by electrochemical deposition at different voltage modes. The influences of electric parameters (applied voltage mode, pulse and time, etc.) on the morphology, crystal structure of MBI films, and device performance were also explored.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1 Materials and Sample Preparation

Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass substrates (a sheet resistance of   $  14 \Omega cm^{-1}  $  , Jinge) used for solar cell fabrication were sequentially cleaned using detergent, deionized water, ethanol, isopropanol, and ethanol again by ultra-sonication for 30 min. Before use, the FTO substrates were UV-cleaned for 15 min. The compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer was formed by magnetron sputtering (SKY Technology Development Co., Ltd) at 100 W for 40 min, followed by annealing at 500 °C for 30 min in a muffle furnace under air atmosphere.

Preparation of bismuth(III) iodide (Bil $ _3 $ ) solution: Bil $ _3 $  (1.3563 g, 99.999%, Sigma-Aldrich), sodium iodide (14 g, AR, 99.5%, Aladdin), and ethylene glycol mono-tert-butyl ether (19.0254 g, 99.0%, TCL) were added into 230 ml isopropanol (super dry, with molecular sieves, J&K, Beijing) and stirred at room temperature for 1 h.

# 2.2 Two-stage electrochemical deposition of   $  (CH_{3}NH_{3})_{3}Bi_{2}I_{9}  $   film

The electrochemical deposition of MBI film included two steps. The first step was performed to fabricate Bi film onto the FTO/TiO $ _2 $  substrate used as a working electrode, which was controlled by an electrochemical workstation (Corrtest, CS350H) together with a Pt counter electrode. The second step of MBI film formation was acted in the isopropanol solution of MAI (10 mg/mL) using FTO/TiO $ _2 $ /Bi as the working electrode also with a Pt counter electrode (the video of the deposition processes can be found in Supplementary data). The obtained MBI films were washed with isopropanol and dried at 35 °C in the air environment.

# 2.3 Characterization

The absorbance spectrum was recorded using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (UV-3600, Shimadzu). The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of electrochemically deposited samples were collected using a Bruker-AXS D8 Advance. The component elements and binding energy of samples were analyzed via X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, ESCALAB 250Xi). The surface topography of MBI films was analyzed by field emission-scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, Jeol JEM 6510LV). The roughness of Bi films was determined by an atomic force microscope (AFM, Nanoscope-IIa). The photocurrent–voltage (J-V) characteristics of solar cells were evaluated by a simulated AM 1.5G radiation (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  irradiance) using a solar simulator (Oriel, model 91192-1000) and a source meter (Keithley 2400, USA). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed on an electrochemical workstation (Zennium, IM6, Germany) over the frequency range of 100 mHz to 2 MHz with 10 mV alternating current (AC) amplitude at -0.9 V bias under simulated AM 1.5G radiation (100 mW•cm  $  ^{-2}  $  irradiance). # 3. Results and Discussion

# 3.1 General Concept

Typically, the electrochemical deposition of MBI films includes two reaction processes. Step 1 Bi deposition:  $ \mathrm{Bi}^{3+} $  gains electrons to form bismuth atoms and deposited on the FTO/TiO $ _2 $  substrate surface (reactions 1). Step 2 perovskite formation: bismuth atoms are re-oxidized to form  $ \mathrm{Bi}^{3+} $ , which react with  $ \mathrm{MA}^+ $  and  $ \mathrm{I}^- $  to form MBI perovskite (reactions 2). Accordingly, the whole experiment process is divided into two steps for investigation (as shown in Fig. 1a), and easily controlled by changing the voltage mode, amplitude, time and so on. The remarkable color change of Bi (black) to MBI film (orange) benefitted the investigation of the deposition process. Moreover, compared with the unstable and easily decomposed  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ , MBI is more stable in the environment. Thus, the external environment showed minimal influence on the electrochemical deposition of MBI thin films. Fig. 1b and c show the waveforms of applied signal during MBI perovskite fabrication. At the beginning of Step 1 of Bi deposition, a highly negative voltage pulse with an extremely short duration (1 s) was used to avoid the strong non-uniformity of products under strongly enhanced distortion of surface field at high bias amplitude for a long period [25]. Then the process was continued at a smaller constant potential lasting for a certain time. For Step 2 of perovskite formation, two different voltage modes have been introduced and compared. The pulse–constant voltage mode is similar to that of Bi deposition, which began with a large negative voltage of 1 s, followed by a constant voltage lasting for few minutes (Fig. 1b). In the square-wave (AC) voltage mode, a square-wave pulse voltage ( $ f = 5\mathrm{Hz} $ ) was introduced with different amplitudes of first and second half-wave, while growth period was kept constant at 5 min (Fig. 1c).

# 3.2 Analysis of Bi Deposition Process

Although reference information can be found about the electrochemical deposition of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  [25], a steady-state polarization test was initially carried out to determine the optimal deposition voltage of Bi atom on  $ \mathrm{FTO / TiO_2} $  substrate for the MBI system. The steady-state polarization curve (Fig. 2a) and actual images of Bi films (Fig. 2c) deposited at different voltages reveal that at a constant voltage of  $ -3\mathrm{V} $ , a high-quality Bi film can be obtained and can fully cover the  $ \mathrm{FTO / TiO_2} $  substrate. The crystalline phase of Bi metal films was further confirmed by XRD investigations (shown in Fig. 2b). The XRD pattern revealed that all reflection peaks can be readily indexed as rhombohedral phase Bi. These peaks and their positions present good agreement with the known XRD data for bulk bismuth (PDF# 44-1246). XRD results also indicate that highly crystallized elemental Bi films were successfully prepared by electrochemical deposition.

The obtained Bi film in Step 1 is an important basis for the MBI perovskite growth in Step 2, as the initial core density of the perovskite grain in Step 2 is proportional to the density of Bi atom. To a certain extent, high-density Bi atoms easily form a dense and compact perovskite film and can benefit the coverage of perovskite on substrates. Considering volume expansion during the transformation of Bi atoms into perovskite, the density and size of Bi atoms in Step 1 must be controlled. In the electrochemical deposition system, the nucleation rate is higher than the growth rate of the crystal, resulting in small-size grains. Thus, a large negative bias voltage is needed to control the growth of high density nucleus. Hence, a high voltage pulse varying amplitudes ranging from -5 to -30 V was applied at the beginning of Step 1, followed by a constant bias at -3 V. The morphologies of Bi films obtained under different high voltage pulses were characterized by FESEM (Fig. 3) and AFM (Fig. S1). As shown in Fig. 3, more Bi grains were gradually deposited on the FTO/TiO  $  _{2}  $  substrate when voltage pulse changed from -5 to -30 V. Evidently, the surface and section morphology showed that several extremely large Bi grains protruded out of the film surface in the samples obtained at the voltage pulse of -5, -10 and -15V (Fig. 3a-c). When voltage pulses of -20 and -25 V were applied, the Bi films became compact with more even-sized Bi grains (Fig. 3d-e). However, when voltage pulse

# 3.3 Analysis of   $  (CH_{3}NH_{3})_{3}Bi_{2}I_{9}  $   Formation Process

For   $  (\mathrm{CH}_{3}\mathrm{NH}_{3})_{3}\mathrm{Bi}_{2}\mathrm{I}_{9}  $   film deposition, the pulse–constant voltage mode was first introduced beginning with a large negative voltage pulse for 1 s, followed by 5 min of constant voltage. To determine the optimal voltage pulse of Bi deposition in Step 1 for MBI deposition in Step 2, the Bi films obtained at different voltage pulses in Step 1 were used for Step 2 of MBI preparation. Fig. 4 displays the morphology of MBI films. The MBI film obtained in Step 2 using the Bi film deposited at a voltage pulse of -25 V exhibited uniform and small grain size with dens structure (Fig. 4e), whereas the MBI film in Fig. 4a-d and f exhibited heterogeneous grain size and contained uncovered   $  TiO_{2}  $   compact layers (especially in Fig. 4a). Therefore, we have selected a voltage pulse of -25 V (1 s) for the Bi deposition in Step 1 as the optimized condition for perovskite and PSC fabrication. # 3.4. Mechanism Study and Performance

During the electrodeposition processes, a constant voltage could persistently transform Bi atom into   $  Bi^{3+}  $   and diffuse it in IPA solution. The presence of constant voltage can further generate a constant electric field to promote shifting of   $  Bi^{3+}  $   and   $  MA^{+}  $  toward the cathode and movement of   $  I^{-}  $  toward toward the anode. With the extension of time,   $  MA^{+}  $  concentration decreased around the anode, whereas   $  I^{-}  $  exhibited a high density. Consequently, the process of perovskite crystal growth was restrained due to   $  MA^{+}  $  deficiency in the anode. As shown in Fig. 6a c, a tremendous difference in MBI grain size can be evidently observed in the pulse–constant voltage mode. This difference is attributed to the concentration of   $  MA^{+}  $ , which decreased to a certain degree. Therefore, it is important to dominate the difference in concentrations of cations and anions caused by ion shifts between the cathode and anode. Compared with the pulse–constant voltage mode, the square-wave voltage electrodeposition method obtained a relatively uniform distribution of MBI grain size (Fig. 6b and d). The key to realizing all these results is to explore the mechanism of square-wave voltage electrodeposition. A large negative voltage, which can oxidize Bi to   $  Bi^{3+}  $ , was applied on the anode. Meanwhile,   $  I^{-}  $  moved toward the anode, and   $  MA^{+}  $  shifted toward the cathode. The shifting of   $  I^{-}  $  and   $  MA^{+}  $  between the anode and cathode was attributed to the electrode rejection generated by the applied electric field. However, an increase in negative voltage to reduce rejection was not permitted because the formation of   $  Bi^{3+}  $   is related to the negative voltage. To compensate for the repulsion, a positive voltage was introduced into the deposition system to fight the large negative voltage. The positive voltage alternated with negative voltage in a short moment (100 ms) acting on the electrode

To analyze perovskite formation, EDS surface scanning technique was used to characterize the perovskite deposition at two different voltage modes. Fig. 8a-c show that, a tabular layer of MBI film was formed on the surface of  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  after 1 s. Comparatively, the MBI films fabricated by square-wave voltage electrochemical deposition method cannot form in an extremely short period, suggesting that perovskite grains formed by this voltage mode followed a long growth cycle (seen in Fig. 8d-f). Comparing Fig. 8c with 8f, large perovskite grains can form under square-wave voltage mode, which would be beneficial in obtaining high-quality PSCs and further verified the above morphological analysis. Current density (J) and time (t) variation (the  $ J $ -t curves shown in the Fig. 8g) of the actual deposition area was simulated to study MBI formation. Results show that  $ J $  in the actual deposition area changed with time in the form of square-wave oscillation following voltage oscillation (-7.3 V/+1 V, 5 Hz). In the beginning,  $ J $  showed a minimal increase in the first several seconds (about 20 s), which is related to Bi atom oxidization and ionic migration. Bi atom oxidation and the arrival of ionic  $ \mathrm{MA^{+}} $  and  $ \mathrm{I^{-}} $  to Bi surface could absorb energy from the electric field, indicating the reason of increased  $ J $  at the earlier stages of deposition. With prolonged time (begin with 30 s), an almost constant  $ J - t $  curve was observed (Fig. 8g), indicating that a balanced system of atom oxidization and ionic migration formed in the electrochemical deposition system. Combined with the simple simulation of perovskite grain growth (shown in the Fig. 8h), the growth of MBI crystals was controllable under square-wave voltage mode, leading to high-quality MBI films with larger grain size and lower defect density. These properties were beneficial in exploring the experimental principle and fabricated devices.

# 4. Conclusion

In summary, MBI films have been successfully prepared by a novel electrochemical deposition method without annealing for solar cell application. FESEM and XRD analyses demonstrated that the morphology and structure, respectively, of MBI films were significantly affected by electric parameters.  $ J - V $  characterization associated with EIS and UV-Vis results suggests that PCSs based on MBI film deposited by square-wave (AC) voltage mode (-7.3 V/+1 V, 5 Hz, 5 min) displayed a corresponding better performance. In addition, the PCE of all devices remained nearly unchanged in ambient air (humidity of  $ >50\% $ ) for more than 300 h. Different from the electrochemical deposition of other type of materials, perovskite formation under electric field involves multiple components and fast speed. Thus deeper understanding by advanced in-situ monitoring techniques, systematic physical/chemical theoretical studies, and modulation are still needed for this complex and fast process; expectedly, the electrochemical deposition method can be widely applied for

# Highlights

(1) A two-stage electric-field-assisted reactive deposition method has been introduced to prepare  $ (\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3)_3\mathrm{Bi}_2\mathrm{I}_9 $  films for the first time.

(2) The influences of electric parameters (the applied voltage mode, pulse and time, etc.) on MBI film morphology and crystal structure correlating with the photovoltaic performance of PSCs have been investigated systematically.

(3) The proposed electrochemical deposition method is facile, controllable, and repeatable, and significant to the commercial production of perovskite

(4) The proposed electrochemical deposition involves an annealing-free fabrication, thus simplifying the process and promoting production efficiency.
