# Room-Temperature and Solution-Processed Copper Iodide as Hole Transport Layer for Inverted Planar Perovskite Solar Cells

Weihai Sun,  $  ^{a,b}  $   Senyun Ye,  $  ^{a}  $   Haixiao Rao,  $  ^{a}  $   Yunlong Li,  $  ^{a}  $   Zhiwei Liu,  $  ^{*a}  $   Lixin Xiao,  $  ^{b}  $   Zhijian Chen,  $  ^{b}  $   Zuqiang Bian  $  ^{*a}  $   and Chunhui Huang  $  ^{a}  $

# Abstract

The inverted planar heterojunction perovskite solar cells with poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrenesulfonate) sulfonic acid (PEDOT:PSS) as hole transport layer (HTL) attract significant attentions during the recent years. However, the devices are suffering from a serious stability issue due to the acidic and hygroscopic characteristics of PEDOT:PSS. In this work, we demonstrated a room-temperature and solution-processed CuI film using as HTL for the inverted perovskite solar cells. As a result, an impressive PCE of  $ 16.8\% $  was achieved by the device based on the CuI HTL. Moreover, the unsealed CuI-based device displayed an enhanced air stability than that of the PEDOT:PSS-based device. In addition, the fabrication of CuI HTL was a simple and time-saving procedure without any post-treatment, making it a promising candidate as the HTL in inverted perovskite solar cells and a potential target for efficient flexible and tandem solar cells.

# Introduction

Over the past few years, organic/inorganic hybrid organometal trihalide perovskite based solar cells have been one of research hotspots for photovoltaic technology due to their high power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) along with relatively simple fabrication.  $  ^{1-3}  $   The advantages of perovskites including their large absorption coefficient, broad-band absorption, low-cost, high charge carrier mobility and diffusion length have promoted the diversity in device architectures, evolved from dye-sensitized solar cells (DSCs) with mesoscopic structure, later to organic photovoltaics (OPVs) with a planar heterojunction configuration.  $  ^{1,4-7}  $   Among these device architectures, the perovskite solar cell in the planar structure without a mesoporous layer attracts great attention because of its relatively higher PCE and low-temperature preparation.  $  ^{8-14}  $   Currently, the most successful state-of-the-art device architecture is that the perovskite layer is sandwiched between a compact n-type metal oxide as an electron transport layer and a hole transport material (HTM) such as   $  [2,2',7,7'  $  -tetrakis-(N,N-di-p-methoxyphenamine)-9,9'-spiro-bifluorene]

(spiro-MeOTAD).  $  ^{15, 16}  $   Although other organic hole transport materials such as poly-hexylthiophene (P3HT) or poly-triarylamine derivatives were successfully introduced into perovskite solar cells with fine efficiencies, the high cost of these organic materials would limit their development for further large-scale and commercial application.  $  ^{17, 18}  $   In contrast with organic materials, inorganic hole transport conductors seem to be good alternatives due to their low cost, high mobility and stability. Copper iodide (CuI) and copper (CuSCN) have been employed in perovskite solar cells through a solution-processed method, yet the PCEs of perovskite solar cells based on these inorganic hole conductors were lower than that of devices based on organic hole conductors.  $  ^{19-21}  $

Another inverted planar heterojunction perovskite solar cell composed of poly(3,4-ethyl-needioxythiophene): poly(styrene-sulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS)/perovskite/fullerene junction, where PEDOT:PSS and fullerene act as a transparent hole transport layer (HTL) and an electron transport layer (ETL), respectively, was

reported with a remarkable PCE.  $  ^{22-24}  $   However, PEDOT:PSS is not an ideal hole/transport conductor owing to its inefficient electron-blocking capability, acidic and hygroscopic nature. There have been several reports about replacing PEDOT:PSS with NiO semiconductor because of its good transparency, appropriate energy levels and high hole transportability in inverted planar perovskite solar cells.  $  ^{25-28}  $   Unfortunately, when compared with the performance of device based on PEDOT:PSS, the PCE of NiO in perovskite solar cell is unsatisfactory due to a lower short-circuit current density (  $  J_{SC}  $  ) and fill factor (FF) in spite of an obviously improved open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ). It could be inferred that the losses in   $  J_{SC}  $   and FF are attributed to the poor electrical conductivity of NiO film. In order to address this problem, Jen et al fabricated planar heterojunction perovskite solar cells based on Cu-doped   $  NiO_{x}  $   film with impressive PCEs up to 15.40%.  $  ^{29}  $   Seok et al prepared a well-ordered nanostructured NiO layer by a pulsed laser deposition method and achieved an overall PCE of 17.3% for perovskite solar cells using this NiO films as HTLs.  $  ^{30}  $   Lately, a heavily p-doped   $  Ni_{x}Mg_{1-x}O  $   film was developed and used as hole selective contacts to extract hole carriers effectively in the inverted planar perovskite-PCBM film architecture. A remarkable PCE of 16.2% was obtained for the corresponding device with active area >1 cm  $  ^{2}  $  , along with a small hysteresis.  $  ^{31}  $   Ding et al introduced   $  Cu_{2}O  $   and CuO films as HTLs by a facile solution-processed method into the inverted perovskite solar cells with good stability.  $  ^{32}  $   Moreover, our group employed a series of inorganic semiconductor containing copper compounds as HTLs in inverted perovskite solar cells, such as CuSCN, CuS and   $  CuO_{x}  $  ,  $  ^{33-35}  $   and the perovskite solar cells with these copper compound semiconductors exhibited excellent device performances compared with that of devices using PEDOT:PSS. However, the relatively complicated preparation methods of inorganic HTLs including the requirement for electro-deposition, post-deposition annealing or treatment with ultraviolet-Ozone are not appropriate to a low-cost and high-throughput production of perovskite solar cells. There are only a few researches on the utilization of pristine solution-processed inorganic HTLs in inverted perovskite solar cells. As a consequence, it is an urgent demand to discover other inorganic HTLs, which can be

solution-processed and applied in perovskite solar cells immediately.

CuI with a wide band gap of 3.1 eV behaves as a p-type semiconductor. We have recently reported CuI layer prepared from a facile solution-processed method, which was successfully introduced in polymer solar cells (PSCs). The results showed that the devices based on CuI pristine films displayed superior performance than conventional devices using PEDOT:PSS.  $  ^{36}  $   Most recently, Chen et al employed CuI as HTL in inverted perovskite solar cells with a PCE of 13.58%, as well as the improved device stability.  $  ^{37}  $   Yet, they selected a conventional two-step sequential deposition for preparing the perovskite films, which probably possesses a high surface roughness and large interface contact resistance between the perovskite layer and CuI HTL, limiting the improvement for the PCE of perovskite solar cells. Herein, we designed a planar inverted perovskite solar cell with a p-i-n configuration composed of ITO/CuI/CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}  $  PbI  $  _{3}  $  /fullerene (C  $  _{60}  $  )/bathocuproine (BCP)/Ag, where the CuI film as HTL was deposited by a low-temperature and solution-processed method and the CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}  $  PbI  $  _{3}  $   film as light absorbed layer was fabricated by a one-step fast deposition-crystallization method. We have improved the PCE of the perovskite solar cells cooperated with CuI layer by optimizing the dropping time of the chlorobenzene (CB) after introducing N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) solution of CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}  $  PbI  $  _{3}  $   to the surface of CuI film. After the optimization, a maximum PCE of 16.8% for the CuI based device was obtained. Furthermore, the optimized perovskite solar cells based on CuI HTL displayed better air stability than that of the conventional perovskite solar cells using PEDOT:PSS.

# Results and discussion

Fig. 1a and Fig. 1b show the SEM and AFM images of the top view of CuI film on ITO electrode prepared from spin-coating method, respectively. After deposited CuI acetonitrile solution, the surface of ITO was covered with CuI nanoparticles, indicating that CuI nanoparticles could avoid the direct contact between the ITO electrode and the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  layer. As shown in Fig. S1, the root mean square (RMS) roughness of bare ITO was  $ 4.7\mathrm{nm} $ , while the RMS roughness slightly

increased to 6.5 nm after being treated with the CuI film. The higher RMS roughness, on the one hand, would increase the contact area between the HTL and the active layer, but on the other hand, may be harmful to the formation of high-quality upper perovskite layer.

A one-step fast deposition-crystallization method (FDC) was reported by Spiccia et al. to form a homogeneous and dense perovskite film.  $  ^{38}  $   Many factors that influence the quality of perovskite film, including the types of additional solvents, the concentration of the perovskite solution and the dropping time of the additional solvent, were investigated in previous literature. Although the dropping time of the CB solution was limited to 4-6 s according to the original article, we proposed a further study for the influence on the nucleation and crystal growth of perovskite by adjusting the dropping time (3.5 s, 4.5 s and 5.5 s) of the second solvent. In order to get better understanding of the evolution of the perovskite film fabricated by the FDC technique, the morphologies of the obtained perovskite film under the different dropping time of the extra solvent were observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As shown in Figure 2, introducing CB in 3.5 s after the start of spin-coating produced a flat and uniform   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   film, although with some cracks between the perovskite particles due to the unreachable supersaturation of the perovskite solution on the substrate. When the dropping time was delayed to 4.5 s or 5.5 s, the evaporation of the DMF solvent would speed up the concentration of the perovskite solution, resulting in the appearance of supersaturation of the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   solution in the film. Then the CB droplets were added to the substrate, which quickly induced the fast nucleation crystallization of perovskite, leaving a high surface coverage and dense film. Additionally, the size of perovskite crystals formed when the dropping time was 5.5 s was smaller than that of perovskite particles formed in 4.5 s, which could be explained by the obtainment of the higher concentration of perovskite solution. However, when the time of adding CB droplets was extended to 6 s or later, a pink perovskite film achieved was similar with that obtained from the conventional method without the addition of CB, which was ascribe to the occurrence of heterogeneous crystallization and aggregation of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   crystals.

The device configuration of perovskite solar cell based on the CuI HTL and the corresponding cross-sectional SEM image are shown in Figure 3. As can be seen from Figure 3b, the CuI buffer layer between the ITO and the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  layer is not clearly identified due to the thin thickness of CuI. The surface of ITO (or CuI) can be covered with the smooth and uniform perovskite film with an average thickness of approximately  $ 300~\mathrm{nm} $ . Besides, the electron transport layer  $ \mathrm{C}_{60} $  and the hole block layer could be observed to completely coat the underlying perovskite film, indicating leakage paths from the perovskite layer to Ag electrode could not occur.

The schematic energy diagram of the material in device is presented in Figure 3c. The ultraviolet photoelectron yield spectroscopy were used to determine the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of the different anode buffer layers and the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  layer (Figure S2 in the supporting information). The HOMO energy level of CuI layer was determined to be -5.4 eV, which is little higher than that of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  layer (-5.5 eV), leading to a benefit for extracting the holes at the interface between the CuI and  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  film and collecting in CuI HTL. However, the HOMO of PEDOT:PSS measured as -4.9 eV is much higher than that of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $ , which would create a high energy barrier between the PEDOT:PSS HTL and  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  layer. In addition, the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of CuI layer (-2.3 eV) is much higher than that of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  layer (-4.0 eV), which could prevent the electron from the perovskite effectively. On the contrary, the electron blocking ability of PEDOT:PSS is weak, resulting in the recombination of charges in PEDOT:PSS layer.

To further investigate the influence of morphology of perovskite film on the performance of photovoltaic device, solar cells with a structure of ITO/CuI/  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 / \mathrm{C}_{60} / \mathrm{BCP} / \mathrm{Ag} $  were fabricated (where the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  film were deposited when the dropping time of CB was  $ 3.5\mathrm{s} $ ,  $ 4.5\mathrm{s} $  and  $ 5.5\mathrm{s} $ , noted as Device A, Device B and Device C, respectively). For comparison, a reference cell using PEDOT:PSS as HTL was also manufactured (noted as Device D). In contrast, all of the photovoltaic parameters ( $ V_{oc} $ ,  $ J_{sc} $ , FF and PCE) of Device B are enhanced to  $ 0.99\mathrm{V} $ ,  $ 22.6\mathrm{mA/cm}^2 $ ,  $ 71.3\% $  and  $ 16.0\% $ , respectively. The reason for the increased  $ V_{oc} $  is due to the better perovskite coverage of substrate (see Figure 2b and Figure 2d), which probably decreases the possibility of leakage paths. Meanwhile, the uniform perovskite film with less defects and traps was formed in Device B, which reduces recombination of charge carriers in  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  film, thus leading to a higher  $ J_{sc} $  and FF. However, as the dropping time of CB was delayed to  $ 5.5\mathrm{s} $ , the  $ V_{oc} $  ( $ 0.97\mathrm{V} $ ),  $ J_{sc} $  ( $ 22.1\mathrm{mA/cm}^2 $ ) and FF ( $ 70.5\% $ ) of Device C are slightly lower than those of Device B, which is ascribed to the enhanced recombination of charge carriers caused by the increased grain boundary between the smaller perovskite particles. Upon insertion of PEDOT:PSS instead of CuI layer, the parameters of  $ V_{oc} $ ,  $ J_{sc} $ , FF and PCE of Device D are  $ 0.79\mathrm{V} $ ,  $ 22.9\mathrm{mA/cm}^2 $ ,  $ 73.1\% $  and  $ 13.3\% $ , respectively. As mentioned above, the high energy barrier between the PEDOT:PSS and  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  layer could be one reason for the decreased  $ V_{oc} $ . Furthermore, another possible reason for the lower  $ V_{oc} $  is that the controversial electron blocking ability of PEDOT:PSS since PEDOT:PSS could be taken as an electron collecting electrode.

In order to evaluate the hysteresis in the  $ J-V $  curves of perovskite solar cells, we recorded the  $ J-V $  curves of the best performance devices based on CuI layer by using both forward (from forward bias to short circuit) and reverse (from short circuit to forward bias) scan mode with a scan rate of  $ 0.3\mathrm{Vs}^{-1} $ . As shown in Figure 4b, the  $ V_{oc} $ ,  $ J_{sc} $  and FF obtained from the  $ J-V $  curve of the reverse scan mode were  $ 1.01\mathrm{V} $ ,  $ 22.8\mathrm{mA/cm}^2 $  and  $ 73.0\% $ , respectively, yielding a PCE of  $ 16.8\% $ . Additionally, the corresponding parameters from the  $ J-V $  curve of the forward scan mode were  $ 0.97\mathrm{V} $ ,  $ 22.7\mathrm{mA/cm}^2 $  and  $ 73.8\% $ , respectively, producing a most similar PCE of  $ 16.2\% $ . Such a slight hysteresis in CuI perovskite solar cells indicates the reliability of the  $ J-V $  measurement. The incident photo-to-current efficiency (IPCE) spectrum of the device, presented in Figure 4c, exhibits a broad range of over  $ 80\% $  between  $ 430\mathrm{nm} $  and  $ 750\mathrm{nm} $ , of which the highest value reaches  $ 90.5\% $  at  $ 490\mathrm{nm} $ . The   $  J_{sc}  $   of the device with PEDOT:PSS calculated from integrating the IPCE spectrum is about 19 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  , which is far less than that of the device measured from J-V curve. As the test environment for the J-V curve and the IPCE spectrum measurements is different (the J-V curve measurement was performed in a glove box while the IPCE spectrum measurement was conducted in air), we speculate that the instability of PEDOT:PSS may be the reason for the difference between the results of the J-V curve and the IPCE spectrum.  $  ^{39}  $   To investigate the reproducibility of the performance of the inverted perovskite solar cells with the CuI HTL, 30 independent solar cells were prepared and measured. The histogram of the PCEs is presented in Fig. 4d. The distribution of PCEs for the 30 cells is mainly concentrated in a small range from 15% to 16%, which indicates our solution-processed CuI layer could be a potential candidate as an inorganic HTL for inverted perovskite solar cells.

As we known, the stability issue needs to be resolved for the commercial production and application of perovskite solar cells. Despite the PEDOT:PSS is widely applied as a HTL in inverted perovskite solar cells with impressive PCEs, the acidic and hygroscopic nature of PEDOT:PSS would exacerbate the device degradation. Accordingly, the long-term stability of the unencapsulated devices incorporating with CuI or PEDOT:PSS as HTL were monitored and recorded in air with 25% humidity and room temperature (Fig 5). Apparently, the device based on PEDOT:PSS showed a rapid decrease in PCE after 216 h of exposure in air, which was mainly ascribed to a dramatic reduction in   $  J_{sc}  $   and FF. Whereas the device using CuI as the HTL exhibited a remarkable improved device stability and maintained more than 93% of its original PCE even after 288 h storage in air, which was due to the hydrophobic nature and superior air stability of CuI.

In conclusion, we fabricated a planar inverted perovskite solar cell by the incorporation of low-temperature solution-processed pristine CuI HTL, where the perovskite layer was deposited by a FDC method. Compared with the devices prepared with the PEDOT:PSS HTLs, the insertion of CuI HTL into inverted perovskite solar cell exhibited more effective in reducing energy loss at the interface

between the HTL and perovskite layer, leading to improved charge extraction and inhibited charge recombination in the HTL/perovskite interface, and hence delivered an enhanced PCE. Furthermore, a highest PCE of 16.8% for the CuI based device was obtained by the optimization of morphology of perovskite film. It is worth noting that the CuI-based perovskite solar cell displayed more excellent air stability than that of the classic PEDOT:PSS-based perovskite solar cell. More importantly, the fabrication of CuI HTL for inverted perovskite solar cell with a remarkable PCE is a facile and time-saving procedure without any post-treatment, indicating that CuI is a promising alternative as a HTL in inverted perovskite solar cells for a low-cost and large-scale industrial production.
