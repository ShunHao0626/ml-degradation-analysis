# ABSTRACT

The ionic nature of perovskites leads to abundant defects at the interface and grain boundaries of perovskite films, which are detrimental to the performance of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Here, I-I-VI group  $ \mathrm{CsCu_5S_3} $  (CCS) nanocrystals (NCs) were used as an interlayer between the perovskite film and hole transport layer in PSCs. The CCS NCs were synthesized via a facile colloidal chemistry approach and exhibited high air stability and strong absorption in the visible-to-near infrared range. The CCS interlayer effectively passivates the interfacial defects, which strongly suppresses non-radiative recombination in the PSCs. The CCS interlayer is favorable for energy levels between the perovskite and HTM to reduce carrier recombination. As a result, the devices with a CCS interlayer delivered a champion power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 22.29\% $  with good reproducibility and stability. In addition, the PSCs retained more than  $ 85\% $  of their original efficiency after storage for approximately  $ 3000\mathrm{h} $  under ambient conditions. This work paves a new avenue for the development of high-efficiency PSCs and the application of Cu-based chalcogenide NCs.

the ionic nature of perovskites. Point defects such as uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  and uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{I}^{-} $  are easily produced during the perovskite preparation process. These ion defects migrate into the bulk films and promote the diffusion of anions and cations to the film surface and grain boundaries, leading to film degradation. In addition, these defects act as recombination centers, which degrade the performance of PSCs. Therefore, it is desirable to decrease the defect density of perovskite layers in PSCs to improve their performance and stability simultaneously.

Several strategies have been used to address these issues, including composition engineering, interface modification, and encapsulation   $  [4–9]  $  . Among these strategies, surface passivation via post-treatment with functional organic compounds is a popular choice to improve the efficiency and stability of PSCs   $  [10–12]  $  . In this strategy, uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   is passivated through Lewis acid–base interactions, resulting in suppressed non-radiative recombination and increased stability. Some inorganic semiconductor nanocrystals (NCs) have also been utilized as an interlayer between the perovskite absorber layer and hole transport layer (HTL) to improve charge transfer and retard charge recombination. Akin et al.   $  [13]  $   introduced a   $  CsPbBr_{1.85}I_{1.15}  $   NC interlayer into PSCs to increase moisture resistance and suppress charge recombination simultaneously. In other work,   $  CsPbBrCl_{2}  $   NCs provided elements to fill defect sites and organic ligands rendered the surface of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   hydrophobic, leading to PSCs with dramatically enhanced efficiency and stability   $  [14]  $  . Chen and co-workers explored the interfacial contact passivation of PbS in cesium/formamidinium double-cation PSCs   $  [4]  $  . In-situ interfacial contact passivation of methylammonium-free cesium/formamidinium double-cation perovskite via the formation of quantum dots in a matrix heterocrystal resulted in highly stable PSCs with an efficiency of over 21%. Perovskite films can also be subjected to post-deposition alteration with   $  CuInS_{2}  $   NCs to enhance their stability under high humidity without sacrificing device performance   $  [15]  $  . The improvements achieved using these NCs have motivated the search for novel materials suitable as interlayers to realize stable and highly efficient PSCs.

The discovery of new materials opens up huge possibilities for scientific and industrial applications   $  [16-19]  $  . In particular, copper-based chalcogenides such as   $  \mathrm{Cu}_{2}(\mathrm{Se},\mathrm{S})  $     $  [20]  $  ,   $  \mathrm{CuIn}(\mathrm{S},\mathrm{Se})_{2}  $     $  [21]  $  ,   $  \mathrm{Cu}_{2}\mathrm{Sn}(\mathrm{S},\mathrm{Se})_{3}  $     $  [22]  $  ,   $  \mathrm{Cu}_{2}\mathrm{Ge}(\mathrm{S},\mathrm{Se})_{3}  $     $  [23]  $  , and   $  \mathrm{Cu(In,Ga)(S,Se)}_{2}  $     $  [24]  $  , have received considerable attention for use in optoelectronic devices. Furthermore, density functional theory-based first-principles calculations have indicated that new I-I-VI group copper-based compounds will be thermodynamically stable, including   $  NaCu_{5}S_{3}  $  ,   $  KCu_{4}S_{7}  $  ,   $  CsCu_{5}S_{3}  $  , and   $  CsCu_{5}Se_{3}  $     $  [25-30]  $  . These alkali metal–copper chalcogenides are interesting examples of copper-rich structures, which are commonly possess favorable features for photovoltaic applications. Solution-based synthetic approaches such as the solvothermal route can provide different reaction and thermochemical pathways compared to those of solid-state reactions and are convenient soft chemistry processes able to produce target materials with desired shapes, sizes, and compositions   $  [28,31]  $  . The alkali metal–copper chalcogenide   $  CsCu_{5}S_{3}  $   (CCS) can be prepared by the solvothermal route, which provides a typical one-dimensional nanorod microstructure   $  [29]  $  . CCS has been obtained in two forms, i.e., tetragonal and orthorhombic structures   $  [30]  $  . A theoretical calculation indicated that CCS has a nearly direct band gap of   $  \sim1.41~eV  $  , suggesting its potential for use in photovoltaic applications   $  [29]  $  . However, CCS nanocrystals (NCs) have not been reported. Therefore, it is desirable to synthesize CCS NCs and explore their suitability for use in photovoltaics.

Here, uniform CCS NCs with a narrow size distribution of  $ 32.3 \pm 4.3\mathrm{nm} $  with a direct band gap of  $ 1.40\mathrm{eV} $  are utilized as an interlayer between the perovskite absorber layer and HTL in PSCs. Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements reveal that the trap states in a perovskite layer can be effectively passivated with a CCS interlayer. The intercalation of CCS NCs between perovskite and HTM can form a

favored energy level alignment to benefit hole transport and reduce carrier recombination. As a result, a PSC with a CCS interlayer yields an open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) of 1.10 V, fill factor (FF) of 81.38%, and high PCE of 22.29%, as well as excellent long-term stability. This report presents a new strategy to achieve stable PSCs with high performance and an alternative application for Cu-based chalcogenide NCs.

# 2. Materials and methods

# 2.1. Chemicals

$ \mathrm{Cs_2CO_3} $  (99.9%), copper (II) acetylacetonate  $ (\mathrm{Cu(acac)_2} $ , 99.99 + %), sulfur (S, 99.998%), oleylamine (OLA, 70%),  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  (99%) and  $ \mathrm{PbBr}_2 $  (98%) were purchased from Aldrich. Ammonium acetate  $ (\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COONH}_4 $ , 99%) and dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%) were obtained from Aladdin. The  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  colloid precursor and oleyl acid (OA, 90%) were obtained from Alfa Aesar. Lead iodide  $ (\mathrm{PbI}_2) $  was bought from TCI Co. Ltd.  $ \mathrm{HC(NH_2)I} $  (FAI),  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{Br} $  (MABr),  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{Cl} $  (MACl), and Sprio-MeOTAD were all purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. Conductive glass, ITO was purchased from South China Science & Technology Co. Ltd. The 1 M OLA-S stock solution was made by dissolving  $ 0.64\mathrm{g} $  (20 mmol) of sulfur powder in  $ 20~\mathrm{mL} $  of OLA under argon atmosphere at room temperature by stirring to yield a perfectly clear solution.

# 2.2. Synthesis of  $ \mathrm{CsCu_5S_3} $  nanocrystals

In a typical, 0.1 mmol 0.0325 g   $  Cs_{2}CO_{3}  $   and 1 mmol   $  Cu(acac)_{2}  $   was first loaded into a 50 mL flask containing 5 mL OLA and 1 mL OA. The flask was degassed by a vacuum pump for 30 min to remove water and other low-boiling point impurities at 120 °C. Afterwards, the temperature was increased to 260 °C, followed by swift injection of 0.6 mL 1 M OLA-S solution into the flask. The reaction solution was then maintained at this temperature for 15 min to finish the reaction. Subsequently, the solution was cooled down to room temperature, then 10 mL hexane was added and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min. The upper yellow solution was discarded and hexane was added to disperse the nanocrystals. Then the product was further purified by adding a certain amount of isopropanol and centrifuging. This process was repeated for three times to yield the nanocrystal product that could be dispersed in common organic solvents such as hexane for later use.

# 2.3. Ligand exchange

The   $  PbX_{2}/AA  $   solution-phase ligand-exchange process was carried out in a test tube in air. Lead halides (lead iodide 0.1 M and lead bromide 0.02 M) and   $  CH_{3}COONH_{4}  $   (0.04 M) are pre-dissolved in DMF. A 5 mL amount of the NCs hexane solution (10 mg/ml) was added to 5 mL of precursor solution. These were mixed vigorously for 1–2 min until the NCs completely transferred to the DMF phase. The DMF solution was washed three times with hexane. After ligand exchange, NCs were precipitated via the addition of toluene, and were separated by centrifugation. After 20 min of drying, the NCs were then redispersed in isopropanol to facilitate the film deposition.

# 2.4. Device fabrication

The ITO glass was sequentially cleaned with deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol (IPA). The substrate was spin coated with a layer of   $  SnO_{2}  $   nanocrystals films (2.67%) at 4000 rpm for 30 s, which was cleaned by UVO for 15 min. Then, the substrate is annealed at 150 °C for 30 min. The deposition of perovskite films is based on a conventional two-step spin-coating method. 1.4 M   $  PbI_{2}  $   in DMF: DMSO solution (950:50, v/v) was prepared at room temperature and stirred for 12 h.   $  PbI_{2}  $   solution was spin-coated onto the   $  SnO_{2}  $   substrates at 1500 rpm for 30 s to form a yellow   $  PbI_{2}  $  -DMSO film. Then 1 mL of 2-

# 2.5. Characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses were performed on a Bruker D2 PHASER SSD160 X powder diffraction system using Cu Kα radiation source (  $  \lambda = 1.54 \AA  $  ) operating at 30 kV and 10 mA. TEM images were obtained using a Hitachi HT 7700, operating at an accelerating voltage of 100 kV. HRTEM images were obtained with JEOL JEM-2100 microscope operating at 200 kV. Element analysis as an ensemble measurement was conducted on JSM 7500F scanning electron microscope equipped with energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) detector. XPS were obtained using a Kratos-XSAM800. UV–Vis-NIR absorption spectra of NCs suspensions in hexane were acquired in 1 cm path length quartz cuvettes using a Cary 5000 spectrophotometer. The UPS spectra was performed with the PHI 5000 Versaprobe III Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscometer for   $  CsCu_{5}S_{3}  $   NC films on Si substrate with   $  0.5 \times 0.5 \, cm^{2}  $  . Photovoltaic performance of solar cells devices was measured under illumination of a simulated sunlight (AM 1.5, 100 mW/  $  cm^{2}  $  , SSF5-3A, Enlitech), and the J-V curves were recorded using a Keithley digital source meter (Model 2400). The active area of the solar cells was confirmed by using a metal aperture of   $  0.09 \, cm^{2}  $   to avoid light scattering through the sides. The electrochemical impedance and admittance spectroscopy measurement were conducted by using a multichannel potentiometer (VMP3, Biologic). EIS data were recorded from 0 to 1.0 V in the frequency range from 1 MHz to 100 mHz with an AC amplitude of 10 mV. The Mott-Schottky data were recorded at the frequency of 50 KHz in the applied voltage range from -1.5 V to 0 V with an AC amplitude of 25 mV.

# 3. Results and discussion

Fig. 1a shows a typical X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the CCS NCs obtained by the hot-injection solution method. Because a standard XRD pattern of  $ \mathrm{CsCu_5S_3} $  was not found in the XRD calibration software, we simulated the XRD pattern of CCS. The XRD results showed that the as-synthesized CCS NCs possessed an orthorhombic structure, which is consistent with the literature [28,30]. All the diffraction peaks of the NCs matched well with the simulated pattern. No diffraction patterns associated with impurity phases were observed in the XRD pattern. We also performed X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to further analyse the chemical composition of the as-synthesized sample (Fig. S1a). High-resolution XPS spectra of Cs 3d, Cu 2p, and S 2p are shown in Fig. S1b-d, respectively. The peaks at  $ 162.7\mathrm{eV} $ ,  $ 161.6\mathrm{eV} $ ,  $ 738.3\mathrm{eV} $  and  $ 724.4\mathrm{eV} $  are assigned as the binding energy of S  $ 2\mathrm{p}_{1/2} $ , S  $ 2\mathrm{p}_{3/2} $ , Cs  $ 3\mathrm{d}_{3/2} $  and Cs  $ 3\mathrm{d}_{5/2} $ , respectively, strongly confirming the existence of  $ \mathbf{S}^{2-} $  and  $ \mathbf{C}\mathbf{s}^{+} $ . The valence of  $ \mathrm{Cu} $  has been confirmed to be  $ +1 $  due to the peaks of 952.4 and  $ 932.5\mathrm{eV} $  assigned as the binding energy of  $ 2\mathrm{p}_{1/2} $  and  $ 2\mathrm{p}_{3/2} $  for  $ \mathrm{Cu}^{+} $ , respectively, as well as the absence of satellite peaks [28]. These results demonstrate that CCS NCs with high purity were synthesized by the hot-injection solution method.

A low-resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image of the as-synthesized NCs is displayed in Fig. 1b. Uniform particles with an almost hexagonal shape and average size of  $ 32.3 \pm 4.3 \mathrm{~nm} $  can be clearly observed. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was performed to analyse the chemical composition of the CCS NCs (Fig. S2).

The Cs/Cu/S atomic ratio determined from the EDS analysis was 0.9:5.0:2.9, which is close to the nominal composition of  $ \mathrm{CsCu_5S_3} $ . The continuous lattice fringes across each NC in high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) image (Fig. 1c) indicate the single-crystal nature of the CCS NCs. The lattice fringe between two adjacent interplanar spacings are 0.33 and  $ 0.34\mathrm{nm} $ , corresponding to the (202) and (111) planes of the simulated orthogonal structure of CCS, respectively. The inset image of Fig. 1c presents the fast Fourier transform (FFT) pattern of the NCs, which further confirmed their high crystallinity. Therefore, CCS NCs with high crystallinity were successfully synthesized.

The optical properties of the CCS NCs were studied via ultraviolet–visible–near-infrared (UV–VIS–NIR) absorption spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 1d, the NCs exhibited broad absorption over the whole wavelength range from visible to NIR. The inset of Fig. 1d displays a Tauc plot determined to evaluate the band gap energy (  $  E_{g}  $  ) of the NCs by plotting the relationship of the absorption coefficient versus photon energy. A direct optical   $  E_{g}  $   of 1.40 eV was found for the CCS NCs, which is consistent with the previously reported direct   $  E_{g}  $   of 1.41 eV [28]. Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS, Fig. S3) was performed to determine the valence band positions of CCS NCs. From Fig. 1e and 1f, the Femi Energy (  $  E_{F}  $  ) and the valence band maximum (VBM) of the as-prepared CCS NCs is -5.32 and -5.58 eV, respectively. Based on the VBM value and   $  E_{g}  $   of the CCS NCs film (1.40 eV), the conduction band minimum (CBM) of CCS NCs film is determined to be -4.18 eV. The schematic representation of calculated band level positions of CCS NCs is depicted in Fig. 1g.

Considering that oleic acid (OA) and oleyl amine (OLA) are typically used in colloidal chemistry-based approaches, ligand exchange is imperative to remove these insulating ligands from the NCs to obtain smooth NC film morphologies for subsequent application   $  [32]  $  . Ligand exchange of the CCS NCs was thus carried out, and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was performed to gain insight into the ligand stripping states. Generally, it is challenging to completely remove organic chemicals with long organic alkyl chain used in hot-injection synthetic process. As shown in Fig. 2a, a trace number of infrared peaks are still present for CCS NCs after ligand removal process. Among them, the peak at   $  2920\ cm^{-1}  $   is attributable to the -C=CH-stretching mode and peak at   $  2852\ cm^{-1}  $   from the C-H stretching vibration mode   $  [33]  $  . Peaks at 1624, 1530, and   $  1456\ cm^{-1}  $   ascribed to carboxylic acid (-COO-) moieties, amine (  $  -NH_{2}  $  ), and aliphatic hydrocarbon chains, respectively, which are characteristic wave numbers of OA and OLA, were also observed   $  [34,35]  $  . Apparently, the intensities of peaks from OA and OLA ligands were significantly weakened after ligand exchange, which may be not optimal for charge transport between the NCs. Actually, long alkyl chain organic reagents, e.g., oleic acid (OA) and oleylamine (OLA) are widely used to directly passivate defects in perovskite solar cells   $  [36,37]  $  . On the other hand, various perovskite NCs with surface ligands produced via hot-injection methods have been used to stabilize perovskite films   $  [11,38–41]  $  . Thus, we decided to test the CCS NCs as an interlayer between the perovskite and hole transport materials (HTMs) in PSCs.

The steady photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved PL (TRPL) spectra were carried out to evaluate the effect of the CCS NCs on the charge recombination and carrier lifetimes of perovskite films. As shown in Fig. 2b, the pristine perovskite film on ITO substrate exhibits a PL emission peak at  $ 800\mathrm{nm} $ , whereas a stronger PL peak blue-shifts to  $ 796\mathrm{nm} $  is monitored for perovskite film with CCS NCs modification, suggesting the surface defects are effectively passivated by CCS layer by spin-coating method [42-44]. The TRPL curves for perovskite films with and without a CCS modification are displayed in Fig. 2c. These curves were both fitted with biexponential functions; the fitting results are summarized in Table S1. The average carrier lifetimes of the unmodified and CCS-modified perovskite films determined from the TRPL curves were 850.59 and 875.35 ns, respectively. To make a clarity of the passivation effects of CCS NCs, the XPS analysis was performed. As shown in Fig. 2d, two obvious peaks at 138.5 and  $ 143.3\mathrm{eV} $  represent the binding energies of  $ \mathrm{Pb4f_{7 / 2}} $  and  $ \mathrm{Pb4f_{4 / 2}} $ , respectively [47]. On the basis of effectively passivated trap states of perovskite films by CCS NCs, PSCs with a CCS interlayer are expected to realize high performance. Thus, we fabricated PSCs with a n-i-p planar structures of ITO/SnO  $  _{2}  $  /perovskite/CCS interlayer/Spiro-OMeTAD/Au, and the cross-sectional SEM image of a typical device is displayed in Fig. 3a. A uniform and thin CCS interlayer is formed on the top perovskite film. To investigate the effects of a CCS interlayer on photovoltaic performance, PSCs with 0 mg/ml, 0.05 mg/ml, 0.1 mg/ml and 0.2 mg/ml CCS NCs were prepared. For simplicity, we named them as pristine,

0.05 CCS, 0.1 CCS, and 0.2 CCS, respectively. Both the reverse and forward scan of current density-voltage (J-V) curves for devices are displayed in Fig. S4, and the corresponding photovoltaic parameters are

summarized in Table S2. The PCE of the pristine device is 20.54%, and it was enhanced to 21.30% upon the inclusion of a 0.05 CCS interlayer. Then the PCE reached the maximum value of 22.29% when the CCS concentration was 0.1 mg/mL, while it slightly dropped to 21.87% with further increase of the CCS concentration to 0.2 mg/mL, probably resulting from more residual ligand for thicker CCS layer, which may act as an energy barrier for efficient charge transport. The J-V curve for champion pristine and CCS modified PSCs are shown in Fig. 3b, c, respectively (All the CCS interlayer below is referred to 0.1 CCS, unless otherwise specified.). The champion PSCs with CCS interlayer yields a high PCE of 22.29% (Reverse scan), along with short-circuit current density (  $  J_{SC}  $  ) of 24.84 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  , open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) of 1.10 V, and fill factor (FF) of 81.38%. As widely recognized in thin-film solar cells, a matching band structure is a decisive factor for carrier transport and recombination at the interface, determining  $  V_{\mathrm{OC}}  $  and PCE [51]. To uncover the origin of the enhancement in photovoltaic performance induced by the CCS interlayer, we first investigate the energy band structure diagram of device. The Schematic cross-sectional view of the device architecture and its corresponding energy-level diagram of device are presented in Fig. 4a and b, respectively. Apparently, the VBM of CCS interlayer leads to a favourable energy alignment with energy level of perovskite and Spiro-OMeTAD, manifesting that the CCS interlayer acts as energy buffer to boost hole transport and acts as an energy barrier to reduce carrier recombination, leading to a higher  $  V_{\mathrm{OC}}  $  and PCE for device with

CCS interlayer.

To investigate the influence of CCS interlayer on device carrier dynamics, thermal admittance spectroscopy (TAS) analysis and electrical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were carried out. TAS is an effective technique to analyse both shallow and deep defects in thin-film solar cells   $  [52,53]  $  . The trap density of states (tDOS) in solar cells can be derived from Eq. (1)   $  [54,55]  $  ,

$$
t D O S (E _ {\omega}) = - \frac {V _ {b i}}{q W} \frac {d C}{d \omega} \frac {\omega}{k _ {B} T}
$$

(1)

where  $ \omega $ ,  $ V_{bi} $ ,  $ q $ ,  $ W $ ,  $ C $ ,  $ k_{B} $ , and  $ T $  are angular frequency, built-in potential, elementary charge, depletion width, capacitance, the Boltzmann constant, and temperature, respectively. The obtained tDOS shown in Fig. 4c is in the range of polycrystalline halide perovskite-based solar cells, which can be roughly divided into two sections. When the perovskite film was subject to spinning coating of CCS NCs, tDOS with an energy level in section II ( $ >0.4 $  eV) was significantly decreased, suggesting that the deeper tap region (0.4 to 0.55 eV) should be assigned to film surface defects, and this is consistent with the enhanced PL intensity and carrier lifetime. The unchanged tDOS in Section I ( $ <0.4 $  eV) indicates that the shallow traps stay deeper in the perovskite films, e.g. grain boundaries, which can't be passivated by the CCS NCs atop the surface of perovskite films [52,53,56]. The devices with a CCS interlayer showed a prominent decrease of tDOS in the deeper trap region compared with that of the devices without a CCS interlayer, consistent with the PL results. # 4. Conclusions

Uniform CCS colloidal NCs were synthesized via the solution hot-injection method. The as-prepared CCS NCs exhibited an orthorhombic structure with a direct band gap of  $ 1.40\mathrm{eV} $ , consistent with a theoretical report, which allowed us to explore their potential for application in optoelectronic devices. The CCS NCs were used as an interlayer in PSCs to passivate the surface trap states of the perovskite films.
