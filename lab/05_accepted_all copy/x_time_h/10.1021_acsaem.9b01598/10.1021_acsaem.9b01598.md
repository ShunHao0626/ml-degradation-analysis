# Dicyanovinylene and Thiazolo[5,4-d]thiazole Core Containing D-A-D Type Hole-Transporting Materials for Spiro-OMeTAD-Free Perovskite Solar Cell Applications with Superior Atmospheric Stability

ABSTRACT: In perovskite solar cell (PSC) devices, hole-transporting materials (HTMs) are vital components affecting the charge separation and playing an important role in achieving high efficiencies. These may also protect active light-absorbing layers from degradation. The current best-in-class HTM Spiro-OMeTAD is prohibitively expensive for large-scale applications, and hence, design of novel cost-effective HTMs yielding comparable device performance is essential. In this paper, we report synthesis of donor–acceptor–donor (D–A–D) type hole-transporting materials (TTz-1 and TPDCN) featuring the dicyanovinylene and thiazolo[5,4-d]thiazole cores and evaluate their performance via integrating them in perovskite solar cells (PSCs). The results suggest that both the HTMs are easy to synthesize and demonstrate favorable structural characteristics for device integration. Detailed analysis reveals that the molecules show appropriate energy level alignment with methylammonium lead iodide   $  \left(\mathrm{CH}_{3}\mathrm{NH}_{3}\mathrm{PbI}_{3}\right)  $   perovskite and possess good thermal stability and high hole mobility. Planar PSC devices fabricated using TTz-1 as an HTM yielded a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 11.37%, while devices using TPDCN show a PCE of 10.11%, comparable to the PCE of 11.62% obtained in control samples based on Spiro-OMeTAD. In addition, stability analysis of the devices shows that devices fabricated using TTz-1 and TPDCN exhibit superior atmospheric stability as compared to those based on Spiro-OMeTAD. These results suggest that the reported HTM architectures are promising leads for designing new HTMs for perovskite solar cells owing to their simple and scalable synthesis and tunability.

KEYWORDS: thiazolo[5,4-d]thiazole, dicyanovinylene, donor-acceptor-donor, hole transporting materials, perovskite solar cell

# INTRODUCTION

Organic–inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells are potential candidates for large-scale applications due to their low cost, easy device fabrication, and remarkable power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of up to 25.2%. $  ^{1-4}  $  Rapid improvement in the performance of perovskite solar cells has been attributed to excellent properties of perovskite such as strong light absorption, low exciton binding energy, low charge carrier loss, and nonradiative recombination. $  ^{5-7}  $  In perovskite solar cell (PSC) devices, light absorption by the perovskite material leads to generation of excitons (hole and electron pair) which need to be separated efficiently for a high quantum yield. $  ^{8,9}  $

This process is strongly affected by the electron and hole transport materials, i.e., ETM and HTM, which play a critical role in charge separation and transport processes and in realizing improved PCEs.  $  ^{10,11}  $

Among ETMs,   $  TiO_{2}  $   is a well-established material due to its favorable electronic properties as well as its environmental stability and low cost.  $  ^{12}  $   Similarly, 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-di-4-methoxyphenylamino)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD)

However, there are several bottlenecks preventing its widespread application such as high cost, complicated multistep synthesis, expensive precursors, and tedious purification process.  $  ^{13-15}  $   Moreover, the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy level of Spiro-OMeTAD (-5.13 eV) does not have an optimal match with the valence band (VB) of perovskite (-5.43 eV) due to its relatively large energy offset.  $  ^{16,17}  $   In order for an HTM to afford a highly efficient PSC, it should satisfy certain criteria such as suitable energy levels (HOMO/LUMO) with perovskite, fast hole extraction/transportation, ability to afford uniform thin film morphology, high intrinsic stability, and cost-effectiveness. As alternatives to Spiro-OMeTAD, various organic and inorganic materials have been widely studied for perovskite solar cells.  $  ^{18}  $   Use of inorganic HTMs such as CuI, CuSCN,   $  NiO_{x}  $  , and CuO have resulted in rather poor photovoltaic performances due to their low conductivity.  $  ^{19,20}  $   Therefore, developing new low-cost organic HTMs with higher conductivity and ambient stability has significant importance from a practical perspective.  $  ^{21-26}  $

In an organic solar cell, donor–acceptor–donor (D–A–D) and donor-  $  \pi  $  -acceptor (D-  $  \pi  $  –A) type donor materials have been widely studied due to convenient synthesis protocols, appropriate optoelectronic properties, and higher hole mobilities.  $  ^{27-30}  $   Additionally, structural versatility of D–A–D type HTMs allows the incorporation of many different acceptor groups that can help in tailoring their photophysical and electronic properties. Hence, exploration of a diverse selection of D–A–D type HTM architectures may lead to the discovery of a suitable molecule with optimal characteristics relevant to PSCs. To engineer these materials, selection of appropriate electron-donating and electron-withdrawing groups is the key to achieving optimal HOMO/LUMO levels and can be tailored to favorably impact intramolecular charge-transfer (ICT) properties between the donor and acceptor moieties. The literature has a limited number of examples where the D–A–D type HTMs have been used in PSC devices,  $  ^{31-35}  $   and hence, we decided to explore this hypothesis by synthesizing and evaluating two new potential HTMs for developing efficient and stable planar perovskite solar cells.

Among the promising candidate materials, triphenylamine-based aromatic compounds are promising HTMs for PSC applications because of their potentially low cost and excellent charge-transport properties.  $  ^{36}  $   The triphenylamine core was identified as one of the best donor groups in A–D–A type HTMs due to efficient synthetic routes and convenient access

to diverse analogues.  $  ^{9,36-44}  $   To identify a suitable electron-withdrawing group, we selected the dicyanovinylene moiety which has been employed as an acceptor in donor–acceptor conjugated small molecules for organic solar cell applications.  $  ^{27,28,45}  $   However, it has not yet been employed as an HTM for PSC applications. Similarly, thiazolo[5,4-d]thiazole (TTz) is also one of the best electron acceptor cores for organic solar cells (OSCs).  $  ^{46-49}  $   Recently, Amen et al.  $  ^{50}  $   employed a thiazolo[5,4-d]thiazole core containing an HTM for inverted PSC devices where they tuned the photophysical and electrochemical properties by introducing furan spacer groups in between triphenylamine and TTz. The oxygen plasma treated devices displayed better performance along with enhanced stability. On the basis of this preliminary information, we proceeded to explore the triphenylamine moiety as the donor and dicyanovinylene and TTz cores as electron-accepting moieties for their integration in the PSC devices.

Our design for the new potential HTMs envisioned the dicyanovinylene and thiazolo[5,4-d]thiazole electron-withdrawing units as acceptors with the triphenylamine electron donor end-capped to get A–D–A type HTMs. The synthesized materials are named 4,4'-(thiazolo[5,4-d]thiazole-2,5-diyl)bis(N,N-bis(4-methoxyphenyl)aniline) (TTz-1) and (2Z,2'Z)-2,2'-(1,4-phenylene)bis(3-(4-(bis(4-methoxyphenyl)amino)phenyl)acrylonitrile) (TPDCN). Both the HTMs can be synthesized via an easy step with a Pd-free synthetic route and cheap starting materials. In terms of design, these HTM structures are simpler than Spiro-OMeTAD and other reported organic HTMs.  $  ^{5,36,51,52}  $   We observed that TTz-1 and TPDCN display better photophysical properties and appropriate HOMO energy level matches with a perovskite   $  (\mathrm{MAPbI}_{3})  $   HOMO energy level enabling better charge extraction. Additionally, these HTMs have good hole mobility and relatively high thermal stability. Upon integration with the planar PSC devices of TTz-1 as the HTM layer, a maximum PCE of 11.37% resulted, while those using TPDCN yielded a PCE of 10.11%, both comparable with standard Spiro-OMeTAD-based devices.

Thermal Properties and Hole Mobility Measurements. The thermal properties of TTz-1 and TPDCN were investigated using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), as shown in Figure 3a. TPDCN and TTz-1 show 5% weight loss at 364 and 251 °C, respectively. TPDCN displays higher thermal stability as compared to TTz-1. In both HTMs, there is no weight loss observed at lower temperatures up to 250 °C, which is an excellent attribute for outdoor photovoltaic applications. These results indicate that the acceptor units influence the thermal properties in a significant manner and the dicyanovinylene units are thermally robust. The DSC curves of TPDCN and TTz-1 show glass transition (  $  T_{g}  $  ) temperatures at 81 and 134 °C, respectively (Figure S1). The endothermic peaks at 190 and 174 °C might be ascribed to the crystallization temperature (  $  T_{c}  $  ) of TPDCN and TTz-1, respectively. The strong peak appearing at 220 and 209 °C

might be ascribed to the melting temperature   $  (T_{\mathrm{m}})  $   of TPDCN and TTz-1, respectively. These results suggest that the acceptor units significantly influence their thermal properties.

PSC devices have a stability issue when exposed under humid conditions. Therefore, it is important to study the stability of the device in humid conditions for newly synthesized HTMs. PSC devices were stored without encapsulation in the dark at room temperature in 70% relative humidity. The stability of the device was measured at regular intervals, and the obtained results are shown in Figure 6d. The planar conformation structure and highly ordered conjugation of TTz-1 and TPDCN devices showed substantially improved device stability as compared to the Spiro-MeTAD-based device.

The excellent stability of the new HTMs may be due to their hydrophobic nature, which can prevent water percolation into the perovskite layer. In order to investigate the hydrophobic nature of TTz-1, TPDCN, and Spiro-OMeTAD, we measured contact angles of a water droplet on the surface of HTMs as shown in Figure 7. The Spiro-OMeTAD film displayed smaller contact angle values of  $ 60.1^{\circ} $ , which may infiltrate water molecules into the perovskite surface. In contrast, TTz-1 and

TPDCN thin films exhibited much larger contact angles of   $  93.8^{\circ}  $   and   $  84.3^{\circ}  $  , respectively, compared to Spiro-OMeTAD. These results conclude that the newly developed HTMs have larger contact angles than Spiro-OMeTAD, protecting the perovskite layer from the ingress of moisture and, thus, leading to better stable photovoltaic devices.  $  ^{65,66}  $

Additionally, we also observed that the light-soaking of devices shows an improvement in the photovoltaic performance. The light-soaking significantly enhances the FF, and slightly improves   $  J_{sc}  $   before saturating after   $  \sim  $  10 min. In contrast, the light-soaking does not affect photovoltaic performance for the Spiro-OMeTAD-based device.

# CONCLUSIONS

In summary, we designed and synthesized two D-A-D type HTMs (TTz-1 and TPDCN), with a triphenylamine donor unit with electron deficient centers of dicyanovinylene and thiazolo[5,4-d]thiazole used for planar PSC applications. These HTMs can be synthesized in three steps without a Pd catalyst yield of low-cost materials. TTz-1-based devices show a better photovoltaic performance (PCE = 11.37%) compared to TPDCN (PCE = 10.11%). As a result, using a thiazolo[5,4-d]thiazole acceptor unit is better for building triphenylamine-based HTMs compared to dicyanovinylene containing TPDCN. These results prove that the synthesized HTMs are potential candidates with good performance, and they will motivate further work over a low-cost and facile synthesis for large-scale applications of PSCs.

Solar Cell Device Fabrication. The photovoltaic properties of synthesized HTMs were characterized using the device configuration glass/FTO/c-TiO $ _2 $ /perovskite (MAPbI $ _3 $ )/HTM/Au. The fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass substrate was etched using 2 M HCl and zinc metal powder. The substrate was sequentially cleaned through sonication using detergent soap water (10 min), deionized (DI) water (10 min), acetone (10 min), and isopropyl alcohol (10 min) followed by drying under nitrogen. Before coating the compact TiO $ _2 $  layer, the substrate was treated with oxygen plasma (10 min). The electron-transporting layer (compact TiO $ _2 $ ) was deposited through spin-coating the solution (35  $ \mu $ L of 2 M HCl was added in 5 mL of anhydrous ethanol and slow addition of 350  $ \mu $ L of titanium isopropoxide) at 4000 rpm for 40 s, followed by annealing at 130  $ ^{\circ} $ C for 10 min and 500  $ ^{\circ} $ C for 30 min. After spin-coating the perovskite layer, the films were annealed at 100  $ ^{\circ} $ C for 60 min over a hot plate. The HTMs were spin-coated onto the perovskite layer at 3000 rpm for 40 s. Spiro-OMeTAD (72.3 mg/mL) was dissolved in chlorobenzene doped with 28.8  $ \mu $ L of 4-tert-butylpyridine (t-BP) and 17.7  $ \mu $ L of lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) (520 mg in 1

mL of acetonitrile). TPDCN (20 mg/mL) and TTz-1 (20 mg/mL) were dissolved in chlorobenzene doped with 7.50   $  \mu  $  L of LiTFSI and 12.0   $  \mu  $  L of t-BP. Finally, gold electrodes were deposited, 80 nm thick, by thermal evaporation to complete the fabrication of the solar cells (device area: 0.06 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ).

Hole Mobility Measurements. The hole mobility of synthesized HTMs was measured using the device configuration ITO/PEDOT:PSS/HTMs/Ag. The ITO substrate was patterned using Zn dust and 2 M HCl solution. Further, it was cleaned in an ultrasonic bath using DI water, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol (IPA). The PEDOT:PSS solution was prepared by using a 1:1 ratio of water and PEDOT:PSS solution and was coated at 2000 rpm for 1 min followed by annealing of films at 150 °C for 10 min. The HTM solution was coated over the PEDOT:PSS film, followed by annealing at 120 °C for 10 min. A Ag electrode of ~100 nm thickness was thermally evaporated.
