# A Scalable Integrated Dopant-Free Heterostructure to Stabilize Perovskite Solar Cell Modules

Yongming Sha, Enbing Bi,  $  ^{*}  $  Yao Zhang, Pengbin Ru, Weiyu Kong, Peng Zhang, Xudong Yang, Han Chen,  $  ^{*}  $  and Liyuan Han

Perovskite solar cell (PSC) modules employing a hole transport layer (HTL) without unstable dopants possess high potential for improving operational stability. However, the low efficiencies of the devices greatly limit their commercial applications owing to the lower efficacy of the dopant-free HTL, introduced by the unintentional n-doping effect of volatile ions from the halide-rich perovskite surface. Here, a scalable heterostructure integrated by a methylammonium-free perovskite film with an iodide-rich surface, an ultrathin interlayer of bridge-jointed graphene oxide nanosheets (BJ-GO), and an HTL without additional ionic dopants is developed. In this heterostructure, the iodide ions are physically immobilized by the compact 2D network, and lead defects are chemically passivated by multiple coordination bonds. Moreover, the BJ-GO with tunable surface energy enables a highly ordered HTL a considerably improved carrier mobility by an order of magnitude. Finally, the PSC module with an area of   $  35.80 \, cm^2  $   employing this heterostructure shows a certified efficiency of 15.3%. The encapsulated PSC modules retain over 91% of initial efficiency after the damp heat test at   $  85 \, ^{\circ}C  $   and   $  \approx85\%  $   relative humidity for 1000 h, while maintaining 90% of the initial value for 1000 h at the maximum power point under continuous 1-Sun illumination at   $  60 \, ^{\circ}C  $  .

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs), in which the perovskite light-absorbing layer is sandwiched between the electron transport layer (ETL) and hole transport layer (HTL), are one of the most promising photovoltaic (PV) technologies. This strategy effectively reduced recombination at the perovskite/HTL interface and enabled a PSC with a certified efficiency of up to 22.7%.  $  ^{[21]}  $   Despite the significant achievement in the efficiency of PSCs based on dopant-free HTLs, there is a ubiquitous issue about the longevity of perovskite PV, i.e., halide ions in perovskite still can diffuse into HTLs to weaken the p-type semiconducting properties of the HTL. However, the effects of halide ions on dopant-free HTLs have rarely been studied and settled, especially in the complicated PSC modules. Moreover, although the use of a 2D network (such as graphene oxide, molybdenum disulfide, etc.) between perovskite and HTL is a considerable strategy for inhibiting ion migration from damaging the HTL,  $  ^{[28-32]}  $   it remains challenging to construct a robust and large-area heterostructure without additional ionic dopants for efficient and stable PSC modules.

Herein, we developed a fully solution-processed scalable heterostructure integrated by a methylammonium-free perovskite with an iodide-rich surface, an ultrathin bridge-jointed graphene oxide (BJ-GO) interlayer, and a dopant-free HTL. Through chemical suturing technology, a large-area 2D network of BJ-GO exhibited high coverage on the surface of the perovskite, effectively inhibited the migration of halide ions into the HTL and passivated lead defects. Additionally, an ordered HTL with an improved mobility of  $ 2.29 \times 10^{-2} \mathrm{~cm}^2 \mathrm{~V}^{-1} \mathrm{~s}^{-1} $  was constructed on the BJ-GO interlayer. Employing dopant-free poly[(9,9-dioctylfluorenyl-2,7-diyl)-co-(4,4'-(N-(4-sec-butylphenyl)diphenylamine)] (TFB) as the HTL, the PSC module with an area of  $ 35.80 \mathrm{~cm}^2 $  achieved a certified PCE of  $ 15.3\% $  with negligible hysteresis. Moreover, the encapsulated PSC module achieved over  $ 91\% $ , and  $ 90\% $  of its initial efficiency after the damp heat test (at  $ 85^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $  and  $ 85\% $  relative humidity (RH) for  $ 1000 \mathrm{~h} $ ) and the light-exposure test (at the maximum power point under continuous 1-Sun illumination at  $ 60^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $  for  $ 1000 \mathrm{~h} $ ), respectively.

We deposited the scalable BJ-GO nanosheets on the surface of perovskite to construct a robust 2D hetero-interface between perovskite and dopant-free HTL. However, the unmodified GO normally prefers to spread on perovskite surface partly with the isolated nanosheets.  $  ^{[30]}  $   To realize the large-area coverage of this BJ-GO interlayer, we employed hydrophobic siloxanes to chemically suture the isolated GO nanosheets. Three types of siloxanes, namely, tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), (3-mercaptopropyl) triethoxysilane (MPTES), and 3-aminopropyl triethoxysilane (APTES) with alkyl   $  (-CH_{3})  $  , sulfhydryl   $  (-SH)  $  , and amine   $  (-NH_{2})  $   groups (Figure S1, Supporting Information), respectively, were selected to join the isolated GO nanosheets for full coverage of the large-area perovskite surface (Figure 1a). From the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of TEOS-GO, MPTES-GO, and APTES-GO (Figure S2, Supporting Information), we found that the APTES-GO shows a uniform 2D network. The TEOS and MPTES suturing agents result in an isolated and overlapped distribution of GO nanosheets, respectively. After depositing these on the perovskite, we found that the APTES-GO formed a high-coverage, smooth, thin network on the perovskite surface (Figure 1b,c). The TEOS-GO showed discontinuous coverage, while the MPTES-GO showed uneven and overlapping

morphology on perovskite. In addition, the APTES-GO film on the perovskite exhibited a lower roughness and narrower height distribution, which could enable a more uniform potential distribution than that of the TEOS-GO and MPTES-GO films (Figure S3, Supporting Information and Figure 1c,d). As shown in Figure 1d, the surface potential of perovskite was uniform and its average value was 0.53 V. Due to full coverage of GO on the perovskite surface in APTES-GO, the surface of APTES-GO/perovskite showed a uniformly distributed surface potential and its average value was 0.14 V. However, the TEOS-GO/perovskite and MPTES-GO/perovskite show nonuniform dark and bright regions in their surface potential mapping images, indicating their inhomogenous distribution on perovskite surface. This indicates that the effective chemical suturing technology for BJ-GO contributes to its full coverage on the perovskite surface on an ultrathin scale. To further demonstrate the uniformity of these films on a large scale, we deposited these interlayers on perovskite with a size of   $  8.0 \times 8.0  $   cm (Figure S4a, Supporting Information). We quantified the mapping distribution of surface potentials on this mini-module scale film by Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM, Figure S4b, Supporting Information). The standard deviation of the surface potentials of the APTES-GO films was only 0.45%, which is much lower than that of TEOS-GO (4.17%) and MPTES-GO (10.06%). This reveals that APTES-GO can realize homogenous surface potentials of perovskite, ensuring its accordant Fermi energy level for carrier extraction.

We further characterized the chemical interactions between these suture agents and GO by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). As shown in Figure 2a, a new absorption peak at 1600 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  in the APTES-GO corresponding to the amine (N–H) group of APTES was observed. This indicates that the GO nanosheets were linked by covalent C–N bonds, which were formed by the condensation reaction between the amine groups of APTES and the hydroxyl groups of GO.  $  ^{[33]}  $  Moreover, there was no significant change in the oxygen-containing groups in TEOS-GO (Figure S5a, Supporting Information), indicating that there was no interaction between GO and TEOS. Meanwhile, the peak of the sulfhydryl (-SH) group from MPTES was eliminated in the MPTES-GO and the peak intensity of the epoxy group (C–O, 1222 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  ) from GO was reduced in the MPTES-GO (Figure S5b, Supporting Information). This was due to the nucleophilic addition reaction between the sulfhydryl group of MPTES and the in-plane epoxy group of GO.  $  ^{[34]}  $  Moreover, as shown in XPS (Figure 2b), the C 1s core level of GO exhibited three peaks representing different component species: C–C (284.70 eV), C–O–C/C–O (286.92 eV), and O=C–O (288.14 eV).  $  ^{[35]}  $  However, the peak intensities of the C–O–C/C–O and O=C–O in the APTES-GO were substantially reduced and a new C 1s peak at 285.41 eV corresponding to C–N group appeared, which was consistent with the FTIR results. In addition, the intensity of the N 1s peak at 400.93 eV corresponding to the N–C group in the APTES-GO increased than that in the APTES (Figure 2c). This further confirms the formation of covalent bonds between APTES and GO. The amine groups in the APTES can react with the oxygen-containing functional groups of GO, and generate the C–N covalent bonds to bridge the GO nanosheets.

To further confirm the structural effect of these modifiers on GO, we performed X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements. As shown in Figure S6 (Supporting Information), the   $  2\theta  $   of the TEOS-GO layers occurred at   $  10.92^{\circ}  $   corresponding to the (002) peak position of GO,  $  ^{[36]}  $   while that of the MPTES-GO and APTES-GO layers occurred at   $  7.74^{\circ}  $   and   $  12.16^{\circ}  $  , respectively. The XRD results indicated that the d-spacing of (002) in MPTES-GO was enlarged, possibly due to the insertion of MPTES into the interlamination of the GO nanosheets. Meanwhile, the d-spacing in APTES-GO was reduced, which was beneficial for constructing a smooth interface layer. Density functional theory calculations were performed to simulate the bonding interactions between suture agents and GO. The theoretical models showed that the bonding of APTES and GO was almost horizontal (  $  168.82^{\circ}  $  ), and that of MPTES and GO was almost vertical (  $  100.52^{\circ}  $  ) (Figure S7, Supporting Information). This indicates that APTES-GO prefers to distribute horizontally on the perovskite surface, while MPTES-GO shows an overlapping distribution, which corresponds to the results of scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM).

Therefore, we can deduce that the TEOS with alkyl  $ (\mathrm{-CH}_3) $  group, has no interaction with GO; thus, it cannot suture the isolated GO into integrated intralayer (Figure S8a, Supporting Information). The MPTES with sulfhydryl (-SH) groups can stick GO nanosheets through covalent bonding between the sulfhydryl group of MPTES and the in-plane epoxy groups of GO (Figure S8b, Supporting Information), which can stack GO nanosheets in the vertical direction layer by layer. This might lead to the overlapping and uneven surface of MPTES-GO on the perovskite surface. However, the APTES with amine  $ (\mathrm{-NH}_2) $  group can form a bridge-joint GO through strong covalent bonding between the amine groups of APTES and the oxygen-containing groups at the edge of GO (Figure 2d), which facilitates the spread of GO nanosheets with a large-area 2D network.

In addition, we also studied the effect of the BJ-GO on the photoexcited charge carriers transfer and recombination dynamics via steady-state and time-resolved photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy. The steady-state PL intensity was higher in the BJ-GO/perovskite film than in the sole perovskite film, indicating reduced surface defects on the perovskite surface as a result of the passivation of undercoordinated lead defects by the coordination bonds of C–O–Pb and Si–O–Pb (Figure S18a, Supporting Information). Surface passivation of perovskite film by wide-bandgap BJ-GO layer plays a major role in improving PL intensity of BJ-GO/perovskite. The BJ-GO/perovskite film exhibited an average carrier lifetime of 901.5 ns, which was longer than 285.3 ns obtained for the sole perovskite film (Figure S18b, Supporting Information). Moreover, after the aging test, the carrier exaction time of the HTL (TFB)/BJ-GO/perovskite film was 4.3 ns and that of the HTL/perovskite film increased to 15.8 ns, consistent with the result of PL spectroscopy (Figure S18c,d, Supporting Information). These results indicate that the BJ-GO has a distinct capability to reduce the recombination loss and maintain efficient charge extraction at the heterojunction between perovskite and HTL, which is important for high-efficiency perovskite photovoltaics.

To further study the performance of this scalable integrated dopant-free heterostructure in the PSC modules, we fabricated devices with the structure of fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)/ETL/perovskite/BJ-GO/HTL/Cr/Au (Figure S19, Supporting Information). We employed MA-free   $  Cs_{1-x}FA_{x}Pb(I_{y}Cl_{1-y})_{3}  $   as the perovskite absorber, which was more thermally stable than the MA-based perovskite.  $  ^{[46]}  $   To further improve the stability of device, a bilayer of   $  TiO_{2}  $   (50 nm)/  $  SnO_{2}  $   (20 nm) as the ETL and a metal electrode of Cr (5 nm)/Au (100 nm) were also adopted in our devices.  $  ^{[5]}  $   The thickness of the BJ-GO film was optimized to ≈8.0 nm. We first employed a PSC with a small size of 0.1 cm  $  ^{2}  $   to optimize the dopant-free HTL.

We demonstrated the universality of the BJ-GO enabled dopant-free heterostructure for application on the PSC. Three types of prototypical organic hole transport materials including

triphenylamine-based small molecules (spiro-OMeTAD and DM), thiophene-based polymers (P3DDT and PCDTBT), and triphenylamine-based polymers (poly-TPD and TFB), were chosen to study the effect of the BJ-GO on the photovoltaic performance of PSCs (Figure S20, Supporting Information).  $  ^{[47-50]}  $   These organic materials act as HTLs with a HOMO level variation from -5.1 to -5.4 eV (Figure 4a and Figure S21, Supporting Information). We observed enhanced device performance and higher values of   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF for all organic HTLs upon insertion of the BJ-GO layer (Figure 4b and Figure S22 and Table S3, Supporting Information). Especially, the PSC with a heterostructure integrated by   $  Cs_{1-x}FA_{x}Pb(I_{y}Cl_{1-y})_{3}  $  , BJ-GO, and TFB, exhibited a high PCE of 21.75% with a   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.12 V,   $  J_{SC}  $   of 24.12 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and FF of 80.51% (Figure 4c). This was much higher than that of the control device without BJ-GO protection, showing a reverse scanned PCE of 18.42%, with a   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.04 V, a   $  J_{SC}  $   of 23.89 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and an FF of 74.14%. For a device with dopant-free HTL based on MA-free perovskite, the efficiency of 21.75% is the highest efficiency reported. In addition, the BJ-GO-based device possesses a higher   $  V_{OC}  $  , FF, and PCE than that of the TEOS-GO and MPTES-GO-based devices (Figure S23 and Table S4, Supporting Information). The Mott–Schottky curves of the PSCs indicated that the device with BJ-GO had a higher flat-band potential than the one without BJ-GO, resulting in an improvement in   $  V_{OC}  $   (Figure S24, Supporting Information). Moreover, the J-V hysteresis of the PSC with BJ-GO decreased significantly (Figure 4c), and the same phenomenon was observed for other dopant-free HTL (Figure S22 and Table S3, Supporting Information). The statistics of the PCE histogram of the BJ-GO-based PSCs confirmed the high reproducibility of BJ-GO (Figure 4d). The incident photo-to-electron conversion efficiency of the device with BJ-GO is shown in Figure S25 (Supporting Information) and the integrated   $  J_{SC}  $   was calculated to be 24.36 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , which matched well with that obtained with the I-V measurement. In addition, a steady-state current density of 22.33 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   and a stabilized PCE of 21.38% were achieved by maintaining the voltage at the maximum power point for the device with the BJ-GO (Figure S26, Supporting Information).

Although these devices based on different HTLs also showed an enhanced   $  V_{OC}  $  , the highest PCE was still obtained using TFB as the HTL (Table S3, Supporting Information). In particular, compared with other HTLs, TFB not only have a high glass transition temperature (  $  T_{g} > 200\ ^{\circ}C  $  ), but also high conductivity (  $  \sigma: 5.52 \times 10^{-3}\ S\ m^{-1}  $  ) and hole mobility (  $  \mu: 1.18 \times 10^{-3}\ cm^{2}\ V^{-1}\ s^{-1}  $  ) (Figure S27, Supporting Information) along with well-matched energy levels (  $  E_{LUMO}: -2.39\ eV; E_{f}: -4.68\ eV; E_{HOMO}: -5.40\ eV  $  ) (Figure 4a and Table S5, Supporting Information), which are beneficial for good hole transport properties and excellent thermal stability.  $  ^{[48]}  $   Additionally, we found that the TFB layer showed a more ordered morphology in the HTL/BJ-GO/perovskite sample rather than a flat morphology on the surface of perovskite (Figure 4e,f and Figure S28a,b, Supporting Information), which had influence on carrier transporting of HTL. We successfully developed a chemical suturing strategy to develop a scalable BJ-GO 2D network sandwiched between methylammonium-free perovskite and a dopant-free HTL, which was integrated into a robust dopant-free heterostructure.

Through ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy and PL spectroscopy, the BJ-GO was demonstrated to effectively maintain p-type conduction of the dopant-free HTL by significantly suppressing ion migration and nonradiative charge recombination in this heterostructure. Notably, by employing dopant-free TFB, the PSC modules with an aperture area of   $  35.80 \, cm^2  $   achieved a certified efficiency of 15.3%. Moreover, 91% of the PSC module's efficiency was maintained for 1000 h under damp heat test at   $  85 \, ^{\circ}C  $   and   $  \approx85\%  $   RH; in addition, 90% of its efficiency was maintained for 1000 h at the maximum power point under continuous 1-Sun illumination at   $  60 \, ^{\circ}C  $   in ambient air. Our study highlights a new strategy of 2D networks for scalable interfacial engineering and paves the way for stable perovskite photovoltaics.
