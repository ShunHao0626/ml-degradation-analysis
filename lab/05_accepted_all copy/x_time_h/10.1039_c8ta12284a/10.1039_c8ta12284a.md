# Surface Modification via Self-Assembling Large Cation for Improved Performance and Modulated Hysteresis of Perovskite Solar Cells

# Abstract

Hybrid organic-inorganic metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been regarded as a low-cost, high-efficiency photovoltaic technology, however their device performance seriously depends on interfacial properties between each contact layer. Interface plays an important role in PSCs as they induce defects, trap states and charge recombination sites etc., which are detrimental to the device performance and stability. Herein, a post-treatment of self-assembling large cation tert-butylammonium (tBA) on the surface of perovskite layer is demonstrated as an effective strategy to passivate interface and grain boundary defects and thus to improve device performance and stability. Particularly, a normal hysteresis has been turned into an invert hysteresis with the increasing amount of tert-butylammonium iodide (tBAI) treatment, which can be ascribed to modified interface states. An impressive power conversion efficiency (PCE) over 20% with a significant suppressed hysteresis is achieved via the careful control of post-treatment conditions, which shows great promise for commercialization in future.

Keywords: perovskite solar cells, large cation, hysteresis, stability

carrier mobility.  $  ^{1-5}  $   The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs experiences at rapid development during the past decade and has been boosted up to 23%. This is realized by various improvements such as fabrication process development, component modulation, interface passivation and charge transporting material optimization.  $  ^{6-12}  $   However, their commercialization is still restricted by the difficulty of upscaling fabrication, as well as insufficient long-term stability.

The presence of surface defect in perovskite polycrystalline thin films is a serious problem for large scale fabrication of perovskite devices.  $  ^{13}  $   These surface states will facilitate non-radiative charge recombination at the interfaces which are harmful to device efficiency and also initiate the degradation of perovskite structures.  $  ^{14}  $   Basically, passivation of defects in perovskite is proved to be an effective engineering method for improving device performance of PSCs.  $  ^{14, 15}  $   To date, various passivation additives such as small molecules, polymers, metal ions, cations, anions, etc. have been employed to passivate the defects at surface, bulk or gain boundary in perovskite.  $  ^{16-19}  $   Chiang et al. utilized the phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PC  $  _{61}  $  BM) to fill pinholes and vacancies between perovskite grains and achieved high-quality perovskite layer.  $  ^{20}  $   Abdi-Jalebi et al. and our previous report both indicated that potassium ions can efficiently passivate the perovskite interface and result in the elimination of hysteresis.  $  ^{17, 21}  $   In addition, Yang et al. also suggested that the addition of iodide decreases the concentration of deep-level defects of perovskite and consequently achieves a certified PCE of 22.1%.  $  ^{6}  $

Selectively growth of two-dimensional (2D) stacking layers such as n-butylammonium (BA),  $  ^{22}  $   phenylethylammonium (PEA),  $  ^{23}  $   and octylammonium (OA),  $  ^{24}  $   etc. onto three-dimension (3D) perovskites have been widely applied to improve stability of PSCs. However, the introduced long chains may inevitably impede charge injection from perovskite material to electron transport layer (ETL) or hole transport layer (HTL). A solution based post-treatment to induce a secondary growth of perovskite via an Oswald ripening process has been a promising way to passivate the perovskite defects and improve the device stability. Yang et al. demonstrated a post-treatment that spin-coats the MABr solution onto perovskite to improve its film quality and thus enhance efficiency and stability.  $  ^{25}  $   In addition, Luo et al. used a large cation of (guanidinium) GA to reduce surface traps, mitigate nonradiative recombination. An increase of 100 mV in   $  V_{oc}  $   and a record efficiency over 21% for inverted PSCs were achieved after surface passivation.  $  ^{26}  $   Pham et al. also reported to passivate

the perovskite surface by using GA thiocyanate assisted Ostwald ripening growth.  $  ^{27}  $

Herein, we demonstrate a post-treatment of using a novel large cation of tert-butylammonium (tBA) to modify the as-deposited  $ \mathrm{Cs_{0.05}(FA_{0.85}MA_{0.15})_{0.95}Pb(I_{0.85}Br_{0.15})_3} $  (CsFAMA) perovskite films. The post-treatment by using tert-butylammonium iodide (tBAI) solution is proved to effectively reduce the trap density at the surface of perovskite layer. Accordingly, the post-treated device shows an improved PCE of  $ 20.12\% $  compared to the reference device  $ (19.32\%) $ . In addition, the common hysteresis phenomenon has also been suppressed after the tBAI treatment. Our results suggest the post-treatment with large cation to be great potential for obtaining high quality perovskite layers and further commercialization of PSCs.

# Results and discussion

The schematic representation of the post-treatment of tBAI/ isopropanol (IPA) solution on the surface of perovskite films is showed in Fig. 1a. The CsFAMA perovskite films were firstly deposited on the FTO/SnO  $  _{2}  $  substrate using a green anti-solvent of ethyl acetate. After annealing treatment, the tBAI/ IPA solution were then spun onto the perovskite surface, followed by a re-annealing process. The perovskite films treated with tBAI solution show darker color compared with the pristine film, as seen from Fig. S1, which indicates the formation of self-assembling layer after post-treatment. The tBA cation is an isomeride of BA, which possesses a shorter chain length but its size is still larger than FA cation. Because it is slightly above the upper limit of the tolerance factor, tBA cations can be considered as partial substituent in organic cations, to form a 3D perovskite structure. As the BA cation can induce a 2D structure perovskite film, a layered surface structure appeared obviously even when a low concentration of BAI/isopropanol solution was employed (Fig. S2). However, perovskite films treated with the tBA cation show similar morphology compared to the 3D perovskite films even with the high concentration (5 mg/mL) of tBAI/isopropanol solution (Fig. 1b). Besides, it can be also seen that with the increased amount of tBAI treatment, the PbI  $  _{2}  $  fragments are reduced obviously and purer and more uniform perovskite films are obtained. Therefore, the small amount of tBA may be incorporate into the perovskite surface 3D structure. However, when the perovskite surface is treated with a high concentration of 10 mg/mL, new phase occurs at the grain boundary which can be ascribed to the formation of tBAPbX  $  _{3}  $  (X=Br,I) due to the large tBA cation.  $  ^{28}  $

To further investigate the microstructure of perovskite films, the X-ray diffraction (XRD) was conducted to compare the films before and after tBAI post-treatment (Fig. 2a). There is no new diffraction peak observed in perovskite films treated with a lower concentration of tBAI solution. In contrast, some new diffraction peaks corresponding to the 2D layered perovskite emerge in the perovskite films treated with BAI solution, as shown in Fig. S3. In addition, the residual PbI $ _2 $  peak diminishes after the tBAI post-treatment, and the typical peak (110) of the perovskite film exhibits an obvious left-shift as a function of the tBAI concentration (Fig. 2b), indicating that the small amount of tBAI has reacted with PbI $ _2 $  and incorporated into 3D perovskite structure, as shown in the schematic in Fig. 3a. When further increases the amount of tBAI to 20 mg/mL, an obvious peak at  $ \sim $ 7.2° belonging to the 1D tBAPb(I $ _x $ Br $ _{(1-x)} $ ) $ _3 $  compound appears (Fig S4). Furthermore, the grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) was also conducted to probe the crystallinity and orientation of the perovskite films with and without surface modification. (Fig. 3b-d). $ ^{29} $  All diffraction patterns exhibit clear isotropic rings, which mean the random orientation of perovskite crystal grains. The BA treated film (Fig. 3c) shows discrete Bragg spots in the out-of-plane (OOP) direction indicating the formation of 2D perovskite phase. However, there is no new scattering peak observed in the tBAI treated perovskite film (Fig. 3d). The tBAI treated perovskite film shows more cambered scattering signals compared to the pristine films (Fig. 3b) which indicate an enhanced crystallinity without significantly changing the orientation of crystal grains. The X-ray diffraction results also confirm the incorporation of the large tBA cation into the perovskite surface crystal.

In addition, optical properties of the pristine and tBAI treated perovskite films are investigated as shown in Fig. 4a. To show the difference of the spectra of the perovskites, the enlarged image is showed in Fig. 4b, where a slight red-shift is found with more tBAI treated. The slight red-shift of UV-Vis spectra could be ascribed to the introduced iodide by the tBAI post-treatment, which is also confirmed by the corresponding PL spectra (Fig. 4c). This post-treatment process has been proved to be an effective method of suppressing the surface and grain boundary defects with enhanced carrier lifetime.  $  ^{30-32}  $   The time resolved PL (TRPL) spectra of perovskite films treated with different concentration of the tBAI solution are presented in Fig. 4d. Notably, a significantly increased PL lifetime is observed in the perovskite films after proper tBAI surface treatment, indicating the reduced defects and suppressed charge recombination (for the lifetime fitting details please refer to

To characterize the impact of post-treatment of the tBAI on the perovskite device performance. PSCs are fabricated with a structure of FTO/ SnO $ _2 $ / PCBM/ CsFAMA/ Spiro-OMeTAD/ Au, as shown in Fig. 5a. Fig. 5b shows the corresponding cross-section SEM image of the tBAI treated device with the different layers of (i) FTO, (ii) compact SnO $ _2 $ / PCBM, (iii) CsFAMA perovskite, (iv) Spiro-OMeTAD, and (v) Au, respectively. The photovoltaic characteristics and the distribution of  $ V_{oc} $ ,  $ J_{sc} $ , fill factor (FF) and PCE from 15 cells for the pristine and tBAI treated devices are plotted in Fig. 5c, and the corresponding detailed parameters are showed in Table S1. All the devices are tested with both reverse scan and forward scan under 1 Sun AM 1.5G illumination with an aperture area of 0.16 cm $ ^2 $ . It exhibits an increase in  $ V_{oc} $  from 1.069 V for the pristine cell to 1.090 V for the champion treated cell with 3 mg/mL of tBAI solution under reverse scan (RS), and more obvious  $ V_{oc} $  increase is observed under forward scan (FS) from 1.019 V to 1.083 V. This increased  $ V_{oc} $  can be attributed to the surface passivation after the tBAI post-treatment. $ ^{33,35} $  The incorporation of large cation into the perovskite film minimizes surface defects, thus suppressing the charge recombination on the interface. The average  $ J_{sc} $  experiences an increase after the proper tBAI treatment, confirmed by the corresponding EQE spectra in Fig. S5. The increased current density can be the result of the reduced PbI $ _2 $  residues and slightly enlarged absorption range. A significant increase in FF and a reduced hysteresis are also found in the device with the proper tBAI post-treatment. However, with the further increase of the tBAI concentration, the  $ V_{oc} $  and  $ J_{sc} $  of the devices dramatically decrease. As the concentration of tBAI solution is above 3 mg/ml, the tBAI can not fully reacts with perovskite film. The residual tBAI on the surface of perovskite and the formation of the pure tBAPbX $ _3 $  phase exert negative effects on the charge injection from perovskite film to hole transport material. In addition, FF under RS decreases much faster than that under FS. An invert hysteresis is found in the higher concentration of tBAI treated devices. The hysteresis index (HI) of different amount of tBAI treated devices is plotted in Fig. 5d, and their corresponding typical  $ J-V $  curves are shown in Fig. S7. A significant decrease in hysteresis after the proper tBAI treatment can be ascribed to the

improved film quality and reduced surface defects. The mechanism for the invert hysteresis after excess tBAI treatment is still under investigation. Some reports consider the invert hysteresis can be ascribed to the charge/ion accumulation and recombination at interfaces in the PSC.  $  ^{36, 37}  $   Thus it may be caused by the barrier of the unreacted tBAI residuals.

The photovoltaic characteristics of the champion devices with and without tBAI post-treatment are listed in Table 1, and the typical  $  J - V  $  curves are shown in Fig. 6a. The tBAI treated champion device has achieved a high PCE of  $  20.12\%  $ , with a  $  V_{\mathrm{oc}}  $  of  $  1.128\mathrm{V}  $  for RS and  $  1.124\mathrm{V}  $  for FS, a  $  J_{\mathrm{sc}}  $  of  $  22.78\mathrm{mA/cm^2}  $  for RS and  $  22.77\mathrm{mA/cm^2}  $  for FS, a FF of 0.78 for RS and 0.79 for FS. The pristine device without treatment shows a lower PCE of  $  19.32\%  $  and a larger hysteresis index (HI) of 0.09. Fig. 6b shows EQE spectra of corresponding perovskite devices. The integrated photocurrent densities are  $  21.90\mathrm{mA/cm^2}  $  and  $  21.81\mathrm{mA/cm^2}  $  for perovskite devices with and without post-treatment, respectively, which perfectly match with the  $  J_{\mathrm{sc}}  $  measured from the  $  J - V  $  curves. The corresponding steady-state output current densities at the maximum power point of the devices are shown in Fig. 6c. The tBAI treated perovskite device not only shows a very stable power output but also an extremely fast photo-response compared to devices without post-treatment. This rapid photo-response reflects more efficient charge extraction in the post-treated devices compared to pristine. Furthermore, the long-term stability of the tBAI treated devices is also remarkably enhanced compared to the pristine devices as devices are stored under ambient air conditions with  $  20\mathrm{RH}\%  $  without encapsulation over 1000 hours (Fig. 6d). Therefore, the application of a post-treatment method for passivating perovskite absorber has greatly improved both PCE and stability of perovskite solar cells.

Interface plays an important role in charge recombination.  $  ^{[16, 38]}  $   To further understand the charge recombination mechanism after post-treatment, light intensity dependent measurements are carried out on these devices as well. The open circuit voltage is dependent on the light intensity with a kT/q slope as shown in Fig. 7a. The PSC with interface passivation shows a slope of 1.43 kT/q, which is much lower than the non-passivated device (1.68 kT/q). It indicates a substantially reduced trap-assisted recombination. We also measured photocurrent density under different light intensities to study bimolecular recombination under the short circuit condition (Fig. 7b). The current density of both devices shows a linear dependence on the light intensity in logarithmic coordinates with a slope

of 1.0, indicate negligible bimolecular recombination. Fig. 7c illustrates the Mott-Schottky (M=S) plots of these perovskite solar cells at a proper frequency of 10 kHz.  $  ^{39}  $   The post-treated PSC presents increased build-in potential (  $  V_{bi}  $  ) of 0.93 V compared with 0.91 V for the non-treated device, which indicates fast charge collection and transportation. In addition, the space-charge-limited current (SCLC) technique is also employed to evaluate the electron trap densities of perovskite films with or without tBAI treatment. The typical J-V curves of the electron only devices with an architecture of FTO/PCBM/perovskite/PCBM/Au were tested under dark as shown in Fig. S8. The calculated electron trap densities of perovskite films after tBAI treatment is decreased from   $  1.79 \times 10^{-16}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $   to   $  1.66 \times 10^{-16}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  , indicating the reduced defects with tBAI treatment.

In addition, we also conducted the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurement, which is considered as a useful technique to reveal underlying carrier transport behaviors in the PSC.  $  ^{40,41}  $   The Nyquist plots of the devices with and without interface passivation are obtained under AM 1.5G with an applied bias voltage of 1.0 V which is close to its   $  V_{oc}  $  , as shown in Fig. 6d. The high frequency component is related to the charge transport resistance (  $  R_{ct}  $  ) of cells, and the low-frequency component corresponds to the charge recombination resistance (  $  R_{rec}  $  ) occurring at the interfaces.  $  ^{42-44}  $   The main difference between these devices is the interface between perovskite and HTL layers. Therefore, the slightly lowered   $  R_{ct}  $   (23   $  \Omega  $  cm  $  ^{2}  $  ) obtained by numerical fitting of the tBAI treated device compare to the pristine device (24   $  \Omega  $  cm  $  ^{2}  $  ) indicates fast charge extraction occurs at the passivated interface. The numerical fitted   $  R_{rec}  $   (20   $  \Omega  $  cm  $  ^{2}  $  ) is much higher compared to the pristine devices (8   $  \Omega  $  cm  $  ^{2}  $  ), which demonstrates less recombination after interface passivation. In addition, the fitted   $  R_{ct}  $   and   $  R_{rec}  $   are plotted with respect to the different applied forward biases as shown in Fig. 6e and Fig. 6f, respectively. The value of   $  R_{ct}  $   in the interface passivated perovskite is decreased as a function of applied voltage. The   $  R_{rec}  $   is much higher compared to the pristine one even at different bias voltages. Therefore, the EIS data clearly shows the improved charge transport and reduced recombination in perovskite devices with the interface tBAI passivation.

# Conclusion

In summary, we provide a post-treatment via self-assembling large cation of tBA on the perovskite surface, which not only improves the device performance but also enhances the stability. This post-treatment enables an incorporation of large cation into the perovskite crystal structure and thus

Journal of Materials Chemistry A

passivates the surface and grain boundary defects. The proper treatment of tBAI solution  $  ^{10}  $  on perovskite surface contributes an impressive power conversion efficiency (PCE) approaching 20.12%, which is much higher compared to the non-treated device (19.32%). It also shows a suppressed hysteresis compared to the pristine devices, which can be ascribed to the fast charge transport and reduced charge recombination. Therefore, this simple post-treatment strategy shows great promise for future commercial application.
