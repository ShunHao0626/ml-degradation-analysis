# ARTICLEINFO

Graphene has shown many advantages over the metal oxide transparent materials that serve as conventional electrodes in solar cells because graphene is more transparent, has greater stability, and is more mechanically flexible. Flexibility and semi-transparency of the perovskite solar cells are challenged to integrate with the flexible electronic devices since the perovskite solar cells have discovered. Herein, we provide the first report of transfer-free, large-scale monolayer graphene employed as a transparent and flexible bottom electrode. High-quality graphene without transfer process was directly synthesized at   $  150^{\circ}  $  C on a polymer substrate via plasma assisted thermal chemical vapor deposition (PATCVD). Additionally, a highly transparent AZO/Ag/AZO (AAA) multilayer was utilized as a top counter electrode to create semi-transparent perovskite solar cells with a remarkable degree of mechanical flexibility. The 300 nm-thick perovskite solar cells with PATCVD-Graphene revealed a high transmittance of   $  \sim26\%  $   at a wavelength of 700 nm. The highest level of power conversion efficiency (PCE) (  $  \sim14.2\%  $  ) was recorded by an illumination from the bottom graphene side. After 1000 bending cycles under a tensile strain of 1.5%, the graphene-based devices maintained a level of PCE that was more than 90% greater than the initial reading. This superior bending robustness highlights the potential for non-transfer, graphene-based, perovskite photovoltaic material to establish a tandem structure for a foldable solar cell.

# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic metal halide perovskite has attracted worldwide attention in arenas of both academia and industry, and is recognized as a promising candidate for the synthesis of photoactive materials for the coming photovoltaic generation. Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) are primarily responsible for the rapid enhancement in power conversion efficiency, which has increased from 3.8 to  $ 25.2\% $  in the past few years [1-5]. This value approaches the efficiency of commercialized Si solar cells. Further investigation is necessary, therefore, in order to realize and apply actual PSC devices.

The high efficiency achieved by PSCs stems from unique optoelectronic properties of the active material that include a tunable bandgap, a high absorption coefficient, and low levels of excitation binding energy   $  [6]  $  . Interestingly, one attractive advantage of PSCs over conventional silicon for device fabrication is the capability for low-temperature processing, which creates opportunities for the development of solar panels with features such as flexibility, customizable shapes, and transparency   $  [7–11]  $  . Therefore, a variety of bendable transparent electrodes have been studied for use in flexible solar cells that include

An ideal flexible transparent electrode must have a high level of transparency, a low sheet resistance, robust chemical stability, and a high degree of durability against mechanical deformation. Graphene is one of the best candidates to fulfill these requirements due to its high transparency, high flexibility, and high electrical conductivity [15–18]. Large-scale graphene synthesis, however, requires processing temperatures as high as 1000 °C or more, which requires a transfer process to polymer substrates. With such high-temperature processing, structural inhomogeneities, such as wrinkles and ripples within graphene film upon the transfer of a free-standing graphene layer to a flexible substrate, which degrades the physical and electrical properties of functional electrodes [19]. Transfer-graphene could possibly meet the flexibility requirements, but these are limited in their usefulness for high-performance applications due to high levels of sheet resistance that are due to defects in crystals, to wrinkles, and to the grain and domain sizes. For these reasons, many studies of graphene have focused on lowering the sheet resistance via various forms of doping with chemicals and plasma, as well as photochemical methodologies. Therefore, a direct synthesis of graphene with no transfer at low temperature onto a polyestersulfone (PES) substrate can be used to provide high flexibility and transmittance. Our group recently reported an advanced technique for defect-free graphene synthesis directly onto a flexible substrate at 150 °C via chemical vapor deposition [20]. In the present work, monolayer graphene films were assessed for their utility as a transparent bottom electrode for super-flexible perovskite solar cells. Furthermore, graphene-based flexible PSCs were found to be light in weight due to the lack of a heavy glass sheet, which led to the creation of transparent or semi-transparent devices that can absorb light from both sides. This development shows promise for special applications such as wearable electronics, photovoltaic curtains, building-integrated photovoltaics, and combinations with other cells to form tandem structures. The excellent electrical and optical properties of multilayer films deposited by facing target sputtering (FTS) created devices with excellent photovoltaic performance when illuminated from both the bottom (graphene side) and top (AAA side) electrodes.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1. The raw materials for   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}(MAPbI_{3})  $   and phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM)

All materials purchased for the fabrication of PSCs were used without further purification.  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  (99.999%) was purchased from Alfar Aesar.  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{I} $  (MAI) was synthesized in our lab according to a reported procedure. PCBM and Chlorobenzene (CB) were acquired from Sigma Aldrich.

# 2.2. Synthesis of graphene on Ti-buffered PES substrates via PATCVD and the transfer of graphene onto PES

In-situ graphene was synthesized at low temperatures via PATCVD with dc sputtering for Ti (10 nm)-buffer layer deposition. The Ti 10 nm buffer layer was deposited onto a cleaned PES substrate at 150 °C via dc sputtering (DC power, 25 W; working pressure, 0.7 Pa; gas flow rate of Ar/H₂, 10/10 sccm (standard cc min⁻¹); deposition time, 10 min; distance between target and substrate (T-S), 12 cm). After deposition of a Ti buffer layer, in-situ graphene was deposited onto the Ti buffer layer at 150 °C via PATCVD under optimal conditions (RF power, 70 W; working pressure, 2.4 × 10² Pa; gas-flow rate of Ar/CH₄/H₂, 12/10/3 sccm; deposition time, 1.5 h; distance between plasma gun and substrate, 10 cm; distance between plasma guns, 10 cm) (See Fig. S1). The transfer graphene as a reference sample was prepared on copper foil via a CVD-growth process at 950 °C. PMMA (poly methyl methacrylate) was spin-coated onto the graphene/copper foil and baked at 180 °C for 2 min. The copper foil was then etched using an aqueous solution of FeCl₃ at 40 °C. Finally, the PMMA/graphene was transferred onto the cleaned PES. The substrate was submerged in an acetone solution to remove the PMMA. The samples were then dried in a nitrogen glove box.

# 2.3. Preparation of flexible PSCs on PES/Graphene

Flexible PSC devices with a planar structure consisting of AAA/PCBM/MAPbI $ _3 $ /NiO $ _x $ /Graphene/PES substrate were fabricated to investigate the influence of perovskite thickness on device performance. First, a conductive area was covered by a tape at the edge of the Graphene/PES substrate (graphene sheet resistance:  $ \sim $ 81  $ \Omega/\square $ ) to allow a measurement connection. Next, an electron-blocking layer (NiO $ _x $ ) with a thickness of 10 nm was deposited via facing target sputtering (FTS) onto the graphene electrode that served as a hole transport layer.

The FTS was designed to prevent the plasma-damage of the graphene for deposition of   $  NiO_{x}  $   blocking layer onto the graphene film. The FTS technique was previously described [21] for an ultrathin, compact   $  NiO_{x}  $   blocking layer whereby two NiO targets (99.99%) were placed in opposition at a distance of 9 cm with plasma filling the space between the surfaces of the targets, and an RF power of 100 W was applied from a gun that created an   $  Ar:O_{2}  $   gas-flow rate of 10:1 sccm with the working pressure maintained at 0.2 Pa. The substrate holder was heated using a halogen lamp at 100 °C placed in a horizontal direction far from the targets at equal distances of 9 cm. A 10 nm thickness of   $  NiO_{x}  $   was obtained after an appropriate deposition time of 9 min.

A smooth and dense perovskite (MAPbI $ _3 $ ) films with a thickness of  $ \sim $ 300 nm were synthesized using a one-step process via chemical vapor deposition (CVD). The schematics of CVD are mentioned in our previous work [22], whereby a 2-inch quartz tube furnace (KJMTI OTF1200X) was used with two separate temperature zones. For the present study, the perovskite materials were simultaneously fabricated through dual-source evaporation from PbI $ _2 $  and MAI (Sigma Aldrich) using argon as a carrier gas. Then, 300 mg of MAI and 150 mg of PbI $ _2 $  were placed in the upper-flow right zone (zone 1) to confine the vapor. According to the differences in the melting-point temperatures of the PbI $ _2 $  and MAI powder, the positions of these sources were located at the center and 10 cm to the right side of zone 1, respectively. The  $ bl $ -NiO $ _x $ /Graphene substrates were placed in the down-flow on the left side of zone 2 and set at a temperature of 100 °C. The furnace was then sealed and pumped down to a pressure of 1.3 × 10 $ ^{2} $  Pa under the control of Ar gas flow at 100 sccm. The perovskite layers were optimized for high crystallinity without PbI $ _2 $  or MAI phases by varying different key parameters such as the gas flow rate, temperature, time, and the amounts of two different sources. After fabrication of the perovskite layer, an  $ in $ -situ annealing process was performed in the second zone of the furnace immediately after deposition at 100 °C for 60 min.

For the electron transport layer, a 40   $  \mu  $  l solution of PCBM dissolved in chlorobenzene (25 mg/ml) was spin-coated onto MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  /bl-NiO  $  _{x}  $  /graphene/PES substrates at 4000 rpm for 10 s, then dried at 80°C for 15 min to allow the PCBM to crystallize and diffuse into the perovskite layer. Finally, devices were completed with the deposition of an AAA top electrode via FTS [21]. The AZO targets (3% Al doping) were used with target-to-target and target-to-substrate distances of 9 and 10 cm, respectively. Initially, the sputtering chamber was evacuated to a base pressure of   $  1.3 \times 10^{-4}  $   Pa using a turbo molecular pump. RF power of 100 W was used for each gun for an Ar flow rate of 10 sccm and a working pressure that was maintained at 0.4 Pa. The Ag layer was deposited at a pressure of 0.4 Pa with dc power of 6.5 W.

# 3. Results and discussion

Fig. 1(a) shows a schematic diagram of the flexible semi-transparent PSC with a structure that was composed of AAA/PCBM/MAPbI $ _3 $ /NiO $ _x $ /Graphene/PES. All steps in the fabrication process were performed at temperatures below 150 °C to enable the photovoltaic devices to form on a flexible PES substrate with a glass-transition temperature of approximately 200 °C. Commonly used MAPbI $ _3 $  was employed as a light-absorbing layer, and inorganic p-type NiO $ _x $  and n-type polymer PCBM were used as the hole transporting and electron transporting layers, respectively. The unique cell in this study was consisted of high-quality graphene grown directly on a polymer substrate without a transfer

process, and the multilayer of AAA deposited via a plasma-damage free FTS to insure the safety of the interfacial properties. To clarify the advantages of a non-transfer graphene electrode, we also fabricated reference devices using CVD-transfer-graphene for comparison. To simplify the subsequent discussion, the terms "PATCVD-Gr device" and "Trans-Gr device" are used to refer to the flexible transparent perovskite solar cells fabricated with the same structure employing PATCVD-graphene (PATCVD-Gr) and transferred graphene (Trans-Gr) as the bottom electrodes, respectively. The energy-band diagram is shown in Fig. 1(b). The PATCVD-Gr and Trans-Gr films showed a work function of 4.47 and  $ 4.25\mathrm{eV} $ , respectively, which was calculated from UPS spectrum (Fig. S2). The  $ 10\mathrm{nm} $ -thick  $ \mathrm{NiO_x} $  layer was optimized to perform the role of hole transfer from light absorption to the graphene anode and to enhance the adhesion between these layers due to the original hydrophobic graphene surface. First, it is notable that  $ \mathrm{NiO_x} $  is a p-type semiconductor with a suitable highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) level ( $ \sim -5.4\mathrm{eV} $ ) with a valence band energy level for  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  of  $ -5.4\mathrm{eV} $ , which benefitted hole injection and enhanced the open circuit voltages [23]. In addition, the p-doping effect of  $ \mathrm{NiO_x} $  on

This type of electrostatic doping has been applied using a thin p-type semiconductor of   $  MoO_{3}  $  , PEDOT:PSS, which dramatically improved the conductivity of the graphene film [13,24]. Fig. 1(c)–(f) revealed a comparison of various properties between PATCVD-Gr and Trans-Gr using graphene grown by CVD on Cu foil at 900 °C. Fig. 1(c) showed the differences in the D, G, and 2D Raman shifts of PATCVD-Gr and Trans-Gr. For PATCVD-Gr, the D peak exhibiting the defects was not observed. The   $  I_{2D}/I_{G}  $   and   $  I_{D}/I_{G}  $   intensity ratios were 1.9 and 0.01, respectively, and these values indicate that the quality of monolayer graphene is comparable to that of defect-free, single-crystalline graphene, as compared with the reported results [25]. The Trans-Gr showed a   $  I_{2D}/I_{G}  $   intensity ratio of   $  \sim2.1  $  , which revealed a monolayer. However, the   $  I_{D}/I_{G}  $   ratio was estimated to be 0.28, which is directly proportional to the level of defects and chemical modifications in the transfer process.

The Z-theta method (the impedance-phase angle) was used to measure the sheet resistance of PATCVD-Gr and Trans-Gr as a function of frequency, as shown in Fig. 1(d). These results were re-confirmed via van der Pauw four-point probe (Hall-effect) method. The sheet resistance values of PATCVD-Gr and Trans-Gr were  $ 81 \pm 6 $  and  $ 2011 \pm 25\Omega/\square $ , respectively. The characteristics of carrier concentration, mobility, and resistivity of PATCVD-Gr and Trans-Gr are summarized in Table S1 to allow comparisons. The optical transmittance for PATCVD-Gr and Trans-Gr was compared with that of a bare PES substrate (Fig. 1(e)). The monolayer PATCVD-Gr showed a transmittance of  $ \sim 85.6\% $  ( $ \sim 87.8\% $  of PES) at a wavelength of  $ 550\mathrm{nm} $ . Optical transmittance of the Trans-Gr was  $ \sim 81.8\% $ , which was attributed to the previous polymer residue in the transfer process. Fig. 1(f) showed the variations in sheet resistance during the bending test of PATCVD-Gr and Trans-Gr. Both samples were bent for 10,000 cycles at a frequency of  $ 1\mathrm{Hz} $  under a tensile strain of  $ 5\% $ . The sheet resistance of PATCVD-Gr was not significantly changed as the number of bending cycles increased. For the Trans-Gr, the sheet resistance was slightly increased possibly due to both formation of defects on the graphene and a weak adhesion with the substrate. Following 10,000 cycles of bending, however, the sheet resistance of the Trans-Gr was increased by only  $ 6\% $  of the initial value, which indicates that the graphene avoided the generation of cracks under the high-strain bending. Therefore, graphene is an ideal candidate to serve as flexible transparent electrodes that require robust mechanical properties and reliability during bending, as well as high levels of transparency and low levels of sheet resistance.

Fig. 2(a) displays the SEM surface image and X-ray diffraction pattern (inset of Fig. 2(a)) of MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite thin films deposited via CVD. The CVD-MAPbI $ _3 $  films showed a compact perovskite layer without pin holes with grain sizes ranging from 500-700 nm, which is similar to our previous work [22]. The surface morphologies of perovskite films played the critical role of determining the thickness of PCBM and the interfacial contact with other cover layers. As shown in inset of Fig. 2(a), CVD-films revealed a pure MAPbI $ _3 $  nature without a PbI $ _2 $  phase. The preferred orientation for the growth of MAPbI $ _3 $  films via CVD can be determined using the texture coefficient's equation [26]. Texture coefficient ( $ TC(hkl) $ ) of each crystal plane is unity for a randomly orientated growth, while  $ TC(hkl) $  is larger than unity for the preferred oriented growth.  $ TC(hkl) $  values of each crystal plane using CVD-MAPbI $ _3 $  films were summarized in Table S2. As a result, CVD-

MAPbI $ _3 $  films were grown with a stable (112) preferred orientation that exhibited the highest  $ TC $  values. Cross-sectional SEM images of MAPbI $ _3 $ /NiO $ _x $ /Gr/glass, PCBM/MAPbI $ _3 $ /NiO $ _x $ /Gr/glass, and the full structure (AAA/PCBM/MAPbI $ _3 $ /NiO $ _x $ /Gr/glass) of a solar device were shown in Fig. 2(b), (c), and 2(d), respectively. PCBM fully filled the spaces in the grains of CVD-MAPbI $ _3 $  and form an effective contact surface with counter electrodes. Regarding the transparency of the device, a thin PCBM layer was required due to the high absorbance of this material. According to the reported work, to accomplish a high-performance of the device, a PCBM thickness was determined at 200 nm [27]. A thicker PCBM layer could provide better diode properties, while a thinner layer would lead to higher levels of short-circuit current [27]. If the surface of perovskite film is not smooth, the electron transport layer (ETL) should be thick enough to cover the rough surface of perovskite in order to decrease the shunting of the device. The surface morphologies of MAPbI $ _3 $  only and MAPbI $ _3 $  films that were covered by PCBM layer are shown in the 3D-AFM images of Fig. 2(e) and (f), respectively. The CVD-MAPbI $ _3 $  films showed a both high crystallinity and smooth surface with rms (root-mean-square) roughness of  $ \sim $ 14 nm. Therefore, the spin coating of a thin PCBM with a thickness of  $ \sim $ 50 nm accomplished a full-coverage of a light absorber (MAPbI $ _3 $ ). The roughness of the perovskite layer by an integration of PCBM layer was decreased with no sign of morphological defects, which resulted in a surface roughness of  $ \sim $ 3.14 nm. This can provide a full contact with the top electrode, which improved the photovoltaic performance.

Fig. 3(a) and (b) showed a  $ J $  (current density)  $ -V $  (voltage) characteristics and a transmittance vs. wavelength of solar cells using PATCVD-Gr and Trans-Gr, respectively. The photovoltaic performances of the devices are summarized in Table 1. The best-performance of PATCVD-Gr device showed a PCE of  $ 14.2\% $   $ (13.2\%) $  with a short-circuit current density of 20.9  $ (19.5)\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $ , an open-circuit voltage of 0.93  $ (0.93)\mathrm{V} $ , and a fill factor of 73 (73) when the solar cells were illuminated from the graphene (bottom) and AAA (top) side, respectively. The average efficiencies of the 5 devices fabricated under the optimum conditions were approximately 13.4 and  $ 12.5\% $  with an illumination from the graphene and AAA sides, respectively. By contrast, in Trans-Gr devices, lower  $ J_{sc} $  and  $ FF $  (fill factor) values than those of PATCVD-Gr were attributed to the poor conductivity of the Trans-Gr electrode, which increased the overall series resistance of the solar cell. A higher level of transmission in the  $ 450 - 750\mathrm{nm} $  wavelength range in the case of PATCVD-Gr could be another reason for the better current density of the device (Fig. 3(b)). As shown in Fig. 3(b), the semi-transparent PATCVD-Gr and Trans-Gr based PSCs showed approximately 13.71 and  $ 12.76\% $  average visible transmittance from 380 to  $ 780~\mathrm{nm} $ , respectively. Transmittances of the PATCVD-Gr and Trans-Gr based PSCs at a wavelength of  $ 700\mathrm{nm} $  were approximately 26 and  $ 25\% $ , respectively. You et al. reported the efficient semitransparent perovskite solar cells with PDMS/PMMA/graphene top-electrodes transferred to the PEDOT:PSS/Spiro-OMeTAD/Perovskite/TiO $ _2 $ /FTO/Glass [13]. They reported the transmittance of approximately 27 and  $ 21\% $  (at a wavelength of  $ 700\mathrm{nm} $ ) with 290 and  $ 350\mathrm{nm} $ -thick  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  films, respectively. As a result, our solar cells ( $ 300\mathrm{nm} $ -thick  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  films) with graphene bottom electrodes showed efficient semitransparent solar properties (inset exhibiting a semitransparent solar cell) comparable to the results that were reported by You et al. The devices with different bottom electrodes barely exhibited  $ J-V $  hysteresis with respect to the scan direction, which was in agreement with the  $ J-V $  behaviors of the inverted planar heterojunction perovskite solar cells reported in previous studies [28]. It is notable that the conductivity of the bottom electrode dramatically affected both the short-circuit current density and the fill factor.

However, the integrated current values that were calculated from EQE was under the value of the short-circuit current density derived from J-V sweep under one sun illumination, which resulted in a mismatch in a range from 0.9 to 1.3 mA/cm². This phenomenon is inextricably tied to the specifics of the measurement conditions and the recombination mechanism in the device [29]. These results are understandable in the wavelength region between 350 and 500 nm, where the EQE value from the top AAA side illumination would be lower than that from the graphene side, due to the strong photon absorption of the PCBM layer when a light passes through before being absorbed by the perovskite film. The transparency of the PSC devices was critically dependent on the thickness of the perovskite layer. It was evident that transparency of the device was increased with the decrease in the perovskite thickness. In the role of the active layer, however, the thickness of the perovskite light absorber would have a significant effect on the PCE. The transmission and absorbance of the MAPbI₃ films with different thicknesses ranging from 100 to 500 nm were shown in Figs. S4a and S4b, respectively. Some reports for a thickness optimization have indicated that increasing the active layer thickness led to a reduction in the electric field, which lowered the carrier collection efficiency and also reduced the FF [30]. Furthermore, a thicker of perovskite layer led to increases in the bulk recombination, which also reduced the Voc. These results demonstrated that a delicate balance between light absorption and carrier transport is required in planar heterojunction structure devices. The optimal thickness of the MAPbI₃ absorber was from 300–350 nm for the best performance of solar cell devices. As expected, the highest levels of efficiency were obtained with an optimal MAPbI₃ thickness of 300 nm (Fig. S4c). The transmittance of the PATCVD-Gr full-structure devices with different MAPbI₃ thicknesses was shown in Fig. S4d.

The light soaking stability of these devices was tested by the continuous exposures to the solar light AM 1.5G, as shown in Fig. 3(d). The results showed that the PATCVD-Gr based device showed an improvement in PCE during the first few days' stability test and then a moderate decrease in performance was observed due to the slightly decreased  $  J_{\mathrm{sc}}  $  and  $  FF  $ , which is attributed to the slightly decomposition of MAPbI3. To be specific, during the whole tests, the PCEs were degraded from the initial  $  14.2\%  $  to the final  $  12.3\%  $  (a drop of a  $  13.4\%  $ ) during  $  500\mathrm{h}  $ . Under the same perovskite fabrication and storage conditions, the PCEs of the Trans-Gr based device were improved in the first 3 days, after this, a dramatically decrease in  $  J_{\mathrm{sc}}  $  and  $  FF  $ , and the PCEs revealed a drop from 11.7 to  $  9.4\%  $ , that remained  $  \sim 80\%  $  of the initial value.

For investigation of mechanical strength of flexible PSC devices, bending tests are commonly performed to observe the influence of stress levels on performance degradation of PSCs. Previous study had demonstrated the relationship between PCE and bending radius [31]. The strength applied to the PCS is influenced by both its own thickness

and the substrate thickness. This relationship is expressed by the following equation [32].

$$
\text { strain } (\%) = \frac {\mathrm{df} + \mathrm{ds}}{2 R} \times 1 0 0
$$

In equation, df and ds are thickness of the cell and the PES substrate, respectively, and R is the bending radius of the curvature. Under bending conditions, the stress experienced by the top surface of a PCS is directly proportional to the total thickness of all layers and to substrate thickness, and is inversely proportional to the radius of the curvature. This means that under the same level of stress, a thinner device will have a lower radius of curvature and can be bent to a greater degree. In each specific case, however, the PSCs were prepared on different flexible substrates with different optimal thicknesses for each layer. Therefore, in the present study, we examined the stability of the devices against repeated bending under various levels of tensile strain. Fig. 4(a) shows the degradation of the two devices after 1000 bending cycles with increasing levels of tensile strain of up to 2.5%. Both devices showed the similar trends in a degradation of performance. The PCEs of the PATCVD-Gr and Trans-Gr devices were decreased slightly when bent at strains lower than 2%, and retained levels of the initial values of 99 and 97, 95 and 94, 91 and 85, and 86 and 80% under bending tensile strains of 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 and 2.5%, respectively. When the tensile strain was increased to 2.5%, however, the PCEs were significantly deteriorated to ~60% for the PATCVD-Gr device and to ~49% for the Trans-Gr device. This degradation was occurred by two reasons. The first reason was attributed to the damage of the bottom and top electrodes, whereby cracks could form under repeated bending, which was led to an increase of the relative resistance of these electrodes. Both the bottom-graphene and the top-AAA (a high transmittance (Fig. S5a)) showed a high flexibility, however, as demonstrated in Fig. 1(f) and Figs. S5b and S5c, respectively, which the sheet resistances of these films were stable under tensile strains of less than 2.5%. Therefore, the degradation in electrical properties by the bending of these electrodes is not main reason. The second possible reason for the abrupt decrease in PCE was due to a damage in the perovskite layer and interfaces under mechanical stress. Since the crystallized MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  perovskite showed no high flexibility, we suggested that the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  films were the main reasons for degradation of efficiency. To verify the reason, the samples after 1000 bending cycles were examined with a SEM cross-sectional image under a tensile strain of 1.5 and 2.5%, as shown in Fig. S6a and S6b, respectively. Compared with no change of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  morphology that was observed at a tensile strain of 1.5% (Fig. S6a), cracks in the perovskite films under a tensile strain of 2.5% were observed (Fig. S6b). Although the PCE of the graphene-based PSCs was decreased under a tensile strain of 2.5%, the potential for this type of device remains adequate for flexible photovoltaic applications. The efficiencies of solar cells using a PATCVD-Gr and Trans-Gr were decreased by ~9 and ~16%, respectively, at a tensile strain of 1.5% for 1000 bending cycles (Fig. 4(b)). This degradation in PATCVD-Gr (Fig. 4(c)) and Trans-Gr devices (Fig. 4(d)) was due to a slight decrease in both fill factor and short-circuit current. # 4. Conclusions

Enhanced semi-transparent flexible perovskite solar cells were fabricated by employing directly synthesized graphene via PATCVD at 150 °C on a PES substrate for utilization as a transparent conducting anode. A smooth and dense perovskite MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  films with a thickness of  $  \sim  $  300 nm were synthesized via CVD instead of solution technique. The high-quality (no defect) and large-scale monolayer graphene electrodes were first prepared at 150 °C on PES substrate without the need for transfer process. PATCVD-Gr showed excellent levels of mechanical flexibility and a low sheet resistance of  $  \sim  $  82  $  \Omega/\square  $ . Solar-cell devices with PATCVD-graphene bottom electrodes demonstrated a PCE of more than 14% with a transmittance of approximately 26% at a wavelength of 700 nm. These devices demonstrated a great flexibility by maintaining more than 90% of the original PCE value even after 1000 bending cycles under a tensile strain of 1.5% (equivalent radii of 4 mm). PATCVD-Gr based solar cells showed predominant electrical and mechanical properties compared with Trans-Gr based solar cells. These results could pave the way for realization of perovskite solar cells for use as add-on components for multi-junction photovoltaic devices and smart windows or as a wearable power supply.
