# Synergistic Effects of Cation and Anion in an Ionic Imidazolium Tetrafluoroborate Additive for Improving the Efficiency and Stability of Half-Mixed Pb-Sn Perovskite Solar Cells

Hongki Kim, Jong Woo Lee, Gi Rim Han, Seong Keun Kim, and Joon Hak Oh*

Narrow-bandgap mixed Pb-Sn perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have great feasibility for constructing efficient all-perovskite tandem solar cells, in combination with wide-bandgap lead halide PSCs. However, the power conversion efficiency of mixed Pb-Sn PSCs still lags behind lead-based counterparts. Here, additive engineering using ionic imidazolium tetrafluoroborate  $ (\mathrm{IMBF}_4) $  is proposed, where the imidazolium (IM) cation and tetrafluoroborate  $ (\mathrm{BF}_4) $  anion efficiently passivate defects at grain boundaries and improve crystallinity, simultaneously relaxing lattice strain, respectively. Defect passivation is achieved by the chemical interaction between the IM cation and the positively charged under-coordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  or  $ \mathrm{Sn}^{2+} $  ions, and lattice strain relaxation is realized by lattice expansion with the intercalation of  $ \mathrm{BF}_4 $  anions into the perovskite lattice. As a result, the synergistic effects of the cation and anion in the  $ \mathrm{IMBF}_4 $  additive greatly enhance the optoelectronic performance of half-mixed Pb-Sn perovskites, leading to much longer carrier lifetimes. The best-performing half-mixed Pb-Sn PSC shows an efficiency above  $ 19\% $  with negligible hysteresis, while retaining over  $ 90\% $  of its initial efficiency after  $ 1000\mathrm{h} $  in a nitrogen-filled glovebox and showing a lifetime to  $ 80\% $  degradation of  $ 53.5\mathrm{h} $  under continuous illumination.

# 1. Introduction

Single-junction lead halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with a typical stoichiometry of ABX $ _3 $  (A = CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $  or NH $ _2 $ CHNH $ _2 $ , B = Pb, and X = I, Br, Cl) have achieved unprecedented improvements in power conversion efficiency (PCE) reaching 25.5% due to their outstanding optoelectronic properties, including long diffusion length, high absorption coefficient, and benign defect density. $ ^{[1]} $  Despite progressive advances, PCEs in single-junction PCSs are inherently limited by the Shockley–Queisser (S–Q) limit as thermalization loss occurs. $ ^{[2]} $  Moreover, the typical optical band gap ( $ E_g $ ) > 1.5 eV of lead halide perovskites, which is greater than the optimum  $ E_g $  of 1.34 eV represented by the S–Q limit, prevents the capture of large amounts of light in the near-infrared region. In addition, the lack of utilization of ionic additives composed of organic cations and inorganic anions that can play complementary roles hinders the search for a highly efficient additive system for mixed Pb-Sn perovskites. More specifically, the tardy study for organic passivator and pseudo-halide anion that can substitute existing I anion in mixed Pb-Sn perovskites solar cells is a major obstacle to exploring optimal composition engineering and better understanding on various properties of mixed Pb-Sn perovskites including morphological, crystallographic, and device characteristic properties. Therefore, there is an urgent need to find effective additive engineering through the introduction of organic passivator and pseudo-halide anion that have not been introduced into the existing Pb-Sn perovskite system. In comparison with pure lead halide perovskites, mixed Pb-Sn perovskite is not only more susceptible to defect formation due to the easily oxidizable  $ \mathrm{Sn}^{2+} $  but also more vulnerable to lattice strain by the lattice distortion originating from the different ionic radii between Sn (1.18 Å) and Pb (1.19 Å). Nevertheless, there has been no investigation of a viable additive system that can simultaneously manage defects and lattice strain in mixed Pb-Sn perovskites.

Here, we first added ionic imidazolium tetrafluoroborate (IMBF $ _4 $ ) consisting of an imidazolium (IM) cation and tetrafluoroborate (BF $ _4 $ ) anion into half-mixed Pb-Sn perovskite films. The BF $ _4 $  anions were mainly distributed on the bottom surface of the perovskite film, and IM cations were evenly distributed throughout the film. Of note, the BF $ _4 $  anions were intercalated into the perovskite lattice resulting in strain relaxation with improved crystallinity, and IM cations passivated the trap sites at GBs through chemical interactions with the perovskite. These synergistic effects of BF $ _4 $  anions and IM cations in IMBF $ _4 $  additives greatly enhanced charge carrier dynamics, leading to markedly longer charge carrier lifetimes. Assisted by IMBF $ _4 $  additives, the best-performing half-mixed Pb-Sn PSCs achieved a PCE of 19.1% under reverse bias with negligible hysteresis, whereas the pristine device exhibited a PCE of 17.3% with prominent hysteresis. In terms of stability, metallic Pb $ ^0 $  and Sn $ ^0 $  peaks in X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra were largely suppressed with IMBF $ _4 $  additives after aging stress. We assumed that strain relaxation by BF $ _4 $  substitution increases the formation energy of iodine vacancy, inhibiting the formation of concomitant metallic Pb and Sn species. As a result, the device with IMBF $ _4 $  showed improved device stability, retaining over 90% of its initial PCE for 1000 h in a nitrogen-filled glove box as well as a much longer lifetime to 80% degradation ( $ t_{80} $ ) of 53.5 h compared to that of the reference (15.9 h).

# 2.1. Synergistic Effects of IM Cation and  $ \mathrm{BF}_4 $  Anion in an Ionic  $ \mathrm{IMBF}_4 $  Additive on Half-Mixed Pb-Sn Perovskites

We chose half-mixed Pb-Sn perovskites with the molecular formula   $  FA_{0.5}MA_{0.5}Pb_{0.5}Sn_{0.5}I_{3}  $   as our reference films.   $  FA_{0.5}MA_{0.5}Pb_{0.5}Sn_{0.5}I_{3}  $   films were prepared by spin-coating the precursor

solution in a nitrogen-filled glove box where the antisolvent treatment was used to verify compact and uniform film coverage without pinholes. Figure 1a shows the chemical structure of the ionic IMBF $ _{4} $  additive used in this study. To investigate the effects of the IMBF $ _{4} $  additive on the perovskite films, IMBF $ _{4} $  was added into the perovskite precursor solution with different molar proportions to the Sn $ ^{2+} $  component. We evaluated the optical properties from the absorption spectra with different amounts of IMBF $ _{4} $ , as shown in Figure 1b,c.  $ E_{\mathrm{g}} $  estimated from the Tauc plot was unaffected by the addition of a small amount of IMBF $ _{4} $  (Figure 1b), indicating that the introduction of additives did not interfere with the production of narrow-bandgap absorbers for tandem solar cell applications. The slight increase in  $ E_{\mathrm{g}} $  with a relatively higher concentration of IMBF $ _{4} $  was mainly due to the IM cation (Figure S1a, Supporting Information). As we could not observe the evidence on the formation of the low-dimensional perovskite by the introduction of IM cations (Figure S1b, Supporting Information), the slight increase in  $ E_{\mathrm{g}} $  may have been due to the non-preferential ordering of the organic cations in the perovskite as large IM cations cause crystallographic disorder. $ ^{[15]} $  We then estimated the Urbach energy ( $ E_{\mathrm{u}} $ ) from the slope of the straight line in an exponential increase of the absorption coefficient with the photon energy to assess the electronic disorder (Figure 1c). The lower  $ E_{\mathrm{u}} $  value is indicative of a lower degree of structural disorder. $ ^{[16]} $  As IMBF $ _{4} $  was incorporated into the perovskite films,  $ E_{\mathrm{u}} $  gradually decreased from 27.4 meV to the lowest value of 23.5 meV at 0.5 mol%, which we set to be the optimized concentration. The reduced  $ E_{\mathrm{u}} $  may result from a lower defect density or improved structural order.

We first examined the effects of IMBF $ _{4} $  additive on the perovskite by checking the chemical distribution within the perovskite film. Time-of-flight secondary-ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) depth profiles were investigated under negative polarity, as shown in Figure 1d,e. We assigned the signal that is highly relevant to the IM cation to C $ _{3} $ H $ _{3} $ N $ _{2}^{-} $  in accordance with its chemical structure, to obtain distinct chemical information from methylammonium (MA) and formamidinium (FA) cations. Compared to the pristine sample (Figure 1d), the IMBF $ _{4} $ -containing sample showed increased intensities of C $ _{3} $ H $ _{3} $ N $ _{2}^{-} $  and B $ ^{-} $  signals (Figure 1e). These observations confirmed that IM cations were distributed throughout the films, while BF $ _{4} $  anions were distributed on the bottom surface of the film. The unwanted IM signal for a pristine sample could be excluded as an interference signal from other organic cations, which was verified from ToF-SIMS depth profiles obtained with increasing the amount of IMBF $ _{4} $  additive (Figure S2, Supporting Information). The resulting chemical distributions of IM and BF $ _{4} $  were visualized in the elemental 3D maps for the corresponding ions traced in the depth profile, as shown in Figure 1f. IM cations and BF $ _{4} $  anions showed different distribution behaviors within the perovskite film, implying that their roles inside the perovskite film may differ. From the structural viewpoint in mixed-ion Pb-Sn perovskites, lattice strain would originate from metal halide cage distortions and octahedral tilting by the ionic size mismatch between organic cations and the metal halide cages. These structural imperfections lead to instability and vulnerability in forming vacancy defects. In addition, the residual tensile strain in perovskite films may also result from the thermal expansion coefficient mismatch between mixed Pb-Sn perovskites and the underlying poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS) layer after the crystallization process.  $  ^{[21]}  $   From the lesson learned from mixed-ion lead halide perovskites, lattice strain can be mitigated by adding isovalent small ions that can release strong tension between Pb and I, otherwise, the lattice strain is released by forming vacancy defects.  $  ^{[22]}  $   Therefore, the insertion of   $  BF_{4}  $   enables the formation of a strain-reduced lattice by inducing lattice expansion.

To identify an additional morphological effect that can be represented by properties such as defect passivation effects at GBs, we performed Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) measurements using an atomic force microscope (AFM), as shown in Figure 3a–d. Figure 3a,c shows the AFM images and corresponding KPFM images for half-mixed Pb-Sn perovskite films without (w/o) or with (Figure 3b,d) IMBF  $  _{4}  $  additive. Statistics for contact potential difference (  $  V_{CPD}  $  ) at GBs and grain interiors (GIs) for each sample were summarized to determine how potential behavior differs between GBs and GIs, as shown in Figure 3e,f. Regardless of additive incorporation, the KPFM image showed lower  $  V_{CPD}  $  at GBs than at GIs. Based on the definition of  $  V_{CPD}  $  by the following equation:  $  V_{CPD} = (W_{tip}-W_{sample})/-e  $ , where  $  W_{tip}  $ ,  $  W_{sample}  $ , and e are the work function of the tip and sample, and the elementary electronic charge, respectively, a lower  $  V_{CPD}  $  indicates lower work function. Therefore, the GB region in our film is positively charged corresponding to downward band bending, which potentially provides a shunt path for electron transport or a hole transport barrier, leading to degradation of device performance.  $  ^{[23]}  $  Notably,  $  V_{CPD}  $  at GIs was maintained at a similar level regardless of the presence of IMBF  $  _{4}  $  additive, whereas  $  V_{CPD}  $  in GBs increased after the introduction of the IMBF  $  _{4}  $  additive. These observations indicated that the IMBF  $  _{4}  $  additive passivates defects at GBs, resulting in the neutralization of charged defects at GBs compared to the pristine films.

A schematic illustration of possible mechanisms for the synergistic effects arising from IM cations and  $ \mathrm{BF}_4 $  anions in  $ \mathrm{IMBF}_4 $  additives is shown in Figure 3g. In the absence of  $ \mathrm{IMBF}_4 $ , perovskite experiences lattice strain due to lattice distortion or mismatch of thermal expansion coefficients between perovskite and the underlying PEDOT:PSS layer. The structural imperfections would produce heavily strain-induced crystals during the crystallization process. In addition, a variety of point and interstitial defects near GBs will lead to high leakage and non-radiative charge recombination. On the other hand, when the  $ \mathrm{IMBF}_4 $  additive is introduced, the lattice strain of perovskite can be alleviated by the insertion of  $ \mathrm{BF}_4 $  into the perovskite lattice. With the  $ \mathrm{BF}_4 $  distributed mainly on the bottom surface of the perovskite, it is thought to form crystals with relaxed strain during the whole crystallization process. Moreover, it is

expected that IM cations distributed in GB regions passivate not only positively charged under-coordinated Pb or Sn defects but also possibly negatively charged Pb (or Sn)-I antisite defects through interaction with positively charged nitrogen atoms in IM cations. The reduced defect density at GBs decreases in the work function difference between GBs and GIs, which in turn implies lower band bending at GBs, leading to suppression of charge recombination and leakage current through GBs.

# 2.2. Charge Carrier Dynamics of Half-Mixed Pb-Sn Perovskites with  $ \mathrm{IMBF}_4 $  Additive

To investigate the effects of IMBF $ _{4} $  additives on the charge carrier dynamics of half-mixed Pb-Sn perovskite films, we performed steady-state and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) analysis of the perovskite films with or w/o IMBF $ _{4} $ , as shown in Figure 4a–d. Samples were irradiated with a laser with a wavelength of 372 nm from the direction of the glass side as well as from the direction of the perovskite surface, to examine the comprehensive charge carrier dynamics throughout the films including the top and bottom surfaces. For the surface side of perovskite, the IMBF $ _{4} $ -introduced film showed a stronger PL intensity compared to the pristine film, indicating more dominant radiative recombination (Figure 4a). The TRPL results of the corresponding films with IMBF $ _{4} $  showed markedly increased carrier lifetime in comparison with the pristine film (Figure 4b), where the TRPL curves were fitted by triexponential decay and the fitted parameters are presented in Table S1, Supporting Information. We obtained a much longer average carrier lifetime of 71.1 ns with the IMBF $ _{4} $ -introduced perovskite film compared to the reference film (18.2 ns). The prolonged carrier lifetimes may be attributed to a suppressed non-radiative recombination pathway through the efficient passivation of defect states and improved crystallinity. These results were also replicated under excitation from the direction of the glass side, indicating that IMBF $ _{4} $ -containing films showed markedly improved steady-state PL intensity and longer carrier lifetimes (Figure 4c,d and Table S1, Supporting Information). Compared to the TRPL results under excitation from the surface side, much longer lifetimes were obtained with excitation from the glass side for each sample, implying more prominent non-radiative recombination on the surface side that was directly exposed to the air. Our steady-state PL and TRPL results showed that the incorporation of IMBF $ _{4} $  additive facilitated the radiative recombination of photogenerated charges throughout the perovskite film by reducing the defect density and improving the structural quality.

# 2.3. Device Performance of Half-Mixed Pb-Sn PSCs with IMBF  $  _{4}  $  Additive

To assess the charge recombination process in half-mixed Pb-Sn PSCs, we measured light intensity dependent   $  V_{oc}  $   as shown in Figure 5h. How closely the diode follows the ideal diode behavior denoted by ideality factor n was evaluated from the slope of the light intensity versus   $  V_{oc}  $   plot in the low-intensity regime, where the   $  V_{oc}  $   increased monotonically with the logarithm of light intensity. The IMBF  $  _{4}  $ -containing device had a smaller n of 1.32 than the pristine device (1.54). An n value closer to 1 implies that bimolecular recombination is prominent with the introduction of IMBF  $  _{4}  $ , while monomolecular recombination via trap states is favorable without IMBF  $  _{4}  $   additive.  $  ^{[31]}  $   Finally, charge recombination resistance (  $  R_{rec}  $  ) was estimated from the electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS), as shown in Figure 5i, to elucidate the extent of charge recombination in the perovskite films. The resulting Nyquist plot obtained at   $  V_{oc}  $   under AM 1.5 G illumination (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) indicated a larger radius of the semicircle in the Nyquist plot with the introduction of IMBF  $  _{4}  $   compared to the reference cell, confirming the suppression of charge recombination. Consequently, the device characteristic results showed that the addition of IMBF  $  _{4}  $   enabled the fabrication of highly efficient mixed Pb-Sn PSCs by efficiently passivating the defects at GBs and improving the morphological and structural qualities.

Stability improvements were demonstrated by XPS and device tests as shown in Figure 6a–d. For Pb 4f and Sn 3d peaks in the pristine aged films, noticeable metallic Pb  $  ^{0}  $  and Sn  $  ^{0}  $  peaks in lower binding energy regions were observed, whereas

the metallic   $  Pb^{0}  $   and   $  Sn^{0}  $   peaks were greatly suppressed for the aged film with IMBF  $  _{4}  $   additive (Figure 6a,b). As has been demonstrated for Pb-based PSCs, intrinsic chemical degradation of the perovskite film can occur upon aging stress due to their fragile chemical reactivity by the soft nature of Pb—I bonding. Along with the easily oxidizable I  $  ^{-}  $   to I  $  ^{0}  $  , Pb  $  ^{2+}  $   tends to be reduced to form metallic   $  Pb^{0}  $   in aged perovskite films, significantly degrading the performance of Pb-based PSCs.  $  ^{[32]}  $   In our half-mixed Pb-Sn perovskite films under aging stress, prominent oxidation of I  $  ^{-}  $   to I  $  ^{0}  $   would have occurred due to its nonvolatility compared to the MA or FA organic cation, possibly resulting in the concomitant reduction of metallic species such as Pb  $  ^{2+}  $   or Sn  $  ^{2+}  $  . Based on the previous results verifying increased formation energy of I vacancy upon strain relaxation,  $  ^{[22]}  $   we speculate that strain relaxation realized by BF  $  _{4}  $   anions could dominantly contribute to the suppressed formation of metallic Pb  $  ^{0}  $   and Sn  $  ^{0}  $   species by increasing the formation energy of I vacancies. To support the speculation, lattice microstrain for the perovskite films with different amounts of IMBF  $  _{4}  $   additive was extracted as shown in Figure S10, Supporting Information. Here, microstrain occurs due to the small fluctuations in the lattice spacing, which can be caused by crystal imperfections and structural defects including vacancy defects and dislocations. The microstrain was extracted from our XRD patterns according to the previously reported Williamson–Hall analysis method.  $  ^{[33]}  $   The extracted microstrain verified the effectiveness of IMBF  $  _{4}  $   additive to reduce the lattice strain. Improved chemical stability was confirmed by performing device stability tests in a nitrogen-filled glove box (H  $  _{2}  $  O < 1.5 ppm, O  $  _{2}  $   < 1.5 ppm) for 1000 h, as shown in Figure 6c and Figure S11, Supporting Information. The device containing the IMBF  $  _{4}  $   additive retained over 90% of its initial efficiency for 1000 h, while the reference cell retained just 70% of its initial efficiency. In addition, to check the stability under continuous illumination, we monitored the stabilized PCEs at their MPP of half-mixed Pb-Sn PSCs for 100 h under continuous 1 sun illumination. As shown in Figure 6d, half-mixed Pb-Sn PSCs with IMBF  $  _{4}  $   showed a much slower decay in stabilized PCE at MPP. This means that the IMBF  $  _{4}  $  -introduced device is less sensitive to defect formation which promotes the migration of bulk and the accumulation of ionic defects at the junction interface under continuous working conditions, thereby degrading the steady-state PCE. As a result, the IMBF  $  _{4}  $  -containing device showed a markedly improved t  $  _{80}  $   of 53.5 h compared to the reference device (15.9 h). These results clearly verify the improved defect tolerance induced by IMBF  $  _{4}  $   under aging stress.

# 3. Conclusion

In this study, additive engineering using  $ \mathrm{IMBF_4} $  enabled the fabrication of high-performance half-mixed Pb-Sn PSCs, achieving PCE above  $ 19\% $  and remarkable operational stability. We demonstrated the synergistic effects of IM cation and  $ \mathrm{BF_4} $  anion in  $ \mathrm{IMBF_4} $ , resulting in efficient defect passivation at GBs and inducing crystallographic improvement, respectively. These effects enabled more efficient charge carrier dynamics, low trap density, low leakage current, and efficient junction properties. As a result, the best-performing device showed PCEs of 19.0

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials: PEDOT:PSS (PVP Al 4083) was obtained from Heraeus Clevios. Tin iodide  $ (\mathrm{SnI}_2) $ , fullerene  $ (C_{60}) $ , and bathocuproine (BCP) were purchased from Alfa Aesar. Formamidinium iodide (FAI), methylammonium iodide (MAI), IMI, and  $ \mathrm{IMBF_4} $  were purchased from GreatCell Solar. Lead iodide  $ (\mathrm{PbI}_2) $ , lead thiocyanate  $ (\mathrm{Pb(SCN)_2}) $ , tin fluoride  $ (\mathrm{SnF_2}) $ ,  $ \mathrm{NH_4BF_4} $ , anhydrous  $ N,N $ -dimethyl formamide (DMF), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 1,2-dichlorobenzene (ODCB), and toluene were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. 6,6-Phenyl  $ C_{61} $  butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) was obtained from EM-index. All chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Perovskite Precursor Solution Preparation: Half-mixed Pb-Sn perovskite precursor solutions with the chemical formula   $  FA_{0.5}MA_{0.5}Pb_{0.5}Sn_{0.5}I_{3}  $   were prepared by mixing separately prepared MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   and FASnI  $  _{3}  $   solutions with a stoichiometric Pb:Sn molar ratio of 1:1. FASnI  $  _{3}  $   solution was obtained by adding 514 mg of SnI  $  _{2}  $  , 237 mg of FAI, and 21.6 mg of SnF  $  _{2}  $   (as an antioxidant to suppress oxidation of tin) into DMF:DMSO (800   $  \mu  $  L:200   $  \mu  $  L). MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   precursor solution was obtained by dissolving

632 mg of   $  PbI_{2}  $  , 218 mg of MAI, and 15.2 mg of   $  \mathrm{Pb(SCN)_{2}}  $   in DMF:DMSO (630   $  \mu  $  L:70   $  \mu  $  L). The perovskite precursor solutions containing the IMBF  $  _{4}  $   additive were prepared by adding IMBF  $  _{4}  $   into the perovskite precursor solution with different molar proportions of IMBF  $  _{4}  $   in relation to Sn.

Device Fabrication: ITO-coated glasses were sonicated sequentially in soapy water, de-ionized water, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol, and the substrates were then treated with UV-ozone for 20 min. A PEDOT:PSS dispersion was filtered with a 0.25 μm hydrophilic filter and then spin-coated onto the ITO substrates at 6500 rpm for 40 s and dried at 150 °C for 30 min. The coated ITO/PEDOT:PSS substrates were transferred to a nitrogen-filled glovebox (H₂O < 1.5 ppm, O₂ < 1.5 ppm). The perovskite precursor solution was spin-coated under two-step coating conditions of 1000 rpm for 10 s and 3500 rpm for 45 s, and then annealed at 100 °C for 5 min. Toluene (400 μL) was used as the antisolvent after 8 s for the second spin-coating process. The PCBM solution (5 mg mL⁻¹) in ODCB was filtered and spin-coated onto the perovskite film at 2000 rpm for 60 s. Then, 20 nm-thick C₆₀, 7 nm-thick BCP, and 100 nm-thick Ag layers were thermally evaporated under vacuum (<10⁻⁶ mbar) in sequence through a shadow mask. The active area of the device was 0.12 cm².

Film and Device Characterization: Absorption spectra were obtained by UV–vis spectroscopy (V-770, JASCO). Steady-state PL spectra were obtained using a fluorescence lifetime spectrometer (C11367-32, Hamamatsu Photonics) with an excitation wavelength of 372 nm. ToF-SIMS (TOF.SIMS-5, ION-TOF) was operated in the spectral mode, using a 30 keV Bi  $  ^{3+}  $  primary ion source with a current of 1 pA. A 1 keV Cs  $  ^{+}  $  ion source with a current of 100 nA was used as an etching source. The depth profile was operated under negative polarity. The XPS (AXIS-HSI, Kratos) was performed on prepared perovskite films after etching for 20 s.
