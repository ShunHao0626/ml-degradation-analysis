# Abstract

Defect passivation is one of the most important strategies to boost both the efficiency and stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Here, nontoxic and sustainable forest-based biomaterial, betulin, is first introduced into perovskites. The experiments and calculations reveal that betulin can effectively passivate the uncoordinated lead ions in perovskites via sharing the lone pair electrons of hydroxyl group, promoting charge transport. As a result, the power conversion efficiencies of the p-i-n planar PSCs remarkably increase from  $ 19.14\% $  to  $ 21.15\% $ , with the improvement of other parameters. The hydrogen bonds of betulin lock methylamine and halogen ions along the grain boundaries and on the film surface and thus suppress ion migration, further

# 1. Introduction

Metal halide perovskite semiconductors are considered as promising light absorbers in photovoltaics due to the combination of excellent optoelectronic properties, e.g. large absorption coefficients   $  [1,2]  $  , tunable bandgaps   $  [3]  $  , and long carrier diffusion lengths   $  [4–6]  $  . With much effort devoted to perovskite compositions   $  [7,8]  $  , charge selective contacts   $  [9–11]  $   and device architectures   $  [12–15]  $  , the certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of the perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has exceeded 25% for single junction   $  [16]  $  , competing with the well-established conventional counterparts (e.g. crystalline silicon cells)   $  [17]  $  . Besides the impressive efficiency, the advantages with low-cost, high specific power, flexibility, and low-temperature solution processing place the PSCs in the vanguard of the third-generation photovoltaic technologies   $  [18–24]  $  .

It is commonly believed that the presence of defects along the grain boundaries and on the surface of perovskite films is responsible for the limited device efficiency and stability   $  [25,26]  $  . The perovskite crystal films are grown via precursor sequential solution deposition and are inevitably polycrystalline in nature with the ubiquitous defects   $  [27–30]  $  . These defects, if not suppressed, can be a major source for nonradiative recombination and trigger quasi-Fermi level pinning at perovskite interface, causing severe energy loss and thus decreasing the whole device performance   $  [31,32]  $  . Furthermore, the defects that diminish the activation energy are sensitive to moisture, oxygen, heat and light illumination, significantly accelerating the device degradation under operation   $  [33–36]  $  .

Defect passivation via additive is an effective method to overcome these serious issues   $  [37–43]  $  . Additive components including Lewis acids   $  [44]  $  , inorganic salts   $  [45,46]  $  , ionic liquids   $  [42,47]  $  , etc., have been introduced into the perovskite precursor to modulate the crystal film growth kinetics with fewer grain boundaries, and directly bind to the sites of uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and Pb-I antisite defects   $  [48–50]  $  . More recently, some additives provide p- or n-type doping character, which obviously change the work function and Fermi level (  $  E_{F}  $  ) position of the passivated perovskite film surface attributed to the strong charge transfer between additive and perovskite   $  [51–53]  $  . The resulting more n-type (p-type) perovskite film with the   $  E_{F}  $   closer toward the conduction (valence) band can suppress recombination at the perovskite/electron (hole) transporting layer interfaces due to the higher density of the carrier states   $  [51–53]  $  .

In this work, we first introduce forest-based biomaterial, betulin, as passivator to create the high-quality perovskite  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  (MAPbI₃) film. Betulin is nontoxic,

earth-abundant, and biologically compatible (biodegradable), which avoids the shortcomings that many of the currently dominating additives are hazardous to the environment and/or expensive. In view of long-term sustainability, there is a need to derive and use natural materials rather than from petroleum. The chemical structure of the betulin is shown in Fig. 1(a). The positive effect of betulin on crystallization, surface electronic structures, trap states, mobilities and stability of perovskites are systematically demonstrated. The results revealed that betulin effectively passivates the uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  through Lewis acid-base reaction, and stabilizes the perovskite crystal structures via hydrogen bonding. As a result, the PCE of the betulin-passivated PSC increases from  $ 19.14\% $  to  $ 21.15\% $  with the significant enhancement of open-circuit voltage ( $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $ ), short-circuit current ( $ J_{\mathrm{sc}} $ ) and fill factor (FF). Moreover, the stability of the passivated device toward humidity, thermal and light stress is remarkably enhanced as compared with the pristine device.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1.Materials

Poly(triarylamine) (PTAA, 99%), lead iodide (PbI $ _2 $ , 99.99%), [6,6]-Phenyl C61 butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM, 99.5%) and bathocuproine (BCP, 99.9%) were received from Polymer Light Technology Corp. 2,3,5,6-Tetrafluoro-7,7,8,8-tetracyanoquinodimethane (F4TCNQ, 99%) was purchased from Jilin OLED Material Tec Co., Ltd. SnO $ _2 $  (15 wt% in H $ _2 $ O of a colloidal dispersion) was received from Alfa Aesar. N, N-Dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.8%), chlorobenzene (CB, 99.5%) and isopropanol (IPA, 99.5%) were provided by Sigma-Aldrich. PTAA was dissolved in CB with a concentration of 2 mg/mL and doped with F4TCNQ by a weight ratio of 25%. PTAA solution was filtered with a 0.22 μm PTFE filter before using. Perovskite MAPbI $ _3 $  precursor was prepared by dissolving 0.4917 g PbI $ _2 $  and 0.1590 g MAI in 785 μL DMF and 85 μL DMSO. Betulin was dissolved in DMF and then added into the MAPbI $ _3 $  precursor with the different weight ratios. PCBM solution was obtained by dissolving 20 mg PCBM in 1 mL CB. BCP solution was prepared with a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL in IPA.

The surface morphology of perovskite film was measured by SEM (Carl Zeiss, Merlin). XRD patterns were recorded to examine the crystallinity of the perovskite film by an X-ray diffractometer with Cu  $ K_{\alpha} $  radiation (Empyrean). The UV-Vis absorption spectra were performed by a spectrophotometer (TU-1901). Steady-state photoluminescence spectrum was obtained from fluorescence spectrometer (PerkinElmer LS 55). Time resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) was to evaluate the carrier lifetime of perovskite through a time-correlated single photon counting (TCSPC) system. The TRPL decays were fitted according to a biexponential function [54]:  $ y = A_{1}\exp \left(-\frac{t}{\tau_{1}}\right) + A_{2}\exp \left(-\frac{t}{\tau_{2}}\right) + y_{0} $ , where  $ A_{1} $  and  $ A_{2} $  are the relative amplitude, and  $ y_{0} $  is a constant.  $ \tau_{1} $  and  $ \tau_{2} $  represent a fast decay component and a slow decay component, respectively. Liquid state NMR were carried out by a nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometer (Bruker Avance 500 MHz). UPS and XPS measurements were performed in an ultrahigh vacuum analysis system with the base pressure of  $ 5\times 10^{-10} $  mbar (Scienta-2000).

# 2.3. Device fabrication and characterization

Indium tin oxide (ITO)-coated glasses were ultrasonically cleaned following a commonly used method   $  [51]  $  . The nitrogen-flow-dried ITO substrates were then treated in UV-  $  O_{3}  $   for 15 min. PTAA layer was spin-coated on the ITO substrate at 5000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 150 °C for 10 min. After cooling down to the room temperature, DMF was further spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 10 s to improve the wettability of PTAA layer. The perovskite precursor was deposited on the PTAA layer at 4000 rpm for 30 s. During the process, 150 μL CB was dropped at the center of the film after spinning coating for 7 s. The resulting perovskite films were immediately put on the hotplate at 100 °C and annealed for 10 min. PCBM was deposited on the perovskite film as electron transporting layer at 1500 rpm for 40 s. After that, BCP was deposited on the PCBM layer at 6000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, 100 nm Ag was thermally evaporated as cathode with the designed mask area of 0.05 cm  $  ^{-2}  $  . The J-V curves of the PSCs were measured in a nitrogen-atmosphere glovebox via a Keithley 2400 system and a simulated AM 1.5 G (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) illumination light source. The EQE spectrum was obtained by QE-R systems (Enli Tech.). The light intensity was calibrated with a NREL-certified Si cell.

The interaction energy between perovskite and betulin was calculated from density functional theory (DFT) using the projector augmented wave (PAW) method implemented in the Vienna Ab-Initio Simulation Package (VASP). The exchange-correlation potential was treated by the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE). A vacuum thickness of 18 Å was adopted along the z-axis to avoid the interaction between adjacent unit cells. An energy cut-off of 500 eV for the plane wave expansion and a 6×6×1 centered k-point mesh grid was used in calculations. The orbitals of Pb   $  6s^{2}  $     $  6p^{2}  $  , I   $  5s^{2}5p^{5}  $  , N   $  2s^{2}2p^{3}  $  , C   $  2s^{2}2p^{2}  $  , O   $  2s^{2}2p^{4}  $   and H   $  1s^{1}  $   were explicitly treated as valence electrons. All atoms were relaxed toward equilibrium until the Hellmann-Feynman forces <1 meV/Å.

# 3. Results and discussion

One-step method assisted with chlorobenzene as anti-solvent is used to fabricate   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   films (see the experiment section for details). To evaluate the interaction between betulin and perovskite, liquid-state   $  {}^{1}H  $   nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra of betulin and   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   are recorded in Figs. 1(b, c) and S1. The proton signal at   $  \delta = 7.83  $   ppm corresponding to   $  -NH_{3}^{+}  $  in MA cation chemically upshifts toward the higher value of   $  \delta = 7.88  $   ppm after incorporating betulin, which indicates the hydrogen bonding interaction between the H of   $  -NH_{3}^{+}  $  in perovskite and the O of -OH in betulin [55]. Meanwhile, it is expected that the halogen I  $  ^{-}  $  forms the hydrogen bond with H of -OH in betulin [56], where the proton signals at both   $  \delta = 4.71  $   and   $  \delta = 4.58  $   ppm belonging to the hydroxyl groups in betulin downshift to 4.64 and 4.51 ppm, respectively. The rich hydrogen bonds would lock the MA and halide ions in place, thereby suppressing ion migration and stabilizing the perovskite crystal structures. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurement was performed to further confirm the interaction between perovskite and betulin in Fig. S2. The XPS I 3d core level spectrum shifts toward lower binding energy after betulin incorporation, verifying the interaction between I  $  ^{-}  $  of perovskite and betulin. Moreover, the similar shift was observed for the XPS Pb 4f core level spectrum, attributed to the coordination between the uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and hydroxyl group of betulin. Furthermore, we calculate the interaction energy (  $  E_{int}  $  ) of betulin on a defect-containing   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   surface by density functional theory (DFT), where the   $  E_{int}  $   is defined as   $  E_{betulin-perovskite} - E_{perovskite} - E_{betulin}  $   [57]. As shown in Fig. 1(d–f), the   $  E_{int}  $   of betulin and perovskite with iodide vacancy (  $  V_{I}  $  ) is estimated to be -0.23 eV, which favors the interaction. Through Lewis acid-base reaction, the hydroxyl group of betulin passivates the uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   resulting from iodide vacancies by donating

electrons [58]. The interaction between betulin and perovskite with MA vacancy (  $  V_{MA}  $  ) is impeded due to a positive value of   $  E_{int}  $   with 0.02 eV. Betulin on a perovskite surface with both   $  V_{I}  $   and   $  V_{MA}  $   defects causes a lower   $  E_{int}  $   of -0.76 eV, meaning that there is a more efficient interaction between betulin and perovskite by the coexistence of MA vacancies attributed to a better spatial configuration between betulin and perovskite [57]. In addition, the betulin has an extremely excellent hydrophobicity with the water contact angle as high as 98.2°, see Fig. 1(g). After betulin passivating, the contact angles of the perovskite films impressively increase from 36.7° to 74.1°, which could effectively prevent the permeation of moisture into perovskites. The multiple positive effects of betulin on perovskites are schematically illustrated in Fig. 1(h).

To explore the impact of the forest-based biomaterial on the photovoltaic performance, we fabricate the inverted p-i-n planar PSCs with the configuration of ITO/PTAA:F4TCNQ/Perovskite/PCBM/BCP/Ag in Fig. 2(a). The current density-voltage  $ (J - V) $  curves of the PSCs with the different betulin concentrations are displayed in Fig. S3 and Table S1. The optimized concentration with the champion device is  $ 0.001\mathrm{wt}\% $ . The similar concentration was demonstrated to be sufficient to passivate perovskite defects in previous reports [39,52]. In this study, more betulin concentration would cause the loss of the device efficiency as the betulin in nature is an insulator. The  $ J - V $  curves of the pristine and passivated devices in both forward and reverse scans are plotted in Fig. 2(b), and the corresponding photovoltaic parameters are summarized in Table 1. The pristine device gives an average PCE of  $ 19.14\% $  with a  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  of  $ 1.078\mathrm{V} $ , a  $ J_{\mathrm{sc}} $  of  $ 22.59\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $  and a FF of  $ 78.62\% $ . The passivated device exhibits a clear improvement in all device parameters and features the high PCE of  $ 21.15\% $ , with a  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  of  $ 1.106\mathrm{V} $ , a  $ J_{\mathrm{sc}} $  of  $ 23.43\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $  and a FF of  $ 81.57\% $ . The hysteresis index  $ (\mathrm{HI} = \frac{\mathrm{PCE}_{\mathrm{reverse}} - \mathrm{PCE}_{\mathrm{forward}}}{\mathrm{PCE}_{\mathrm{reverse}}}) $  evidently decreases, resulting from the suppression of ion migration and surface charge trapping. Over 20 devices from the different batches confirm the excellent reproducibility as shown in Fig. S4. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra in Fig. 2(c) exhibit a significant enhancement over the whole absorption region for the passivated devices. The integrated  $ J_{\mathrm{sc}} $  of the pristine and passivated devices are calculated to be 21.77 and  $ 22.50\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $ , respectively, in accord with the values obtained from the  $ J - V $  curves. The champion device exhibits a steady-state photocurrent of  $ 22.21\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $  and a high stabilized PCE of  $ 20.88\% $  under a maximum power point of  $ 0.94\mathrm{V} $  for  $ 300~s $ , see Fig. 2(d).

To gain more insight into the improved device performance, a series of technical characterizations of the morphological, optical, structural, and electronic properties are carried out. The top view scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images show that the perovskite crystal grain sizes increase after introducing betulin (Fig. S5). A possible cause is that the coordination and hydrogen bonding interactions between betulin and perovskite increase the critical Gibbs free energy of nucleation and slow down the growth of the polycrystalline perovskites. The larger grain sizes yield slightly improved UV-vis absorbance of the passivated perovskite films (Fig. S6). The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns imply that the presence of betulin does not affect the cubic perovskite phase crystal structures (Fig. S7). The surface root-mean-square (RMS) roughness of the passivated perovskite films decreases from 9.74 to  $ 8.34\mathrm{nm} $  in the atomic force microscopy (AFM) images (Fig. S8), which can enhance the top-down charge transport at contact. The ultraviolet photoelectron spectra show that there is no clear change for the work function and ionization potential before and after betulin modification (Fig. S9), which is attributed to too small amount of the insulating passivator.

The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) of the perovskite films are performed to further explore the charge carrier dynamics in Fig. 3(a and b). The improved PL density provides the compelling evidence that betulin reduces the defects and improves the charge transport. The PL lifetimes are estimated by fitting the TRPL decay via a biexponential equation (see the experiment section for details). The fast decay lifetime  $ (\tau_{1} = 4.19 $  ns) and slow decay lifetime  $ (\tau_{2} = 49.48 $  ns) in the betulin-passivated perovskite film are larger than the pristine perovskite film  $ (\tau_{1} = 3.64 $  ns,  $ \tau_{2} = 30.68 $  ns). The prolonged carrier decay lifetimes suggest the formation of the high-quality perovskite film with lower trap densities, further proving that the betulin is an effective defect passivator. Fig. 3(c) presents the transient photovoltage (TPV) decay. The pristine device has a charge carrier lifetime of  $ 36.1~\mu \mathrm{s} $ , which agrees with the previous report [59]. It is clear that the passivated device shows a longer lifetime of  $ 54.3~\mu \mathrm{s} $ , verifying that the charge recombination has been prohibited after betulin modification.

We carefully characterize the dark J-V curves of the electron-only device with a structure of   $  ITO/SnO_{2}/perovskite/PCBM/BCP/Ag  $   to evaluate the trap density and charge mobility using the space charge limit current (SCLC) model. The plots in Fig. 3d contain an ohmic contact regime at low bias, a trap-filled limit (TFL) regime at medium bias and a trap-free SCLC regime at high bias. The trap state density (  $  N_{t}  $  ) is determined by the onset of the trap-filled limit voltage (  $  V_{TFL}  $  ) according to the following equation [60]:

$$
V _ {\mathrm{TFL}} = \frac {e N _ {\mathrm{t}} L ^ {2}}{2 \varepsilon \varepsilon_ {0}},
$$

where   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   is the vacuum permittivity,   $  \varepsilon  $   is the relative dielectric constant of perovskite, and L is the thickness of the perovskite layer (  $  \sim360~nm  $  ). The calculated electron trap density decreases from   $  2.35\times10^{16}  $   (pristine) to   $  1.80\times10^{16}~cm^{-3}  $   (with betulin). The electron mobility (  $  \mu_{e}  $  ) is determined in the SCLC regime by the Mott-Gurney law [61]:

$$
J _ {\mathrm{d}} = \frac {9}{8} \varepsilon \varepsilon_ {0} \mu \frac {V ^ {2}}{L ^ {3}},
$$

where  $  J_{\mathrm{d}}  $  is the current density, and  $  V  $  is the bias voltage. The electron mobility increases from  $  1.73 \times 10^{-2}  $  (pristine) to  $  2.09 \times 10^{-2} \, \mathrm{cm}^2 \, \mathrm{V}^{-1} \, \mathrm{S}^{-1}  $  (with betulin). We further investigate the leakage current originating from the carrier recombination of the devices under dark in Fig. S10. The reverse leakage current density ( $  J_0  $ ) of the passivated device is smaller than that of the pristine device by one order magnitude.

According to the relationship between  $  J_0  $  and  $  V_{\mathrm{oc}}  $ ,  $  V_{\mathrm{oc}} = \frac{KT}{e}\ln \left(\frac{J_{\mathrm{ph}}}{J_0} + 1\right)  $  [62], (where  $  k  $

is the Boltzmann constant,  $  T  $  is the temperature, and  $  J_{\mathrm{ph}}  $  is the photocurrent density), a smaller  $  J_0  $  results in a higher  $  V_{\mathrm{oc}}  $ . Fig. S11 and Table S3 display the Nyquist plots of the electronic impedance spectroscopy (EIS) under dark. The fitted sheet series resistance ( $  R_{\mathrm{s}}  $ ) of the conductive electrode before and after betulin passivation is nearly constant because of the same device structure. Contrarily, the recombination resistance ( $  R_{\mathrm{rec}}  $ ) drastically increase from 353.0 (pristine) to 559.5  $  \Omega  $  (with betulin), which implies the suppressed recombination rate due to the reduced trap density in the perovskites.

To understand the charge recombination behavior in the PSCs, the  $ J - V $  curves under various light intensities ( $ P_{\mathrm{light}} $ ) and the results are shown in Fig. 3(c and d). The slope of the dependence of  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  versus  $ P_{\mathrm{light}} $  is used to investigate the degree of trap-assisted recombination. The biomolecular recombination in the primary process has a slope of  $ kT / e $  (where  $ k $  is Boltzmann constant, and  $ T $  is temperature), while the trap-assisted recombination dominates with the slope of  $ 2kT / e $  [63]. The slope diminishes from  $ 1.73kT / e $  for the pristine device to  $ 1.46kT / e $  for the passivated device, indicating that the betulin effectively suppresses the trap-assisted recombination. The relationship between  $ J_{\mathrm{sc}} $  and  $ P_{\mathrm{light}} $  can be expressed by  $ J^{\infty}P_{\mathrm{light}}^{a} $ , where the exponential factor  $ \alpha $  represents the extent of biomolecular recombination [64]. The extracted  $ \alpha $  value increases from 0.90 to 0.95 after betulin modification, meaning that the bimolecular recombination is also reduced in the passivated devices. The suppressed trap-assisted recombination and bimolecular recombination provide a better charge transport that leads to the improvement of the device PCE.

Device stability is one of the most important issues for the PSC commercialization. As previously discussed, betulin passivates the defects through Lewis acid-base reaction, and stabilizes the perovskite crystal structure by hydrogen bonding, as well as it is very excellent at transforming the perovskite film surface into a more hydrophobic phase that prevents moisture penetration. Fig. 4(c) shows the normalized efficiency decay of the bare PSCs without encapsulation under ambient air in  $ 60\% \pm 5\% $  relative humidity at room temperature. The betulin-passivated device could maintain  $ 90\% $  of the initial PCE after 30 days, exhibiting the better stability, while the PCE of the pristine device largely drops to  $ 57\% $  for the same time. Thermal stability of the device is also tested under nitrogen atmosphere at  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  in Fig. 4(d). The passivated device obtains  $ 75\% $  of its initial PCE after thermal aging for 300 hours, in striking contrast, the pristine device quickly declines to  $ 47\% $ . The photostability of the device was measured with light soaking ( $ \approx 100\mathrm{mWcm}^{-2} $ ,  $ 5000\mathrm{k} $  white LED,  $ T_{\mathrm{device}} = 50^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ ) in nitrogen atmosphere (Fig. S12). The device with betulin remained  $ 55\% $  of the initial efficiency after light soaking for  $ 250\mathrm{h} $ , while the pristine device only maintained  $ 20\% $ . These results provide the strong positive impact of the betulin on the enhanced stability of the PSCs.

# 4. Conclusions

The biomaterial betulin was used as a passivating additive for perovskites. The coordination and hydrogen bonding between  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  and betulin can improve both efficiency and stability of PSCs. Betulin can effectively passivate uncoordinated lead ions in perovskites via sharing the lone pair electrons of hydroxyl group, thus promoting charge transport. The PCEs increase from  $ 19.14\% $  to  $ 21.15\% $ , with the increase of  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $ ,  $ J_{\mathrm{sc}} $ , and FF. The passivated devices kept  $ 90\% $ ,  $ 75\% $  and  $ 55\% $  of the initial efficiency after 30 days at room temperature in ambient air with  $ 60 \pm 5\% $  relative humidity, after  $ 300\mathrm{h} $  at  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  under nitrogen, and after  $ 250\mathrm{h} $  light soaking in nitrogen atmosphere, respectively. This work demonstrates the potential of biomaterials in developing high-performance PSCs.

# Highlights

1. Nontoxic biomaterial Botulin as additive can effectively passivate the defects in perovskite and yield a decent PCE of 21.15%.

2. Betulin passivates uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  via Lewis acid-base reaction, stabilizes perovskite crystal structures via hydrogen bonding, and suppresses ion migration.

3. Betulin on perovskite surface with  $ \mathrm{V_I} $  and  $ \mathrm{V_{MA}} $  defects causes a lower  $ E_{\mathrm{int}} $  of -0.76 eV, suggesting that there is a more effective interaction between betulin and perovskite. (DFT calculation)
