# Stable Inverted Planar Perovskite Solar Cells with Low-Temperature-Processed Hole-Transport Bilayer

Zhongmin Zhou, Xing Li, Molang Cai, Fengxian Xie, Yongzhen Wu, Zhang Lan, Xudong Yang,  $  ^{*}  $   Yinghuai Qiang, Ashraful Islam, and Liyuan Han  $  ^{*}  $

Low-temperature-processed perovskite solar cells (PSCs), which can be fabricated on rigid or flexible substrates, are attracting increasing attention because they have a wide range of potential applications. In this study, the stability of reduced graphene oxide and the ability of a poly(triarylamine) underlayer to improve the quality of overlying perovskite films to construct hole-transport bilayer by means of a low-temperature method are taken advantage of. The bilayer is used in both flexible and rigid inverted planar PSCs with the following configuration: substrate/indium tin oxide/reduced graphene oxide/polytriarylamine/CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ PbI $ _3 $ /PCBM/bathocuproine/Ag (PCBM = [6,6]-phenyl- $ \mathbf{C}_{61} $ -butyric acid methyl ester). The flexible and rigid PSCs show power conversion efficiencies of 15.7 and 17.2%, respectively, for the aperture area of 1.02 cm $ ^2 $ . Moreover, the PSC based the bilayer shows outstanding light-soaking stability, retaining  $ \approx $ 90% of its original efficiency after continuous illumination for 500 h at 100 mW cm $ ^{-2} $ .

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have recently attracted considerable interest owing to their high power conversion efficiency (PCE) and low production cost.  $  ^{[1-3]}  $   Two types of cell structures have been widely adopted for PSCs: mesoporous structure and inverted planar heterojunction structures.  $  ^{[4,5]}  $   Mesoporous-structured PSCs, for which the highest certified PCE is 22.1%,  $  ^{[6]}  $   are generally constructed with the following standard configuration: front electrode/compact   $  TiO_{2}  $  /mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $  /perovskite/hole-transporting material (HTM)/rear electrode. However, the mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer is obtained by sintering at high temperature (  $  \approx500\;^{\circ}C  $  ), which restricts the choice of substrate materials and requires high energy consumption during processing. Therefore, the development of low-temperature-processed

$ \mathrm{Cu_2O} $ , CuI, CuSCN,  $ \mathrm{NiO_x} $ , and  $ \mathrm{CuO_x}^{[13-17]} $  have been applied as alternative HTMs in inverted PSCs, but the PCEs of such PSCs are inferior to those of PEDOT:PSS-based PSCs. Therefore, developing low-temperature-processed HTMs for stable, efficient inverted PSCs is a high priority.

Graphene oxide (GO) has been used as a HTM in inverted PSCs,  $  ^{[18-20]}  $   and GO-based PSCs show excellent long-term stability because GO is compatible with perovskite materials and absorbs strongly in the near-UV region (300–400 nm).  $  ^{[20]}  $   However, the PCEs of devices using GO or reduced GO (r-GO) as a HTM are unsatisfactory, perhaps because the nonuniformity of GO films result in poor morphology of the overlying perovskite films. Recently, inverted PSCs fabricated with an organic polymer, poly(triarylamine) (PTAA), as a HTM have been reported to show good performance,  $  ^{[21,22]}  $   due mainly to the film-forming ability and hydrophobicity of PTAA, which facilitate the growth of large perovskite grains.  $  ^{[23]}  $   However, unlike GO, PTAA absorbs only weakly in the near-UV region, and thus PTAA-based devices show poor light-soaking stability.

The use of interfacial bilayer has been reported to improve the performance of inverted PSCs,  $  ^{[24]}  $  and it is reasonable to expect that combining GO and PTAA to form hole-transport bilayer for low-temperature-processed inverted PSCs would result in high PCE and long-term stability. Therefore, in this study, we investigated the use of low-temperature-processed (<150 °C) hole-transport bilayer consisting of r-GO and PTAA to fabricate PSCs. The bilayer, which completely covered the ITO, cut off the near-UV band, and the alignment of the energy level of each layer was appropriate. Flexible PSCs fabricated on

charge-transport layers is highly desirable, especially to offer a wider selection of flexible substrates for practical applications.

Inverted PSCs have the advantages of easy fabrication, long-term stability, and negligible hysteresis, but the certified highest PCE (18.2%, 1.02 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ) is still lower than that of PSCs based mesoporous TiO  $  _{2}  $  .  $  ^{[7]}  $   Many inverted PSCs are constructed with the PEDOT:PSS/perovskite/PCBM heterojunction configuration (PEDOT = poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) and PSS = polystyrene sulfonate),  $  ^{[8-11]}  $   and all the constituent materials can be fabricated layer by layer at low temperatures (<150 °C). In summary, a low-temperature-processed r-GO/PTAA bilayer was introduced into inverted PSCs. The bilayer improved the stability of the devices because r-GO strongly absorbs near-UV light and the PTAA layer can act as defect complement for r-GO into the device structure. The bilayer had the benefit of resulting in larger perovskite grains, thus decreasing grain boundary density and enhancing light harvesting. In addition, the energy-level gradient of different layers effectively inhibited charge recombination at the HTM/perovskite interface and facilitated charge extraction and transport within the devices. The PCEs for devices fabricated on rigid and flexible substrates were 17.2 and  $ 15.7\% $ , respectively, on an active area of  $ 1.02~\mathrm{cm}^2 $ . Moreover, a device with the hole-transport bilayer exhibited out

surfaces and changes that occur inside the perovskite film under illumination. We were encouraged to find that the device with the hole-transport bilayer retained   $  \approx90\%  $   of its original PCE even after 500 h of continuous illumination. Besides, the long-term stability measurement of device with r-GO as HTM was also conducted under the same condition as device with r-GO/PTAA, and the device showed good stability similar to the device with r-GO/PTAA after 500 h of continuous illumination as shown in Figure 5a. In addition, we also measured the stability of devices with r-GO, PTAA, and r-GO/PTAA at their maximum power point (MPPT) under continuous 1 sun illumination, respectively. Both solar cells with r-GO/PTAA and r-GO kept   $  \approx96\%  $   of their original efficiencies, and the cell based PTAA showed inferior stability for retaining only   $  \approx67\%  $   of the original PCE after 100 h continuous illumination as shown in Figure S8 (Supporting Information), indicating that the high stability of the device with bilayer as HTM is due to the introduction of r-GO.

# Experimental Section

Materials: The following chemicals were obtained from commercial suppliers and used as received:   $  PbI_{2}  $   (99%, Sigma-Aldrich),   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $   (>98%, Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Japan), PCBM (99.5%, Lumtec Co., Taiwan), bathocuproine (Wako), Graphene oxide (2 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  , Sigma-Aldrich), and PTAA (Sigma-Aldrich). Superdehydrated dimethylsulfoxide, dimethylformamide, toluene, chlorobenzene, and methanol were purchased from Wako, Japan.

Device Fabrication: An aqueous dispersion of GO (100 μL, 0.5 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was spin-coated onto an ITO substrate at a rotation speed of 3000 rpm for 30 s, and the resulting GO film was heated at 100, 150, or 200 °C for 10–90 min to afford a r-GO film. The r-GO film was completely covered with a uniform layer of PTAA (5–50 nm thickness) by spin-coating 60 μL of a PTAA solution in toluene (10-80 mg ml  $  ^{-1}  $  ) at 4000 rpm for 30 s and then heating the film at 100 °C for 2 min. Next, a MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  precursor solution (100 μL) consisting of PbI  $  _{2}  $  (1660 mg) and CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}  $  I (569 mg) dissolved in 3 mL of 1:5 (v/v) dimethylsulfoxide/dimethylformamide was dropped onto the r-GO/PTAA film, and the film was spin-coated at 1000 rpm for 12 s and then at 5000 rpm for 30 s. Chlorobenzene (100 μL) was dropped onto the perovskite-coated r-GO/PTAA film 10 s after the rotation speed was increased to 5000 rpm, and the film was heated at 100 °C for 10 min. Then a solution of PCBM in chlorobenzene (20 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was spin-coated on top of the film at a rotation speed of 1000 rpm for 30 s. A saturated methol solution of bathocuproine (140 μL) was dripped onto the PCBM film within 15 s when the spinning began at a rotation speed of 6000 rpm for 30 s. Device fabrication was
