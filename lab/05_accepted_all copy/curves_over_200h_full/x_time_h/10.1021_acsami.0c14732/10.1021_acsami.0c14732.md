# Sodium Dodecylbenzene Sulfonate Interface Modification of Methylammonium Lead Iodide for Surface Passivation of Perovskite Solar Cells

Yuqin Zou, Renjun Guo, Ali Buyruk, Wei Chen, Tianxiao Xiao, Shanshan Yin, Xinyu Jiang, Lucas P. Kreuzer, Cheng Mu, Tayebeh Ameri, Matthias Schwartzkopf, Stephan V. Roth, and Peter Müller-Buschbaum*

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been developed as a promising photovoltaic technology because of their excellent photovoltaic performance. However, interfacial recombination and charge carrier transport losses at the surface greatly limit the performance and stability of PSCs. In this work, the fabrication of high-quality PSCs based on methylammonium lead iodide with excellent ambient stability is reported. An anionic surfactant, sodium dodecylbenzene sulfonate (SDBS), is introduced to simultaneously passivate the defect states and stabilize the cubic phase of the perovskite film. The SDBS located at grain boundaries and the surface of the active layer can effectively passivate under-coordinated lead ions and protect the perovskite components from water-induced degradation. As a result, a champion power conversion efficiency

(PCE) of  $ 19.42\% $  is achieved with an open-circuit voltage  $ (V_{\mathrm{OC}}) $  of  $ 1.12\mathrm{V} $ , a short-circuit current  $ (J_{\mathrm{SC}}) $  of  $ 23.23\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $ , and a fill factor (FF) of  $ 74\% $  in combination with superior moisture stability. The SDBS-passivated devices retain  $ 80\% $  of their initial average PCE after  $ 2112\mathrm{h} $  of storage under ambient conditions.

KEYWORDS: perovskite solar cells, functional ligand, stability, defect passivation, GIWAXS

# 1. INTRODUCTION

Lead-halide perovskite materials have recently attracted significant attention for their impressive optoelectronic properties including high absorption coefficients, high defect tolerance, and long charge carrier diffusion lengths.  $  ^{1-5}  $   Moreover, low-cost and large-scale fabrication is also an essential advantage for perovskite functional films to be considered as promising candidates in various optoelectronic applications. Benefiting from these favorable properties, perovskite solar cells (PSCs) present remarkable power conversion efficiency (PCE) values and outstanding operational stability.  $  ^{6-9}  $   In the past decade, the PCE has been significantly improved from 3.8% to a certified PCE of 25.2%, reflecting enormous development prospects and tremendous potential for commercialization.  $  ^{10,11}  $

However, for achieving real-world applications, the long-term stability of PSCs under ambient conditions in terms of thermal load, light, oxygen, and moisture exposure is still a huge challenge.  $  ^{12-16}  $   The instability of PSCs mainly arises from the inherently unstable nature of perovskite materials caused by component degradation,  $  ^{17}  $   defect states,  $  ^{18}  $   and undercoordinated cations and anions (I  $  ^{-}  $  , MA  $  ^{+}  $  , and Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  ).  $  ^{19,20}  $

The commonly used methylammonium (MA $  ^{+}  $ ) cation has relatively poor stability and is prone to degrade and volatilize, resulting in a deterioration of the active layer components. $ ^{21} $  Furthermore, the free lead ions have low formation energy and accumulate at the surface and grain boundaries, which is considered to be one of the main sources of defect states. $ ^{18} $  To effectively solve these stability issues, various methods have been employed such as compositional engineering, $ ^{21,22} $  dimension regulation, $ ^{23-25} $  and surface passivation. $ ^{26-28} $  Among all methods, surface passivation has been regarded as an effective strategy to fabricate high-performance PSCs under ambient conditions with remarkable moisture stability. For example, Fu and Jiang et al. revealed that long-chain alkyl cations, such as BA $  ^{+}  $ , PEA $  ^{+}  $ , or FPEA $  ^{+}  $ , bind to the surface of the perovskite and thereby reduce the surface energy. Based on this approach, they obtained high-performing PSCs with good light stability and low defect density.  $  ^{35}  $   Therefore, passivating the detrimental defects accumulating at the grain boundaries and at the surface of the perovskite thin films and improving the longevity of the device by functionalizing the film surface with functional ligands is a promising approach.

In the present study, we demonstrate the fabrication of highly stable and efficient PSCs by interface modification and surface passivation with sodium dodecylbenzene sulfonate (SDBS). We illustrate that the SDBS surfactant does not only improve the resistance of the perovskite film against moisture by its hydrophobicity but also interacts with uncoordinated lead ions by its sulfonic functional groups. The introduction of SDBS can simultaneously passivate defect states, facilitate charge carrier extraction, and reduce charge carrier recombination. By introducing SDBS, the PCE of the PSCs is increased from an average of 15.23% (without SDBS) to 17.01% (with SDBS). In addition, we introduce a dark storage test under the ISOS-D-1 condition.  $  ^{36}  $   It demonstrates the significantly enhanced tolerance of the PSCs against oxygen, moisture, and other aggressive atmospheric components naturally present in air. We maintain 80% of the initial PCE of the PSCs after 3 months without device encapsulation.

# 2. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

SDBS is a typical anionic surfactant with a hydrophobic alkylphenyl group (long alkyl chain) and a hydrophilic sulfonic group   $  (-SO_{3}^{-})  $  . The molecular formula is   $  C_{18}H_{29}NaO_{3}S  $   (see Figure 1). SDBS is chosen as an additive to tune the perovskite crystallization and passivate the detrimental defects. As previously reported, the negatively charged sulfonic groups of SDBS can form complexes with uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and   $  MA^{+}  $  existing on the surface of the perovskite thin film (Figure 1a).  $  ^{35,37,38}  $   The electrostatic interaction between   $  -SO_{3}^{-}  $  and   $  MA^{+}  $  causes the hydrophilic sulfonic group of the SDBS molecules to face the inside of the perovskite film, as shown in Figure 1b. This will result in a decrease of the surface tension between the hole transport layer (HTL) and the active layer and increase the adhesion.  $  ^{38-40}  $   Because of the steric effects, the hydrophobic long alkyl chain aggregated on the surface of the perovskite film will point outward for resisting the infiltration of external moisture, which improves the stability of the active layer components under humid ambient conditions. More importantly, poor contact between the HTL (low polarization of the organic material) and the perovskite (high polarization) is not sufficiently conducive to obtain high-performance PSCs. SDBS as an ambipolar passivation agent, with a polar part   $  (-SO_{3}^{-}, good\ contact\ with\ polar\ perovskite\ surface)  $   and a non-polar part (alkyl chain, contact with spiro-OMeTAD), can greatly enhance contact between the polar perovskite and relatively lower polarized HTM. Thereby, SDBS minimizes the barrier and accelerates the charge carrier transport, which is beneficial for obtaining high current densities.  $  ^{41}  $

2.2. Optoelectronic Characterization. To verify the coordination between sulfonic groups and lead ions on the perovskite surface, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy measurements are done on  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  films without and with SDBS modification (Figure 3a). SDBS is measured as a reference as well. Thereby the peaks appearing in the spectra at 2858 and  $ 2926~\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $  are assigned to the asymmetric and symmetric  $ -\mathrm{CH}_2 $  stretching from SDBS. The peaks located at 1046 and  $ 1191~\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $  are attributed to the  $ \mathrm{S = O} $  stretching from the sulfonic group of SDBS. Different from the pristine sample, these peaks are also found in the FTIR spectra of the SDBS-modified  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  films, which proves that SDBS remained on the surface of the perovskite layer. The peak located at  $ 1010~\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $  is attributed to the coordination complex between  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2 + }} $  and the  $ \mathrm{S - O} $  stretching vibrations from SDBS,

where the oxygen in the sulfonic group contains two lone electron pairs and the 6p orbitals of Pb contain empty orbitals. Compared to pure SDBS, there is a slight shift in the peak position by   $  -2\ cm^{-1}  $  , which can be attributed to the complexation in the perovskite layer (Figure S5). Hence, the   $  S-O^{-}  $  could provide lone electron pairs to occupy the empty orbitals of Pb, which gives rise to a coordination complex and inhibits the formation of morphological defects during the processing of the perovskite film.  $  ^{35,37}  $

To explore the impact of SDBS on the active layer band gap, we measure ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption spectra of the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  films without and with SDBS modification (Figure 3b). We compare two different SDBS concentrations (1 and 2 mg/mL). A slight red shift occurs in the UV–vis spectra for the SDBS-passivated perovskite films, which increases with SDBS concentration increase (Figure S6). Thus, the SDBS modification decreases the apparent band gap because SDBS passivation is reducing the traps inside the MAPI band gap. To further confirm the influence of SDBS on the apparent band gap of the perovskite layer, we collect steady-state photoluminescence spectra (PL) as seen in Figures 3c and S7. The red shift in the PL increases with an increasing amount of SDBS. After SDBS passivation, the PL peak position red-shifts about 10 nm, which indicates that the SDBS-passivated sample reveals a lower apparent optical band gap and consequently allows for more light absorption than the pristine MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  sample from the sunlight spectrum. Moreover, the 1 mg/mL SDBS-passivated MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  films exhibit a larger rise in the steady-state PL intensity in comparison to the pristine film (Figure S7) and when 2 mg/mL SDBS is used. Thus, a moderate amount of SDBS (1 mg/mL) significantly reduces the trap state density, acting as non-radiative recombination centers in the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  film, which will contribute to the

2.3. Structure Characterization. The effect of SDBS on the morphology of the perovskite films is probed with top-view scanning electron microscopy (SEM) as shown in Figure S8. Only minor changes are seen with SEM. Thus, the SDBS surface passivation does not affect the surface morphology of the perovskite films.

We attribute the applied soaking time before spin coating SDBS and the thermal annealing after spin coating to enable the penetration of SDBS inside the perovskite film, instead of having SDBS only on the perovskite film surface.

It is worth noting that additional diffraction signals are found in the GIWAXS data of the pristine and the SDBS-modified perovskite films, respectively. The intensity rings located near   $  q = 0.69 \, \AA^{-1}  $   and   $  q = 0.91 \, \AA^{-1}  $   positions are attributed to the methylammonium iodide (MAI) (001) and   $  PbI_{2}  $   (001) Bragg peaks, respectively.  $  ^{55}  $   With SDBS modification, the MAI ring disappears while the   $  PbI_{2}  $   ring appears. Hence, we hypothesize that the residual MAI may be consumed by SDBS because the negatively charged benzenesulfonic acid ion of SDBS can easily coordinate with the positively charged   $  MA^{+}  $  to form complexes.

By performing an azimuthal integration, we obtain pseudo-X-ray diffraction (XRD) data (Figure 5a). The Bragg peaks at  $  q = 1.03 \, \text{\AA}^{-1}  $  and  $  q = 2.08 \, \text{\AA}^{-1}  $  arising from the (110) and (220) lattice plane reflections of the perovskite crystals are well visible. The peak located at  $  q = 2.32 \, \text{\AA}^{-1}  $  is assigned to the Si peak. Irrespective of the SDBS treatment, the peak intensities and widths do not change significantly. Because of the missing wedge in GIWAXS, the pseudo-XRD data are complemented with XRD measurements (Figure 5b). We observe the same Bragg peaks from the tetragonal MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite structure which is in good agreement with the literature. $ ^{52} $  The position of the (110) Bragg peak shifts slightly toward higher  $  q  $  values (Figure S12) in case of an SDBS treatment, which indicates that the lattice constant changes by 0.08% after SDBS treatment. The smaller lattice constant is attributed to the penetration of SDBS into the active layer, which squeezes the perovskite crystal and impacts on deep defects.

To confirm the reaction between SDBS and MA  $  ^{+}  $  , XRD patterns of pure MAI, pure SDBS, MAI, and SDBS mixed films

are measured, and the results are shown in Figure 5c. The Bragg peaks located at 0.70, 1.41, and 1.86 Å  $  ^{-1}  $  belong to MAI.  $  ^{56,57}  $  After SDBS passivation, a new peak marked with an asterisk (*) appears at 1.29 Å  $  ^{-1}  $ . We attribute this peak to complexes, which are formed by the reaction between MAI and SDBS. To further study the interactions of SDBS and MAI, we also measure XRD of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  films doped with 0, 10, and 30% MAI excess under the same SDBS concentration treatment (1 mg/mL) as shown in Figure 5d. Again, we find the Bragg peaks from the tetragonal MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  perovskite structure. The small peak located at q = 0.91 Å  $  ^{-1}  $  (marked with a blue triangle) is assigned to unreacted PbI  $  _{2}  $ . Previous literature reports that a small amount of PbI  $  _{2}  $  can passivate the defect states and improve the performance of PSCs.  $  ^{58,59}  $  In addition, we see a peak located at q = 0.83 Å  $  ^{-1}  $  (marked with a blue asterisk) which does not match either the pure PbI  $  _{2}  $  or MAI tetragonal phase. According to the literature, this peak can be assigned to the (MA)  $  _{4}  $  PbI  $  _{6}  $  phase.  $  ^{60}  $  With increasing MAI concentration, the PbI  $  _{2}  $  peak disappears and the peak located at q = 0.83 Å  $  ^{-1}  $  appears. After SDBS passivation, the peak disappears. This finding can be well explained by an interaction of SDBS and MA  $  ^{+}  $ , resulting in a reduced number of defect states caused by free MA  $  ^{+}  $  ions. In addition, as seen in Figure S13, the peak at q = 1.01 Å  $  ^{-1}  $  shifts toward higher q values after SDBS passivation, which is in good agreement with previous results (Figure 5b). It is worth mentioning that an indication of a phase transition is seen at (220) Bragg peak of the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  film with excess MAI (Figure S14).  $  ^{61,62}  $  The two Bragg peaks indexed with (004) and (220) are located at q = 1.97–2.04 Å  $  ^{-1}  $  signify the tetragonal phase of the CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}  $  PbI  $  _{3}  $  perovskite. They appear with increasing MAI content.  $  ^{63,64}  $  After SDBS treatment, these peaks disappear and merge to the (200) Bragg peak of the cubic phase at q = 2.01 Å  $  ^{-1}  $  (Figure S14). Thus, SDBS can stabilize the cubic phase of the perovskite and inhibit the transformation of the cubic phase to the tetragonal phase.

# 3. CONCLUSIONS

In summary, we demonstrate the role of an anionic surfactant with a long-alkyl-chain in improving the optoelectronic properties by suppressing trap-state density and controlling the crystal orientation. The incorporation of SDBS additive can effectively passivate defects with the strong interaction between   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and the sulfonic group (  $  -SO_{3}^{-}  $  ) and improve the ambient stability by a hydrophobic alkyl-phenyl group. Benefiting from the excellent properties of SDBS, the PCE of the PSCs is further improved to over 19% for our champion device. Moreover, the ambient stability is also significantly improved by SDBS passivation. Under ambient conditions (30–40% relative humidity), 80% of its initial efficiency remains for PSCs after 2112 h without encapsulation. Thereby, our work represents an economic pathway toward the manufacturing of high-performance PSCs in combination with the high stability of the devices. It provides valuable insights into further understanding of structure passivation via interface modification in polycrystalline perovskite films, including defect type and concentration.
