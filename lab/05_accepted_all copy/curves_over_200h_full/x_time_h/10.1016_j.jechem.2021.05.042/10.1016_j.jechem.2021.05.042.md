# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic hybrid three-dimensional (3D) perovskites with superior photoelectrical properties have emerged as an outstanding material category for various applications, especially in next-generation photovoltaics. The power conversion efficiency (PCE) for hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with 3D perovskite has dramatically risen to a certified value of  $ 25.5\% $  in 2020, which is comparable to that of monocrystalline silicon  $ (26.1\%) $  and copper indium gallium selenide solar cells  $ (23.4\%) $  (Best Research-Cell Efficiency Chart, https://www.nrel.gov/pv/cell-efficiency.html, accessed 09/2020). The replacement of small cations with bulkier organic cations containing long alkyl chains or benzene rings to form a thin two-dimensional (2D) perovskite passivation layer on three-dimensional (3D) perovskite (2D/3D) has become a promising strategy for improving both the efficiency and stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). The 2D layer defines the interfacial chemistry and physics at the 2D/3D bilayer and endows the 2D/3D structure with better chemical and thermal stability. Herein, 2D/3D  $ (\mathrm{CF}_3 $  -PEA)  $ 2\mathrm{FA}_{0.85}\mathrm{MA}_{0.15}\mathrm{Pb}_{2}\mathrm{I}_7 / \mathrm{FA}_{0.85}\mathrm{MA}_{0.15}\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  planar heterojunction perovskite was produced using a facile interfacial ion exchange process. The 2D  $ (\mathrm{CF}_3 $  -PEA)  $ 2\mathrm{FA}_{0.85}\mathrm{MA}_{0.15}\mathrm{Pb}_{2}\mathrm{I}_7 $  capping layer can not only passivate the  $ \mathrm{FA}_{0.85}\mathrm{MA}_{0.15}\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  film but also act as super-hydrophobic layer to inhibit water diffusion and significantly enhance the stability. The 2D capping layer can also establish a unique graded band structure at the perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD interface and lead to p-type doping for Spiro-OMeTAD layer which is beneficial for efficient charge transport. Optimized PSCs based on this 2D/3D heterojunction yield a champion power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 23.1\% $  and improved stability. The device maintains  $ 84\% $  output for  $ 2400\mathrm{h} $  aging under ambient environmental conditions without encapsulation, and maintains  $ 81\% $  for  $ 200\mathrm{h} $  under illumination with encapsulation. This work will inspire the design of more fluorinated 2D perovskite interfaces for advanced photovoltaics and beyond.

It is also interesting to find that the 2D perovskites, such as  $ \mathrm{PEA}_2\mathrm{PbI}_4 $  and  $ \mathrm{BA}_2\mathrm{PbI}_4 $  are capable of passivating the surface defects of 3D perovskite, thus reducing the non-radiative recombination and improving the open-circuit voltage  $ (V_{\mathrm{oc}}) $  of devices [5,6]. Compared to the state-of-the-art n-butylammonium (BA) and phenylethylammonium (PEA) cations for popular 2D Ruddlesden-Popper perovskite, a large 4-trifluoromethyl phenylethylammonium (CF₃PEA) cation with three F ions will further enhance the hydrophobicity of the cations and 2D perovskite and reduce the free energy of the perovskite surface to endow the perovskite with superior stability against moisture in ambient air [9–11]. In this work, we demonstrated a new 2D (CF₃-PEA)₂FA₀.₈₅MA₀.₁₅Pb₂I₇ perovskite top layer formed on the surface of FA₀.₈₅MA₀.₁₅PbI₃ through a facile solvent-assisted interfacial ion exchange process. A combined theoretical and experimental study of the 2D/3D perovskite film and devices revealed that the fluorinated 2D perovskites with CF₃PEA cations can enhance the crystallinity, passivate the surface defects, prolong the carrier lifetimes of 3D perovskite, retard charge recombination at the interface and form better energy-level-matching with the hole transport layer (HTL), showing a positive effect on the open circuit voltage of PSCs. Importantly, the presence of fluorine atoms induces the creation of kinetic barriers that slow the intrusion of O₂ and H₂O and leads to p-type doping for the Spiro-OMeTAD layer [12–14]. As a consequence, the PSCs based on the new 2D/3D heterojunction exhibit a champion PCE of 23.1%, one of the highest values for 2D/3D hybrid PSCs to date, and substantially improved air and illumination stability compared to classical PEA₂PbI₄-based 2D/3D PSCs.[15,16] In addition, thanks to the ultra-hydrophobic nature of these fluorinated organic cations, the 2D/3D hybrid PSCs show excellent operational stability, retaining over 84% of their initial PCE after 2400 h aging in ambient air, and over 80% of their initial PCE for a period of 200 h under continuous simulated solar irradiation. This work highlights the importance of using fluorinated cations for protective 2D perovskite, providing a novel (CF₃-PEA)₂FA₀.₈₅MA₀.₁₅Pb₂I₇ capping layer to stabilize FA₀.₈₅MA₀.₁₅PbI₃ perovskite, which is paramount for the proper design of 2D/3D hybrid perovskite for various optoelectronic applications.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1.Materials

N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%) and dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO, 99.9%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co. Ltd. 4-tert-butylpyridine (t-BP), chlorobenzene (CB) and bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide lithium salt (Li-TFSI) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Lead (II) iodide (PbI  $  _{2}  $  , 99.99%), formamidinium iodide (FAI,   $  \geq  $  99.5%) methylammonium iodide (MAI,   $  \geq  $  99.5%) and 4-trifluoromethylphenethylamine hydroiodide were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp.

# 2.2. Device fabrication

Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)/glass substrates were cleaned by ultra-sonication in a special cleaning concentrate, which was a mixed solvent of detergent and ultrapure water with a volume ratio of 1:100 for 30 min. Then, the FTO/glass substrates were cleaned by ultra-sonication in ultrapure water for 15 min, with the washing repeated three times. The cleaned substrates were dried by nitrogen flow and treated by an ultraviolet source for 10 min before deposition of TiO₂. 200 mL ultrapure water was put into a beaker to freeze, 4500 microliters of titanium tetrachloride (TiCl₄) solution was dropped on the ice to melt it gradually. Then the FTO glass was immersed into the melted water that mixed with TiCl₄, which was put into an oven at 70 °C for 1 h. After the reaction is over, the FTO was rinsed by ultrapure water three times. TiO₂ films with 40 nm thickness can be prepared by chemical bath deposition on FTO substrates. The FA₀.₈₅MA₀.₁₅PbI₃ precursor solution (1 M) was prepared by dissolving FAI (0.1462 g), MAI (0.0238 g) and PbI₂ (0.4610 g) in 1 mL a mixed solvent of DMF and DMSO with a volume ratio of 4:1. Different quantities of CF₃PEAI were dissolved in 1 mL isopropanol and then stirred for at least 6 h to dissolve completely. The resulting solution was filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter before use. The substrate deposited with TiO₂ was dried by nitrogen flow, annealed in air at 200 °C for 30 min, and then subjected to UV-ozone treatment for 10 min to improve wettability. The perovskite solution was spin-coated onto the substrate at 1000 rpm for 10 s and then 4000 rpm for 40 s while dripping chlorobenzene onto the substrate 20–25 s before the end of the program. The spin-coated film was heated at 150 °C for 30 min to obtain a dark film. Then 80 μL of the isopropanol solution with the CF₃PEAI additive was spin-coated onto the dark film at 4000 rpm for 30 s and was heated at 80 °C for 5 min. After cooling to room temperature, 45 μL of Spiro-OMeTAD solution was spin-coated at 5000 rpm for 30 s to form the hole transport layer (HTL). The Spiro-OMeTAD solution was prepared by dissolving 90 mg of Spiro-OMeTAD in 1 mL chlorobenzene, in which 22 μL Li-TFSI (520 mg/mL in acetonitrile) solution and 36 μL 4-tert-butylpyridine solution were additives. Finally, the 80 nm Au electrode was thermally evaporated on top of the Spiro-OMeTAD after more than 8 h of oxidation. The active area of each device is 0.09 cm².

# 2.3. Device characterization

XRD patterns were acquired using a Bruker D8 GADDS Diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation. The surface morphologies of perovskite films and cross-sectional images of PSCs were obtained using a field-emission SEM (HITACHI, SU-8020). Contact angle measurements were performed on an OCA20 instrument (Data-physics). The band structure of the perovskite films were analyzed using UPS (ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher). Absorption spectra of perovskite films were characterized on a UV/Vis NIR spectrophotometer (Ocean Optics Instrument). PL (excitation at 532 nm) spectra were investigated using an Edinburgh Instruments Ltd. FLS980 spectrometer under 510-nm wavelength excitation, and TRPL spectra were measured using a PicoQuant FluoQuant 300. J-V characteristics of PSCs were measured using a solar simulator (AM 1.5G, SAN-EIELECTRIC XES-40S2-CE) calibrated with a standard silicon solar cell. All devices were tested with reverse and forward scans following standard procedures at a rate of 10 mV/s. EQE spectra of PSCs were measured using a Q Test Station 500TI system (Crowntech, Inc. USA). The monochromatic light intensity was adjusted using a reference silicon detector.

# 2.4. Density functional theory calculation

The calculation was performed using VASP code   $  [17,18]  $   in the framework of the PBE approximation   $  [19]  $  . The van der Waals interaction was treated by the Tkatchenko-Scheffler method   $  [20]  $  . The lattice constant of   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   3D perovskite was set to 6.38 Å, which was obtained by optimizing a cubic   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   unit cell. In the calculation of the adsorption energies of water and oxygen, a layer of PEAI or   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $   covered the   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   layer, which is composed of three   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   unit cell layers. The volume used in the calculations is   $  6.38 \times 6.38 \times 50 \AA^{3}  $  . In the calculation of band levels relative to vacuum, one 2D (PEA)  $  _{2}  $  FAPbI  $  _{2}  $  I  $  _{7}  $   or (CF  $  _{3}  $  PEA)  $  _{2}  $  FAPbI  $  _{2}  $  I  $  _{7}  $   layer was attached to a FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   layer, which is composed of three FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   unit cell layers. The volume used in those calculations is   $  6.38 \times 6.38 \times 100 \AA^{3}  $  . To obtain accurate energy levels relative to vacuum, dipole correction was performed. In all the calculations, a k-mesh of   $  10 \times 10 \times 1  $   was used. The cut-off energy was set to 500 eV. The convergence condition was set to   $  10^{-6}  $   eV to ensure convergence of the system to a stable state.

Optical properties of the control and perovskite films treated with   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $   at different concentrations were evaluated by UV-vis measurements, and the results are shown in Fig. 1(f) and S2. In order to make the 2D absorption peak more obvious, the concentration of the 3D perovskite precursor solution is diluted to a quarter of the original concentration. It can be found that a pronounced peak located at   $  \sim515  $   nm appears. Meanwhile, the absorption spectrum of   $  CF_{3}PEA_{2}(MA/FA)Pb_{2}I_{7}  $   film was measured and exhibited in the supporting information for comparison (Fig. S2c). According to the previous reports and our previous analysis of XRD results (Fig. 1c), the peaks at 515 nm can be attributed to absorption of 2D perovskite (  $  CF_{3}PEA_{2}(MA/FA)Pb_{2}I_{7}  $  ) [22]. As shown in Fig. S2, the perovskite film with 1.5 mg/mL   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $   treatment exhibits enhanced absorption from 400 to 500 nm compared with that of the control perovskite, which is probably attributed to the increased crystalline quality with   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $   treatment. In addition, the control and 1.5 mg/mL-  $  CF_{3}PEAI  $  -treated perovskite films exhibit very similar absorption edges, demonstrating that the optical bandgap of the perovskite has not changed significantly. Thus, the bandgap of the perovskite was barely influenced by a small amount of   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $   treatment [21,23].

Top-view SEM was applied to characterize the surface morphologies of the perovskite films. As shown in Fig. 2a), the grain size of the perovskite film is gradually increasing with the increase of  $ \mathrm{CF_3PEAI} $  concentration. The average grain size increases from 0.36 to  $ 1.05\mu \mathrm{m} $  when the concentration of  $ \mathrm{CF_3PEAI} $  increases from 0 to  $ 10\mathrm{mg / mL} $  (Fig. 2b). Though the grain size is larger when the perovskite films are treated with high concentrations of  $ \mathrm{CF_3PEAI} $  ( $ \geq 2.0\mathrm{mg / mL} $ ), many pinholes are found on the surfaces of crystalline grains (Fig. S3a). When the  $ \mathrm{CF_3PEAI} $  concentration is very high ( $ \geq 25\mathrm{mg / mL} $ ), the whole surface was damaged, as shown in Fig. S3(b, and c). In brief, it is found that  $ 1.5\mathrm{mg / mL} $  is the optimal concentration for treatment to balance the grain size, surface morphology and roughness of the perovskite films (Fig. 2c).

Then, ultrafast transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy was performed to identify the charge transfer between the 2D interfacial layer and 3D perovskite film. Due to the large absorption coefficient of the 3D perovskite at short wavelengths (400–500 nm, Fig. S2a), the TA signal of the 3D perovskite film will overwhelm that of the 2D perovskite. Therefore, the concentration of the 3D perovskite precursor solution for TA measurement was adjusted to one quarter of its original value. The perovskite films were excited with a femtosecond laser pulse (450 nm, 70.5   $  \mu  $  J/cm  $  ^{2}  $  ), and the photoinduced changes in the absorption (  $  \Delta  $  A) were probed with a time-delayed laser-generated white light pulse [24,25].

Fig. 3(a and d) shows the TA spectra of the control and  $ 1.5\mathrm{mg}/ $  ml- $ \mathrm{CF}_3 $ PEAI-treated perovskite films. Fig. 3(b and e) shows the TA spectra at various delay times extracted from Fig. 3(a and d), respectively. For the control sample, only one bleaching signal at  $ 784~\mathrm{nm} $  was observed, which is attributed to the 3D perovskite film.

For the   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $  -treated sample, besides the 3D bleaching signal at 784 nm, there are two other bleaching signals at 512 and 563 nm, which are attributed to the 2D interfacial perovskite layer. Fig. 3(c and f) shows the kinetic curves of the bleaching peaks for the control and   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $  -treated films. For the control sample, the bleaching signal at 784 nm holds nearly constant without a delay time below 320 ps and decays afterwards. In contrast, for   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $  -treated sample, the 2D bleaching signal starts to decay very early after a delay time of 8 ps. The 3D bleaching signal shows a different behavior compared to that of the control sample. The 3D bleaching signal does not decay below 600 ps but rather rises. This phenomenon may be due to the feeding of 2D perovskite excitons transferring to the 3D perovskite film [26]. When the TA signal of the 2D perovskite diminishes after a delay time of 600 ps, the feeding effect of the 2D perovskite is finished and the TA signal of the 3D perovskite begins to decay. All these TA results prove that 2D perovskite appears on the surface of the 3D perovskite film, and there is a fast charge transfer from the 2D interfacial layer to the 3D perovskite film.

Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements were applied to investigate the influences of the   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $   treatment on the photophysical properties of perovskite thin films (Fig. 4a–c). As shown in Fig. 4b, the 1.5 mg/mL-  $  CF_{3}PEAI  $  -treated perovskite thin film exhibited the highest PL peak intensity among all samples, indicating that the formation of the fluorinated 2D/3D heterojunction can passivate surface defects and reduce non-radiative recombination [27,28]. However, the PL peak intensities of perovskite films treated with high-concentration (10.0, 25.0, 50.0 and 100.0 mg/mL)   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $   were decreased compared to that of the control film, which is due to the destructive effect of high-concentration   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $   on the surface of perovskite as discussed previously (Figs. 2, S3). As expected, a PL peak at 521 nm was observed for perovskite films with   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $   treatment, which is attributed to the luminescence of 2D perovskite and consistent with the TA measurements [29]. The TRPL decay curves are shown in Fig. 4c and can be fitted by a bi-exponential decay function:   $  f(t) = A_{1}\exp(-t/\tau_{1}) + A_{2}\exp(-t/\tau_{2})  $  , where   $  A_{1}  $   and   $  A_{2}  $   are the decay amplitudes, and   $  \tau_{1}  $   and   $  \tau_{2}  $   are the decay time constants [30,31]. The fitted parameters are summarized in Table S1, Supporting Information. Consistently, the 1.5 mg/mL-  $  CF_{3}PEAI  $  -treated film shows the longest carrier lifetime (411.64 ns) among all the perovskite films. The ultra-long lifetime supports the reduction of defects on the surfaces and suppression of non-radiative recombination in the perovskite film [32,33]. The steady-state PL on the back (glass side) was also measured (Fig. S4). Only PL peaks related to 3D   $  FA_{0.85}MA_{0.15}PbI_{3}  $   can be observed, manifesting that the 2D perovskites only formed on the top surface of the 3D perovskite thin films. All these PL results further testify to the formation of a fluorinated 2D/3D heterojunction on the surface of the 3D perovskite thin film, which can effectively passivate the defects on the perovskite surface.

As shown in Fig. 4e, the glass/ $ \mathrm{CF_3PEAI} $  (1.5 mg/mL)-treated perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD device shows the lowest PL intensity, manifesting that the formation of an ultra-thin 2D/3D heterojunction results in a more efficient process for hole extraction from the perovskite to the Spiro-OMeTAD [23,34]. To further study the effect of  $ \mathrm{CF_3PEAI} $  and PEAI treatment on the built-in potential  $ (V_{\mathrm{bi}}) $  of the hybrid PSCs, a capacitance-versus-voltage (Mott-Schottky) analysis was carried out. As show in Fig. 5(f), the  $ V_{\mathrm{bi}} $  value is improved from  $ 0.91\mathrm{V} $  for the control device to  $ 0.96\mathrm{V} $  for the PEAI-treated device and further increased to  $ 1.10\mathrm{V} $  for the  $ \mathrm{CF_3PEAI} $ -treated device. The higher  $ V_{\mathrm{bi}} $  is the main reason for the improved  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  of the  $ \mathrm{CF_3PEAI} $ -treated device. The regulated energy levels (Fig. 5b) provide a larger driving force for charge separation and collection of the photo-generated carriers. Therefore,  $ \mathrm{CF_3PEAI} $ -treatment and PEAI-treatment directly contribute to the enhanced output voltage and current of the hybrid PSCs. It is obvious that  $ \mathrm{CF_3PEAI} $ -treatment has a more beneficial effect than PEAI-treatment.

As shown in Fig. 5(g), the relationship between light-intensity and  $  V_{\mathrm{oc}}  $  was measured to further confirm the internal charge recombination mechanism. The control device shows the largest slope of  $  1.87\mathrm{kT / q}  $ , while the  $  \mathrm{CF_3PEAl}  $ -treated device exhibits the lowest slope of  $  1.15\mathrm{kT / q}  $ . As is well-known, the smaller deviation of the slope from kT/q means lower Shockley-Read-Hall (SRH) recombination in the devices [48]. This result suggests that the device with the fluorinated 2D/3D heterojunction has less defect-assisted recombination [24,33], which is consistent with the results of the TRPL measurements.

Electrical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed to further unravel the charge transfer and recombination process in these PSCs. Nyquist plots of devices are shown in Fig. 5(h). The inset displays the equivalent circuit model used in fitting. Table S6, Supporting Information, summarizes the fitting parameters. The  $ \mathrm{CF_3PEAI} $ -treated device shows the smallest series resistance  $ (R_{\mathrm{s}}) $  and the largest recombination resistance  $ (R_{\mathrm{rec}}) $ , demonstrating that  $ \mathrm{CF_3PEAI} $  treatment can effectively reduce charge recombination in the PSCs. The fluorinated 2D/3D heterojunction PSC device exhibits a superior steady-state current  $ (24.86\mathrm{mA} / \mathrm{cm}^2) $  and stabilized efficiency of  $ 22.71\% $  after 420 min illumination (initial PCE is  $ 22.77\% $ ). In practical applications, humidity, temperature and light will affect the long-term stability of PSCs. These environmental conditions can induce a phase transition of FA-based perovskite from a black   $  \alpha  $  -phase into a yellow   $  \delta  $  -phase, resulting in serious efficiency degradation. Due to the large variation of humidity with changes in the weather and season, the hydrophobic property of PSCs is crucial for their long term stable operation. Water contact angle measurements were performed to evaluate the hydrophobicity of these perovskite films. As shown in Fig. S12, Supporting Information, with PEAI- or   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $  -treatment, the perovskite surface becomes more hydrophobic. The water contact angle of the control 3D perovskite film is 54.4°. With PEAI treatment with the concentration increasing from 1 to 10 mg/mL, the contact angle of the perovskite films gradually increases from 61.5° to 77.9°. For   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $  -treated perovskite films, the contact angle is further enhanced and increases from 71.9° to 85.3°. It has been shown that the hydrophobic phenyl group increases the water resistance of perovskite and the introduction of the fluorinated methyl group will enhance the water resistance further. Fig. 6(a) displays the retention of the hydrophobic property of control, PEAI-treated and   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $  -treated perovskite films. The contact angle for the control film decreased from 54.4° to 26.9° within 10 min. In contrast, for PEAI-treated perovskite film, it decreased from 65.5° to 34.6° during the same time. For   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $  -treated perovskite film, it only decreased from 74.2° to 53.8°, meanwhile the water drop did not spread over a larger area. The long-term trend of contact angle change is shown in Fig. S13,

Supporting Information. The relation between contact angle and time is nearly linear for a long-term measurement. The curve slopes for the control, PEAI-treated and  $ \mathrm{CF_3PEAI} $ -treated perovskite films are -3.29, -2.26 and -1.25 degree/min respectively. The above experiments prove that the fluorinated 2D capping layer has a high waterproofing ability to resist moisture and preserve the underlying 3D perovskite [49].

Due to the highly waterproof coating of 2D perovskite, the environmental stability of the underlying 3D perovskite films will be significantly enhanced. The control, PEAI-treated and  $ \mathrm{CF_3PEAI} $ -treated perovskite films are exposed to ambient environment ( $ T: \sim 30^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ , RH:  $ \sim 25\% $ ) for 100 days to investigate the protective effect of the fluorinated 2D layers (Fig. 6b). From the photograph, it is obvious that the color of the control sample becomes lighter after 80 days, suggesting that the 3D perovskite film has been degraded. After 100 days, the PEAI-treated perovskite film is obviously decomposed. However, under the same conditions, the  $ \mathrm{CF_3PEAI} $ -treated perovskite film still appears black, indicating the fluorinated 2D/3D perovskite film has excellent air stability. To understand the mechanism responsible for the environmental stability of the fluorinated 2D/3D heterojunction, DFT calculations of oxygen and water adsorption on perovskite film were performed. The simulation models for the adsorption of oxygen and water molecules on the  $ \mathrm{CF_3PEA} $ -passivated  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  surface are shown in Fig. 6(c). The calculation results are shown in Fig. 6(d), and the oxygen adsorption energy of perovskite with  $ \mathrm{CF_3PEA} $  cation is slightly smaller than that with PEA cation. More importantly, the water adsorption energy of the former is much smaller than that of the latter, which indicates the excellent waterproofing ability of  $ \mathrm{CF_3} $ -PEA-treated perovskite film and is consistent with the contact angle results.

Finally, to better understand the air stability of control, PEAI-treated and   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $  -treated PSCs, the unencapsulated devices were stored under ambient conditions (  $  T: \sim30\ ^{\circ}C  $  , RH:  $  \sim25\%  $  ) for 2400 h. As shown in Fig. 6(e), the   $  CF_{3}PEAI  $  -treated device still retained   $  \sim84\%  $   of its initial PCE after storage for 2400 h, while the PEAI-treated device retained   $  \sim76\%  $  , and the PCE of the control PSCs decayed to less than 20% of the initial value. These results indicate that the PSCs with the fluorinated 2D perovskite capping layer have effectively improved air stability.

Furthermore, encapsulated devices were illuminated by simulated AM 1.5G light without using any ultraviolet filter to investigate their light stability (Fig. 6f). The results show that the PCEs of the control devices exhibited a sharp drop to  $ 33\% $  of their original values within  $ 200\mathrm{h} $ . Encouragingly, the  $ \mathrm{CF_3PEAl} $ -treated devices still retained over  $ 80\% $  of the original PCE after  $ 200\mathrm{h} $  illumination, which is better than the behavior of the PEAl-treated device. All of these air- and light-stability testing results indicate that the introduction of an ultra-thin fluorinated 2D perovskite capping layer on 3D perovskite can improve the long-term stability of PSCs. This can be attributed to the passivation effect and hydrophobicity of the  $ (\mathrm{CF}_3\text{-PEA})_2\mathrm{FA}_{0.85}\mathrm{MA}_{0.15}\mathrm{Pb}_{2}\mathrm{l}_7 $  capping layer, which could effectively reduce the defect density and water/oxygen adsorption of perovskite films to largely enhance the stability.
