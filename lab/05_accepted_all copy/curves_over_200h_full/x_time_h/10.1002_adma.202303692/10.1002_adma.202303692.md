# Sequential Molecule-Doped Hole Conductor to Achieve >23% Perovskite Solar Cells with 3000-Hour Operational Stability

Guozheng Du, Li Yang,  $  ^{*}  $   Peiyao Dong, Lianlian Qi, Yuliang Che, Xiao Wang, Xiaoli Zhang,  $  ^{*}  $   and Jinbao Zhang  $  ^{*}  $

Although hole transport layers (HTLs) based on solution-processed doped Spiro-OMeTAD are extremely popular and effective for their remarkable performance in n-i-p perovskite solar cells (PSCs), their scalable application is still being held back by poor chemical stability and unsatisfied scalability. Essentially, the volatile components and hygroscopic nature of ionic salts often cause morphological deformation that deteriorate both device efficiency and stability. Herein, a simple and effective molecular implantation-assisted sequential doping (MISD) approach is strategically introduced to modulate spatial doping uniformity of organic films and fabricate all evaporated Spiro-OMeTAD layer in which phase-segregation free HTL is achieved accompanied with high molecular density, uniform doping composition, and superior optoelectronic characteristics. The resultant MISD-based devices attain a record power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 23.4\% $ , which represents the highest reported value among all the PSCs with evaporated HTLs. Simultaneously, the unencapsulated devices realize considerably enhanced stability by maintaining over  $ 90\% $  of their initial PCEs in the air for  $ 5200\mathrm{h} $  and after working at maximum power point under illumination for  $ 3000\mathrm{h} $ . This method provides a facile way to fabricate robust and reliable HTLs toward developing efficient and stable perovskite solar cells.

# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic metal halide perovskite semiconductors become one of the most encouraging candidates for photovoltaics due to their low-cost fabrication, high absorption coefficient, superior defect tolerance, and long carrier lifetime.  $  ^{[1-5]}  $   After tremendous optimization of interfacial materials,  $  ^{[6-8]}  $   perovskite compositions,  $  ^{[9-13]}  $   and device architecture,  $  ^{[14]}  $   certified power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of over 25% have been achieved in single-junction perovskite solar cells (PSCs).  $  ^{[15]}  $   The perovskite semiconductors with adjustable bandgaps also achieve high efficiency of >30% in monolithic silicon-based tandem solar cells.  $  ^{[16,17]}  $   Therefore, PSCs currently become one of the most emerging photovoltaic technologies in a scalable and affordable manner.

Various attempts have been made to improve the doping efficiency by rationally designing substituted ionic dopants such as metal-based salts,  $  ^{[27,28]}  $  radical cations,  $  ^{[29]}  $  and Lewis acids.  $  ^{[30,31]}  $  However, adverse impacts on the film stability still accompany these additives, and their performance in PSCs still lags behind those with LiTFSI.  $  ^{[32-34]}  $  To avoid phase segregation in the doped organic films, ions and volatile species should be strictly excluded in the doping precursor in order to achieve satisfactory component compatibility, which can be realized by molecular doping.  $  ^{[35-37]}  $  There are several benefits derived from this method. First, the elimination of ionic dopants favors mitigating the migration-induced structural deformation and suppressing the degradation of the electrical properties of HTLs. Second, high compositional uniformity in the molecular mixture facilitates charge transportation and collection in the devices. Third, the hydrophobic nature of organic materials is conducive to prevent moisture ingress, which promotes the effectiveness of HTL barrier protection. In previous investigation, various molecular dopants based on tetracyanoquinodimethane (TCNQ) derivatives, such as F4TCNQ,  $  ^{[38]}  $  F6TCNQ,  $  ^{[39]}  $  were explored in both vacuum and solution-processed HTLs. However, the majority of these dopants display similar limitations of low device efficiencies (<20%) as a tradeoff for gaining device longevity.  $  ^{[40]}  $  Although some dopant-free HTMs have been developed for PSCs, they mostly encounter issues of high material cost or unsatisfied device performance. Therefore, there is an urgent need to fundamentally understand the mechanism of molecular doping and to optimize the doping configuration for obtaining high-efficiency and stable HTLs.

In this work, we propose a versatile strategy of molecular implantation-assisted sequential doping (MISD) to fabricate all evaporated HTLs featured with uniform doping composition and segregation-free microstructures. The MISD approach enables the formation of ion-free Spiro-OMeTAD film along with dense molecular assembly, remarkable electrical conductivity, and superior chemical stability, all of which considerably enhance the charge collection yield and improve the film reliability. As a result, a record PCE of  $ 23.4\% $  has been achieved in MISD-based devices, which represents the highest reported value for PSCs with evaporated HTLs. Moreover, the unencapsulated devices demonstrate substantially improved stability by maintaining over  $ 90\% $  of initial PCEs in the air for  $ 5200\mathrm{h} $  and under the operational conditions (maximum power point) for  $ >3000\mathrm{h} $ .

# 2. Results

# 2.1. Molecular Doping

Fine control of the HTL composition and morphology is crucial to inhibiting phase segregation and preserving their photovoltaic properties. In contrast to conventional SP technology, which generally involves detrimental solvent effects on the underlying perovskite films, we used the thermal evaporation (TE) method to fabricate the HTL in which the solid film is formed from gaseous molecular materials (Figure 1B). As learnt from previous studies,  $  ^{[41,42]}  $  the evaporated undoped Spiro-OMeTAD film as the control suffers from insufficient carrier collection and intense light-soaking response, which limits both device efficiency and performance reliability. To improve the electrical properties, we applied a layer-by-layer sequential doping (SD) strategy to fabricate HTL on the perovskite films via vacuum deposition. In this way, the molecular dopant F4TCNQ is mostly located on the top of the SpiroOMeTAD layer, which could induce an inadequate doping reaction in the bulk HTLs. Alternatively, we introduce a solvent vapor annealing process as a method to modulate the dynamics of molecular implantation in MISD. Rather than simply forming double-layer structure, the MISD approach adopts an orthogonal solvent iso-propanol (IPA) as the vapor agent to facilitate F4TCNQ infiltration in Spiro-OMeTAD film.  $  ^{[43]}  $   In addition, chlorobenzene (CB) vapor medium was subsequently used for further condensing the composite film. A demonstration of the fabricated hybrid HTLs by MISD in flexible PSCs is exhibited in Figure 1C. Consequently, MISD creates a pathway for intermolecular interaction to achieve a desired molecular composite across the whole film in contrast to hierarchical architecture in SD (Figure 1C). This method favors improving the compositional uniformity and molecular density in the HTLs, and is also independent on the dissolving process and thus appropriate for large-area application.

To quantify the doping efficiency of F4TCNQ in the HTL, we monitored the compositional evolution of the HTLs by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra. The characteristic peak of   $  2193.58 \, cm^{-1}  $   was used as an indicator for Spiro-OMeTAD  $  ^{+}  $   formation. The MISD films showed stronger absorption than the control and SD films at this wavenumber (Figure S1, Supporting Information). FTIR mapping at   $  2193.58 \, cm^{-1}  $   further proves higher absorption intensity in the MISD film than the other two (Figure 2A–C), indicating MISD led to a significantly larger doping yield of Spiro-OMeTAD in the MISD-based HTL. The homogeneous distribution of absorption intensity across these films suggests high doping uniformity and little phase segregation in the HTLs.

Furthermore, a red shift of the peak at the wavenumber of   $  1510.00 \, cm^{-1}  $   representing the change of C–N stretching mode in the triphenylamine group was observed after molecular doping (Figure 2D). A larger peak shift in the MISD film (  $  1508.16 \, cm^{-1}  $  ) than the SD (  $  1509.74 \, cm^{-1}  $  ) potentially indicates that the MISD sample obtained a greater doping degree of Spiro-OMeTAD.  $  ^{[44]}  $   To further examine the film composition, the light absorption at 525 nm assigned for Spiro-OMeTAD  $  ^{+}  $   was measured by UV–vis measurement (Figure 2E). MISD resulted in a stronger absorption from the oxidized radicals due to full electron transfer between two molecules rather than forming a charge-transfer complex.  $  ^{[18]}  $   To evaluate the doping uniformity in the vertical direction, we examined the depth dependence of the energy-level structures in the HTLs by UV photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS). The SD samples showed the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of -5.12 eV when the etching depth reached 15 nm toward the perovskite layer (Figure 2F). By contrast, MISD led to a deeper HOMO level of -5.25 eV under the same condition. Similar HOMO values were obtained when etching to a higher depth of 30 nm (Figures S2,S3, Supporting Information). A deeper HOMO level in MISD-based HTL proves stronger p-doping (Figure S4, Supporting Information), which favors superior band alignment so as to minimize the voltage deficit in

the devices. To confirm this, we analyzed the fluorine (F) ions distribution across the film by time-of-flight secondary-ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS). A pronounced F elemental signal was detected at the HTL/Ag interface in the SD-based devices, implying that F4TCNQ molecules were mostly accumulated on the surface of the Spiro-OMeTAD layer (Figure 2G). Whereas, MISD enabled a homogeneous distribution of F elements spatially within the whole HTL, which validates the essential role of solvent vapor treatment on the dynamics of molecular implantation (Figure 2H). Overall, these results demonstrate that the MISD approach increases the doping uniformity and accordingly adjust the interfacial energy alignment, which could potentially facilitate carrier transportation and minimize the voltage losses at the interfaces.

# 2.2. Film Characterization

MISD contributed to a considerable improvement in the film properties. Conductive Atomic Force Microscopy (C-AFM) revealed that MISD samples had higher charge-conducting capacity (Figure 3A–C; Figure S5, Supporting Information) compared to the control and SD films by evidencing stronger spatial current across the film. The space charge limited current (SCLC) spectra further confirm MISD led to higher hole mobility in the HTLs than the other two (Figure S6, Supporting Information). Moreover, the HTLs by MISD exhibited higher surface potential (1143 mV) than the control (741 mV) and SD (592 mV), which were estimated from contact surface potential (CPD) by scanning Kelvin Probe microscopy (SKPM). This indicates MISD resulted in a deeper work function (Figure 3D; Figure S7, Supporting Information) and narrower CPD distribution, which emphasizes the critical role of MISD in realizing uniform doping schemes. These characteristics could strengthen interfacial charge transfer and assist the charge collection in devices.

Furthermore, we analyzed the molecular ordering in the HTLs to discover the charge-transport mechanism in the film by examining the temperature-dependent film conductivity.  $  ^{[45,46]}  $   The current density–voltage (J-V) curves of hole-only devices based on the MISD and the control were measured by varying the temperature from 200 to 300 K (Figure S8, Supporting Information). MISD contributed to a much lower energetic disorder (  $  \sigma = 20.17  $   meV) compared to the control (  $  \sigma = 27.03  $   meV), which suggests that MISD favors enhancing the molecular ordering and thus the charge-collection ability in the film (Figure 3E). The improved film conductivity could be attributed to the synergistical effects of high doping efficiency and also the enhanced film density.

In addition, MISD reinforces the structural stability of the HTLs. The HTL based on SP suffered from heat-induced phase segregation which threatens the thermal stability of HTL, as reflected by over 60% degradation of film conductivity after thermal treatment (80 °C) for 8 h (Figure 3; Figure S9, Supporting Information). # 2.4. Device Stability

To access the device stability, we first studied the photostability of the devices by measuring the light-soaking effect (LSE) under continuous illumination (AM 1.5, 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ),  $  ^{[42]}  $  as illustrated in Figure 6A and Figure S38 (Supporting Information). The control device exhibited a profound LSE where light soaking for 1 h was required to reach a stabilized PCE. However, MISD-based devices attained the maximal efficiency within only 4 mins, as similarly behaved in the SP-based devices (Figure S39, Supporting Information). This result implies that sufficient HTL conductivity is highly beneficial to mitigate the LSE. The fast device degradation for SP was mostly related to light-induced metal diffusion.

We further investigated the moisture resistance of these HTLs by measuring the contact angle (CA) of these films (Figure S40, Supporting Information). The elimination of Li salts and incorporation of hydrophobic molecule F4TCNQ in MISD enables a high CA of 101.9° compared to SP (82.1°). # 3. Conclusion

In this study, we develop a facile MISD approach for the thermal deposition of Spiro-OMeTAD-based HTLs. The MISD process enables obtaining high doping uniformity in the hybrid film, which effectively enhances the film conductivity, energy-level alignment, and superior film stability. As a result, a record PCE of 23.4% has been achieved in MISD-based devices, which represents the highest reported PCE for PSCs with all evaporated HTLs. Moreover, MISD favors eliminating the post-oxidation and light soaking process, which significantly boosted the device's stability and reliability. Specifically, the devices with MISD retained over 90% of initial PCEs after being stored in ambient conditions with 30% RH for 5200 h, and maintained 90% of peak performance under continuous illumination for 3000 h. Our results offer new insights into the molecular doping mechanism and provide a facile doping approach for fabricating efficient and stable organic HTLs.
