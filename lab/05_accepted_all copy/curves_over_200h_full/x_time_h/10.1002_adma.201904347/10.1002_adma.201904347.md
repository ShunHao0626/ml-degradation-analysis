# Suppressing Vacancy Defects and Grain Boundaries via Ostwald Ripening for High-Performance and Stable Perovskite Solar Cells

Yuqian Yang, Jihuai Wu,* Xiaobing Wang, Qiyao Guo, Xuping Liu, Weihai Sun, Yuelin Wei, Yunfang Huang, Zhang Lan, Miaoliang Huang, Jianming Lin, Hongwei Chen, and Zhanhua Wei

As one kind of promising next-generation photovoltaic devices, perovskite solar cells (PVSCs) have experienced unprecedented rapid growth in device performance over the past few years. However, the practical applications of PVSCs require much improved device long-term stability and performance, and internal defects and external humidity sensitivity are two key limitation need to be overcome. Here, gadolinium fluoride  $ (\mathrm{GdF}_3) $  is added into perovskite precursor as a redox shuttle and growth-assist; meanwhile, aminobutanol vapor is used for Ostwald ripening in the formation of the perovskite layer. Consequently, a high-quality perovskite film with large grain size and few grain boundaries is obtained, resulting in the reduction of trap state density and carrier recombination. As a result, a power conversion efficiency of  $ 21.21\% $  is achieved with superior stability and negligible hysteresis.

Herein, to restrain the internal defects and external environment strain, we design a novel strategy in which gadolinium fluoride  $ (\mathrm{GdF}_3) $  is introduced as a redox shuttle and growth-assistant, and Ostwald ripening is carried out with aminobutanol vapor in the perovskite growth process. Therefore, a high-quality perovskite film with large grain size, few grain boundaries, and defects is obtained. The resultant PVSCs simultaneously possess high PCE and good stability.

The effect of Ostwald ripening on the film morphology is investigated by top-view SEM images of perovskite films, as shown in Figure 2c–f. It can be seen that compared with the pristine sample (without   $  GdF_{3}  $   or aminobutanol treatment), the   $  GdF_{3}  $   added or the aminobutanol annealed perovskite films have larger grain sizes and smoother surfaces. The perovskite growth assisted with both   $  GdF_{3}  $   and aminobutanol has the largest grain size (in micrometer-scale), and no small perovskite grain was observed. In other words, during the perovskite crystal ripening, the large particles will devour smaller particles, which induces the decrease of grain boundaries and increase of average grain size.  $  ^{[29,30]}  $

Moreover, the grain size of aminobutanol treated or   $  GdF_{3}  $   treated perovskite is obviously smaller than that of the   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol-treated perovskite. This is due to different roles of   $  GdF_{3}  $   and aminobutanol molecules, that is,   $  GdF_{3}  $   can improve the crystal growth of perovskite as growth-assist, and aminobutanol can gather smaller perovskite grains and adhere to the larger grains via its amino and hydroxyl groups. The combination of   $  GdF_{3}  $   and aminobutanol treatment results in larger grain size than only   $  GdF_{3}  $   or aminobutanol treated film. Ostwald ripening mechanism is further demonstrated by inspecting the morphology of perovskite films as a function the   $  GdF_{3}  $   concentration and the AB ripening time shown in Figures S1a–d and S2a–d, Supporting Information. It can be concluded that the perovskite film shows the best quality in the   $  GdF_{3}  $   concentration of 0.06 wt% and aminobutanol anneal time of 60 s.

Water contact-angle measurements were carried out to investigate the water resistance ability of the perovskite films (Figure S3a–d, Supporting Information). The contact angles are   $  50^{\circ}  $  ,   $  68.11^{\circ}  $  ,   $  69.21^{\circ}  $  , and   $  77.81^{\circ}  $   for pristine,   $  GdF_{3}  $  -based, aminobutanol-based, and   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol-based films, respectively. It can be seen that the hydrophobicity of perovskite film increases rapidly after   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol Ostwald ripening, which should enhance the humidity tolerance of perovskite films and mitigate their degradation in air.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of different perovskite films are shown in Figure S3e, Supporting Information. The XRD patterns exhibit a set of sharp characteristic peaks, implying the formation of the tetragonal structure of perovskite. Compared with other perovskite films, the   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol-based one shows stronger and sharper characteristic peaks, especially for the (111) and (222) planes. The peak at   $  12.76^{\circ}  $   is ascribed to the (001) planes of   $  PbI_{2}  $   of the pristine perovskite film. The intensity of the peak at   $  12.76^{\circ}  $   decreases after adding   $  GdF_{3}  $ , then disappears after   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol Ostwald ripening, indicating that the   $  PbI_{2}  $   is completely converted into perovskite owing to the optimization of crystal growth process via Ostwald ripening by   $  GdF_{3}  $   and aminobutanol.  $  ^{[31-33]}  $

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was performed to examine the surface roughness of these perovskite films. From Figure 2g–j and Figure S3f–i, Supporting Information, it can be seen that the root-mean-square (Rq) value of the pristine film is 20.1 nm, the GdF  $  _{3}  $  added or aminobutanol annealed perovskite film has

smaller Rq value of 17.9 and 19.8 nm, and the   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol based perovskite has the smallest roughness (Rq = 14.6 nm).

High-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) of perovskite films with and without   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol ripening are shown in Figure S4, Supporting Information. The XPS peaks at around 142.6 and 137.7 eV are assigned to   $  4f^{5/2}  $   and   $  4f^{7/2}  $   of   $  Pb^{2+}  $ , respectively. The two main peaks slightly shift to higher binding energy after   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol ripening, which is due to the fact that   $  Gd^{3+}  $   replaces part of   $  Pb^{2+}  $ , resulting in a weaker electron density of   $  Pb^{2+}  $ .  $  ^{[34]}  $   At the same time, the existence of metallic   $  Pb^{0}  $   is responsible for formation of two shoulder peaks of 141.3 and 136.4 eV. It can be observed that the   $  Pb^{0}  $   intensity is reduced after the   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol ripening process, which is due to the relation:   $  2Gd^{3+} + Pb^{0} \rightarrow 2Gd^{2+} + Pb^{2+}  $ .  $  ^{[17]}  $   On the other hand, the peak intensity of   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol-based film shows a significant decrease in   $  I_{4d}  $   and   $  N_{1s}  $   (Figure S4b,c, Supporting Information), which is related to the I vacancy defects (  $  I^{0}  $ ).  $  ^{[17]}  $   The decrease of   $  I^{0}  $   defects is ascribed to the reaction:   $  Gd^{2+} + I^{0} \rightarrow Gd^{3+} + I^{-}  $ .  $  ^{[17]}  $   In Figure S4d, Supporting Information, the   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol-based perovskite shows a stronger peak of   $  C_{1s}  $   at 283.3 eV than the pristine perovskite film, denoting some additional organic species on the surface of perovskite after aminobutanol vapor annealing. Combining the both reactions,   $  GdF_{3}  $   plays a significant role in the redox shuttle and the reduction of   $  Pb^{0}  $   and   $  I^{0}  $   defects.

To evaluate photovoltaic performance of the as-prepared four perovskite films, PVSCs with cell structure of FTO/TiO  $  _{2}  $  /perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD/Au were fabricated in several batches. The photocurrent–voltage (J-V) curves were measured under AM 1.5 G illumination with a light intensity of 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and the data are shown in Figure 3a and Table S1, Supporting Information. Pristine, GdF  $  _{3}  $  -based, and aminobutanol-based devices exhibit moderate performance with PCEs of 18.10%, 19.46%, and 19.67%, respectively. Impressively, the GdF  $  _{3}  $  -aminobutanol-based device displays a PCE of 21.21% with open-circuit voltage (V  $  _{OC}  $  ) of 1.17 V, short-circuit current density (J  $  _{SC}  $  ) of 23.21 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   and fill factor (FF) of 0.78. Huang et al. revealed a correlation between V  $  _{OC}  $   and grain boundaries,  $  ^{[35]}  $   and we also attribute the increasing V  $  _{OC}  $   in GdF  $  _{3}  $  -aminobutanol-based device to the reduction of grain boundaries.  $  ^{[36,37]}  $   Likewise, the larger grain size could efficiently reduce grain boundaries which is beneficial for the suppression of charge carrier recombination, resulting in an increase of FF and J  $  _{SC}  $  .  $  ^{[38,39]}  $

Figure 3b displays monochromatic incident photon-to-electron conversion efficiency (IPCE) curves with wavelength ranged from 300 to  $ 800\mathrm{nm} $ . The overall IPCE values are larger than  $ 80\% $ , and current densities increase from 20.04 to  $ 22.06\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $ , which is in good agreement with the corresponding  $ J_{\mathrm{SC}} $  value from  $ J - V $  curves. The stabilized power outputs of the four type of devices were measured under the voltage at the maximum power point  $ (V_{\mathrm{max}}) $ . In Figure 3c, the PCEs rapidly stabilize at  $ 19.25\% $ ,  $ 16.97\% $ ,  $ 16.32\% $ , and  $ 14.54\% $ , respectively. In order to verify the impact of the ripening on device performance, the reproducibility of all devices was evaluated by testing 20 cells. As shown in Figure 3d, the pristine device exhibits the lowest average PCE of  $ 17.33\% $  and shows a relatively broader distribution. Hysteresis effect is investigated by J-V test under different scan directions. Figure 3e and Table S2, Supporting Information, show the results. Hysteresis index (HI) is calculated according to ref. [40]:   $  \mathrm{HI}=\frac{J_{\mathrm{RS}}(0.8V_{\mathrm{OC}})-J_{\mathrm{RS}}(0.8V_{\mathrm{OC}})}{J_{\mathrm{RS}}(0.8V_{\mathrm{OC}})}  $  . We believe that the reduced hysteresis in   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol-based device is due to the lower trap density.  $  ^{[41]}  $

The long-term stability test results for the devices without any encapsulation under room temperature and 30% relative humidity are shown in Figure 3f. The   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol-based device retains over 90% of its initial PCE after 1000 h of storage,

showing a slow degradation in air. The long-term PCE test result under continuous illumination is provided in Figure S5, Supporting Information, where the devices were in open circuit and J-V scans were performed periodically to evaluate PCE. The   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol-based PVSCs only undergoes a small degradation of about 15% under illumination for 1000 h, while the other three types of devices suffer from intense performance loss. We believe that the stability enhancement of   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol-based PVSC is attributed to the suppression of defects by reducing grain boundaries and vacancies in perovskites.

UV–vis absorption spectra of the different perovskites are provided in Figure 4b. All films exhibit a similar absorption edge, and   $  GdF_{3}  $  -based, aminobutanol-based, and   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol-based films show slightly high absorbance, possibly due to the enlarged crystal size. Compared with the energy level of 1.610 eV for pristine perovskite film in Figure S6a–d, Supporting Information, the   $  GdF_{3}  $  , the aminobutanol, and the   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol-treated films show little different levels of 1.609, 1.607, and 1.605 eV, respectively. The slight change of bandgap may be due to the larger grain size after treatment.  $  ^{[26]}  $   The result above indicates the   $  GdF_{3}  $   or aminobutanol treatment hardly affects the UV–vis absorption of the perovskite films.

PL spectra of different perovskite films (Figure 4c) are measured to evaluate the nonradiative recombination characteristics. The   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol-based perovskite exhibits a stronger PL intensity compared with the other perovskites, indicating an excellent ability to dissociate excitons into free charges.  $  ^{[42]}  $   Interestingly, the PL peak at 770 nm for pristine perovskite shifts to 762 nm for the   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol-based sample. The blue shift originates from the reduction of spontaneous non-radiative recombination due to the trap states, which provides an explanation for the improvement of   $  V_{OC}  $   and FF.  $  ^{[43]}  $   Figure 4d shows the TRPL spectra, which are fitted by a biexponential rate law. The pristine film exhibits the fastest decay of   $  \tau_{1}=9.3  $   ns and   $  \tau_{2}=75.6  $   ns, respectively. Conversely, the   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol film shows the slowest decay with   $  \tau_{1}=17.8  $   ns and   $  \tau_{2}=148.5  $   ns, which determines lower trap state density. The fast decay (  $  \tau_{1}  $  ) depends on the quenching by trap states and interfacial charge transfer, while the slower decay (  $  \tau_{2}  $  ) is related to free carrier recombination.  $  ^{[44,45]}  $   Thus,   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol ripening can suppress defects and minimize recombination.

Dark  $ J-V $  curves of electron-only (FTO/TiO $ _2 $ /perovskite/PCBM/Au) devices are presented in Figure S7a–d, Supporting Information,the trap-filled limited region is at intermediate bias region with dramatic change in current ( $ V_{\text{TFL}} = 0.667 $ , 0.551, 0.520, 0.317 V, respectively). The trap density ( $ n_{\text{trap}} $ ) values are calculated[46] and shown in Figure S7e, Supporting Information. The GdF $ _3 $ -aminobutanol-based device shows the smallest  $ n_{\text{trap}} $  value ( $ 5.79 \times 10^{15} $  cm $ ^{-3} $ ) among the four samples. The significant reduction in the trap density provides the condition for improvement of  $ V_{\text{OC}} $  and the suppression of hysteresis.[47]

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is used to investigate the charge transfer process of the PVSCs and the results are shown in Figure 4e. GdF $ _3 $ -aminobutanol-based PVSCs show a lower recombination resistance ( $ R_{\text{rec}} $ ) value, $ ^{[48]} $  further indicating the Ostwald ripening largely suppresses recombination. Dark  $ J-V $  curves are shown in Figure 4f. $ ^{[49]} $  The GdF $ _3 $ -aminobutanol-based PVSCs exhibit lower lost current than the pristine one, suggesting that the charge transfer at the interface is smoother. $ ^{[50]} $  We attribute the improvement to the large grain size and less trap state of the perovskite.

The dependency of   $  J_{SC}  $   on illumination intensity curves shown in Figure 4g demonstrates that both devices obey the power law equation:  $  ^{[37]}  $     $  J_{SC} \propto I^{\alpha}  $  . Larger   $  \alpha  $   value for the   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol-based (0.844) device than the pristine device

(0.823) indicates less bimolecular recombination  $  ^{[51,52]}  $  after the Ostwald ripening treatment.  $  V_{OC}  $  versus log-scaled I plots (Figure 4h) are linearly fitted by the equation:  $  n = \frac{q}{KT} \frac{dV_{OC}}{d\ln(I)}  $  where n is ideal factor to judge the dominant recombination mechanism. The n value for pristine PVSCs is calculated as 1.46, implying a trap-dominated recombination. By contrast, n value for  $  GdF_{3}  $ -aminobutanol-based device is 1.15, indicating that the trap-assisted non-radiative recombination is suppressed.  $  ^{[53]}  $

In summary, a novel design is realized by introducing   $  GdF_{3}  $   as growth-assist and redox shuttle and aminobutanol vapor for Ostwald ripening in the formation of perovskite phase. It is found that   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol-based Ostwald ripening can diminish vacancies, enlarge grain size, reduce grain boundaries, suppress trap density, and charge carrier recombination. Therefore, the optimized planar PVSC achieves a maximum PCE of 21.21% with negligible hysteresis, while the pristine device only shows an efficiency of 18.10% under the same condition. Moreover, the   $  GdF_{3}  $  -aminobutanol-based device shows superior stability in the dark and continuous illumination.
