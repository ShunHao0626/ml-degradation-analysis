# Mitigating Open-Circuit Voltage Loss in Pb–Sn Low-Bandgap Perovskite Solar Cells via Additive Engineering

Nabin Ghimire, Raja Sekhar Bobba, Ashim Gurung, Khan Mamun Reza, Md Ashiqur Rahman Laskar, Buddhi Sagar Lamsal, Khalid Emshadi, Rajesh Pathak, Mohammad Adil Afroz, Ashraful Haider Chowdhury, Ke Chen, Behzad Bahrami, Sheikh Ifatur Rahman, Jyotshna Pokharel, Abiral Baniya, Md Tawabur Rahman, Yue Zhou, $ ^{*} $  and Quinn Qiao $ ^{*} $

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Lead (Pb)–Tin (Sn) mixed perovskites suffer from large open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ) loss due to the rapid crystallization of perovskite films, creating Sn and Pb vacancies. Such vacancies act as defect sites expediting charge carrier recombination, thus hampering the charge carrier dynamics and optoelectronic properties of the perovskite film. Here, we report the passivation of these defects using a controlled amount of 2-phenylethylazanium iodide (PEAI) in perovskite precursor solution as a dopant to enhance the performance of the 1.25 eV Pb–Sn low-bandgap perovskite solar cell. It was found that the incorporation of PEAI in the perovskite precursor not only improves the perovskite film quality and crystallinity but also lowers the

electronic disorder, thereby enhancing the open-circuit voltage up to 0.85 V, corresponding to   $  V_{oc}  $   loss as low as 0.4 V and the power conversion efficiency up to 17.33%. The value of   $  V_{oc}  $   loss obtained with this strategy is among the least obtained for similar band gap Pb–Sn low-bandgap perovskite solar cells. Furthermore, the ambient and dark self-stability of the PEAI-treated devices were also enhanced. This work presents a simple doping strategy to mitigate the   $  V_{oc}  $   loss of Pb–Sn mixed low-bandgap perovskite solar cells.

EYWORDS: low-bandgap perovskite, PEAI salt, defect passivation, Pb–Sn hybrid perovskite, additive engineering,  $  V_{oc}  $  deficit

# 1. INTRODUCTION

Organic–inorganic metal halide perovskite solar cells have witnessed significant progress in the last decade with a single-junction cell-level certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 25.2%, already surpassing the widely deployed multicrystalline silicon and thin-film technologies.  $  ^{1-7}  $   Extensive research on device architecture, chemical composition, and efficient fabrication protocol, including engineering of interfaces, band gap, solvents, and additives, have been the backbone of this success.  $  ^{8-11}  $

Although the efficiency of single-junction perovskite solar cells has reached an impressive value, their performance is limited, as governed by the Shockley–Queisser limit.  $  ^{12,13}  $   Benefiting from the advantages such as long wavelength absorption and less thermalization loss, perovskite–perovskite multi-junction (tandem) solar cells have been pursued to overcome the efficiency limit of the single-junction perovskite solar cells.  $  ^{14-17}  $   Hybrid Pb–Sn perovskites have gained much attention to be applied as low-bandgap perovskites for all-perovskite tandem solar cells because of their tunable band gaps (1.2–1.5 eV) and extended range of photon absorp-

Much focus has been on surface, interface, solvent, and additive engineering to improve both the film quality and overall device performance of the Pb–Sn mixed low-bandgap solar cell.  $  ^{7,14,17,19-32}  $   In particular, Yan group adopted several strategies such as thickness optimization and incorporation of bromine and guanidinium thiocyanate in the perovskite precursor which has led to a PCE of over 20%.  $  ^{7,25,33-35}  $   Xu and co-workers used the ultrathin bulk-heterojunction organic semiconductor (PBDB-T:ITIC) layer as an intermediary between the hole-transporting layer and the Pb-Sn-based perovskite layer to minimize the photovoltage loss in a 1.25 eV low-bandgap perovskite solar cell and obtained a remarkably high   $  V_{oc}  $   up to 0.86 V.  $  ^{36}  $

bandgap perovskite and achieved an efficiency of 21.1%. $  ^{39}  $  Recently, the Sergent group used a strategy to passivate the perovskite surface, eventually achieving a PCE up to 18.95% with in-film-passivated solar cells. $  ^{40}  $

Achieving a defect-free morphology to realize highly efficient and stable Pb–Sn mixed low-bandgap perovskite solar cells remains a challenge. In this work, we studied the additive effect of 2-phenylethylazanium iodide (PEAI) salt to the Pb–Sn mixed low-bandgap perovskite precursor solution and found that the addition of a trace amount of PEAI into the perovskite precursor not only modulates the perovskite formation process but also improves crystallinity, passivates the surface defects, reduces electronic disorder, and improves the overall perovskite film quality. As a result, an open-circuit voltage up to 0.85 V was achieved, which is among the best   $  V_{oc}  $   obtained for Pb–Sn hybrid perovskite solar cells. A significant enhancement in PCE from 14.61 to 17.33% was obtained with the PEAI-incorporated perovskite. Furthermore, the ambient and dark self-stabilities of unencapsulated PEAI devices were also enhanced where 85 and 87% of initial PCEs were maintained under ambient conditions (RH (60 ± 5%)) for 33 h and in the   $  N_{2}  $   environment for 1080 h, respectively.

# 2. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The Pb–Sn mixed perovskite film fabrication process is shown in Figure 1a. The perovskite precursor of the composition of   $  FA_{0.8}MA_{0.15}Cs_{0.05}Pb_{0.5}Sn_{0.5}I_{3}  $   was fabricated using the anti-solvent-assisted single-step spin-coating method.  $  ^{[17,41]}  $   Perovskite films with different PEAI concentrations were fabricated and characterized and are designated as 0.5 PEAI, 1.5 PEAI, and 2.5 PEAI to represent 0.5 mg of PEAI per mL, 1.5 mg of PEAI per mL, and 2.5 mg of PEAI per mL of the perovskite precursor, respectively. The reference cell without PEAI addition is denoted as “Control”.

Figure 1b depicts the top-view scanning electron microscopic (SEM) images for control, 0.5 PEAI, 1.5 PEAI, and 2.5 PEAI perovskite films, respectively. All perovskite films showed a compact, pinhole-free morphology, but incorporation of the PEAI additive showed enhancement in compactness and smoothness of the film. The control perovskite film contained some white particles that might be agglomerated  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 / \mathrm{SnI}_2 $  complex which is considered detrimental to the device performance. In contrast, with introduction of PEAI into the perovskite precursor, there existed no such visible agglomerations, also contributing to film smoothness. No significant change in grain sizes in all films was observed. AFM topography images of the prepared films are presented in Figure 1c which reveals reduction in root-mean-square

roughness from 27.3 nm for control to 25.8, 23.9, and 24.6 nm for 0.5, 1.5, and 2.5 PEAI perovskite films, respectively.

AFM result is in line with SEM observation that the 1.5 PEAI facilitates the optimum morphology in terms of film compactness and smoothness. The crystal phase of the Pb–Sn mixed perovskite was confirmed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) which is depicted in Figure 1d. The observed diffraction peaks at   $  2\theta  $   of   $  14.09^{\circ}  $  ,   $  28.2^{\circ}  $  ,   $  32^{\circ}  $  , and   $  41^{\circ}  $   can be assigned to the (110), (220), (310), and (214) lattice planes of the perovskite crystals, respectively. There exists a peak at   $  12.5^{\circ}  $   which can be attributed to the uncoordinated   $  PbI_{2}/SnI_{2}  $   complex. The diffraction patterns for the PEAI-doped films did not show any new peaks and are consistent with that of the control perovskite film. This suggests that no 2D phases were formed in the PEAI-doped cell. This might be due to the addition of a minimal amount of PEAI in the precursor which only acts as a dopant and not a part of the main lattice. This observation has a good agreement with the study conducted by the Sargent group in which it was concluded that at least >5 mol % of large A site cation is required to form 2D/3D heterostructures.  $  ^{42}  $   In this study, the amount of PEAI was selectively used to avoid the formation of 2D/3D perovskites because the formation of 2D perovskite is sought to be detrimental from charge transport prospective. Hence, we believe that a trace amount <2 mol % of PEAI used in this study did not contribute to form 2D/3D heterostructures.

To further elucidate the effect of PEAI doping into the perovskite precursor, the full width at half-maximum (FWHM) and peak intensity of the perovskite main characteristic peak at 14.1° were calculated for each perovskite film, as shown in Figure S1 in Supporting Information. The FWHM value for 1.5 PEAI narrows down to 0.22° from 0.32° for the control perovskite film, indicating the improved crystallinity of perovskite films with PEAI incorporation. Furthermore, the perovskite peak intensity increased with increasing concentration of PEAI with an optimal value demonstrated at 1.5 PEAI.

Since N in PEAI has   $  sp^{3}  $  -hybridization with electronic configuration as   $  1s^{2}  $     $  2s^{2}  $     $  2p^{3}  $   in the chemical compound, the three hybridized orbitals can form three covalent bonds. One leftover lone pair of electrons in N of PEA, upon deprotonation, has the ability to interact with another hybridized orbital via formation of coordination bonds.  $  ^{43,44}  $   Delocalization of such lone pair electrons into the empty orbitals of   $  Pb^{2+}  $   or   $  Sn^{2+}  $   is beneficial for perovskite crystallization as it can elongate the crystallization process facilitating a smooth, compact, and defect-free morphology. To

investigate the possible interaction between the PEAI additive and perovskite, we conducted Raman spectroscopy of PEAI/PbI $ _2 $  and PEAI/SnI $ _2 $  complexes following our previous work. $ ^{43} $  As shown in Figure 2a, the characteristic Raman peaks of PEAI were found at 79, 105, 141, 167, 482, 818, 916, 1004, 1027, 1177, 1205, 1464, 1541, 1585, and 1606 cm $ ^{-1} $ . The vibrational Raman bands for SnI $ _2 $  were found at 108, 145, 261, and 1790 cm $ ^{-1} $ . Similarly, the vibrational Raman bands for PbI $ _2 $  were found at 98, 167, and 241 cm $ ^{-1} $  (see Supporting Information, Figure S2). The PEAI/SnI $ _2 $  complex has characteristic peaks at 80, 110, 145, 265, 1076, and 1250 cm $ ^{-1} $ . The strong vibrational bands near 1076 cm $ ^{-1} $  and 1250 cm $ ^{-1} $  can be attributed to the formation of Sn–N bonds through delocalization of lone pair electron of N into the empty orbital of Sn $ ^{2+} $ . $ ^{45} $  On the other hand, the PEAI/PbI $ _2 $  complex showed a new peak at 131 cm $ ^{-1} $  and peak shifts from 482 to 423 cm $ ^{-1} $ , 818 to 842 cm $ ^{-1} $ , and 1004 to 1052 cm $ ^{-1} $  which can be attributed to the Pb–N vibration. $ ^{46} $  These interactions are further illustrated by the graphical schematic in Figure 2b.

To gain further insights into film quality after the incorporation of PEAI into the perovskite precursor, electronic disorder below the optical band gap known as Urbach energy was calculated by linear fitting   $  \ln(\alpha)  $   versus   $  h\gamma  $   profile using the relation   $  \alpha = \alpha_{0} \exp(E/E_{u})  $  , where   $  \alpha  $   is the absorption coefficient,   $  E(=h\gamma)  $   is the photon energy, and   $  E_{u}  $   is the Urbach energy.  $  ^{38}  $   As shown in Figure 2d, the control sample has   $  E_{u} \sim 100.95  $   meV, where 1.5 PEAI showed a reduced value of 64.28 meV. The observed reduction in the Urbach energy is an indication of reduced density of trap sites (see Figure S4a,b in Supporting Information for fitting details). These findings are in line with the steady-state photoluminescence (PL) study of perovskites films (see Figure S5 in Supporting Information). It was found that the PL intensity for the 1.5 PEAI film was higher compared to that for the control film. This indicates reduced non-radiative recombination loss due to possible passivation of the surface- and bulk-related defects. There is no noticeable peak shift on the PL spectra after the treatment which is also supported by the UV–vis spectra where no shift in the onset wavelength was observed for the perovskite films after PEAI treatment (see Supporting Information, Figure S6a). The optical band gap remained almost constant as 1.25 eV (see Tauc plot in Supporting Information, Figure S6b). The 1.5 PEAI film has slightly higher absorbance than that of the control.

The most obvious impact of PEAI doping was reflected in the enhancement of   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF. Considering this, we further studied the devices to gain insights into charge recombination kinetics. For this, light-dependent   $  V_{oc}  $  , FF, and   $  J_{sc}  $   experiments were performed. Splitting of the quasi-Fermi levels of electrons and holes is directly related to   $  V_{oc}  $  , where quasi-Fermi level positions are set up by free charge carrier concentrations.  $  ^{38,50}  $

The relationship between   $  V_{oc}  $   and light intensity (I) is given by   $  V_{\mathrm{oc}} = (nK_{\mathrm{B}}T/q) \ln (I/I_{0} + 1)  $  , where n is the ideality factor, q is the elementary charge, k is the Boltzmann constant, and T is the temperature. In ln (light intensity) versus   $  V_{oc}  $   profile, deviation of the slope   $  K_{B}T/q  $   from unity indicates the dominance of trap-assisted monomolecular Shockley–Read–Hall (SRH) recombination. As the value of n approaches to 2, the recombination is solely due to traps. As shown in Figure 4a, the 1.5 PEAI device showed a lower value of ideality factor (1.22) than that of the control (1.73), indicating dominance of bimolecular recombination in the 1.5 PEAI devices while trap-assisted SRH recombination in the control devices. Furthermore, Figure 4b illustrates the light intensity dependence of   $  J_{sc}  $  , where the 1.5 PEAI device displays a higher slope of 0.94 compared to control with 0.87, suggesting reduced extent of non-radiative trap-assisted recombination with PEAI incorporation. These observations are further confirmed by performing the light-dependent FF test, as shown in Figure 4c. The FF is related to trap filling which occurs at a low light intensity of photo-generated charge carriers and bimolecular recombination which occurs at a high density of photo-generated carriers.  $  ^{27,51-53}  $   In the case of the 1.5 PEAI device, the value of FF decreased initially up to 45 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $   and then remained almost constant at higher light intensities. To see the impact of the PEAI on carrier lifetime and transport time, we performed transient photovoltage (TPV) and transient photocurrent (TPC) measurements. Figure 5a shows the TPV decay profiles of the control and 1.5 PEAI devices. The charge recombination lifetime or charge carrier lifetime (  $  \tau_{rec}  $  ) was calculated from fitting of the exponential decay curve with mono-exponential function.  $  ^{61}  $   The value of   $  \tau_{rec}  $   for the 1.5 PEAI device was found to be   $  \sim2.14\ \mu s  $   which is longer than that of control with   $  \sim0.99\ \mu s  $  . Figure 5b shows the TPC decay profiles of the control and 1.5 PEAI devices. Devices were kept under short-circuit current conditions using a low input impedance of   $  \approx50\ \Omega  $   and excited by a nanosecond laser pulse without background light. The value of charge transport time (  $  \tau_{tr}  $  ) for each case was calculated via exponential fitting of TPC curves, and it was found that   $  \tau_{tr}  $   decreased from   $  \sim0.94\ \mu s  $   for the control to   $  \sim0.87\ \mu s  $   for the 1.5 PEAI device, indicating better charge transport with the PEAI incorporation. These improvement actions in charge carrier dynamics further indicate defect passivation ability of the PEAI.

The results obtained from the measurement were fitted with the commonly used one R–C circuit.  $  ^{62-66}  $   The equivalent circuit and corresponding parameters are provided in Figure S9 and Table S3 in Supporting Information, respectively. The

control device demonstrated an   $  R_{rec}  $   of   $  \sim5~K\Omega  $  , whereas the 1.5 PEAI devices showed an   $  R_{rec}  $   of   $  \sim12.45~K\Omega  $  . Figure 5d presents the dark J-V curves for control and 1.5 PEAI devices. The control device has a reverse saturation current density of   $  3.8\times10^{-17}~mA~cm^{-2}  $  . In the case of 1.5 PEAI devices, the value significantly decreases to   $  4.38\times10^{-19}~mA~cm^{-2}  $  , indicating a reduced number of traps with PEAI incorporation.  $  ^{67}  $   These results are also in line with charge carrier mobility calculations from photo-CELIV measurements, where a higher value of mobility (  $  5.67\times10^{-3}~cm^{2}~v^{-1}~s^{-1}  $  ) was obtained for the 1.5 PEAI devices compared to that for control (  $  2.33\times10^{-3}~cm^{2}~v^{-1}~s^{-1}  $  ) (see Figure S10 in Supporting Information). The higher value of mobility is credited to the better charge transport and less carrier recombination with the PEAI treatment.

The stability of the unencapsulated devices was then evaluated by tracking the evolution of PCE over time while storing the devices in ambient and inert   $  [N_{2}  $  -filled glovebox (GB)] conditions. Figure 6a–d presents the evolutions of PCE and   $  V_{oc}  $   of PSCs with storage time. As shown in Figure 6a, the PCE of 1.5 PEAI devices retained almost   $  \sim85\%  $   of initial PCE over a storage period of 33 h outside at the ambient atmospheric condition of RH   $  60 \pm 5\%  $  , while the control device only lasted for 27 h. Similarly, as shown in Figure 6b, the 1.5 PEAI devices retained almost 90% of its   $  V_{oc}  $   over 36 h, while the control device could retain only 28%. Furthermore, the 1.5 PEAI devices retained 87% of PCE and 98% of   $  V_{oc}  $   when stored in an inert environment for 1080 h, as presented in Figure 6c,d, respectively. In contrast, the control devices could only retain 47% of PCE and 63% of   $  V_{oc}  $   over the same storage time.

# 3. CONCLUSIONS

In summary, we developed a simple strategy to obtain high-quality Pb–Sn mixed low-bandgap perovskite films with a smooth pin-hole free surface morphology, high crystallinity, and low electronic disorder by incorporating a suitable amount of PEAI for perovskite precursor doping. As a result, improvements were reflected on PV parameters of Pb–Sn low-bandgap perovskite solar cells. The high open-circuit voltage of 0.85 V achieved by our strategy corresponds to one of the least   $  V_{oc}  $   loss among similar Pb–Sn mixed low-bandgap perovskites while delivering a high PCE of 17.33% with negligible hysteresis. Furthermore, the ambient and dark self-stabilities of the PEAI-treated devices significantly enhanced due to the hydrophobic nature of PEAI. This study paves a pathway to obtain defect-passivated, high-quality Pb–Sn mixed low-bandgap perovskite films with lower   $  V_{oc}  $   deficit and higher efficiency and stability.

# 4. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

4.1. Materials. Indium-doped tin oxide (ITO) substrates (sheet resistance: 10 Ω/□, size: 1.5 cm × 1.5 cm, thickness: 1.1 mm) were purchased from Hartford Glass Co, USA. Lead (II) iodide (PbI₂, ultra-dry, 99.999%) was acquired from Alfa Aesar. Methylammonium iodide (MAI or CH₃NH₃I, 99.98%) and formamidinium iodide (FAI or CH₃N₂I, 99.9%) were obtained from Greatcell Solar. Cesium iodide (CSI, 99.9%), tin iodide (SnI₂, 99.99%), tin fluoride (SnF₂, 99%), phenethylammonium iodide (PEAI, 99.9%), and bathocuproine (BCP, 99.9%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, USA. Buckminsterfullerene, that is, C-60 (sublimed, 99.98%) was obtained from Nano C, USA. PEDOT: PSS (Clevios P VP AI 4083) was obtained from Heraeus. All solvents [dimethyl formamide (DMF), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and ethyl acetate] used in this study were anhydrous and were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, USA. All solvents and materials were used as received.

4.2. Perovskite Precursor Solution Preparation. The stock solutions were prepared for both   $  PbI_{2}  $   and CsI following the work by Saliba and co-workers.  $  ^{69}  $   1 g of   $  PbI_{2}  $   was dissolved in 1.46 mL of mixed solvent (DMF:DMSO, 4:1 v/v) and heated at 180 °C for 10 min. Similarly, 78 mg (1.5 M) of CSI was dissolved in 200 μL of DMSO and heated at 150 °C for 5 min. Upon cooling, 464 μL and 45.25 μL of   $  PbI_{2}  $   and CsI solution from respective precursor solutions were mixed with 233 mg of   $  SnI_{2}  $ , 30 mg of MAI, and 172 mg of FAI. Together with these chemicals, 10 mol % of   $  SnF_{2}  $   (15.6 mg) was mixed in DMF:DMSO (4:1 v/v) to retard the rapid oxidation of   $  SnI_{2}  $ .

For perovskite with PEAI additive, a stock solution of 10 mg of PEAI in 100   $  \mu  $  L DMF:DMSO (4:1 v/v) was prepared and added to the primary precursor as per need. For example, 10   $  \mu  $  L of stock PEAI solution was added to a 666   $  \mu  $  L of perovskite precursor to get 1.5 mg of PEAI per mL of the perovskite precursor. The overall concentration of the perovskite precursor was 1.25 mol.

4.3. Device Fabrication. The patterned ITO glasses were cleaned using detergent mixed deionized (DI) water, DI water, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol for 20 min each sequentially under ultrasonication. The substrates were then further cleaned using an UV–ozone system for 25 min. A thin layer of PEDOT:PSS was then spin-coated on the cleaned substrate. The spinning protocol used for deposition was 5000 rpm speed/4500 rpm acceleration for 40 s, followed by an annealing at 120 °C for 20 min. After cooling to the room temperature, the samples were immediately transferred to a nitrogen-filled GB for further processing. A 50 μL of the perovskite precursor was dripped over the PEDOT:PSS-coated surface and then spun at 1000 rpm speed/350 rpm acceleration for 10 s and 5000 rpm speed/3500 rpm acceleration for 35 s. Then, samples were quickly washed with 200 μL of anhydrous ethyl acetate at 25th sec of the second step to facilitate the crystallization process. Here, we used ethyl acetate which is a green solvent and less harmful than commonly used anti-solvents such as toluene and chlorobenzene. The substrates were then transferred to a preheated hotplate and annealed at 60 °C for 3 min and 90 °C for 7 min. After cooling to room temperature, C $  _{60}  $  (28 nm), BCP (7 nm), and silver (110 nm) were thermally evaporated inside a thermal evaporator under a vacuum of 3 × 10 $  ^{-6}  $  Mbar. The area of each device was 0.13 cm $  ^{2}  $ . We found that the initial deposition rate of C $  _{60}  $  and BCP was very crucial for the final device performance, especially in the moderate vacuum level (~10 $  ^{-5}  $  to 10 $  ^{-6}  $  Mbar). Especially,  $  V_{oc}  $  and FF of final devices were seriously compromised with a high deposition rate (0.5 Å/s). For the first 10 nm, a very slow deposition rate of 0.05 Å/s and then 0.2 Å/s was used for rest of the thickness.

4.4. Perovskite Film Characterization. The morphology of the perovskite films was investigated using a field emission scanning electron microscope (Hitachi S-4700) at an accelerating voltage of 3 kV. For imaging the samples, a thin conductive copper tape was used to connect the ITO glass and sample metal stub. 3 kV of acceleration voltage and 10   $  \mu  $  A of current were applied at the ultrahigh magnification mode. For the AFM topography measurement, Agilent 5500 scanning probe microscopy with a MAC III controller was used. A resonance frequency (  $  f_{1}  $  ) of 71 kHz from the tip was used in the first lock-in amplifier for imaging. Steady-state PL was carried out with an Edinburg FLS 920 fluorescence spectrometer. The perovskite layer was spin-coated on bare glass with the same fabrication protocol as that used for the actual devices. The excitation wavelength was set at 510 nm for both control and 1.5 PEAI films. XRD patterns were obtained using a Rigaku SmartLab system (2.2 kW, Cu K  $  \alpha  $   = 1.54 Å). UV–vis spectra were recorded using an UV–vis spectrometer (Hitachi, U-3010).

4.5. Device Characterization. AM 1.5 solar simulator (Model no. 91192, Oriel, USA) in conjunction with a semiconductor parameter analyzer (Agilent 4155C) was used to measure the current density–voltage (J-V) characteristics at a light intensity of 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  determined by the NREL calibrated silicon reference cell. The devices were scanned in both forward (0–1 V) and reverse (1–0) at a scan speed of 20 mV s  $  ^{-1}  $ . A metal mask of aperture area 0.08 cm  $  ^{2}  $  was used to define the active area of the devices.
