# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have achieved immense progress in virtue of admirable photoelectrical properties and low cost   $  [1-6]  $  . Their power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) have rapidly ascended from 3.8% to 25.5% in the past decade   $  [7,8]  $  . However, the poor thermal stability of organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite seriously impedes their commercial utility owing to the volatile nature of organic cations   $  [9-12]  $  . Replacing the organic components with inorganic components could fundamentally address the thermal volatility problem   $  [13-16]  $  . # A B S T R A C T

The presence of numerous trap states on the perovskite surface severely affects the performance of inorganic   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite solar cells. Surface modification has been proven to be an effective strategy to passivate the surface trap states of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite. However, most modifiers behave high volatility and insulation, not enough to further develop the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   solar cells. Herein, an ionic liquid of 1-viny-3-propionate ethyl imidazolium chloride ([PEVIM]Cl) is applied to modify the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film surface, yielding a compact film with enhanced crystallinity. The surface trap states of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film are effectively passivated via the interaction between carbonyl group of [PEVIM]Cl and uncoordinated metal cations of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite, leading to charge recombination suppression and charge transport enhancement. Consequently, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of [PEVIM]Cl modified   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   device is obviously enhanced from 12.49% to 14.19% with an improved open-circuit voltage of 1.160 V. Moreover, the non-encapsulated device presents excellent thermal stability, still maintaining 91% PCE when heated at 85 °C in nitrogen atmosphere for 360 h. Meanwhile, the non-encapsulated device degrades only 11% PCE after stored at 50% relative humidity for 960 h. This simple and efficient approach provides a promising direction to fabricate high-efficiency and stable inorganic perovskite devices.

the perovskite film [31,32]. Accordingly, considerable efforts have been dedicated to overcome these problems. Additive engineering is an efficient strategy to control the crystallization and reduce the trap states of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   films. Many researchers reported that the metal cations such as   $  Mn^{2+}  $   [33],   $  Zn^{2+}  $   [34], and   $  Sr^{2+}  $   [35] incorporated into   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   precursor could accumulate at GBs and surface to passivate the trap states. Recently, Zeng et al. [36] demonstrated that the PbO emerged from decomposition of   $  \mathrm{Pb(Ac)_{2}}  $   additive at high temperature could passivate the surface trap states and suppress the recombination for   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   films. However, the additives may bring new phase and carrier recombination or degradation centers in the bulk perovskite [37–39]. In comparison, surface modification is an alternative method that not only passivates the surface trap states effectively, but also improves the interface between perovskite film and top layer. Yuan et al. [40] developed a post-processing method to modify the perovskite film with   $  \mathrm{Pb(NO_{3})_{2}}  $   solution, which could passivate the trap states of Pb vacancy and I interstitial, finally achieving an enhanced efficiency of 12.34%. Organic cations such as   $  FA^{+}  $   [41], TH-  $  N^{+}  $   [31], and CFPEA  $  ^{+}  $   [42] were capped on the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite to reduce the surface recombination and prevent external erosions, bringing about the improved efficiency and moisture stability of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   PSCs. Although these materials have efficiently passivated the trap states and enhanced the performance for   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   PSCs, their high volatility and insulation build barriers for their further application. Hence, it is urgent to find a multifunctional material that not only effectively passivates the trap states, but also behaves excellent thermal stability and high electrical conductivity.

In this work, we synthesize an ionic liquid of 1-viny-3-propionate ethyl imidazolium chloride ([PEVIM]Cl) via a facile process and employ it to modify the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   surface. A compact film with improved crystallinity is achieved after [PEVIM]Cl modification. Meanwhile, it is found that the carbonyl group of [PEVIM]Cl can interact with under-coordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and   $  Cs^{+}  $  of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite. And the surface trap states of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite film are efficaciously passivated, thereby suppressing charge recombination and promoting charge transport. Finally, optimum PCE of 14.19% is attained with an improved   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.160 V. The PCE of optimal device without encapsulation still maintains 91% after heated at 85 °C in nitrogen atmosphere for 360 h. Besides, the optimal device without encapsulation remains 89% of its original PCE after stored under ambient condition (relative humidity of 50%) for 960 h.

# 2. Results and discussion

We readily synthesize the   $  [PEVIM]Cl  $   via a facile reaction between ethyl 2-chloropropionate and 1-vinylimidazole as shown in Fig. 1a. The corresponding details of synthesis method are provided in Experimental Section. The surface modification of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite by   $  [PEVIM]Cl  $   additives is shown in Fig. 1b. The   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite film is firstly prepared via typical one-step spin-coating approach. Before the modification of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite using   $  [PEVIM]Cl  $ , the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite film is quickly heated at 200 °C for 10 min. After that, the   $  [PEVIM]Cl  $   isopropanol (IPA) solution (0, 0.3, 0.6, and 0.9 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $ ) is deposited onto the as-prepared   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite films. The   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film treated with only IPA is termed as the control   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film.

The effects of [PEVIM]Cl modification on the morphology of  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  films are explored via the scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As can be seen in Fig. 2a, the SEM image of the control film exhibits some observable pinholes, which could disturb charge carrier diffusion and promote charge recombination, resulting in device performance degradation. After modification with different concentration of [PEVIM]Cl, the pinholes on the perovskite film gradually decrease. More and more dense perovskite films are

obtained (Fig. 2b and Fig. S1 in the Supporting Information). Obviously, some of dark region can be found on the perovskite film when the concentration of [PEVIM]Cl increases to  $ 0.9\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1} $ , which may be the excess [PEVIM]Cl. The  $ 0.6\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1} $  [PEVIM]Cl modified  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  film is henceforth noted as [PEVIM]Cl modified film. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping presents that C, N, O, Cl atoms are evenly distributed on the  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  surface, which confirms the coverage of [PEVIM]Cl on the  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  film (Fig. S2, Supporting Information). We further study the cross-sectional SEM images of the control and [PEVIM]Cl modified  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  devices with multilayer architecture of fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)/TiO $ _2 $  (~40 nm)/perovskite (~350 nm)/Spiro-OMeTAD (~220 nm)/Au (~80 nm). It is demonstrated that the interfaces of the control and [PEVIM]Cl modified  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  devices are all well bonded (Fig. 2c and d, respectively).

To analyze the impacts of [PEVIM]Cl modification on the perovskite structure and crystallization, we perform the X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurement. We can find that all the perovskite films exhibit intense XRD peaks at 14.4° and 29.4°, which respectively represent the typical (100) and (200) planes of cubic   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   phase (Fig. 2e) [43]. Notice that there are no other impurity peaks in all perovskite films except a few peaks of   $  SnO_{2}  $   belonging to the FTO substrate [44,45], implying that there is no new phase forming during the [PEVIM]Cl modification. Furthermore, no obvious shifts are observed, suggesting that [PEVIM]Cl is unincorporated into the crystal lattice. As shown in Fig. 2f, we can find that the XRD intensity of [PEVIM]Cl modified   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film is obviously higher than the control film, implying a better crystallinity for [PEVIM]Cl modified   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film, which is in agreement with the improved film quality with pinhole-free morphology.

To further make sure the presence of [PEVIM]Cl on the perovskite film, we conduct the Fourier transformed infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) for the control film, [PEVIM]Cl modified   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film, and pure [PEVIM]Cl as depicted in Fig. 3a. The pure [PEVIM]Cl shows a peak around 1741.4 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  , which is ascribed to the typical stretching vibration mode of group C=O [46]. The C=O characteristic stretching vibration still exists in [PEVIM]Cl modified   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film, but a red-shift from 1741.4 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   to 1736.5 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   is observed, which reveals that there is a possible interaction between C=O of [PEVIM]Cl and ions of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   (Fig. 3b) [31,46]. Then we undertake the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) characterization to further validate the interaction between [PEVIM]Cl and   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite (Fig. S3, Supporting Information). The typical N 1s peak (located at 401.49 eV) and Cl 2p peak (located at 197.60 eV and 199.21 eV, respectively) are observed in [PEVIM]Cl modified   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film, which further proves the existence of [PEVIM]Cl on the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   surface (Fig. 3c and d, respectively) [31,44]. It can be seen in Cs 3d spectra that the control   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film shows two peaks around 724.2 and 738.2 eV, which are indexed to   $  3d_{5/2}  $   and   $  3d_{3/2}  $  , respectively, corresponding to the   $  Cs^{+}  $  cations (Fig. 3e) [47]. Two peaks around 138.0 and 142.8 eV are found for the control   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film in Pb 4f spectra, which are attributed to   $  4f_{7/2}  $   and   $  4f_{5/2}  $  , respectively, corresponding to the   $  Pb^{2+}  $   cations (Fig. 3f) [48]. It is clearly observed that the Cs 3d and Pb 4f peaks in the [PEVIM]Cl modified   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film both shift towards lower binding energy, which suggests the strong interaction between [PEVIM]Cl and under-coordinated Pb and Cs metal ions, revealing that under-coordinated ions trap states on the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   surface are efficiently passivated. The possible passivation mechanism of [PEVIM]Cl for   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite film is also illustrated in Fig. 1b. The [PEVIM]Cl with carbonyl group is expected to form a chemical interaction with under-coordinate metal ions on the surface of perovskite, where the lone unpaired electron on the O atom could donate to Pb and Cs metal cations. In this case, the trap states originated from under-coordinate metal ions could be effectively passivated.

To explore the impact of [PEVIM]Cl modification on the charge carrier recombination and transfer dynamic, we conduct the Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) characterizations. As exhibited in Fig. 5a, the [PEVIM]Cl modified  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  film presents a much higher PL intensity compared with the control film, indicating a reduced charge recombination in the [PEVIM]Cl modified  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  film. When Spiro-OMeTAD is added on the  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  surface, the PL intensity extremely decreases because of the strong hole extraction of Spiro-OMeTAD. Apparently, the FTO/perovskite/[PEVIM]Cl/Spiro-OMeTAD exhibits the lowest PL intensity owing to the super properties of ionic liquid [52,53], resulting in the better hole

transfer at   $  CsPbI_{2}Br/Spiro-OMeTAD  $   interface. Meanwhile, the small blueshifts can be found owing to the enhanced crystallinity of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   and passivation effects of [PEVIM]Cl. Fig. 5b depicts the corresponding TRPL curves, and the average lifetime (  $  \tau_{ave}  $  ) is calculated by using a biexponential function to fit the TRPL curves (Table S2, Supporting Information). The results show that the   $  \tau_{ave}  $   of perovskite has been extend from 36.68 to 55.09 ns after [PEVIM]Cl modification, further validating the reduced trap states density in the [PEVIM]Cl modified   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film. Notably, the   $  \tau_{ave}  $   is greatly shortened when Spiro-OMeTAD is capped on the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $ . Moreover, the   $  \tau_{ave}  $   of FTO/perovskite/[PEVIM]Cl/Spiro-OMeTAD (6.91 ns) is much lower than that of the FTO/perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD (13.75 ns), verifying the more efficient hole transfer at perovskite/[PEVIM]Cl/Spiro-OMeTAD interface. Hence, the [PEVIM]Cl modification on perovskite not only passivates the surface trap states but also promotes hole transfer at interface. We measure the variation of  $  V_{OC}  $  on the light intensity to better understand recombination behavior in the devices. Fig. 5c illustrates the linearly fitted  $  V_{OC}  $  as a function of light intensity. We calculate the ideality factor (n) through the following equation [54].

$$
V _ {\lambda} = V _ {s} + \frac {n k _ {B} T}{q} \ln \frac {P _ {\lambda}}{P _ {s}}
$$

(2)

where   $  V_{i}  $   represents the open-circuit voltage at light intensity   $  P_{i}  $  , and   $  V_{S}  $   represents the open-circuit voltage at the standard light intensity (Ps), n,   $  k_{B}  $  , T, and q respectively represent the ideality factor, Boltzmann constant, absolute temperature, and electron charge. It is reported that a deviation of the n value from 1 suggests the occurrence of trap-assisted recombination. It is obtained from fitted lines that the n value of the control device is 1.94, and drops to 1.53, 1.27, 1.77 for the devices modified with 0.3, 0.6, 0.9 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   [PEVIM]Cl, respectively. The smaller ideality factor indicates an effectively suppressed trap-assisted recombination in the devices.

To quantitatively evaluate the effects of [PEVIM]Cl modification on the trap states of  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_2\mathrm{Br} $  films, we carry out the  $ J - V $  characterization on the hole-only devices (FTO/PEDOT:PSS/perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD/Au) under dark condition. The dark current has a linear relationship with voltage at low bias voltage, revealing an ohmic-type response (Fig. 5d). The dark current boosts sharply as the bias voltage surpasses a kink-point, implying that the injected carriers begin to occupy the trap states. The kink-point is commonly referred to the trap-filled limit voltage  $ (V_{\mathrm{TFL}}) $ , and we could gain the trap state density via the following equation [55].

$$
N _ {t} = \frac {2 \varepsilon_ {0} \varepsilon_ {r} V _ {T F L}}{q L ^ {2}}
$$

(3)

where   $  \varepsilon_{r}  $   (8.5) and   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   (  $  8.85 \times 10^{-12}  $   F m  $  ^{-1}  $  ) represent relative vacuum permittivity and vacuum permittivity, respectively [34], L (350 nm) represents the CsPbI  $  _{2}  $  Br film thickness and q (  $  1.6 \times 10^{-19}  $   C) represents the electron charge. It is obtained that the   $  V_{TFL}  $   are 0.49, 0.42, and 0.55 V (for 0.3, 0.6, 0.9 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   [PEVIM]Cl modified CsPbI  $  _{2}  $  Br films, respectively), which is lower than that of the control film (0.61 V). The trap state density is correspondingly decreased from   $  4.68 \times 10^{15}  $   to   $  3.76 \times 10^{15}  $  ,   $  3.22 \times 10^{15}  $  , and   $  4.22 \times 10^{15}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  . The reduction in trap states density could be put down to the effective passivation of [PEVIM]Cl.

The capacitance-voltage (C–V) characterizations are also taken to further comprehend the effect of [PEVIM]Cl modification on the perovskite surface. Fig. 5e displays the (C–V) plots of the devices, and the Mott-Schottky equation is shown as follows [22].

$$
\frac {1}{C ^ {2}} = \frac {2 (V _ {b i} - V)}{A ^ {2} q \varepsilon \varepsilon_ {0} N _ {A}}
$$

(4)

where   $  V_{bi}  $  , V, q, and   $  N_{A}  $   respectively the built-in potential, applied voltage, electron charge, and carrier concentration,   $  \varepsilon  $   and   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   respectively represent the relative vacuum permittivity and vacuum permittivity. Estimated from the Mott-Schottky impedance analysis, the   $  V_{bi}  $   for 0.3, 0.6, and 0.9 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   [PEVIM]Cl modified   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   devices are 1.01, 1.02, and 0.98 V, respectively, which are larger than that for the control device (0.97 V). The enlarged   $  V_{bi}  $   suggests the positive effects of [PEVIM]Cl modification, i.e. the increase in driving force for carrier transfer and reduction in charge carrier recombination, which conduces to the increase of   $  V_{OC}  $   and FF [19,56].

# 3. Conclusion

In general, we have improved the photovoltaic performance of all-inorganic   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   PSCs by the surface modification using [PEVIM]Cl. We find that the carbonyl group of [PEVIM]Cl can interact with under-coordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and   $  Cs^{+}  $  of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $ , which realizes the effective surface passivation, contributing to charge recombination suppression and facilitating charge transport. As a result, the optimal device delivers a significant increase in PCE from 12.49 to 14.19%, with an enhanced   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.160 V. Moreover, the improved thermal and air stabilities of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   device are actually fulfilled with [PEVIM]Cl modification. Our work offers a practicable strategy to elevate the performance of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   PSCs.

Materials: Ethyl 2-chloropropionate (  $  C_{5}H_{9}ClO_{2}  $  , 97%), 1-vinylimidazole (  $  C_{5}H_{6}N_{2}  $  , 99%), and Acetonitrile (  $  C_{2}H_{3}N  $  , 99.9%) were purchased from Aladdin. Chlorobenzene (CB, 99.8%), Ethyl acetate (EtOAc, 99.8%), N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%),

Preparation of [PEVIM]Cl: Ethyl 2-chloropropionate and 1-vinylimidazole (molar ratio 1:1.25) were mixed in acetonitrile, followed by stirring in round-bottomed flask at 65 °C for 48 h. The mixture was washed with solvent of ethyl acetate serval times when the clear solution is attained. Then aforementioned solution was rotary evaporated to obtain the sticky tawny and followed by heating under vacuum at 50 °C for 24 h. Finally, the ion liquid of [PEVIM]Cl is acquired.   $  {}^{1}  $  H NMR ([PEVIM]Cl): 1.22 (t, 3H), 3.85 (s, 3H), 4.30 (q, 2H), 5.12 (s, 2H), 6.24 (s, 1H), 6.89 (s, 1H), 7.41 (s, 1H), 7.45 (s, 1H), 8.78 (s, 1H).

Device Fabrication: The FTO glasses were successively ultrasonicated in detergent, deionized water, acetone, and ethanol for 10 min. Before using, the FTO glasses were blown by   $  N_{2}  $   and then treated in UV ozone for 15 min [58]. The   $  TiO_{2}  $   precursor prepared conforming to previous approach was coated onto the glasses at 3000 rpm for 45 s for preparing the   $  TiO_{2}  $   compact layers [59]. Then the layers were heated at 160 °C (15 min), naturally cooled down. 1 M   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   precursor solution was made by stoichiometrically adding   $  CsI  $ ,   $  PbI_{2}  $ , and   $  PbBr_{2}  $   into the solvent (DMSO:DMF = 7:2, v: v). Then, the solution was coated onto the substrates at 1000 rpm for 15 s and 4000 rpm for 40 s with anti-solvent (CB) dripped on the substrates before the end of second spinning procedure with 20 s left. After that, the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   layers were quickly heated at 200 °C (10 min). The as-prepared perovskite layers were then spin-coated with 70 μL [PEVIM]Cl solution at 3000 rpm for 40 s, while [PEVIM]Cl solution was obtained via adding 0.3, 0.6, and 0.9 mg into 1 mL solvent of IPA, respectively. Finally, the [PEVIM]Cl modified perovskite films were heated at 200 °C (2 min). Hole transport layer precursor solution was attained via adding 90 mg Spiro-OMeTAD into 1 ml CB with 36 μL t-BP and 24 μL Li-TFSI. The final solution was dropped on   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   films at 5000 rpm for 30 s 80 nm gold was finally capped onto Spiro-OMeTAD film using thermal evaporator under vacuum. The device area is 0.1 cm².

The XPS and UPS spectra were measured by Kratos Axis Supra and the UPS was measured under the He I (21.22 eV) emission line. The UV–Vis absorption properties were explored via UV–Vis–NIR spectrophotometer (Lambda 35, PerkinElmer). The J-V characteristics of the devices were conducted via sourcemeter (keithley 2400).
