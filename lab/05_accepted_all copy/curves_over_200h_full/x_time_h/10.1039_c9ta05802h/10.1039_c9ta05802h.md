# Introduction

Organic-inorganic halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have realized significant development over the past few years, and the solar-to-electric power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) have been certified to over 24%.  $  ^{1-3}  $   However, the long-term stability of either the organic-inorganic perovskite absorber layer or the organic charge-transporting layer (CTL) is still problematic for practical applications.  $  ^{4}  $   Especially at an elevated temperature, the thermal stress may induce accelerated decomposition of volatile organic components in hybrid perovskites or crystallization of organic CTLs.  $  ^{5,6}  $   Thus, building all-layer-inorganic PSCs composed of inorganic perovskite absorber layers and inorganic CTLs may be a good solution to resolve the instability issues of conventional PSCs.

For the inorganic absorber layer, caesium lead halides   $  [CsPbX_{3}(X=halide)]  $   have been developed greatly during the recent 2-3 years with reports of compelling intrinsic thermal stability.  $  ^{7-14}  $   PCEs of inorganic   $  CsPbX_{3}  $   PSCs have been achieved up to and exceeding 16%.  $  ^{15}  $   Thus, it is foreseeable that   $  CsPbX_{3}  $   perovskites

# A General Strategy to Prepare High-quality Inorganic Charge-transporting Layers for Efficient and Stable All-layer-inorganic Perovskite Solar Cells †

Shasha Zhang,  $  ^{a}  $   Weitao Chen,  $  ^{a}  $   Shaohang Wu,  $  ^{a}  $   Rui Chen,  $  ^{a}  $   Yuqian Huang,  $  ^{a}  $   Zhichun Yang,  $  ^{a}  $   Jiangyu Li,  $  ^{b}  $   Liyuan Han,  $  ^{c}  $   and Wei Chen  $  ^{*a,b}  $

Due to the drawbacks of long-term stability encountered in organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs), building all-layer-inorganic PSCs composed completely of an inorganic perovskite absorber layer and inorganic charge-transporting layers (CTLs) attracts increasing interest, due to removal of thermally-unstable organic components. To do this, one of the greatest challenges rests with the deposition of efficient and stable inorganic CTLs on top of perovskites. Here, a general strategy, i.e., depositing monodispersed nanocrystals followed by a special thermal treatment, has been proposed to fabricate high-quality carbide-metal oxides (C-MOXs) CTLs on top of the inorganic perovskite of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $  . Effects of the energy levels, conductivities and chemical stabilities of different CTLs on the efficiency and long-term stability of their corresponding PSCs have been systematically studied. It is found that inverted   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   PSC based on   $  C-TiO_{2}  $   yields the highest power conversion efficiency up to 14.0%, mainly attributed to its best interfacial band alignment and suitable conductivity; and except that based on C-ZnO with lower chemical resistance to halide perovskites, all-layer-inorganic PSCs present excellent stabilities, which could maintain >90% of their initial PCEs after aging at 85 °C in the dark and continuous light-soaking at 45 °C for 1000 hour.

For CTLs, although organic CTLs can provide effective charge transport, their cost is prohibitively high for large-scale applications, and their long-term operational and thermal instability is concerning.  $  ^{[17,18]}  $   One strategy to combat the issues of cost and instability is to use inexpensive inorganic CTLs. In organic-inorganic halide PSCs, inorganic CTLs have shown improved light-soaking and thermal stability under long term aging conditions.  $  ^{[19-23]}  $   For example, using CuSCN as the hole transporting layer material, the device retained   $  >95\%  $   PCE of its initial value after light-soaking for 1000 hours at   $  60^{\circ}C  $  .  $  ^{[22]}  $

However, building efficient inorganic CTLs, especially depositing adequate inorganic CTLs on top of the inorganic perovskite layer, is still a significant challenge. There are several reasons, as follows. First, due to their low formation energies, perovskites are susceptible to degradation when exposed to oxygen, moisture, polar solvents, heat or other high-energy irradiation. Thus, the conventional thin-film deposition techniques, like magnetron sputtering or electron beam evaporation, etc., are inapplicable; whereas for the low-temperature solution method, the solvents used are largely limited.  $  ^{24-26}  $   Second, to obtain a sufficient blocking effect for interfacial charge-recombination, and to prohibit the directed contacts and the further chemical reactions between highly corrosive perovskite layer and metal electrode layer, CTLs are required to be pin-hole free in morphology.  $  ^{27}  $

In our previous work, high-quality CsPbI $ _2 $ Br film of sufficient thickness was prepared; however, due to the unmatched band alignment of the CsPbI $ _2 $ Br/PCBM interface, a small open-circuit photovoltage ( $ V_{OC} $ ) of just 0.98 V and a nonideal  $ PCE $  of 9.14% were obtained. $ ^{29} $  Thus, material screening for CTLs is required because, without it, only through fortuitous accident would one obtain a material with both the desired conductivity and band structure. In this work, a general strategy to fabricate inorganic CTLs on top of perovskite has been proposed, and a series of all-layer-inorganic PSCs based on pin-hole free carbide-metal oxide (C-MOX) hybrid electron-transporting layers (ETLs) have been successfully fabricated. Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS), time-resolved photoluminescence (PL) decay and Mott-Schottky measurements have been implemented to illuminate how the energy levels and conductivities of ETLs determine the special photovoltaic parameters of PSCs, and the effects of the ETLs' chemical properties on PSCs' stability have been thoroughly analysed. Through material screening, the carbide- $ \mathrm{TiO}_2 $ -based PSC obtained the champion PCE of  $ 14.0\% $  and the

all-layer-inorganic PSCs exhibit good long-term thermal stability:  $ \sim 8\% $  PCE loss at  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  in the dark and  $ \sim 5\% $  under continuous light-soaking at  $ 45^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 1000\mathrm{h} $ . The present study provides methodological guidance for preparing all-layer-inorganic PSCs and provides inspiration for researchers to improve the photovoltaic performances (both efficiency and stability) of PSCs.

# Results and discussion

Fig. 1 presents the schematic of the process to fabricate efficient inorganic ETLs on top of inorganic perovskite. As the first step, oleic acid (OA)-capped nanocrystals (NCs) monodispersed in nonpolar solvents were prepared. The as-prepared solutions could be stably stocked for several months, which are convenient for the future large-scale device fabrication. Nonpolar solvents were chosen to avoid damaging the perovskite. This choice usually brings about the agglomeration of NCs due to the uncontrolled evaporation of solvents, leading to a heterogeneous morphology with many pin-holes in the prepared NC films. Thus, to prevent this, OA, with long alkyl chains and large steric hindrance, has been used in the synthesis here. A series of OA-capped NCs monodispersed in chlorobenzene (CB) were prepared with appropriate concentrations. As the second step, OA-capped NCs were deposited uniformly upon the perovskite layer. decomposition of the functional groups of OA, which was demonstrated by the thermogravimetric (TG) analysis (Fig. S1, ESI†). From the largely decreased moist angle (Fig. S2, ESI†), the removal of long chains of OA can be demonstrated. The annealing time was optimized to be 30 min (Fig. S3, ESI†). As seen, when the annealing time extended to 1 h, no obvious changes in the photovoltaic properties have been found, indicating annealing at 250 °C for 30 min will not destroy the underlying perovskite. The simple annealing process gives NC films much improved electrical behaviour and preferred energy band structures for electron transport and hole blocking. As a result, uniform, pinhole-free and effective charge-extracting inorganic carbide-NC ETLs have been fabricated on top of perovskite layers by this simple, inexpensive, easily repeatable and commercially-scalable method.

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) results in Fig. 2a demonstrate the pure phases of the as-synthesized   $  TiO_{2}  $  ,   $  In_{2}O_{3}  $  ,   $  SnO_{2}  $  , and ZnO NCs, without any other impurity peaks.  $  ^{32-36}  $   The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images in Fig. 2b demonstrate the monodisperse character of the NCs. As seen, all NCs are well separated and possess uniform particle sizes of a few nanometers (5-15 nm). Photographs of the as-prepared NC solutions are shown in the inserts. As seen, each kind of the NCs dispersed in CB can be stable for several months, which is greatly beneficial for future commercial production. Additionally, the dynamic light scattering (DLS) results (Fig. S4a, ESI†) also demonstrate the monodisperse granulometric distribution of the NCs. The high degree of crystallization of the as-prepared NCs can be verified from the high-resolution TEM images (Fig. S4b, ESI†), in which the interplanar spacing of each kind of crystal shows good consistency with the XRD results. The demonstrated high-crystallinity and pure phases of the NCs indicate low defect densities and limited charge trapping and non-radiative recombination, which benefit efficient charge transfer and charge extraction when they are employed as ETLs.

After annealing at  $ 250^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for 30 min in an  $ \mathbf{N}_2 $  atmosphere, homogenous and pin-hole free carbide-MOX (C-MOX) hybrid layers on top of perovskite layers have been fabricated. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Fig. 2c) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) (Fig. 2d) have been used to characterize the morphologies of the obtained C-MOX hybrid layers. From the pictures' uniformity and the low roughness, it can be found that flat, homogeneous and pinhole-free films have been achieved, with continuous and close stacks of nanoparticles. The perfect morphologies obtained here demonstrate the general applicability of our method.

Having obtained the expected morphologies, the UPS (Fig. 3a) combined with the UV–vis absorption (Fig. S5, ESI†) has been taken to characterize the band structures of the C-MOXs. The calculated Fermi energy ( $ E_F $ ) values of C-TiO $ _2 $ , C-In $ _2 $ O $ _3 $ , C-SnO $ _2 $  and C-ZnO are -3.82, -4.15, -3.93 and -3.87 eV versus vacuum, respectively, a little more positive than the corresponding pure MOXs. $ ^{19,37-39} $  This may be caused by the carbon hybridization

effects, which induce many shallow donor levels near the bottom of the conduction band, leading to the upward movement of  $  E_F  $ . $  ^{40}  $  The more positive  $  E_F  $  of C-MOX hybrid ETLs indicates that their corresponding PSCs may achieve larger build-in fields and higher  $  V_{OC}  $  values. The conduction band energies ( $  E_{CB}  $ ) of C-TiO $ _2 $ , C-In $ _2 $ O $ _3 $ , C-SnO $ _2 $  and C-ZnO are -3.63, -3.70, -3.74 and -3.68 eV, respectively. All of them are more negative than that of CsPbI $ _2 $ Br (-3.56 eV from Fig. S6, ESI†), indicating band alignments well-matched with the perovskite for charge extraction. The conductivities of the C-MOX ETLs are measured and present in Fig. 3b. As seen, the electric conductivities for C-TiO $ _2 $ , C-In $ _2 $ O $ _3 $ , C-SnO $ _2 $  and C-ZnO take the order of C-In $ _2 $ O $ _3 $  (3.5× 10 $ ^{-4} $  S cm $ ^{-1} $ ) > C-ZnO (1.1× 10 $ ^{-4} $  S cm $ ^{-1} $ ) > C-TiO $ _2 $  (4.3 × 10 $ ^{-5} $  S cm $ ^{-1} $ ) > C-SnO $ _2 $  (2.3 × 10 $ ^{-5} $  S cm $ ^{-1} $ ). The conductivity was calculated by the equation  $  I = \sigma_0 Ad^{-1} V  $ , where A is the sample area and d is the thickness which are defined as 0.04 cm $ ^2 $  and 240 nm. $  ^{41}  $  It is unexpected that C-SnO $ _2 $  has the lowest conductivity, as crystalline SnO $ _2 $  is reported to be more conductive than crystalline TiO $ _2 $ . $  ^{39}  $  Here, the possible reason may be as follows. For C-SnO $ _2 $ , the obviously smaller grain size compared with the other three C-MOXs would lead to many more grain boundaries, which are disadvantageous for electron transport in the film, resulting in an unsatisfactory conductivity. Thus, more accurate control of the morphology and grain size of NCs is needed in future work. Different band structures and conductivities of C-MOXs may give different photovoltaic performance of their corresponding PSCs.

Since the humidity stress can be avoided by perfect encapsulation, we focused on aging in N $ _2 $  to remove the effects of humidity in this work. The simply encapsulated all-inorganic PSCs in N $ _2 $  atmosphere present robust thermal stabilities. As shown in Fig. 5a, after 1000 h aging at 85  $ ^{\circ} $ C in dark, all the kinds of the devices maintained > 90 % of their initial efficiencies except for those based on C-ZnO. Fig. S10 $ ^{\dagger} $  presents the changes of photovoltaic parameters. As seen, the decay of PCE mainly came from the decline of the J $ _{SC} $ . The operational stability has also been evaluated. According to the light-soaking standards in IEC 61646: 2008 (Thin-film Terrestrial Photovoltaic (PV) Modules—Design, Qualification and Type Approval) and the suggestions of Grätzel et al. and Snaith et al., $ ^{46-48} $  the light-soaking tests were carried out under continuous illumination (white light LED array with UV filter; the intensity was calibrated to achieve the same J $ _{SC} $  from the PSCs as for 1 sun AM1.5G solar irradiation) in N $ _2 $  atmosphere at 45  $ ^{\circ} $ C under maximum power point tracking (MPP). As shown in Fig. 5b, the all-layer-inorganic PSCs with C-TiO $ _2 $ , C-In $ _2 $ O $ _3 $ , and C-SnO $ _2 $  ETLs retained 95.1 %, 93.1%, and 92.2% of their initial average PCEs after 1000 h, respectively. The thermal and light-soaking stabilities demonstrate that the all-layer-inorganic PSCs in this work are among the most stable PSCs based on CsPbI $ _2 $ Br or other inorganic caesium lead halides

(Table S1, ESI†). The robust thermal stability of the whole PSCs originated not only from the stable inorganic perovskite and Bi/Ag electrodes, but also from the chemically stable inorganic ETLs.

As shown in Fig. 5c, XRD patterns of different   $  CsPbI_{2}Br/MOX  $   samples before and after annealing at 250 °C for 30 min have been measured. After heated at 250 °C for 30 min, there is obviously a decrease of the perovskite intensity for the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br/C-ZnO  $   sample, while the reference C-  $  TiO_{2}  $  , C-  $  In_{2}O_{3}  $   and C-  $  SnO_{2}  $   have negligible changes indicating their robust chemical resistance to halides perovskites. By magnifying the low-intensity spectra, there are additional peaks for the annealed   $  CsPbI_{2}Br/C-ZnO  $   sample at 23.3° and 33.1°, which may be attributed to the (100) and (110) planes of   $  Zn_{x}Pb_{1-x}O  $   (Jade PDF#23-1497). The decreased intensity of CsPbI2Br and the newly-appeared   $  Zn_{x}Pb_{1-x}O  $   phase demonstrate that C-ZnO can react with   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $  , leading to the inferior photovoltaic performance and poor stability of C-ZnO-based PSCs.

# Experimental

Synthesis of metal oxide nanocrystals (NCs). All the precursors used here were purchased from Aldrich and used without further purification.   $  TiO_{2}  $   NCs were synthesized by a solvothermal method. 5 mmol of TB (titanium butoxide), 25 mmol OA (oleic acid), and 100 mmol absolute ethanol were added to a 40 mL Teflon cup. The obtained mixture was stirred for 10 min followed by transfer into a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave containing a 20-mL mixture of ethanol and water (96% ethanol in volume). The system was then heated at 180 °C for 18 h. The obtained white precipitates were washed several times and then dried at RT to obtain the   $  TiO_{2}  $   NCs.   $  In_{2}O_{3}  $   NCs were synthesized by a simple thermal decomposition method. A mixture of indium acetylacetonate (300 mg, 0.73 mmol), OA (3 mL), and 1-octadecene (17 mL) was stirred under   $  N_{2}  $   flow in a four-neck flask with a distillation setup. This mixture was heated to 270 °C for 30 min at 10 °C/min. Then the mixture was held at 270 °C for an additional hour. After it was cooled to RT, 30 mL ethanol was added. The product was separated by centrifugation, and the precipitates were dissolved in hexane. After washing several times and drying at RT, the   $  In_{2}O_{3}  $   NCs were obtained.   $  SnO_{2}  $   NCs were synthesized by a two-phase solvothermal synthesis approach. In a three-neck 100-mL flask, 1 mmol of tin(IV) acetylacetonate dichloride was dissolved in 5 mL OA by gradually heating to 140 °C with magnetic agitation in a   $  N_{2}  $   Atmosphere. Then the temperature was kept at 140 °C for 1 h. After cooling in a   $  N_{2}  $   flow, 30 mL toluene was added to obtain the oil phase. The aqueous solution was prepared by dissolving NaOH in 10 mL freshly refluxed deionized water. The aqueous solution and the oil phase were placed into a 100-mL Teflon cup, which was sealed tightly into a stainless-steel autoclave and placed into a preheated furnace at 180 °C for 18 h.

Film preparation and device fabrication. The FTO-coated glass substrates (TEC-15, Nippon Sheet Glass Co., Japan) were sequentially cleaned with detergent, deionized water, alcohol, and acetone under sonication. After that, they were dried in a   $  N_{2}  $   flow. A p-type NiMgLiO compact layer (  $  \sim  $  20-nm-thick) was deposited on the FTO glass by spray pyrolysis according to our previous work.  $  ^{20}  $   After cooling, the glass was immediately placed in a   $  N_{2}  $  -filled glovebox. The perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving CsI (99%, Sigma-Aldrich),   $  PbI_{2}  $   (99%, TCI), and   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (99%, Sigma-Aldrich) stoichiometrically in a mixed solvent of DMF (Sigma-Aldrich) and DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich) with a volume ratio of 2:3. The perovskite film was deposited by an anti-solvent-extraction method: typically, 70   $  \mu  $  L perovskite precursor solution was spin-coated at a rotation speed of 4000 rpm for 45 s; after the first 3~5 s, a quick drop-casting of diethyl ether was done, and the perovskite film was then annealed at 260 °C on a hot plate. Then 70   $  \mu  $  L metal oxide NC solutions were spin-coated at a rotation speed of 5000 rpm for 30 s. After annealing at 250 °C for 30 min, the electron transport layers were obtained. Finally, 90-nm metal electrodes were thermally evaporated under high vacuum (<5 × 10-4 Pa) to complete the device fabrication. After evaporating the electrodes, the encapsulation was conducted. Cover glasses and UV curing adhesive were used to seal the devices, and the sealant areas were illuminated under UV light for 10 min to curing the adhesive. It should be noted that the whole encapsulation process has been conducted in the   $  N_{2}  $  -filled glove box.

# Conclusions

In summary, a general methodology to deposit high-quality inorganic CTLs on top of inorganic perovskites has been proposed, and a series of all-layer-inorganic PSCs with robust long-term stability have been fabricated. The flat, uniform and pin-hole free morphologies of CTLs were achieved through uniform deposition of the monodispersed OA-capped MOX NCs. Annealing in   $  N_{2}  $   at high temperature improved the electrical conductivity and the band structure of the resultant C-MOX hybrid films but avoided damage to the perovskite layer. The special effects of band structure and conductivity of ETLs on each photovoltaic parameter have been discussed thoroughly in this work. Our experiments demonstrate that more positive   $  E_{F}  $   leads to larger   $  V_{OC}  $ , and better conductivity results in higher   $  J_{SC}  $ . Finally, the all-inorganic   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   PSC with the hybrid C-  $  TiO_{2}  $   ETL achieved the champion PCE of 14.0%, among the highest PCE of inverted   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   PSCs. Furthermore, the chemical stability of ETLs has been tested. It has been demonstrated that C-  $  TiO_{2}  $ , C-  $  In_{2}O_{3}  $   and C-  $  SnO_{2}  $   have enough chemical stabilities, while C-ZnO can react with the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   perovskite and form   $  Zn_{x}Pb_{1-x}O  $ , leading to the unexpected poor efficiency and the fast decay of PCE. Robust long-term stabilities of C-  $  TiO_{2}^{-}  $ , C-  $  In_{2}O_{3}^{-}  $  and C-  $  SnO_{2}  $ -based PSCs have been proven. They maintain >90% of their initial PCEs at 85 °C in the dark and >92% under continuous illumination at 45 °C for 1000 h, which make them of interest for practical photovoltaic applications. The present study provides a general method to prepare top CTLs simply and conveniently, which is also favourable for the future scaled-up mass production of all-layer-inorganic PSCs.
