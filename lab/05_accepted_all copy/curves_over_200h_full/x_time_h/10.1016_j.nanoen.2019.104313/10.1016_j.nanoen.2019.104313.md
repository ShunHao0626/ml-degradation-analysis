# Abstract

The defects in perovskite crystals and the penetration of moisture/oxygen into the perovskite layer are major problems for perovskite solar cells (PSCs) to achieve long-term stability and high power conversion efficiency (PCE). However, there is still a lack of multifunctional passivation materials to solve these problems. Herein, for the first time, we report oleyl amine-coated  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  quantum-dots (QDs), as a passivation material with dual functions to simultaneously passivate the surface defects and block the penetration of moisture/oxygen into the perovskite layer for stable and efficient PSCs. The  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs significantly reduce the defect density of the as-prepared  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  films by passivating under-coordinated Pb ions and I anions and effectively enhance charge extraction efficiency at the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / CH_3NH_3PbI_3} $  and  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3/ $  spiro-OMeTAD interfaces. Moreover, the hydrogen bond between H atoms of the OA and I atoms of the perovskite and the interface electric field at  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3/ $  OA interface also contribute to the improvement of efficiency and stability of PSCs. Finally, higher PCE  $ (20.02\%) $  is achieved by the PSCs with OA-coated  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs compared to that  $ (16.86\%) $  of the PSCs without OA-coated  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $ , corresponding to a  $ 18.7\% $  enhancement. Moreover, the PSCs with OA-coated  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs maintain  $ 90\% $  of initial efficiency after operation for  $ 280\mathrm{h} $ , indicating better stability than the PSCs without  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs.

Keywords: Perovskite solar cells;   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   quantum-dots; Passivate; Efficiency; Stability.

# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cell (PSC) is one of the hot research topics in the field of photovoltaics. Its power conversion efficiency (PCE) has dramatically risen to more than  $ 24.2\% $  (certified) [1, 2] from the initial  $ 3.8\% $ , [3] which is mainly attributed to some unique photoelectric properties of trihalide perovskite materials  $ (\mathrm{ABX}_3, \mathrm{A} = \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3^+, \mathrm{HC}(\mathrm{NH}_2)_2^+, \mathrm{Cs}^+; [4-8]\mathrm{B} = \mathrm{Sn}^{2+}, \mathrm{Pb}^{2+}; [9, 10]\mathrm{X} = \mathrm{Cl}^{-}, \mathrm{I}^{-}, \mathrm{Br}^{-}[11-14]) $ , such as high optical absorption coefficient ( $ >10^4\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $ ), [15-16] long charge diffusion length ( $ >1\mu \mathrm{m} $ ) [17] and high charge

mobility.[18] Although the PCE of the PSC has made great progress, there are still challenges remaining which hinder its further development for practical applications. One of the most critical challenges is to improve the performance stability while increasing the PCE of the PSC. In addition to the intrinsic chemical and structural instability of perovskite materials,[19-22] another major factor leading to the device instability is due to the existence of a large number of defects on the surface of the perovskite crystals prepared by solution method.[23-26] These defect states are typically dangling bonds on the perovskite surface, such as under-coordinated ions from Pb atoms or halide anions,[27, 28] which could cause moisture/oxygen to permeate into perovskite layer and subsequently destroy the device stability in ambient environment.[23, 29, 30] Meanwhile, these surface defects also create the carrier recombination centers to greatly degrade the performance of PSC. Therefore, reducing the surface defects on the of perovskite films may not only improve the PCE, but also promote the stability of the PSCs.

So far, surface defect passivation of perovskite crystals has been proved to be an effective interface engineering to simultaneously enhance the PCE and stability of PSCs. A typical surface defect passivation strategy is the use of Lewis acids [28] or Lewis bases.[31, 32] In addition, [6]-phenyl-C61-butyric acidmethyl ester (PCBM) or fullerenes/graphene derivatives are also employed.[33-36] Other organic materials with functional groups also displayed superior passivation effects, such as polystyrene,[37] hexamethylenetetramine,[38] 3-hexylthiophene,[39] phenyl ring containing molecules PEAI  $ (\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_2CH_2NH_3I}) $  ,[1] and nitrogen-doped carbon dots with rich nitrogen- and oxygen-containing groups.[40] Moreover, some inorganic materials including  $ \mathrm{Cd(SCN_2H_4)_2Cl_2} $  [4]  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  nanoparticles, [41] potassium halide,[42] Cu(tu)I [43] and gallium (III) acetylacetonate [44] have been deposited on the perovskite film to suppress the surface defects. By analyzing the chemical components of these passivation materials, it can be found that these passivation materials usually contain highly active chemical components, such as sulfur atoms, nitrogen atoms, halogen atoms or organic functional groups, through which the passivation materials interact with under-coordinated Pb ions, halide ions or organic cations in the perovskite crystals to achieve passivation of the defect state. However, pure inorganic salt compounds without the above-mentioned highly chemical active atoms and functional groups have rarely been reported so far as a passivation material.[45]

In this study, for the first time, we find that oleyl amine (OA)-coated inorganic salt   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   quantum-dots (QDs) with dual functions of surface passivation and moisture/oxygen blocking are highly effective to modify the surface of perovskite films for improving the performance and stability of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $  -based PSCs. Fig. 1a-d show the structure and energy levels of prepared PSCs and the single cell structure of the   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   and molecular structure of OA, respectively. Our experimental results show that, on one hand, the PSCs based on the perovskite films passivated by the   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   QDs achieves a significantly improved PCE of up to 20.02%, due to the effective passivation of under-coordinated Pb ions and I anions in the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   and interfacial electric field-assisted charge extraction; on the other hand, the PSCs with the OA-coated   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   produce a better stability, which is mainly attributed to the passivation of the defect states in perovskite and the remarkably blocked penetration of moisture/oxygen into the perovskite.

# 2. Results and discussion

The as-synthesized   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   QDs exhibited the XRD peaks at   $  2\theta  $   of   $  14.3^{\circ}  $  ,   $  17.2^{\circ}  $  ,   $  21.2^{\circ}  $  ,   $  24.1^{\circ}  $   and   $  41.1^{\circ}  $   (Fig. S1), corresponding to the (110), (012), (022), (031) and (312) planes of monoclinic   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   (PDF#01-073-4029), respectively. TEM and HRTEM images of the   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   QDs (Fig. S2) showed that the   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   QDs were almost spherical with a size of 2-5 nm and exhibit the lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.32 nm, matching the (032) plane of monoclinic   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   and indicating a high crystallinity of the QDs.

Comparison between the XRD patterns of the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  and  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 / \mathrm{PbSO}_4(\mathrm{PbO})_4 $  films (Fig. S3) showed that, the diffraction peak positions of the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  crystals in both films are not evidently varied, but the peak intensity of the diffraction peak is remarkably enhanced after the  $ \mathrm{PbSO}_4(\mathrm{PbO})_4 $  QDs treatment, indicating that the presence of  $ \mathrm{PbSO}_4(\mathrm{PbO})_4 $  QDs did not change the crystalline structure of the perovskite film but improve its crystallinity; moreover, the full widths at half maximum (FWHM) of the (111) and (220) peaks of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3:\mathrm{PbSO}_4(\mathrm{PbO})_4 $  film are much narrower than that of the pristine  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  film, suggesting that the  $ \mathrm{PbSO}_4(\mathrm{PbO})_4 $  QDs treatment significantly increases the size of perovskite crystals. The improved crystallinity of perovskite thin film after  $ \mathrm{PbSO}_4(\mathrm{PbO})_4 $  QDs treatment may be due to the electric attraction interaction between the H atoms of the OA and the I atoms of the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{I} $  and between the  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  of the  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and  $ \mathrm{SO}_4^{2-} $  ions of the  $ \mathrm{PbSO}_4(\mathrm{PbO})_4 $  (refer to our FT-IR results in Fig. 3 and calculated results in Fig. 7), which can reduce the nucleation rate of perovskite crystal and therefore improve the crystallinity of perovskite crystals.

The effect of  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs on the size of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  crystals and the surface morphologies of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  films were further examined by SEM, as shown in Fig. 2. For both films, no obvious pinholes are found, demonstrating good compactness of the film. Fig. 2a shows that the surface of the perovskite film with pure CBZ anti-solvent treatment is uniformly covered by the perovskite crystals ranging from  $ 100~\mathrm{nm} $  to  $ 400~\mathrm{nm} $ , which is consistent with the previous results.[46] Fig. 2b shows that after the  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  treatment,  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs are successfully deposited on the surface of the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  film. More importantly, compared to pure perovskite film (Fig. 2a), the size of the perovskite crystals in the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 / \mathrm{PbSO}_4(\mathrm{PbO})_4 $  film becomes larger, varying from 200 nm to about  $ 700~\mathrm{nm} $ , indicating the improved crystallinity of the perovskite crystals. These results are in good agreement with XRD results (Fig. S3). Besides, the boundary between the perovskite grains is partially filled with  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs, which further promotes the compactness of the film. Fig. S4 showed the 2-dimensional (2D) AFM height image and corresponding 3D topographic image of both films. Clearly, due to the local aggregation of  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs (Fig. 2b), the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 / \mathrm{PbSO}_4(\mathrm{PbO})_4 $  film shows a rougher surface with a root-mean-square (RMS) roughness of  $ 10.45~\mathrm{nm} $  than the pure  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  film ( $ \mathrm{RMS} = 6.29~\mathrm{nm} $ ). Notably, no evidences show that the  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs have penetrated deeply into the perovskite layer. We speculate that the  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs are mainly distributed in the inner part of the perovskite film near the top surface, similar to the case of PCBM modifier.

To further analyze the chemical composition of thin films, FT-IR spectroscopy was used to characterize OA-coated   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   QDs,   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   and   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}/PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   powders and the results are shown in Fig. 3a. For OA-coated   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   QDs, the typical peak of tetrahedral symmetric sulfate ions at   $  1100\ cm^{-1}  $   and characteristic peaks of OA at   $  2852\ cm^{-1}  $   and   $  2926\ cm^{-1}  $   are observed, confirming the coexistence of OA and   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   QDs.

To evaluate the influence of the OA-coated   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   QDs on the charge recombination and transfer kinetics in the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   film, the PL spectra and time-resolved PL (TRPL) decay transients of the FTO/cp-  $  TiO_{2}  $  /mp-  $  TiO_{2}/CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   and FTO/cp-  $  TiO_{2}  $  /mp-  $  TiO_{2}/CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}/PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   films are measured. As shown in Fig. S6a, the PL intensity of the film with   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   is significantly lower than that of the native   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   film. To understand the PL quenching, the electronic structures of   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   QDs are first investigated by UPS spectra, as shown in Fig. 3b. The   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   QDs has the valence band-edge energy (  $  E_{v}  $  ) is   $  E_{v} = -21.20\ eV + (E_{kin,\max} - E_{kin,\min}) = -5.15\ eV  $  , which is higher than that (-5.43 eV) of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $  . [47] The conduction band-edge energy (  $  E_{c}  $  ) of the   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   can be further estimated from the band gap (2.34 eV) calculated by first-principles calculations in Fig. 3c, which is -2.8eV. Therefore, the much higher quenching of the PL intensity in the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}/PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   film is reasonably attributed to the transfer of the photogenerated holes from   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   film to   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   QDs at the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}/PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   interface for the exciton dissociation (Fig. 1b). Further, the charge transfer processes in the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   and   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}/PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   films on   $  TiO_{2}  $   surfaces were quantitatively analyzed by TRPL, as shown in Fig. S6b. It appears that the charge recombination lifetime   $  \tau  $   (32.10 ns) in the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}/PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   film is much shorter than that (63.07 ns) of the pristine   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   film. [8] For the FTO/cp-  $  TiO_{2}  $  /mp-  $  TiO_{2}/CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   film, there is only the   $  TiO_{2}/CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   interface for transferring electrons from   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   to the   $  TiO_{2}  $   for spatial separation of photogenerated electrons and holes in the perovskite layer. However, the FTO/cp-  $  TiO_{2}/mp-  $  TiO_{2}/CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}/PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   film has both the   $  TiO_{2}/CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   interface for injecting electrons into   $  TiO_{2}  $   and the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}:PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   interface for injecting holes into   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $ , generating a much efficient spatial separation of photogenerated electrons and holes in the perovskite layer. Therefore, the shorter charge recombination lifetime is mainly due to the efficient extraction of holes in the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   by the   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $ . [48, 49]

These results that are derived from the dark  $ J $ -V curves and the dependence of  $ J_{\mathrm{sc}} $  and  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  on  $ I $ , strongly prove that the cell with the  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs has a fewer defect state density and the reduced charge recombination.

To estimate the effects of the  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QD treatment on the density of defect states ( $ n_{\mathrm{trap}} $ ), the space-charge-limited current (SCLC) measurements are employed for the cells, as shown in Fig. 6d. As the bias voltage increases and exceeds the defect state filling voltage ( $ V_{\mathrm{TFL}} $ ), a sharp-rise region can be observed in the  $ J $ -V curves of these two cells, corresponding to the trap-filled limit. The  $ V_{\mathrm{TFL}} $  can be used to estimate the  $ n_{\mathrm{trap}} $  based on equation  $ n_{\mathrm{trap}} = 2\varepsilon_0\varepsilon V_{\mathrm{TFL}} / \mathrm{e}L^2 $ , [54] where  $ \varepsilon_0 $  is vacuum permittivity ( $ \varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times 10^{-12} $  F/m),  $ \varepsilon $  is the dielectric constants of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  ( $ \varepsilon = 32 $ ),  $ e $  is the elementary charge, and  $ L $  is the thickness of the perovskite layer. With the  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QD treatment, the  $ V_{\mathrm{TFL}} $  value are increased from  $ 0.11\mathrm{V} $  to  $ 0.14\mathrm{V} $ , and consequently the  $ n_{\mathrm{trap}} $  value are reduced from  $ 1.72\times 10^{15}\mathrm{cm}^{-3} $  to  $ 1.35\times 10^{15}\mathrm{cm}^{-3} $ . Therefore, the  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QD treatment effectively suppresses the defects in the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  in agreement with the TRPL results.

In order to understand the mechanisms of defect state passivation and stability enhancement after surface treatment using the OA-coated  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs, DFT calculations were performed to get insight into the atomic structures at the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 / \mathrm{PbSO}_4(\mathrm{PbO})_4 $  and interface, as shown in Fig. 7. Since the (001) plane of the perovskite is most stable [55] and has a better lattice match to the (100) plane of the  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $ , so they were chosen to constitute the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 / \mathrm{PbSO}_4(\mathrm{PbO})_4 $  interface for calculation and analysis. It can be observed that the O atoms of the  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  have a strong interaction with Pb atoms of the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $ . However, this interaction does not cause the disintegration of the Pb-I bonds in the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  and the octahedron  $ \mathrm{PbI}_6 $  cages still exist despite weak distortion. Generally, when the pure  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  are exposed to the oxygen in the air, the  $ \mathrm{O}_2 $  near the perovskite surface turns into the  $ \mathrm{O}_2^- $  because the photogenerated electrons are captured from the perovskite.[56] Furthermore, the  $ \mathrm{O}_2^- $  will be adsorbed on the  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ -terminated surfaces of the perovskite due to the coulombic attraction of cations, such as  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3^+ $  and  $ \mathrm{Pb}_2^+ $ . However, due to the higher electronegativity of O atom compared to I atom, the I in the Pb-I bond of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_6 $  octahedron is replaced by O and the volatile iodine ( $ \mathrm{I}_2 $ ) is formed, which eventually causes the disintegration of the perovskite structure.[56] Fortunately, in our case, since the O atoms have formed a Pb-O bond with the Pb atoms in the  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $ , the electronegativity of the O atoms in the  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  has been greatly reduced, which effectively prevents these O atoms from replacing the I atoms in the perovskite, thereby maintaining the  $ \mathrm{PbI}_6 $  octahedron. More importantly, the formation of Pb-O bonds at the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 / \mathrm{PbSO}_4(\mathrm{PbO})_4 $  interface effectively prevents the oxidation of Pb in the perovskite by  $ \mathrm{O}_2 $ , which is believed to be the intrinsic reason for the improved device stability.

On the other hand, the interaction between the I atoms of the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  and the Pb atoms of the  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  can be observed in Fig. 7a. It has been revealed that I ions can be lost from the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  crystals during the thermal annealing process, resulting in under-coordinated Pb atoms (or defect states) both on the crystal surface and at the grain boundaries. [23, 24] Therefore, in our case, the electrical attraction of Pb atoms of the  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  to the I atoms of the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  will effectively reduce the loss of I atoms, which is very helpful for the improved stability of the perovskite material. The much more effective interfacial charge transfers after the  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs treatment are also supported by the calculated hole and electron wave function distributions in the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  layer as shown in Fig. 7b. Calculated results reveal that, due to the formation of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3/\mathrm{PbSO}_4(\mathrm{PbO})_4 $  interface, the hole wave function appears more concentrated near the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3/\mathrm{PbSO}_4(\mathrm{PbO})_4 $  interface, while the electron wave function appears more concentrated near the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / CH_3NH_3PbI_3} $  interface. The more concentrated the hole (electron) wave function is, the greater the probability the holes (electrons) will appear. Therefore, our calculations demonstrate that, the deposited  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  on the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  facilitates to the transfer of the electrons into  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  layer and the holes to the spiro-OMeTAD layer, which effectively promotes charge extraction from the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  active layer.

Since the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  film prepared by solution method is usually P-type with a Fermi level  $ (E_f) $  of  $ -4.73\mathrm{eV} $ , [49, 58] much lower than the  $ E_{f} $  (i.e.,  $ -2.65\mathrm{eV} $ ) of the  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs (Fig.3), the Fermi of the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3:\mathrm{PbSO}_4(\mathrm{PbO})_4 $  film is inevitably increased to a higher location by  $ \Delta E_{\mathrm{f}} $ , which is confirmed by the Fermi energy level (-4.12 eV) of the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3:\mathrm{PbSO}_4(\mathrm{PbO})_4 $  film measured by UPS spectra (Fig. S9). Accordingly, we here propose a Fermi-level induced interfacial barrier model to explain the improved electron and hole charge transfers at two different interfaces, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 8. At the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / CH_3NH_3PbI_3} $  interface, due to the electron trap states both in the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  and  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3,[59,60] $  the charge depletion regions exist on both sides of the interface, which results in the downward bending of the energy bands within the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  layer (Fig. 8a). Such a band bending inevitably results in an energy barrier  $ \Delta \delta_{\mathrm{e}} $  that would prevent electrons from moving across this interface to the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ . However, in the case of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3:\mathrm{PbSO}_4(\mathrm{PbO})_4 $  film, the up-shifted  $ E_{f} $  of the film may significantly reduce the band bending of the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  and the energy barrier  $ \Delta \delta_{\mathrm{e}} $  (Fig. 8b) for the much easier electron transfer from the perovskite to the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ . On the other hand, at the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3/ $  spiro-OMeTAD interface, due to the slightly higher  $ E_{f} $  (-4.70 eV) of the spiro-OMeTAD than that (-4.73 eV) of the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $ , the energy

# 3. Conclusions

In summary, OA-coated  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs are successfully synthesized as a passivation material for stable and efficient PSCs. Our experimental results demonstrate that after the passivation with OA-coated  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs, the PCE of the PSCs with  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  is as high as  $ (20.02\pm 0.33)\% $  , increased by  $ 18.7\% $  compared with the PSCs without  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  . Moreover, the stability of PSCs with OA-coated  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs has been significantly enhanced with  $ 90\% $  of initial efficiency maintained after  $ 280\mathrm{h} $  in ambient environment (65% relative humidity). Based on the first-principles calculations, it is found that due to the formation of Pb-O bonds at the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 / \mathrm{PbSO}_4(\mathrm{PbO})_4 $  interface and the formation of hydrogen bonds between the hydrogen atoms in the OA and the I atoms in the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  , under-coordinated Pb ions and I anions are passivated, which effectively reduces defect density in perovskite and improves the charge extraction efficiency at the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / CH_3NH_3PbI_3} $  and  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3/ $  spiro-OMeTAD interfaces, leading to improved  $ J_{sc} $  and PCE of the PSCs with OA-coated  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs. A Fermi-level induced interfacial barrier model is proposed and explains the improved interface charge extractions at both  $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / CH_3NH_3PbI_3} $  and  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3/ $  spiro-OMeTAD interfaces. The OA-coated  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs exhibit dual functions to simultaneously passivate the surface defects in perovskite and block the penetration of moisture/oxygen into the perovskite layer. Our research provides a new way to simultaneously improve the performance and stability of perovskite solar cells by using a passivation material with dual functions.

# 4.2. Preparation of   $  PbSO_{4}(PbO)_{4}  $   QDs.

$ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs were synthesized as follows. First, 2 mmol PbO, 7 mmol oleyl amine (OA) and 4 mmol octadecene (ODE) were degassed for  $ 60\mathrm{min} $  at  $ 120^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  under  $ \mathbf{N}_2 $  flow. Then, a mixed solution of  $ 180~\mu l $  bis(trimethylsily)sulfide (TMS) and 5ml 1-octadecene (ODE) was injected quickly and the heating stopped after 1 min. After the solution is cooled to room temperature, 5ml toluene and  $ 10\mathrm{ml} $  ethanol were added to the solution to obtain the precipitation of dark brown PbS QDs. The product was isolated by centrifugation at  $ 4500~\mathrm{rpm} $ , and then the centrifugal material was cleaned with toluene/ethanol solvent/antisolvent for at least three times and dried to obtain the PbS QDs powder. Finally, the PbS powder was placed in an air atmosphere and slowly oxidized for 36 months to synthesize  $ \mathrm{PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $  QDs following reaction:  $ \mathrm{PbS + O_2\rightarrow PbSO_4(PbO)_4} $ .

# 4.3. Preparation of   $  TiO_{2}  $   films for solar cells

The compact (cp- $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ ) film is prepared by spin-coating cp- $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  precursor solution on the ITO glass substrates. Before that, the etched ITO glasses were ultrasonically cleaned with a standard regiment of deionized water, isopropanol (IPA) and acetone in sequence, followed by UV/Ozone (UVO) treatment for 20min. The cp- $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  precursor is prepared as follows. 1 ml titanium butoxide, 1 ml glacial acetic acid and 1 ml acetaldehyde acetonide were added gradually into 9 ml absolute ethanol solution at room temperature under stirring for 2h, then 1 ml deionized water was slowly added and the mixed solution was stirring for a day. The obtained orange sol is spin coated onto the ITO substrates at 6500 rpm for 30 s, after which the substrates were calcined at  $ 550^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for half an hour to obtain the FTO/cp- $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  films.  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  paste (Dyesol 18NR-T) is diluted with absolute ethanol at 1:7 by weight to prepare mesoporous  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  (mp- $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ ) film. The  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  nanoparticle dispersion was spin coated onto the FTO/cp- $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  film, followed by the annealing at  $ 550^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for 30 min, resulting in the FTO/cp- $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ / mp- $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  film as electron transport layer (ETL).
