# $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 / \mathrm{TiO}_2 $  Electron Transporting Bilayers: A Route to Light Stable Perovskite Solar Cells

Mousa Abuhelaiqa, Naoyuki Shibayama, Xiao-Xin Gao, Hiroyuki Kanda,  $  ^{*}  $   and Mohammad Khaja Nazeeruddin  $  ^{*}  $

Cite This: ACS Appl. Energy Mater. 2021, 4, 3424–3430

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Perovskite solar cells have driven a paradigm shift in the research domain of photovoltaics. Although the field has progressed tremendously, device stability holds back further progress and application to the market. In this work, we aim to gain insights into electron transport layer (ETL)/perovskite interfacial degradation by characterizing perovskite films with   $  TiO_{2}  $  ,   $  SnO_{2}  $  , and   $  TiO_{2}/SnO_{2}  $   ETLs. Our results demonstrate that   $  SnO_{2}  $   and   $  TiO_{2}/SnO_{2}  $   maintained long-term stability of 1000 h under maximum power point tracking, while the   $  TiO_{2}  $   sample degraded upon onset. We attempt to link this analogy to the perovskite film properties by monitoring the optoelectronic and morphological features under light soaking for each electron transporting layer configuration. Our results demonstrate reduced photoluminescent emission and decay, perovskite film morphological degradation, and a surge in   $  PbI_{2}  $   in   $  TiO_{2}  $   samples, trends that were not found in   $  SnO_{2}  $   and   $  TiO_{2}/SnO_{2}  $   samples. We highlight the passivation

effect of   $  SnO_{2}  $   films in the bilayered ETL configuration (  $  TiO_{2}/SnO_{2}  $  ) that results in stability enhancement phenomena as speculated from our constructed energy diagram. The results show that the   $  SnO_{2}  $   layer could suppress charge recombination with the perovskite layer and improves the optical durability.

KEYWORDS: perovskite solar cells, light soaking, long-term stability, electron transport layers, ETL/perovskite interfacing,   $  TiO_{2}  $  ,   $  SnO_{2}  $  ,   $  TiO_{2}/SnO_{2}  $

# INTRODUCTION

Organic–inorganic metal halide perovskite materials have rapidly changed the landscape of developing photovoltaic (PV) technologies since the first finding,  $  ^{1-4}  $  with high-achieving perovskite solar cells (PSCs) accomplishing power conversion efficiencies (PCEs)  $  ^{5-9}  $  over 25% in just over a decade.  $  ^{10,11}  $  Light-absorbing perovskite materials based on tunable organic–inorganic components, traditionally methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI  $  _{3}  $ ), have yielded desirable band gaps,  $  ^{12-15}  $  high carrier diffusion length exceeding 1  $  \mu  $  m,  $  ^{16,17}  $  and exceptional defect tolerance.  $  ^{18,19}  $  Moreover, such high-performing perovskite materials are made possible with scalable and facile, cost-effective fabrication techniques including blade coating,  $  ^{20,21}  $  inkjet printing,  $  ^{22,23}  $  and sublimation.  $  ^{24,25}  $  The astounding progression and low cost of production have made perovskite solar cells a domain of interest for versatile applications from flexible surfaces  $  ^{26}  $  to commercial perovskite modules.  $  ^{27,28}  $  However, challenges imposed by long-term operational stability remain a barrier that hampers the introduction of perovskite technology into the market.

Operational stability remains to this day a challenge mostly attributed to the decomposition of sensitive components in the PSC stack including the perovskite film under light irradiation

# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Planar PSCs were fabricated with the following architecture fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)/ETL/perovskite/spiro-OMe-TAD/Au where the ETL film was varied, as shown in the schematic in Figure 1a. A triple-cation perovskite based on  $ (\mathrm{FAPbI}_3)_{0.875}(\mathrm{MAPbBr}_3)_{0.125}(\mathrm{CsPbI}_3)_{0.1} $  was utilized as a photoactive layer. Three different ETL configurations were fabricated with the same PSC architecture with  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ ,  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ , and  $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / SnO_2} $ . Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the perovskite surface and device cross section were recorded for all ETLs (Figures S1 and S2, respectively). Figure S1 shows no change in the perovskite grain size and Figure S2 shows no change in the perovskite thickness across the device cross section when ETL configurations are varied. These SEM images indicate identical grain growth during device fabrication irrespective of the ETL. PSC devices were placed inside an encapsulated stability apparatus in ambient nitrogen with an approximate relative humidity of  $ 0\% $  and exposed to light intensity equivalent to AM 1.5G illumination. The bias was maintained at MPPT and cells were kept at room temperature during the stability testing. Figure 1b demonstrates the normalized PCE against time for the three PSC samples with the different ETLs.  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  and  $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / SnO_2} $  samples show excellent PCE retentions of 95 and  $ 97\% $ , respectively, after  $ 1000\mathrm{h} $ , while the PCE of the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  sample abruptly deteriorated from the start to  $ 25\% $  after  $ 1000\mathrm{h} $ . The sharp decrease in  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  samples is identical to stability behavior previously reported as a result of the direct contact of  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  with the perovskite layer,[31,33] causing perovskite degradation. The stability enhancement in  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  and  $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / SnO_2} $  samples proves the detrimental role ETL layers have on stability. To investigate the effect of UV light on stability, we measured MPPT with UV light (Figure S3 in the Supporting Information). The PCE of the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  sample dropped significantly to  $ 49\% $  from the initial efficiency at  $ 50\mathrm{h} $ , a faster

rate than the   $  TiO_{2}  $   samples without UV light (82% at 50 h, Figure 1b). The sharper drop in PCE with UV light is a consequence of the photocatalytic tendency of   $  TiO_{2}  $  ,  $  ^{33,42}  $   whereas for the case without UV light, Ahn et al. demonstrated that interfacial   $  TiO_{2}  $  /perovskite degradation occurs due to the accumulation of trapped carriers at the interface.  $  ^{31}  $   We further run a series of characterizations to understand features of stable and degraded perovskite samples.

To understand degradation processes with respect to carrier dynamics occurring with each ETL configuration, photoluminescence (PL) emission and decay characterization were performed to analyze charge generation and transport processes. Figure 2a–c demonstrates the PL emission spectra for   $  TiO_{2}  $  ,   $  SnO_{2}  $  , and   $  TiO_{2}/SnO_{2}  $   PSC samples, respectively, before and after 50 h of MPPT operation. The PL emission analysis highlights a sharp decrease in the PL peak of the   $  TiO_{2}  $   sample (Figure 2a), whereas   $  SnO_{2}  $   and   $  TiO_{2}/SnO_{2}  $   samples (Figure 2b,c, respectively) demonstrated no prominent changes in the peak before and afterlight soaking. The lower PL emission peak suggests faster and possibly more charge carrier recombination,  $  ^{33}  $   resulting in a reduction in the normalized PCE for   $  TiO_{2}  $   with photovoltaic (PV) operation, as observed in Figure 1b. Further, Figure 2d–f demonstrates PL decay curves for each   $  TiO_{2}  $  ,   $  SnO_{2}  $  , and   $  TiO_{2}/SnO_{2}  $   PSC samples, respectively. Most notably, the decay time has significantly decreased for the   $  TiO_{2}  $   sample, while for the other two samples, the decay time has no significant changes. The decay curves imply that the lifetime of charge carriers in the   $  TiO_{2}  $   sample is dramatically reduced from 90.9 to 34.0   $  \mu  $  s (Table S1) by opening recombination pathways after PV operation as compared with other ETL samples, which could be due to the photodegradation with   $  TiO_{2}  $  . To elucidate the charge carrier recombination caused by the photodegradation of   $  TiO_{2}  $  , we measured the diode factor that is related to Shockley–Read–Hall recombination using the SunsVoc method.  $  ^{43,44}  $   The samples were measured after light soaking for 100 h (Figure S4 in the Supporting Information). The diode factor of   $  TiO_{2}  $   was 4.25, whereas those of   $  SnO_{2}  $   and   $  TiO_{2}/SnO_{2}  $   were 2.02 and 1.48, respectively. This result

Cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were captured for perovskite samples with   $  TiO_{2}  $  ,   $  SnO_{2}  $  , and   $  TiO_{2}/SnO_{2}  $   ETLs before and after subjecting the samples to AM 1.5G simulated light for 500 h, as shown in Figure 3. The images demonstrate a significant deterioration of the perovskite film with   $  TiO_{2}  $   after light exposure, as shown in Figure 3a,d, while perovskite films for other ETLs have relatively maintained morphological integrity as seen with   $  SnO_{2}  $   (Figure 3b,e) and   $  TiO_{2}/SnO_{2}  $   (Figure 3c,f). This result, coupled with the PL measurements, indicates that due to morphological deterioration, photoactive regions in the bulk of the perovskite have been diminished, explaining the quenching in the PL emission peak and decay.

To confirm this analogy, X-ray diffraction (XRD) and ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectroscopy were conducted on light-exposed perovskite samples, as demonstrated in Figure 4. The XRD analysis was conducted on samples before light soaking (Figure 4a), showing roughly equal initial   $  PbI_{2}  $   peaks for all ETLs at 12.7° due to the excess   $  PbI_{2}  $   in the perovskite recipe. After light soaking (Figure 4b), the analysis shows a relatively higher increase in the   $  PbI_{2}  $   peak at 12.7° in the   $  TiO_{2}  $   sample when compared with   $  SnO_{2}  $   and   $  TiO_{2}/SnO_{2}  $   samples, suggesting perovskite decomposition, as shown in Figure 4b. Moreover, UV–vis analysis was carried out before and after light soaking (Figure 4c,d) for 500 h. The UV–vis analysis clearly showed that the absorbance in fact decreased for   $  TiO_{2}  $   samples throughout the exposed light spectrum (600 to 850 nm), which is consistent with the SEM and XRD results.

Energy band diagram analysis was obtained for   $  TiO_{2}  $  ,   $  SnO_{2}  $  , and   $  TiO_{2}/SnO_{2}  $  , as shown in Figure S5. The energy band structure was measured by ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) and optical band gap measurement (Figures S6 and S7). The conduction band edge of   $  SnO_{2}/TiO_{2}  $   (-3.70 eV) is approximately the same as that of   $  SnO_{2}  $   (-3.71 eV). However, the Fermi level of   $  TiO_{2}/SnO_{2}  $   (-4.01 eV) is more aligned with that of   $  TiO_{2}  $   (-3.98 eV). We note that the different Fermi levels between   $  TiO_{2}/SnO_{2}  $   and   $  SnO_{2}  $   could be due to the thin thickness (  $  \sim  $  10 nm) of the   $  SnO_{2}  $   films. Due to the small thickness, the Fermi level could be affected by the underlying   $  TiO_{2}  $   film. This phenomenon is observed similarly for   $  MoO_{X}  $   where the Fermi level of thin   $  MnO_{X}  $   films was affected by the underlayer film.  $  ^{45}  $   We construct an ETL/perovskite interface band diagram as shown in Figure 5 for the bilayered   $  TiO_{2}/SnO_{2}  $   configuration. It was reported that when charge recombination occurred at the   $  TiO_{2}  $  /perovskite interface, the perovskite will be decomposed and an increase of   $  PbI_{2}  $   content would occur, which is consistent with our XRD result (Figure 4b).  $  ^{31,33}  $   Thus, we presume that   $  SnO_{2}  $   plays a role of a passivation layer to suppress the degradation of the perovskite, resulting in high stability under light illumination. In addition to stability improvement,   $  TiO_{2}  $  /  $  SnO_{2}  $   bilayers result in improved PV performance when compared with   $  TiO_{2}  $   and   $  SnO_{2}  $   PSC devices (Figure S8). # CONCLUSIONS

We investigated perovskite degradation characteristics based on   $  TiO_{2}  $  ,   $  SnO_{2}  $  , and   $  TiO_{2}/SnO_{2}  $   underlying ETLs. Our MPPT testing showed a strong performance decline in PSCs with   $  TiO_{2}  $  , whereas PSCs with   $  SnO_{2}  $   and   $  TiO_{2}/SnO_{2}  $   showed no significant drop over the course of 1000 h. Further, a series of characterizations were done on perovskite films to gain insights into stability trends observed with the different ETLs.   $  TiO_{2}  $   sample characterizations after light soaking show a prominent decline in charge dynamic processes as interpreted from the PL peak and lifetime drop. Moreover, SEM images clearly show a morphological deterioration of the perovskite film after light exposure. These trends were not observed with   $  SnO_{2}  $   and   $  TiO_{2}/SnO_{2}  $   samples, demonstrating the passivation ability of   $  SnO_{2}  $   films. The energy band diagram indicates the suppression of the charge recombination at the   $  TiO_{2}/perovskite  $   interface by   $  SnO_{2}  $   passivation. This result indicates that photostability can be enhanced by preventing charge recombination at the ETL/perovskite interface.

# EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

Complete perovskite solar cell samples were prepared for the JV and stability measurements. Laser-scribed FTO glass (Nippon Sheet Glass) was bath-sonicated with detergent diluted in water (1:4) for 20 min, in only water for 10 min, and in ethanol and acetone for 5 min each. TiO  $  _{2}  $  layers were deposited by spray pyrolysis of the precursor (0.25 mL of titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetylacetonate) in 4.5 mL of ethanol) on FTO at 450 °C and left 30 min for sintering. The SnO  $  _{2}  $  layers were deposited by spin-coating SnO  $  _{2}  $  nanoparticles diluted in water (3.66%) at 3000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 150 °C for 30 min. The substrates were UV ozone treated for 15 min before depositing the perovskite layer. A triple-cation perovskite recipe with excess lead (FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  )  $  _{0.875}  $  (MAPbBr  $  _{3}  $  )  $  _{0.125}  $  (CsPbI  $  _{3}  $  )  $  _{0.1}  $  was prepared by adding methylammonium bromide (0.15 M), lead bromide (0.15 M), cesium iodide (0.13 M), formamidinium iodide (1.0 M), and lead iodide (1.2 M) into dimethylformamide (DMF)/dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) = 4:1 (volumetric ratio). The above perovskite solution was spin-coated on top of the substrate at a two-step program set at 1000 and 5000 rpm for 12 and 30 s, respectively. One-hundred microliters of an antisolvent (chlorobenzene) was deposited on the substrate after 15 s of the second step of the spin-coating program. The substrate was placed on a heating plate at 100 °C for 1 h to allow perovskite thermal post-processing. The hole-transporting material (HTM) (spiro-OMeTAD) precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 60 mg of spiro-OMeTAD in 768 μL of chlorobenzene, 24 μL of 4-tert-butylpyridine, 14 μL of bis(trifluoromethane)-sulfonimide lithium in an acetonitrile solution (517 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ), and 11 μL of tris(2(1H-pyrazol-1-yl)-4-tert-butylpyridine)cobalt(III) tri

[bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide] in an acetonitrile solution (376 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ). The HTM precursor solution was deposited dynamically on the spin-coated samples at 5000 rpm for 20 s. Subsequently, 70 nm of gold was deposited through a sublimation procedure. The light-soaked (for XRD and SEM) samples were prepared by depositing the ETL layer first (TiO  $  _{2}  $  , SnO  $  _{2}  $  , and TiO  $  _{2}  $  /SnO  $  _{2}  $   configurations) on the FTO substrate. The perovskite layer was deposited on top. The PL samples were similarly prepared; however, the substrate used was glass.

# CHARACTERIZATION

The stability test was performed by keeping the samples in the dark without encapsulation at 0% relative humidity under a nitrogen atmosphere and 25 °C. Microscopy images were captured by a cold field emission scanning electron microscope (SU8200, Hitachi High-Tech Co.), with an active area mask of   $  4 \times 4 \, mm^2  $  . The scanning step and the speed were set at 10 mV and 50 mV   $  s^{-1}  $  , respectively. UV/vis spectroscopy was measured by using a Lambda 950S (PerkinElmer Inc.) instrument. Photoluminescence emission spectra were recorded using an LS-55 fluorescence spectrometer (PerkinElmer Inc.) and an excitation wavelength of 640 nm. Time-resolved photoluminescence spectroscopy was performed by a Fluorolog TCSPC with an excitation wavelength of 640 nm (HORIBA Ltd.). Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) equipped with a He-I source (  $  h\nu = 21.22 \, eV  $  ) (AXIS Nova, Kratos Analytical Ltd., U.K.) was performed to determine the valence band energy and the Fermi level. The Fermi level of the samples was referred to as that of Au, which was in electrical contact with a sample in UPS measurements. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was carried out using a D8 Advance diffractometer (Bruker Corporation) with Cu Kα radiation (  $  \lambda_{K\alpha} = 1.5418 \, \AA  $  ). I-V measurements were performed using an Oriel VeraSol solar simulator (Newport Corporation) using an LCE-50 calibrator (Centronics) from 1.2 to 0 V.
