# Abstract

The ionic nature of hybrid perovskites indicates the existence of inevitable and multi-type defects. Design of effective passivation molecules becomes a vital means to suppress charge recombination and reduce the voltage loss in perovskite solar cells. However, most of the reported passivation molecules show single effect and the passivation mechanism of molecules with multiple functional groups is unclear. Herein, on the premise of making sure the combined passivation effect of amine, carbonyl group and benzene ring, we tailored multifunctional passivation molecules (4-fluorobenzamide and 4-chlorobenzamide) by introducing halogen functional group. After employing high-electronegativity halogen groups, the hydrogen bond with organic cations and ionic bond with  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} $  were formed, which is the key to prevent defect regeneration and ion migration. The perovskites with halogenated passivation molecules displayed reduced trap densities and increased carrier lifetimes. Finally, multifunctional passivation molecules can not only effectively heal defects but also improve the stability of perovskite materials. The optimal devices with 4-fluorobenzamide

# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic metal halide perovskites are raising a promising revolution in the field of photovoltaics and optoelectronics [1]. Owing to the unique properties such as brilliant absorption coefficient [2], high carrier mobility [3,4] and low exciton binding energy [5,6], the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has quickly increased from  $ 3.8\% $  to a certified  $ 25.2\% $  since 2009 [7-11]. However, a large number of studies has reported that the polycrystalline perovskite films own a large density of charge traps near the surface or at the grain boundaries [12-14], which will not only result in nonradiative charge-recombination loss but also worsen the intrinsic stability of perovskites [15-18]. Effective passivation has become the primary focus to further improve the performance and stability of perovskite photovoltaics [19-24].

The trap states are generated from the structural defects in perovskite materials during both the preparation and operation process caused by the under-coordinated ions and ionic migration at crystal surface [25,26]. The defect density in polycrystalline film is several orders of magnitude higher than that in single crystal [27,28]. It has been proved that the magnitude in  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  polycrystalline thin film is about  $ 10^{16}\mathrm{cm}^{-3} $ , which is  $ 10^{9} \sim 10^{10}\mathrm{cm}^{-3} $  in its single crystals [22]. Thus, it is critical to reduce defect sites and

then suppress nonradiative charge recombination to refrain from charge and energy losses. Currently, the main strategies to heal defects in PSCs are screening passivation agents and employing interface modification layers [30-34]. Although the passivation agents can penetrate into the polycrystalline perovskite along the grain boundary when spin-coated as interface modification [17,35], the passivation effect displays some limitations compared to the direct introduction in the preparation of polycrystalline films.

The design of effective passivation molecules without exerting negative impact on the morphology and optical properties of perovskite films is an ideal means to improve their photovoltaic performance and stability. To heal the defects with different characteristics, larger number of chemical molecules were employed by taking full advantage of the interaction [17,36-41]. Sargent and co-workers confirmed that the phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) with the nature of Lewis acid exhibited excellent passivation by forming coordination bond with halide ions [36]. 2-Mercaptopyridine (2-MP), as an anchoring strength, could substantially enhance the binding strength and adhesion of the passivator with perovskite and thus improve the photoluminescence lifetime and moisture tolerance [37]. Yang et al. studied the influence of different molecular structures on the passivation effect and new-type D-4-tert-butylphenylalanine (D4TBP) molecule was tailored to neutralize the charged defects [17]. Other passivation molecules such as sulfonic zwitterion [38], n-trioctylphosphine oxide [39] and pyridine derivatives [40] also have been successfully demonstrated. However, there still remains a great challenge to design outstanding passivation molecules which could heal various kinds of defects without complication. It is due to the mechanism of combined effect of multiple functional groups in a single molecule has not been studied systematically, which will serve as a guide for developing new-type and effective passivating molecules in perovskite photovoltaics.

Here, we reported a simple strategy of tailoring multifunctional passivation molecules to heal charged defects and suppress charge recombination at the surface and grain boundaries. After verifying the good passivation effect of benzamide, the halogen functional groups (F and Cl) were further introduced to adjust the property of passivation molecules by taking advantage of their electronegativity. It was found that halogen atoms can anchor the  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  and organic cation, thus substantially inhibiting defect generation and enhancing material stability. The perovskite film with better crystallization and morphology exhibited further improved quality compared to that passivated by benzamide. As a result, the devices with 4-fluorobenzamide obtained an increased PCE from  $ 19.16\% $  (pristine) to  $ 21.35\% $  with  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  approaching 1.14 V. In addition, owing to the excellent hydrophobicity and inhibited ionic migration, the devices displayed outstanding environmental stability exposed to humidity, thermal and continuous UV irradiation, respectively.

# 2. Results and Discussion

The passivating molecules with specific chemical structure and suitable functional groups have been proved to exhibit better passivation effects for different defects in the perovskite materials [17,38]. We first studied the passivation of benzamide (H-BA) molecule with both phenyl and acylamide groups. The delocalized  $ \pi $  bond was formed based on the nitrogen atom, carbonyl group and benzene ring, which is expected to present combined passivation effect. The formamid (FMA) and benzylamine (BYA) were also selected as references. The chemical structures of the three passivation molecules are showed in Fig. 1b.  $ \mathrm{Cs}_{0.025}\mathrm{FA}_{0.825}\mathrm{MA}_{0.15}\mathrm{Pb(I_{0.85}Br_{0.15})_3} $  mixed perovskite was employed as the pristine. The passivation reagents were respectively injected into the perovskite precursor solution at the concentration of  $ 8\mathrm{mM} $ . Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra was employed to evaluate the passivation effects of the three

molecules. As shown in Fig. 1c, the perovskite films with FMA, BYA and H-BA on the glass substrates all displayed increased PL intensity compared with pristine, revealing less quench owing to the effect of passivation molecules. In addition, the blue shift in the PL peaks of passivated films was attributed to the reduction of non-radiative recombination caused by trap states at the grain boundaries and near the surface [14,35]. Notably, the best passivation from H-BA molecule indicated combined effect of acylamide group and benzene ring, which could bring about the further enhanced open-circuit voltage  $ (V_{\mathrm{oc}}) $  and fill factor (FF) [42].

The defects with negative or positive charges in hybrid perovskites not only occurred during the process of crystallization or preparation, but also caused by the migrations of cations and anions. To further improve the passivation effect and inhibit the ion migration, halogen functional groups were introduced into the H-BA molecule. The chemical structure of H-BA, 4-fluorobenzamide (F-BA) and 4-chlorobenzamide (Cl-BA) are showed in Fig. 3a. In the perovskite films with H-BA (Fig. 2), the defects of organic cation vacancy and iodine vacancy can be partly passivated by the acylamide group in the molecule [38]. The trap states caused by the ion migration of  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  and  $ \mathrm{FA}^+ $  would not be effectively inhibited. Regulation of the electronegativity helps to improve the passivation effect. Both the positive and negative defect can be healed by the halogen and amide functional groups. After employing halogen functional groups, owing to the formed ionic bond and hydrogen bond, the  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  and  $ \mathrm{FA}^+ $  could be anchored rather than readily migrated (Fig. 2). The formed ion bonds can be verified by the XPS spectra of Pb 4f for F-BA and Cl-BA films (Fig.

3c), which displayed slight shift to high-energy direction compared to pristine, indicating the different chemical environments. According to the fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of F-BA and pristine perovskites in Fig. S3, the shift of the N-H vibration (3500-3350 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  ) of F-BA could indicate the formation of hydrogen bonds N-H  $  \cdots  $  F [19]. The halogen functional groups also can adjust the electron cloud density of passivation molecule, which is helpful to enhance the passivation effect [19,43,44]. Moreover, the multifunctional passivation molecules may avoid the complication induced by film morphology and device performance caused by the mixing of supernumerary additives in perovskite solution. As a result, the interaction of the halogen and amide groups will work synergistically, leading to reduced trap states and improved stability.

For pristine film in Fig. 3b, a characteristic peak near  $ 11.6^{\circ} $  assigning to  $ \delta $ -phase  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  caused by the incomplete reaction can be found, which will lead to poor photovoltaic performance. The optical properties were further studied by Ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) and PL spectra characterization. Fig. 3d shows the UV-vis absorption spectra of pristine and perovskite films with different passivation molecules, among which there is no noteworthy difference in absorption band edge. It suggests that the passivation molecules have no impact on the major crystal structure of pristine perovskite. It should be noted that the films with passivation molecules displayed enhanced absorption from 500 to  $ 700\mathrm{nm} $  and the F-BA film owned the best optical absorption. This result can be explained by better crystallization and higher film quality. The corresponding PL spectra are presented in Fig. 3e and all the perovskite films were spin-coated on the glass. Although the PL intensity of H-BA was enhanced to about 1.8 times larger compared to pristine, it was still lower than that of F-BA (2.6 times) and Cl-BA (3.1 times), which implied the introduction of halogen functional groups is beneficial to improve the passivation effect. In addition, the blue shifts of 8 and  $ 7\mathrm{nm} $  for F-BA and Cl-BA films further verified the action of defect passivation [17,42].

To understand the better passivation effect of molecules with halogen functional groups, we executed the space-charge limited current (SCLC) measurements and obtained the trap density of different perovskite films. The dark current-voltage  $ (I - V) $  curves of electron-only devices with an architecture of glass/FTO/TiO $ _2 $ /perovskite/PCBM/Au are showed in Fig. 5a-d. According to the formula of

$  N_{t}=2V_{TFL}\varepsilon_{r}\varepsilon_{0}/(qL^{2})  $  , in which the   $  V_{TFL}  $   means the trap-filled limit voltage,   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   is vacuum dielectric constant,   $  \varepsilon_{r}  $   represents the relative dielectric constant of sample, q refers to the elementary charge of the electron and L is the film thickness of perovskite layer, the   $  N_{t}  $   can be calculated [45,46]. Getting from the I-V curves, the   $  V_{TFL}  $   were 0.43, 0.35, 0.21 and 0.26 V for pristine, H-BA, F-BA and Cl-BA, respectively. Finally, the devices based on the F-BA and Cl-BA films displayed the   $  N_{t}  $   of   $  2.79\times10^{15}  $   and   $  3.63\times10^{15}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  , which are observably lower than that of pristine (  $  7.34\times10^{15}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  ) and H-BA (  $  5.36\times10^{15}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  ). The dramatically reduced trap states in the perovskite films with halogenated passivation molecules imply the halogen functional groups is also involved in defect passivation, which will give rise to apparent improvement in   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was employed to evaluate the charge transfer behaviors in the perovskite devices with different passivation molecules. Tested at under  $  V = -0.8 \mathrm{~V}  $  in dark, the Nyquist plots are showed in Fig. 5e and corresponding fitted parameters are presented in Table S3. The charge transport resistance ( $  R_{\mathrm{tr}}  $ , at high frequency) and recombination resistance ( $  R_{\mathrm{rec}}  $ , at low frequency) mean the carrier dynamic behavior at the interfaces and inside the solar cells, respectively [47]. The devices with passivation molecules displayed lower  $  R_{\mathrm{tr}}  $  and larger  $  R_{\mathrm{rec}}  $  compared to pristine device. Especially, the F-BA exhibited about half the size of  $  R_{\mathrm{tr}}  $  and twice the size of  $  R_{\mathrm{rec}}  $  of the pristine, which are more effective than that of H-BA and Cl-BA. The results can be in good agree with that in PL spectra and TA responses.

According to the equation of  $  V_{\mathrm{oc}} = \alpha (k_{\mathrm{B}}T / q)\ln \varphi  $ , we measured the light intensity ( $  \varphi  $ )-dependent  $  V_{\mathrm{oc}}  $  property of different devices to make sure the charge recombination behavior. The  $  k_{\mathrm{B}}  $  means the Boltzmann's constant,  $  T  $  refers to Kelvin temperature and  $  q  $  represents the elementary charge. The ideal slope of  $  k_{\mathrm{B}}T / q  $  indicates the optimal trap-assisted recombination (free-carrier recombination) [48]. Whereas as the slope gets closer and closer to  $  2k_{\mathrm{B}}T / q  $ , the trap assisted recombination (monomolecular recombination)

The hysteresis phenomenon and reproducibility were further investigated. As shown in Fig. 5h and Fig. S7, the F-BA and Cl-BA devices displayed the low hysteresis factor of  $ 2.4\% $  and  $ 2.5\% $ , respectively, which is  $ 8.4\% $  for the pristine. The PCE histograms of pristine, H-BA, F-BA and Cl-BA devices with a Gaussian distribution are showed in Fig. 5i. The F-BA owned the best photovoltaic parameter with narrower distribution and better reproducibility among 30 devices. The above excellent photovoltaic performance can be given the credit to the lower trap density and inhibited ion migration.

The environmental stability of PSCs is an important inspection for further practical application [49-52]. We first tested the humidity stability of devices with different passivated molecules and pristine under  $ 50 \pm 5\% $  RH. The normalized PCE variation curves of non-encapsulated PSCs are showed in Fig. 6a. It can be observed that all the devices kept more than  $ 90\% $  of the initial efficiency after enduring 480-hour (h) aging. Unfortunately, the PCE of pristine without encapsulation declined to  $ 69\% $  after  $ 2160\mathrm{h} $ . At the same condition, the F-BA and Cl-BA still maintained about  $ 90\% $  performance, which is higher than that of H-BA ( $ 78\% $ ). The aged XRD measurements of perovskite films are showed in Fig. 6b. After  $ 2160\mathrm{-h} $  humidity aging, the pristine displays a strong  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  peak at about  $ 12.7^{\circ} $  caused by the degradation of perovskite. The

# 3. Conclusions

In summary, we demonstrated a simple and effective strategy by tailoring multifunctional passivation molecules to achieve high-performance and stable perovskite solar cells. By introducing halogen functional groups into the passivation molecule with acylamide group, both the negative and positive charged defects in hybrid perovskites have been addressed owing to the combined effect of amine, carbonyl and halogen

14

Journal Pre-proofs

groups. Also attributing to the formation of ionic bond and hydrogen bond which could anchor the  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  and  $ \mathrm{FA}^+ $  rather than readily migrated, the trap density is significantly reduced and the perovskite decomposition has been effectively suppressed. Effective passivation would result in reduced voltage loss. The perovskite devices with halogenated passivation molecules show increased open-circuit voltage ( $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $ ) from  $ 1.08\mathrm{V} $  to  $ 1.14\mathrm{V} $ . Finally, the optimal device passivated by 4-fluorobenzamide exhibits the PCE as high as  $ 21.35\% $  with excellent environmental stability. Our work provides a feasible design guideline for efficient passivation molecules in perovskite photovoltaics.
