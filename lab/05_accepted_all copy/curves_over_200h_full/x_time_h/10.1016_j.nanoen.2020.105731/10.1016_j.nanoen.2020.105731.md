# The poly(styrene-co-acrylonitrile) polymer assisted preparation of high-performance inverted perovskite solar cells with efficiency exceeding 22%

Jiabao Yang  $  ^{a,1}  $  , Qi Cao  $  ^{a,1}  $  , Ziwei He  $  ^{a}  $  , Xingyu Pu  $  ^{a}  $  , Tongtong Li  $  ^{a}  $  , Bingyu Gao  $  ^{a}  $  , Xuanhua Li  $  ^{a,*}  $

# ABSTRACT

# ARTICLEINFO

Solution treatment of perovskite films introduces many defects in the grain boundaries (GBs), which affects the power conversion efficiency (PCE) and long-term stability of the perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Designing suitable modification materials is the key to solving these problems. Herein, we synthesize the poly(styrene-coacrylonitrile) polymer (PS-PAN polymer) through a facile polymerization method. The polymer successfully introduces nitrile  $ (\mathrm{C}\equiv \mathrm{N}) $  into the PS chain and exhibits good thermal stability. When the PS-PAN polymer is added to the antisolvent to modify the perovskite film, it can passivate the GBs/surface defects and restrain the carrier recombination of the perovskite film. As a result, we obtain an enhanced PCE in inverted PSCs increasing from  $ 18.18\% $  to  $ 22.02\% $ . More importantly, the nonencapsulated PS-PAN-modified PSCs demonstrate good illumination and thermal stabilities and maintain  $ 90\% $  PCE under full-spectrum illumination at  $ 75^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  in nitrogen glove box for  $ 800\mathrm{h} $ . In addition, the designed device is resistant to moisture, retaining  $ 91\% $  PCE in air ( $ 50 \pm 5\% $  relative humidity) for  $ 4000\mathrm{h} $ .

# 1. Introduction

Metal halide perovskite semiconductors have attracted wide interest due to simple processing approaches   $  [1-3]  $  , strong light absorption   $  [4-7]  $  , and long carrier lifetime   $  [8-11]  $  . The performance and stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been continuously improved through crystal engineering   $  [12-14]  $  , passivation techniques   $  [15-17]  $  , and interface engineering   $  [18-20]  $  , but challenges still remain   $  [21-23]  $  . The photovoltaic performance of inverted PSCs still falls behind the conventional PSCs   $  [24]  $  . The reason may be that there are numerous defects in the perovskite absorption layer and interface, which leads to the non-radiative recombination of photo-generated carriers.

So far, additive was proved to be a facile path to alleviate the grain boundaries (GBs)/surface problems and prepare high-quality perovskite films. Additives such as salts   $  [25–28]  $  , small molecules   $  [17]  $  , and polymers   $  [29–33]  $   were introduced into perovskite to passivate defects. The small molecules such as pyridine   $  [34]  $  , thiophene   $  [34]  $  , and fullerenes   $  [35]  $   could coordinate with   $  Pb^{2+}  $   by Lewis base-acid reaction, thus passivating defects and improving photoelectric performance of

perovskite. However, the high diffusion coefficients and volatility of small molecules limited further applications under harsh environments. Introducing polymers such as polystyrene (PS) [36–38], poly(4-vinylpyridine) (PVP) [39] and poly (methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) [40] into PSCs were attempted to overcome these shortcomings. For the PS polymer, it was considered to be a promising material and was widely used to solve the surface and interface problems of perovskite films. Kim et al. reported that adding the PS polymer to the perovskite precursor resulted in a uniform perovskite film and a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 13% [37]. Rohit et al. reported that the PS polymer could improve the crystallization kinetics of perovskite, and a PCE of 16.8% has been achieved [38]. Unfortunately, the PS polymer modified PSCs get low efficiency, which is probably caused by the lack of passivation functional groups in the PS polymer.

Here, we successfully synthesize the poly(styrene-co-acrylonitrile) polymer (PS-PAN polymer) through a facile polymerization reaction and introduce the nitrile (C≡N) into the side chain of the PS polymer (Fig. 1a). Then we dissolve it in anti-solvent (chlorobenzene) to modify the perovskite film via passivating uncoordinated defects at GBs/

# 2. Experimental section

# 2.1.Materials

Chlorobenzene (CB, 99.8%), isopropanol (IPA, 99.9%), anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.8%), nickel (II) nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO  $  _{3}  $  )  $  _2  $  ·6 H  $  _{2}  $  O, 99.999%) and N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. NiO  $  _{x}  $  nanoparticles were synthesized based on previous reports [41]. All photoelectric materials were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. (China).

# 2.2. Synthesis of the PS-PAN polymer

Styrene (3.48 g), acrylonitrile (0.88 g), α-bromoisobutyric acid tert-butyl ester (t-BBiB, 0.14 g), 2,2'-Bipyridine (Bpy, 0.40 g) and Copper (I) bromide (Cu $  ^{I}  $ Br, 0.12 g) were placed in a three-necked flask containing 10 mL of toluene. The flask was sealed with rubber septa and then backfilled with nitrogen (99.9%) more than three times to remove oxygen. The reaction mixture was heated (70 °C) with stirring, collected regularly (1 mL) and diluted in tetrahydrofuran (2 mL). Finally, the reaction can be stopped by exposure to air. The weight-average molecular weight (Mw) of the PS-PAN polymer is about 67345 obtained by the gel permeation chromatography (GPC) (Fig. S1).

# 2.3. Preparation of perovskite solutions

Precursor solution was made by mixing lead bromide (PbBr $ _2 $ , 0.21 M), lead iodide (PbI $ _2 $ , 1.30 M), methylammonium bromide (MABr, 0.21 M), formamidine iodide (FAI, 1.19 M), and 70  $ \mu $ L cesium iodide (CsI, 1 M in DMSO) in DMSO/DMF (1/4 V/V), then Cs $ _{0.05} $ (MA $ _{0.15} $ -FA $ _{0.85} $ ) $ _{0.95} $ Pb(Br $ _{0.15} $ I $ _{0.85} $ ) $ _3 $  triple-cation perovskite precursor solution was obtained.

# 2.4. Device fabrication

ITO glasses were rinsed by deionized water, acetone and IPA, respectively. A 20 nm NiO  $  _{x}  $  was spin-coated onto ITO glasses treated with O  $  _{2}  $  plasma before using. Then the perovskite was spin-coated onto NiO  $  _{x}  $  film, and an anti-solvent with different concentrations of the PS-PAN polymer (0, 0.03, 0.05, 0.1 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $ , respectively) was added after 30 s spinning time. Thereafter, these samples were post-baked at 100 °C for 20 min. PC  $  _{61}  $  BM + C  $  _{60}  $  solution (25 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $ , 4/1, w/w) and BCP solution (0.5 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $ ) were spin-coated at 3000 rpm for 40 s and 5800 rpm for 60 s. The latter layers were post-baked at 60 °C for 10 min. Lastly, Cr (4 nm) and Au (100 nm) were thermally evaporated (2 × 10  $  ^{-6}  $  mbar, 0.1 cm  $  ^{2}  $  effective area).

We further understand the surface chemical state of the perovskite film and confirm the potential interaction between the PS-PAN polymer and perovskite film through X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Fig. 1e, f, and S4). Fig. 1e depicts N 1 s XPS spectra, and the appearance of C≡N peak around 400.0 eV indicates that the PS-PAN polymer is successfully introduced on the perovskite surface [42]. Meanwhile, the N 1 s peak of C≡N is observed at 399.8 eV in the PS-PAN polymer, which means that electron density decreases near N in C≡N. From the Pb 4 f XPS spectra (Fig. 1f), two main peaks are observed at 143.6 (Pb 4 f $  _{5/2}  $ ) and 138.7 eV (Pb 4 f $  _{7/2}  $ ), and they shift towards lower binding energy (143.4 and 138.5 eV) after the PS-PAN polymer modification, which means that electron density increases near Pb. It clearly indicates an interaction between Pb and the N in C≡N via sharing electronic [43]. In addition, the appearance of two additional peaks at 141.4 and 136.9 eV is metallic Pb in the control perovskite film. The metallic Pb may originate from the existence of anion and cation vacancies, which are recognized as main non-radiative centres [44]. In contrast, there is no signal from metallic Pb in the PS-PAN polymer-modified perovskite, indicating that the C≡N in PS-PAN polymer can effectively passivate the uncoordinated lead atoms [45].

# 4. Conclusion

In conclusion, we synthesize the PS-PAN polymer with the passivation group  $ (\mathrm{C}\equiv \mathrm{N}) $  by a facile polymerization method for highly efficient inverted PSCs. The introduced N atoms can provide lone pair electrons to coordinate with the  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2 + } $ , thereby improving perovskite crystallization, facilitating carrier transport, passivating the GBs/surface defects and suppressing non-radiative recombination of perovskite film. Therefore, the PSC with the PS-PAN polymer achieves the champion PCE
