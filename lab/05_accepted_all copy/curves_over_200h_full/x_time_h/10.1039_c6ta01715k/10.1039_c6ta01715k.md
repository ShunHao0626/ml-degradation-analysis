# Cross-stacked Superaligned Carbon Nanotube Electrode for Efficient Hole Conductor-free Perovskite Solar Cells

Qiang Luo,  $  ^{a,\dagger}  $   He Ma,  $  ^{b,\dagger}  $   Ye Zhang,  $  ^{a}  $   Xuewen Yin,  $  ^{a}  $   ZhiBo Yao,  $  ^{a}  $   Ning Wang,  $  ^{c}  $   Jianbao Li,  $  ^{a,d}  $   Shoushan Fan,  $  ^{b}  $   Kaili Jiang,  $  ^{b,*}  $   Hong Lin,  $  ^{a,*}  $

Cross-stacked superaligned carbon nanotubes (CSCNTs) sheets drawn from CNT arrays with excellent conductivity, porosity and flexibility are used as low-cost back contact for hole conductor-free mesoscopic  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 / \mathrm{TiO}_2 $  heterojunction perovskite solar cells (PSCs). PSC integrated with CSCNTs achieves a power conversion efficiency of up to  $ 8.65\% $  and over  $ 10.54\% $  by further doping the CSCNTs with iodine. Moreover, PSC encapsulated with a layer of polymer poly(methylmethacrylate) exhibits outstanding stability in dark and under illumination. This work represents a significant step toward the commercialization of the low-cost, stable and high-efficiency hole conductor-free PSCs

# Introduction

Since the seminal work by Kojima et al. in 2009,  $  ^{1}  $  organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have gained considerable research momentum during the past years,  $  ^{2-6}  $  primarily due to their high power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) and energy-saving device processing techniques. Continued development of the compositional engineering of perovskite absorbers, optimization of device interfaces and improvement of fabricated technologies have steady increased the certified PCEs of PSCs over 20%.  $  ^{7-10}  $  Generally, high efficiency PSCs prevalently employ an expensive organic semiconductor, such as 2, 2', 7, 7'-tetrakis(N, N-di-p-methoxyphenyl-amine)-9, 9'-spiro-bifluorene (spiro-OMeTAD), to act as hole transport material (HTM). The intrinsically low hole mobility and conductivity of spiro-OMeTAD generally need to be p-doped with a redox-active lithium-bis(trifluoromethane)-sulfonimide to achieve appreciable conductivity.  $  ^{11,12}  $  However, such doping commonly leads to the wide variance in term of photovoltaic performance, reproducibility and device stability especially.  $  ^{13,14}  $  Some inorganic materials like CuSCN, CuI, and NiO have been demonstrated with success to work

as HTMs.  $  ^{15-20}  $   On the other hand, the perovskite absorbers themselves, except for acting as light absorber, also could assume the role of HTM.  $  ^{21-23}  $   Etgar et al. first reported that   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   in mesoscopic   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}/TiO_{2}  $   heterojunction solar cell could simultaneously function as both light harvester and hole conductor, obviating the need for employing an additional HTM.  $  ^{21}  $   However, the most often used back contacts in PSCs have still been achieved with a vapor deposited precious metals (usually gold or silver), whose high cost and limited availability are very likely to hamper the industrial deployment of perovskite photovoltaic technology. Therefore, it is highly desirable to develop alternative back contacts that are earth-abundant and energy non-intensive for HTM-free and high efficiency PSCs.

Efforts have been devoted to develop low cost back contacts capable of replacing the precious metals in HTM-free PSCs.  $  ^{24-28}  $   Han et al. designed a promising HTM-free mesoscopic   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}/TiO_{2}  $   heterojunctions that use a double layer of   $  TiO_{2}  $   and   $  ZrO_{2}  $   as scaffold, and porous carbon/graphite composite electrode as a back contact, in which the perovskite solution was casted into the   $  TiO_{2}/ZrO_{2}  $   scaffold from carbon/graphite electrode side by drop-infiltration deposition.  $  ^{26}  $   Currently, PCEs exceeded 11% have been achieved by judicious modification of the perovskite absorbers,   $  TiO_{2}  $   scaffolds and carbon contacts.  $  ^{27,29-33}  $   Notably, the performance of this kind of PSCs should be significantly affected by at least three key factors associated with the carbon contact: conductivity, porosity and uniformity.  $  ^{31,32}  $   To obtain porous electrode for facilitating the perovskite solution infiltration from carbon contact side, however, the carbon/graphite composite electrode requires to be sintered at high temperature (450~500 °C) with the aid of polymeric binders, for example, ethylcellulose, which is energy-consuming and also renders them incompatible with flexible plastic substrates.

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) have been recently studied as conductive back contact or HTM for PSCs due to their excellent conductivity and chemical stability.  $  ^{24,25,33-35}  $   For example, Wei et al. recently used a multi-walled carbon nanotube (MWCNT) as

Moreover, we showed that the PSCs encapsulated with poly(methylmethacrylate) (PMMA) could efficiently prevent the device performance degradation when exposed to highly humid atmosphere and light soaking. This work demonstrated that the highly conductive, porous and uniform CSCNTs is an excellent back contact for HTM-free mesoscopic PSCs and the facile and convenient integration of CSCNTs also can provide a new opportunity for fabricating flexible PSCs by roll-to-roll manufacturing.

# Experimental

# Fabrication of CSCNTs.

The superaligned CNT arrays comprising CNTs with a length of 300   $  \mu  $  m were synthesized in batches on 6 inch silicon wafers with iron as the catalyst and acetylene as the precursor in a low-pressure chemical vapor deposition (LP-CVD) system.  $  ^{36-38}  $   Briefly, the 6 inch silicon wafer coated with thin Fe films (3.5–5.5 nm thick) was first was placed horizontally in the middle of the tube furnaces and then was heated up in the flowing argon gas to the growth temperature of 680~720 °C for 15 min. Then hydrogen and acetylene were added to the argon gas to start the growth of superaligned CNT arrays. Uniform superaligned arrays with a length of 300   $  \mu  $  m on silicon wafer can be achieved under the conditions of 500 sccm acetylene plus 50 sccm hydrogen. The growth of superaligned CNT arrays on silicon wafers has been successfully scaled up with wafer diameter up to 8 inch to meet the industrial requirements. The cross-stacked superaligned CNT films were directly drawn out from the arrays and stacked onto stainless steel holders layer by layer. A CSCNTs sheet is fabricated by sequential stacking the superaligned CNTs on a metallic frame along one direction, followed by stacking in its perpendicular direction. After that, the CSCNT sheets were

shrunk and tightened by dipping them into ethanol for 1 min. I-CSCNTs were prepared by placing the CSCNTs sheets loaded on the holders in the iodine vapor at  $ 200^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for 8h. For comparison, the T-CSCNTs were also obtained by placing the CSCNTs sheets in a nitrogen atmosphere at  $ 200^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for 8h.

# Material synthesis and preparation

Methylammonium iodide (CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ I) was synthesized using a previously described method. $ ^4 $  In brief, CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ I was prepared by neutralizing equimolar amounts of HI and aqueous CH $ _3 $ NH $ _2 $  solution. The product was then crystallized by removing the solvent in hot plate at 100  $ ^{\circ} $ C. The produced CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ I was washed with diethyl ether three times and dried in vacuum at 60  $ ^{\circ} $ C overnight for 24 h. To prepared the perovskite precursor, CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ I was firstly dissolved into  $ \gamma $ -butyrolactone (GBL) and stirred for 2 h. PbI $ _2 $  was subsequently added to the CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ I/GBL solution (PbI $ _2 $ :CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ I, 1 : 1 molar ratio). The total concentration of perovskite precursor was 22 wt%. After that, the mixed precursor solution was heated and stirred on a hotplate at 70  $ ^{\circ} $ C overnight to encourage dissolution of solid material. The precursor solution was filtered through PTFE filters (0.45  $ \mu $ m) in a nitrogen-filled glove box before use.

# Device Fabrication

Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) conducting glass (sheet resistance 7  $ \Omega $  sq $ ^{-1} $ ) was ultrasonically cleaned with detergent, deionized water, acetone and isopropanol, separately. Ultraviolet/O $ _3 $  was subsequently used to treat the cleaned FTO glass for 15 min. A compact layer of TiO $ _2 $  was deposited by spin-coating an acidic solution of titanium isopropoxide in ethanol onto the cleaned FTO substrate at a speed of 2000 rpm for 60 s. The compact layers were drying at 150  $ ^{\circ} $ C for 30 min and then annealed in air at 500  $ ^{\circ} $ C for 30 min. A mesoporous layer was deposited on compacting layer by spin-coating a colloidal dispersion of TiO $ _2 $  paste (20 nm, PST-18NR, JGC Catalysts and Chemicals Ltd., Japan, diluted with ethanol at a weight of 1/1.8) at 3500 rpm for 45s, and then drying at 125 $  ^{\circ} $ C for 10 min and further annealing in air at 500  $ ^{\circ} $ C for 30 min. After the mesoporous film was cooled down to room temperature, a layer of Al $ _2 $ O $ _3 $  was deposited by a colloidal dispersion of Al $ _2 $ O $ _3 $  nanoparticles (<50 nm), followed by drying at 200  $ ^{\circ} $ C for 45 min. After the films were cooled to room temperature naturally, the CSCNTs sheets were transferred onto Al $ _2 $ O $ _3 $  scaffold to form CSCNT cathodes with the aid of a small droplet of chlorobenzene. After fabricating the CSCNTs electrodes, samples were transferred into a nitrogen-filled glovebox. 20  $ \mu $ L of perovskite precursor was then dropped on the top of the CSCNTs layer and standing for 15 min. Then 100  $ \mu $ L of perovskite precursor was dropped on the top of the CSCNTs and then spin-coated at 7000 rpm for 45s, followed by drying at 70  $ ^{\circ} $ C on a hot plate for 45 min and 100 $  ^{\circ} $ C for 45 min, respectively. After the device was cooled to room temperature, a solution of PMMA (110 mg mL $ ^{-1} $  in toluene) was spin-coated on the devices at 4000 rpm for 60 s.

For the fabrication of standard PSCs with spiro-OMeTAD HTM and evaporated gold electrode,, a mesoporous layer was deposited on compacting layer by spin-coating a colloidal dispersion of   $  TiO_{2}  $   paste (20 nm, PST-18NR, diluted with ethanol at a weight of 1/4) at 5000 rpm for 45s, and annealing in air at 500 °C for 30 min. # Material and device Characterizations

The morphology of the samples was characterized by field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM: LEO 1530, Gemini, Zeiss, Germany). The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra were performed on X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher SCIENTIFIC INC., USA). Raman spectroscopy was recorded on a laser micro-Raman spectroscopy (Hr800, Horiba Jobin Yvon, France) with a laser beam wavelength of  $ 633~\mathrm{nm} $ . The sheet resistance of the CSCNTs sheets was measured by a digital four-point probe system (SX1934, Suzhou, China) at room temperature. The Hall measurement (HL5500PC, NANOMETRICS, USA) was performed at room temperature to obtain the semiconductor type and carrier mobility of the CSCNTs sheets using the van der Pauw method. The  $ J - V $  characteristics of the PSCs were measured using a solar simulator (91192, Oriel, USA, calibrated with a standard crystalline silicon solar cell) with a digital source meter (2400, Keithley Instruments, USA) at  $ 100\mathrm{mWcm}^{-2} $  AM 1.5G illumination. The  $ J - V $  curves for all devices were measured by masking the active area with a metal mask  $ (0.12\mathrm{cm}^2) $ . The IPCE was characterized by using the QEX10 solar cell quantum efficiency measurement system (QEX10, PV measurements, USA). Prior to measurement, a standard silicon solar cell was used as reference. The impedance measurements were carried out with a Zahner Ennium electrochemical station (Zahner, Zahner-Electrik GmbH & Co. KG, Germany) in the frequency range of  $ 2\mathrm{MHz} $  to  $ 1\mathrm{Hz} $  with a amplitude of  $ 5\mathrm{mV} $ . Impedance spectra were recorded in the dark under varying bias voltage. The temperatures and humidity of the stability measurements were controlled by a temperature humidity chamber (RP-80A, Beijing Hongzhan Instrument Co., Ltd.).

# Results and discussion

A CSCNTs sheet is fabricated by stacking the superaligned CNT layers crossly in sequence on a metallic frame, and then dipping them in ethanol for densifying.  $  ^{38}  $   By changing the cross-stacking times, different thickness of CSCNTs sheets can be obtained. To obtain I-CSCNTs, CSCNTs loaded on the metallic frame were treated in the iodine vapor at 200 °C. The reason for selecting iodine as the dopant atoms is that iodine

has been considered as an efficient and stable dopant to enhance the conductivity of CNTs,  $  ^{42-45}  $  which could be beneficial for the further PCE improvement of PSCs.

Figure 1a and 1b show the representative SEM images of the as-fabricated CSCNTs and I-CSCNTs, which revealed the typical cross-stacking structure. CNTs in CSCNTs are generally parallel to each other within the same layer, and perpendicular to each other between neighboring layers.  $  ^{38}  $   The individual CNT in CSCNTs has fairly uniform diameter, while most of them have formed bundles when drawn out from the superaligned arrays. The formation of bundles of CNTs in the same layer undoubtedly favors the conductivity of CSCNTs. The sequential stacking of superaligned CNTs leads to a 3D porous network with interconnected channels. After being treated in iodine vapor at 200 °C, the I-CSCNTs shows similar morphology with CSCNT, as shown in Figure 1d. However, it is obviously observed that after doping the CNTs in I-CSCNTs are packed more densely than those in pristine CSCNTs, and similar phenomenon also was observed for the thermal treated (without iodine doping) CSCNTs (T-CSCNTs) (Figure S1). Those results indicate that the doping or thermal treatment process could draw cross-stacked CNTs close, which enhances interaction between CNTs and thereby could improve the conductivity of CSCNTs to some extent. Raman spectroscopy was employed to differentiate the crystalline structure of those CSCNTs. Both CSCNTs and I-CSCNTs exhibited a disorder-induced D-band (  $  \sim  $  1350 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  ), a doubly degenerated G-band (  $  \sim  $  1580 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  ) and a strong single-sharp 2D band (  $  \sim  $  2700 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  ) (Figure S2). Notably, after doping with iodine a peak appeared at   $  \sim  $  155 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   for I-CSCNTs, which can be assigned to the polyiodide (  $  I_{3}^{-}  $  ,   $  I_{5}^{-}  $  etc) doped in I-CSCNTs.  $  ^{42,46}  $   The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) of I-CSCNTs shows two I 3d peaks (619.7 eV, 631.5 eV), further confirming the presence of iodine in I-CSCNTs (Figure S3).  $  ^{42,47}  $   The iodine content in I-CSCNT was ca. 0.74 at%. The successful doping of iodine was expected to tune the properties of I-CSCNT with improved conductivity. Therefore, with the alternative cross-stacked mode, the CSCNT and I-CSCNT possess the merit of robust structural stability, abundant porosity, as well as 3D charge freeway, thereby rendering them as prospective back contacts for mesoscopic HTM-free PSCs.

We have firstly evaluated the influence of layer numbers of CNTs on the photovoltaic performance of PSCs. For simplicity, the CSCNTs sheets with 25, 50 and 75-layer CNT were abbreviated as CSCNT-25, CSCNT-50, and CSCNT-75, respectively. Figure 3 shows the J-V characteristics of the PSCs with different CSCNTs under simulated AM1.5, 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $   solar irradiation and a summary of the key device parameters is tabulated in Table 1. All PSCs in this study were tested at a scan rate of 0.0215 V s  $  ^{-1}  $  . PSC with CSCNT-25 presents a promising photovoltaic performance with an open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ) of 878 mV, a short-circuit photocurrent density (  $  J_{sc}  $  ) of 14.43 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , a fill factor (FF) of 0.53, yielding an overall PCE of 6.81%. Interestingly, the PCE was improved with increased CNT layers, that is, both CSCNT-50 and CSCNT-75-based devices exhibit better photovoltaic performance than that of the PSCs with CSCNT-

25, due to their higher conductivity (Table 1). The device with CSCNT-50 delivers a   $  V_{oc}  $  ,   $  J_{sc}  $   and FF of 14.91 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , 849 mV and 0.67, respectively, leading to a PCE of 8.65%. Yet it is difficult to further improve the photovoltaic performance by increasing the thickness of CNT to 75 layers although the conductivity of CSCNT-75 is higher than that of CSCNT-50. This may be attributed to those CSCNTs far away from the FTO side, which made a long distance travelling for the holes produced in CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}  $  PbI  $  _{3}  $  , contribute less to the hole extracting and collecting. Compared with that of the pristine CSCNT-50, the PCE of T-CSCNT-50-based PSCs was slightly improved from 8.60% to 9.37%. Impressively, when integrating I-CSCNT-50 into PSCs, the PCE of I-CSCNT-50-based PSC could further be improved to 10.54%, a value is comparable to the carbon-based HTM-free PSCs based on random network-type single-walled carbon nanotube back contact (11%).  $  ^{24}  $   However, the PCEs of our devices seem to be lower than the state-of-the-art carbon-based HTM-free PSCs due to the relatively low   $  J_{sc}  $   or poor FF,  $  ^{27,35}  $   which could be attributed to the difficulty to fill enough perovskite into the   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer or not ideal carriers transporting property.  $  ^{51}  $   In addition, we also fabricated standard PSCs with the spin-coated spiro-OMeTAD HTM and evaporated gold back contact and I-CSCNT-based PSCs with and without spiro-OMeTAD HTM. With regards to their commercial viability, one big challenge for PSCs is the aspect of long-term stability.  $  ^{60}  $   The water-solubility of the organic constituent of the perovskite makes PSCs highly prone to rapid degradation in moist environments. The poor stability is unacceptable for practical applications. Encapsulating the PSCs with water-resistant PMMA polymer offer enhanced protection from moisture and temperatures. We investigated the ambient stability of PSCs encapsulated with PMMA polymer, as a function of storage time in dark under ambient conditions (constant relative humidity 75%, constant temperature 45 °C). As shown in Figure 7a, the PCE of the I-CSCNT-50-based device remained above 93% of the initial value even after 2500 h of storage in air. In contrast, the PCE of the

device fabricated without PMMA encapsulation decreased to 58% of its initial value. It was found that the PCE of a PMMA protected PSCs remained above 87% of the initial value after light soaking for about 400 h. While the PCE of the unencapsulated device decreased to 46% of its initial value within 400 h under light soaking. The encapsulated device exhibited good stability under light soaking, which is in good agreement with the literatures.  $  ^{[27,61]}  $   The above results evidently suggest that it is possible to fabricate humidity-resistant and stable PSCs using PMMA encapsulation.

# Conclusions

The conclusions section should come in this section at the end of the article, before the acknowledgements. In conclusion, we have successfully integrated, highly conductive and porous CSCNTs sheet as metal-free back contact at low temperature in HTM-free mesoscopic PSCs. The device fabricated with CSCNT-50 present a promising PCE of 8.65% due to the 3D hole extraction and collection of highly conductive CSCNTs. By doping the CSCNTs with iodine, the PCE of the I-CSCNT-50 based PSC can be increased to 10.54% due to the significant improvement in FF and   $  J_{sc}  $   resulting from the increased grain size and improved crystallinity of perovskite in perovskite/I-CSCNT layer as well as the enhanced conductivity of I-CSCNT back contact. Additionally, PSCs encapsulated with PMMA show outstanding stability in dark and prolonged light soaking. Our results may provide references for the design and fabrication of effective back contact for stable and high efficiency carbon-based PSCs.
