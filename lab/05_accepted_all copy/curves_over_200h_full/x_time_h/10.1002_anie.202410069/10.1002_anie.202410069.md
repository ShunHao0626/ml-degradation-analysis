# Enhancing Efficiency and Durability of Perovskite Solar Cells With Chlorine-Functionalized Fully Conjugated Porous Aromatic Framework

Yege Jing  $  ^{+}  $  , Chen Wang  $  ^{+}  $  , Bo Zhu  $  ^{+}  $  , Songwen Xiao, Wei Guan, Shuainan Liu, Ning Zhang,  $  ^{*}  $   Shanpeng Wen,  $  ^{*}  $   and Guangshan Zhu  $  ^{*}  $

# Introduction

Abstract: Non-radiative recombination, caused by trap states, significantly hampers the efficiency and stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). The emerging porous organic polymers (POPs) show promise as a platform for designing novel defect passivation agents due to their rigid and porous structure. However, the POPs reported so far lack either sufficient stability or clear sites of interactions with the defects. Herein, two chlorine-functionalized, fully conjugated porous aromatic frameworks (PAFs) were constructed via a decarbonylation reaction. The chlorinated PAFs feature unique long-range conjugated networks bearing multiple chlorine atoms, significantly improving the photovoltaic performance and stability of doped solar cells. Combined experimental and theoretical analyses confirmed the strong passivation effects of conjugated structure to the defect through Cl sites. Specifically, PAF-159, bearing a triphenylamine moiety, demonstrated stronger Cl–Pb bonding and higher passivation efficiency due to the presence of   $  \pi^{*}  $   anti-bonding orbitals, which elevate the HOMO energy level and facilitate Cl–Pb charge transfer. Consequently, we obtained high-performance PAF-159-doped devices with advanced PCE (24.3%), good storage stability (retaining 86% after 3000 hours), and good long-term operational stability (retaining 92% after 350 hours).

Organic-inorganic halide perovskites have emerged as promising photovoltaic materials for cost-effective, high-performance solar cells due to their excellent photoelectric properties.  $  ^{[1,2]}  $   Over the past decade, the power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have risen from an initial 3.8% to a certified 26.1%, which is comparable to crystalline silicon solar cells.  $  ^{[3-11]}  $   Despite the unparalleled growth in photovoltaic performance, there is still a gap between the record and the theoretical Shockley-Queisser efficiency limit, with device stability remains unsatisfactory. One major limiting factor identified is the defects-induced non-radiative recombination loss.  $  ^{[12,13]}  $   To solve this problem, strategies such as precursor solution optimization and perovskite dimensionality engineering have emerged.  $  ^{[14,15]}  $   Among them, a simple yet efficient strategy method is additive engineering, which plays a key role in improving the efficiency and stability of PSCs.  $  ^{[16,17]}  $   Unlike conventional additives such as organic/inorganic ionic salts and Lewis acids and bases, porous organic polymers (POPs) have attracted interest in the perovskite photovoltaic community due to their unique porous structure in the solid state.  $  ^{[18]}  $   At the same time, POPs are more stable due to covalent bonding compared to other framework materials (e.g. MOFs).  $  ^{[19]}  $   In particular, porous aromatic frameworks (PAFs) synthesized by irreversible coupling reactions, which have ultra-stable rigid pores and high chemical resistance, show great potential for enhancing device stability.  $  ^{[20]}  $   To be an effective additive for PSCs, extended π-conjugation throughout their backbone is required in PAFs. Compared to other soft additives, fully conjugated PAFs form their topological network through robust covalent bonds, effectively defining the charge transport channel. Additionally, their high porosity and large specific surface area allow PAFs to make closer contact with perovskite components.  $  ^{[21]}  $   As a result, fully conjugated PAFs are expected to enhance both device performance and durability by promoting rapid charge separation, providing trace doping passivation, and inhibiting halogen segregation.

In conjugated PAFs reported so far,   $  \pi  $  -electrons are relatively evenly distributed throughout the backbone. Consequently, the interaction between fully conjugated PAFs and perovskite occurs mainly through   $  \pi  $  -electrons, which may lead to insufficiently strong interactions and unclear active sites. # Results and Discussion

In this study, we aim to ensure the PAFs backbone is fully conjugated while uniformly introducing halogen sites with stronger electronegativity and passivation effects. Miura and co-workers have presented a pioneering rhodium-catalyzed addition of aroyl chlorides to terminal alkynes by decarbonylation to obtain the corresponding vinyl chloride derivatives stereoselectively in good yields.  $  ^{[28]}  $   This reaction has also been used to produce conjugated linear poly(arylene chloro Z-vinylene)s (PACVs) with high molecular weights.  $  ^{[29]}  $   Chlorine atoms can be used as defective binding sites due to their stronger bonding with Pb compared to iodine and bromine. Therefore, this reaction is expected to be a simple and effective method to introduce passivation sites into the fully conjugated backbone of PAFs. Initially, we selected two conjugated monomers, i.e. 1,3,5-tris(4-ethynylphenyl) benzene (TEB) and tris-(4-ethynyl-phenyl)-amine (TEPA), for the decarbonylation reaction with 4,4'-biphenyldicarbonyl chloride (BPDC), respectively. Successively, PAF-158 and PAF-159 were prepared in the presence of a catalytic amount of   $  [\mathrm{RhCl}(\mathrm{cod})]_{2}  $   and   $  PPh_{3}  $   (Scheme 1). PAF-158 was obtained as an orange-red powder in 89% yield, and PAF-159 was acquired as a deep green powder in 92% yield.

The micromorphology of PAFs was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). As shown in Figure S6 and S7, both PAF-158 and PAF-159 exhibit tiny irregular nanoparticle stacking morphology. Meanwhile, high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images reveal worm-like porous textures in PAFs without clear lattice fringe, indicating that the PAFs have no crystallinity. In addition, typical amorphous features of these frameworks are also confirmed by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) analysis (Figure S8).

The nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms were used to assess the permanent porosities of these PAFs at 77.3 K. As shown in Figure 1d, both PAF-158 and PAF-159 exhibit mixed sorption isotherms of type I. All PAFs exhibit steep uptakes at low relative pressures (  $  P/P_{0} < 0.1  $  ), which indicates the existence of micropores in their frameworks. According to the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) model, PAF-158 and PAF-159 present high specific surface areas of   $  589 m^{2} g^{-1}  $   and   $  814 m^{2} g^{-1}  $  , respectively. Both PAF-158 and PAF-159 show wide pore size distributions based on the calculation determined by nonlocal density functional theory (NL-DFT). As shown in Figure S9, the pore sizes of PAF-154 and PAF-159 are 1.4 nm and 1.2 nm, respectively. This variation can be attributed to PAF-159's more compact geometry relative to PAF-158. The observed diversity in pore sizes likely results from the aggregation of nanoparticles and the interconnection of pore channels within the frameworks.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed to investigate the thermal stability of PAFs. Figure 1e shows that the decomposition of PAF-158 and PAF-159 starts at  $ 350^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  under an air environment, and the complete degradation temperature is about  $ 500^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . This indicates that both materials have remarkable thermal stabilities, with no traces of metal catalyst residue existed in the material powder. The chemical stability of the PAFs was also rigorously tested. Both PAF-158 and PAF-159 were immersed in a variety of solvents and conditions, including dichloromethane  $ (\mathrm{CH}_2\mathrm{Cl}_2) $ , N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF), boiling water  $ (\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}) $ , HCl (6 M) solution, and NaOH (6 M) solution for three days. As shown in the FT-IR spectra (Figure S10), the structures of PAF-158 and PAF-159 were unchanged, demonstrating high chemical resistance to acidic, alkaline,

and humid environments. This combination of high thermal and chemical stability is appealing for their application in perovskite solar cells.

The ultraviolet-visible (UV/Vis) absorption spectra of both PAF-158 and PAF-159 revealed a broad light absorption range (Figure 1f), indicative of a high conjugation degree in these PAFs. To further investigate the electrochemical properties of PAFs, we analyzed their energy band structures. The energy band gap ( $ E_g $ ) values of PAF-158 and PAF-159 were estimated to be 2.28 eV and 1.46 eV based on the plots of  $ E_g $  for the semiconductor (Figure S11a). To ensure the accuracy of the results, we used the Mott–Schottky and VB-XPS methods to estimate the energy band structure of the PAFs, and the calculation procedure is detailed in the Supporting Information. The results from both methods were consistent, confirming that both PAF-158 and PAF-159 are n-type semiconductors with relatively deep energy levels (Figure S11). $ ^{[30,31]} $  All these results indicate that Cl-functionalization did not disrupt the fully conjugated structure of the PAFs backbone. These also provide an exciting basis for exploration of the application of two PAFs in photovoltaic devices.

To corroborate the potential interaction between the chlorine-containing PAFs and the   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ions in perovskites, we employed Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) analysis (Figure 2a and b).

Considering that organic components in the perovskite affect the peak positions in the FT-IR spectra, we used pure   $  PbI_{2}  $   instead of perovskite to avoid complexity. The spectra of PAFs doped in   $  PbI_{2}  $   films, reveal a discernible shift in the C–Cl vibrational modes (764, 757 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  ) to lower wave-numbers compared to the undoped PAF powders. This shift is indicative of Cl–Pb bond formation, leading to the delocalization of the electronic cloud around the C–Cl bond, and consequently a weakening of the C–Cl bond.  $  ^{[32,33]}  $

To delve deeper into the interaction between the PAFs and the perovskite structure, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) characterization was conducted. The XPS spectra of perovskite films doped with PAF-158 or PAF-159 exhibited chlorine signals, unlike the spectra from untreated perovskite films (as illustrated in Figure S13). This confirms the successful incorporation of PAFs into the perovskite matrix. After doping the perovskite films with PAFs, we observed a distinct shift in the Pb 4f peaks towards lower binding energies in the Pb 4f XPS spectra (Figure 2c). This shift is indicative of the interaction between the uncoordinated lead ions (Pb $  ^{2+}  $ ) in the perovskite and the chlorine atoms present in the PAFs. Such interaction may increase the electron density around the lead, resulting in the observed shift of the Pb peaks. Correspondingly, the Cl 2p peaks for perovskite films doped with either PAF-158 or PAF-159 show a shift towards higher binding energies (Figures 2d and 2e).[34]

In addition, the I   $  3d_{3/2}  $   and I   $  3d_{5/2}  $   peaks of the doped PAFs samples were also shifted towards the lower binding energy compared to the control (Figure 2f). This can be ascribed to that the Pb–I bond was weakened due to the competing interaction with the chlorine atoms in the PAFs.  $  ^{[35]}  $   These findings further confirm that chlorine atoms

The observed strong interaction resulting from Cl–Pb coordination prompted us to explore in depth the effect of PAFs doping on the quality and defect passivation of perovskite films. Initially, we compared pristine perovskite with the PAFs-doped perovskite films by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) images (Figure S14). We observed that PAFs-doped perovskite films exhibited a slightly larger grain size than pristine film, which is consistent with the Nanoanalysis calculations (Figure S15). Meanwhile, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis showed that the PAFs-doped perovskite films exhibited stronger diffraction peaks (Figure S16). These results suggest that PAFs may affect the crystal nucleation and crystallization kinetics, which may have a positive impact on the properties of perovskite films.  $  ^{[36]}  $

We further carried out the steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved PL (TRPL) measurements. Figure 3b shows the PL spectra for the pristine perovskite film and the PAFs doped perovskite films. It is noted that doping with PAF-158 and PAF-159 in the perovskite films significantly increased the PL intensities of perovskite films. PAFs doping didn't change the film absorption (Figure 3a), implying a suppressed non-radiative charge recombination.

Furthermore, blue-shift in PL peaks compared to pristine perovskite films were observed, indicating the trap states situated at the band tail are effectively mitigated. The corresponding TRPL spectra corroborate this trend, mirroring the results obtained in the steady PL measurements (Figure 3c).

The average PL lifetime significantly increased from 489.2 to 1056.6 and 1152.3 ns, with detailed fitting parameters presented in Table S1.  $  ^{[37]}  $   Such a prolonged carrier lifetime indicates that the trap-assisted recombination is reduced. We attribute this to the enhanced passivation effect of Cl atoms on the backbone of fully conjugated PAFs. It worth mentioning that PAF-159 exhibit a superior passivation effect. The zoomed-in micro-area PL mapping was used to further study the passivation effect of Cl-bearing PAFs, as depicted in Figure 3d, e, and f. These images reveal an increase of the PL intensity of the PAFs-doped films, with high spatial uniformity. PAF-159 based film showed stronger emission and a larger area of bright emission, which is consistent with previous results. This clearly indicates that PAFs doping leads to perovskite films with a lower trap density and reduced charge recombination.  $  ^{[38]}  $

These values are quite close to those recorded from the J-V curves.

Since the long-term operational stability is critical to the possible commercialization of PSCs in the future. We fabricated control and PAFs-doped PSCs to evaluate the long-term operational stability of the encapsulated devices at the MPP under continuous AM 1.5G illumination in air. As depicted in Figure 4e, the control device experienced a rapid decline in performance, losing  $ 50\% $  of its initial PCE after just 350 hours. In contrast, devices doped with PAFs exhibited enhanced stability, with the PAF-158 and PAF-159 doped devices retaining  $ 85.7\% $  and  $ 92.3\% $  of the maximum PCE after identical duration. We further examined the long-term storage stability of the unencapsulated control and PAFs-doped devices at room temperature with a relative humidity of  $ 20\% $ . Figure 4g shows the normalized stability records of devices, the short and rapid decay of the efficiency before  $ 400\mathrm{h} $  is due to the burn-in loss of the devices.[41] It is obvious that both devices doped with PAF-158 and PAF-159 exhibited significantly improved stability, maintaining  $ 74.6\% $  and  $ 85.5\% $  of their initial PCE after  $ 3000\mathrm{h} $ , while the PCE of the control device underwent a rapid decay after  $ 300\mathrm{h} $  under the same storage condition and eventually lost more than  $ 70\% $  of its efficiency. The above significantly improved stability of the devices is

mainly attributed to the reduction of halide vacancies by passivation of Cl atoms on the backbone of both PAFs suppressing the defect induced ion migration.

As humidity stability is critical for the potential future commercialization of PSCs, we also tested the humidity stability of all the devices. To isolate the stability issue caused by a single factor, the devices were stored separately in a dark, moist environment at room temperature during the humidity stability test. All devices were unencapsulated. A saturated NaCl solution was used to create a high-humidity environment for device storage, with relative humidity fluctuating between 60% and 65% (Figure S19, inset). As shown in Figure S19, after 300 hours in high humidity, the efficiency of the control device decreased by more than 60%. In contrast, the devices doped with PAF-158 and PAF-159 showed higher humidity stability, maintaining about 85% and 90% of their initial PCE, respectively. We also performed a surface wettability study, as shown in Figure S20. The water contact angle (WCA) on the PAF-158- and PAF-159-doped perovskite films was 73° and 80°, respectively, which were higher than the control perovskite film's 58°. This suggested that PAF-doped perovskite films were more hydrophobic, facilitating the devices' resistance to humidity. We then assessed the stability performance of the unencapsulated films under aging conditions for 300 hours in the same dark environment (RH ≈65%, room temperature). As shown in Figure S21, the recorded UV/Vis absorption intensity of the control films dramatically decreased over time, while the PAF-158- and PAF-159-doped films exhibited greater stability. This trend is consistent with the humidity stability of the devices. This result further suggests that doping with PAFs significantly improves humidity stability. This improvement is likely due to the passivation of defects by PAFs, which enhances the quality of the perovskite film and prevents   $  H_{2}O  $   penetration and degradation of the perovskite film.

# Conclusions

In summary, we synthesized two chlorine-functionalized fully conjugated aromatic frameworks (PAFs) through a decarbonylation reaction, using triphenylbenzene and triphenylamine as core building blocks for PAF-158 and PAF-159, respectively. These materials showcased high specific surface areas, remarkable stability, and unique, extended conjugated networks featuring multiple chlorine atoms. Due to the stronger electronegativity of chlorine atoms, the electron cloud of the fully conjugated backbones moves toward the Cl atoms, designating them as defect binding sites and enhancing the passivation of fully conjugated PAFs. The introduction of Cl-containing PAFs into perovskite films led to enhanced emission quality, reduced trap density, and decreased non-radiative recombination rates. Consequently, the perovskite solar cells incorporating these PAFs achieved PCEs of  $ 23.43\% $  with PAF-158 and  $ 24.25\% $

with PAF-159, with significant improvements in both operational and storage stability. Further insights were gained through DFT calculations, which highlighted the presence of   $  \pi^{*}  $   anti-bonding orbitals associated with the triphenylamine core in PAF-159. This elevates the HOMO energy level of PAF-159, enhancing the Cl–Pb charge transfer. The mechanism indicates the superior passivation effectiveness of PAF-159 over PAF-158, illustrating how the molecular design contributes to its performance.
