# Dual-Interface Modulation with Covalent Organic Framework Enables Efficient and Durable Perovskite Solar Cells

Junjun Guo, Genping Meng, Xuliang Zhang, Hehe Huang, Junwei Shi, Baodui Wang, Xiaotian Hu, Jianyu Yuan,  $  ^{*}  $   and Wanli Ma  $  ^{*}  $

Dual-interface modulation including buried interface as well as the top surface has recently been proven to be crucial for obtaining high photovoltaic performance in lead halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Herein, for the first time, the strategy of using functional covalent organic frameworks (COFs), namely HS-COFs for dual-interface modulation, is reported to further understand its intrinsic mechanisms in optimizing the bottom and top surfaces. Specifically, the buried HS-COFs layer can enhance the resistance against ultraviolet radiation, and more importantly, release the tensile strain, which is beneficial for enhancing device stability and improving the order of perovskite crystal growth. Furthermore, the detailed characterization results reveal that the HS-COFs on the top surface can effectively passivate the surface defects and suppress non-radiation recombination, as well as optimize the crystallization and growth of the perovskite film. Benefiting from the synergistic effects, the dual-interface modified devices deliver champion efficiencies of 24.26% and 21.30% for 0.0725 cm $  ^{2}  $  and 1 cm $  ^{2}  $ -sized devices, respectively. Moreover, they retain 88% and 84% of their initial efficiencies after aging for 2000 h under the ambient conditions (25 °C, relative humidity: 35–45%) and a nitrogen atmosphere with heating at 65 °C, respectively.

During the past decade, to tackle the critical instability issue of PSCs, numerous efforts have been dedicated to passivating defects and improving the long-term stability of materials and devices. Strategies such as perovskite dimensionality engineering,  $  ^{[11,22]}  $   precursor solution optimization,  $  ^{[5,16,23]}  $   and device interfacial modification  $  ^{[24,25]}  $   have been demonstrated with the strength to enhance both PCE and stability. Among them, a simple yet efficient strategy is incorporating organic functional molecules with Lewis acid/base groups to effectively passivate defects through acid-base interactions to fabricate a high quality perovskite absorber layer with enhanced resistance to thermal stress and illumination.  $  ^{[12,26-28]}  $   As a novel class of crystalline and functional organic semiconducting materials, covalent organic frameworks (COFs) display properties like unique porous structures in the solid state, which have been widely explored in the field of sorption and catalysis.  $  ^{[29-31]}  $   Interestingly, there are a few recent reports of COFs in the application of PSCs. Zhao et al. first incorporated the spirobifluorene-based COFs in PSCs, and an enhanced PCE of 18.0% with excellent leakage prevention was achieved.  $  ^{[32]}  $   Seok et al. introduced TTDA-TTA COFs into the FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite absorber layer to improve the perovskite crystallization with a best efficiency over 23%.  $  ^{[33]}  $   Although pioneering studies have demonstrated the potential application of COFs in PSCs, none has announced the detailed and intrinsic mechanism, which is extremely important for further materials screening toward the-state-of-the-art photovoltaic performance.

Due to the conjugated structure and   $  \pi-\pi  $   interaction, most reported COFs exhibit a high absorption coefficient in the ultraviolet region, which could potentially prevent changes in ionic bonds in perovskite materials under illumination.  $  ^{[34]}  $   More interestingly, the ordered COFs with multiple organic functional groups could provide more contacts to modify both the bottom and top surfaces of perovskite films, optimizing crystalline film growth as well as passivating surface defects.  $  ^{[35-37]}  $   Along this line, herein, we first reported a dual-interface modulation strategy by using functional COFs with sulfhydryl functional groups (HS-COFs) for efficient and stable PSCs. The HS-COFs can successfully turn tensile strain into compressive strain and enhance the resistance of perovskite to ultraviolet irradiation when employed at the bottom of the perovskite film, and definitely passivate the top surface defects and inhibit non-radiative recombination when located at the top surface of the perovskite film. Consequently, the dual-interface modified device outperforms a champion efficiency of 24.26%, which is the highest efficiency for COFs-modified PSCs. Meanwhile, the COFs modulation can simultaneously enhance the stability and uniformity of the perovskite film, contributing to the excellent long-term device stability and the best PCE of 21.30% for 1 cm  $  ^{2}  $  -sized device.

# 2. Results and Discussion

The mixed PSCs were fabricated with a chemical component of   $  Cs_{0.05}FA_{0.85}MA_{0.10}Pb(I_{0.90}Br_{0.10})_{3}  $   described in the Experimental Section. As illustrated in Figure 1a, we integrate COFs into PSCs by depositing a thin COFs layer at the bottom and dissolving COFs in anti-solvent chlorobenzene for the top surface treatment, respectively. Under these circumstances, we could intensively understand the role of COFs in modulating the surface properties of perovskite films. COFs normally bear multiple aromatic rings and amino functional groups. Here, we adopted a recently reported HS-COFs (Figure 1b) due to its additional sulfhydryl group, which could further provide strong bonding with Pb.  $  ^{[31]}  $   Furthermore, according to photoluminescence (PL) and UV–vis absorption properties (Figure S1, Supporting Information), the HS-COFs modulation layer could serve as a UV filter and protect the perovskite film from high-energy photons, which could cause oxygen vacancies and defects in the perovskite crystal.  $  ^{[34]}  $   The corresponding PSCs were fabricated using a planar architecture of glass/fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)/TiO2/perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD/Au (Figure 1b), and the various layers are clearly discernible through the cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image. The steady-state PL was first introduced to study the effect of HS-COFs on the carrier dynamic process of perovskite films. As shown in Figure 1c, in comparison with the control perovskite film (790 nm), the buried HS-COFs modulated film exhibits enhanced intensity and a slightly blue-shifted emission peak centered at 786 nm, and the dual-interface modulated sample displays further enhanced PL intensity as well as a blue-shifted peak to 781 nm. In addition, from the PL intensity mapping characterization (Figure S2, Supporting Information), we observe a more identical intensity distribution in the HS-COFs modulated perovskite films than the control. Such an augmentation may be attributed to the passivation of trap states and inhibition of nonradiative recombination.  $  ^{[38]}  $   These observations are also confirmed by the change of carrier lifetime in time-resolved PL (TRPL) measurements (Figure 1d), with detailed parameters listed in Table S1 (Supporting Information). The HS-COFs modified perovskite films exhibit a significantly prolonged carrier lifetime (579 ns for buried and 716 ns for dual-interface modification) relative to the control film (393 ns), indicating reduced density of surface trap states.  $  ^{[12]}  $   Meanwhile, to understand the change in film optical properties, we performed UV-vis absorption spectra characterization on the control and HS-COFs modulated perovskite films. As shown in Figure 1e, the HS-COFs have a negligible influence on the characteristic absorption region of perovskite while increasing absorption in the near ultraviolet region.  $  ^{[39]}  $

Subsequently, hole-only devices were prepared to investigate the defect density of perovskite films before and after HS-COFs dual-interface modulation using the space charge limited current (SCLC) method. The HS-COFs dual-interface modulated hole-only device exhibits a lower trap-filled limited voltage   $  V_{\mathrm{TFL}}  $   of 0.284 V compared to that of the control device (0.437 V), and the corresponding trap density   $  n_{t}  $   value is calculated to be   $  0.36 \times 10^{16}  $   and   $  0.58 \times 10^{16}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  , respectively (Figure 5a). Moreover, the thermal admittance spectroscopy was introduced to determine the trap density of state (tDOS) of the HS-COFs modulated device and control device. As shown in Figure 5b, the spectra could be separated into three regions depending on the energy depths. Distinctly, the trap density states of the HS-COFs modulated device are smaller than those of the control device in band I region, suggesting suppressed shallow defects in the target perovskite film.  $  ^{[52]}  $   The dark current curves (Figure S14, Supporting Information) indicate that the HS-COFs modified devices present a reduced leakage current, which confirms that the functional group of HS-COFs could efficiently passivate the defects in perovskites. versus different intensities of illumination were further determined to comprehend the trap-related recombination in perovskites. The target presents exponential factor (  $  \alpha  $  ) values (0.996) similar to those of the control device (0.995) (Figure S15, Supporting Information), indicating that both devices have negligible bimolecular recombination.  $  ^{[53,54]}  $   In comparison, the slope of the target device is 1.51 kT/q, which is smaller than the control device (1.87 kT/q) in the   $  V_{oc}  $   versus light intensity measurements, indicating that trap-induced charge recombination is remarkably inhibited through HS-COFs dual-interface modulation (Figure 5c). As shown in Figure 5d and Table S5 (Supporting Information), the HS-COFs modulated target device exhibits a reduced charge-transfer resistance (28.1   $  \Omega  $  ) and increased recombination resistance (1250.1   $  \Omega  $  ), indicating improved charge transfer and suppressed carrier recombination at the perovskite interface, which supplies a smooth channel for carrier extraction and creates a barrier for charge recombination.  $  ^{[17,26]}  $   A similar conclusion could be obtained from the capacitance-voltage characteristics (Figure 5e). The HS-COFs dual-interface modified device demonstrates an increased built-in potential (  $  V_{bi}  $  ) from 0.89 to 0.97 V, implying an enlarged driving force of charge carrier extraction and separation, and contributing to the large   $  V_{oc}  $  .  $  ^{[55]}  $   In addition, the recombination lifetime   $  \tau  $   evaluated by the transient photovoltage decay for the HS-COFs modulated target device (44.7   $  \mu  $  s) is significantly longer than that for the control one (5.1   $  \mu  $  s), indicating that the HS-COFs modulation could effectively suppress the nonradiative recombination.

# 3. Conclusion

In conclusion, through incorporating functional HS-COFs as a dual-interface modulation layer, we first demonstrated a simple yet efficient strategy for high-performance PSCs. By depositing a thin HS-COFs layer at the bottom of the perovskite film, it can release lattice stress and absorb light in the ultraviolet region. Furthermore, by dissolving HS-COFs in chlorobenzene at an optimal concentration to post-treat the perovskite films, we found that the existence of -SH functional groups is important for defects passivation in perovskite films. Combined film and solar cell device characterizations confirmed that the strong interactions between HS-COFs and perovskite films via S-Pb coordination, which effectively reduce the number of trap-states in the film and suppress trap-induced nonradiative recombination. Meanwhile, with the assistance of dual-interface HS-COFs, the resultant perovskite film presents an eliminated interfacial lattice mismatch with the strain-free perovskite film relative to the control film due to the change of thermal expansion coefficient between the interface. The dual-interface modulation enables an excellent PCE of 24.26% together with enhanced thermal and ambient storage stability relative to 20.79% in the control one. Furthermore, the best PCE of 21.30% was derived for the large-area device (1 cm²). Overall, these joint advances in nonradiative recombination inhibition, tensile strain release, as well as PCE and stability enhancement highlight the potential of such COFs dual-interface modulation strategy, which could further boost the development of highly efficient and stable photovoltaic devices.

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials: HS-COFs were composed according to recent studies.  $  ^{[31]}  $   The other materials listed were purchased directly without any further purification. Then, lead iodide (PbI  $  _{2}  $  , 99.999%) and lead bromide (PbBr  $  _{2}  $  , 99.99%) were purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry, cesium iodide (CsI, 99.99%) was purchased from Alfa Aesar, formamidinium iodide (FAI, 99.5%) and methylammonium bromide (MABr, 99.5%) were purchased from Greatcell Solar, and Spiro-OMeTAD [2,2',7,7'-tetrakis-(N,N-dipmethoxyphenylamine)-9,9'-spiro-bifluorene] was purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. All the solvents applied for perovskite precursors included N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.9%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.7%), and chlorbenzene (CB, 99.5%) were purchased from Alfa Aesar.

Preparation of Perovskite Solar Cells: First, the FTO glass substrates were cleaned ultrasonically with deionized water, isopropanol, and acetone. Next, clean FTO substrates were used for the   $  TiO_{2}  $   deposition.  $  ^{[38]}  $   Then, the FTO substrates with   $  TiO_{2}  $   were washed with ethanol and dried with dry nitrogen. And then, the substrates were thermally annealed at 200 °C for 30 min, before being transferred into a nitrogen glove box, and the substrates were treated using ultraviolet ozone (UVO) for 15 min. For the HS-COFs buried device, HS-COFs dissolved in chlorobenzene at different concentrations (0–2.0 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) were spin coated onto   $  TiO_{2}  $   at 3000 rpm for 30 s. For the fabrication process of perovskite thin films, a 30 μL of perovskite precursor solution was sequentially spin-coated at 1000 rpm for 10 s and 6000 rpm for 20 s. For the high-speed spin-coating process, an antisolvent containing different concentrations of HS-COFs (0-1.0 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was dripped onto the perovskite surface 8 s before the end of spin-coating. The glove box was continuously purged in   $  N_{2}  $   for 30 min before fabricating the perovskite films to ensure that there was no residual solvent atmosphere. Furthermore, the fabricated perovskite films were persistently thermally annealed at 100 °C for 60 min. In addition, the Spiro-OMeTAD (91 mg Spiro-OMeTAD, 20.63 μL lithium bis

imide (520 mg lithium bis imide dissolved in 1 mL acetonitrile) and 35.5 μL of 4-tert-butylpyridine in 1 mL of chlorobenzene) was spin-coated on the perovskite film at 4000 rpm for 20 s after the perovskite films cooled down. Ultimately, 100 nm Au was evaporated using thermal vacuum evaporation under   $  5 \times 10^{-6}  $   mbar.
