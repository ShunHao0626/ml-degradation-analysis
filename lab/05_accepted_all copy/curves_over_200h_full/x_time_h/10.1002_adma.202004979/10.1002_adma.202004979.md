# High-Pressure Nitrogen-Extraction and Effective Passivation to Attain Highest Large-Area Perovskite Solar Module Efficiency

Minyong Du, Xuejie Zhu, Likun Wang, Hui Wang, Jiangshang Feng, Xiao Jiang, Yuexian Cao, Youming Sun, Lianjie Duan, Yuxiao Jiao, Kai Wang,* Xiaodong Ren, Zhe Yan, Shuping Pang, and Shengzhong (Frank) Liu*

Slot-die coating holds advantages over other large-scale technologies thanks to its potential for well-controlled, high-throughput, continuous roll-to-roll fabrication. Unfortunately, it is challenging to control thin.film uniformity over a large area while maintaining crystallization quality. Herein, by using a high-pressure nitrogen-extraction (HPNE) strategy to assist crystallization, a wide processing window in the well-controlled printing process for preparing high-quality perovskites is achieved. The yellow-phase perovskite generated by the HPNE acts as a crucial intermediate phase to produce large-area high-quality perovskite film. Furthermore, an ionic liquid is developed to passivate the perovskite surface to reduce surface defect density and to suppress carrier recombination, resulting in significantly increased efficiency to  $ 22.7\% $ , the highest for large-area fabrication. The strategies are successfully extended to large-area device fabrication, making it possible to produce a  $ 40 \times 40 \mathrm{~mm}^2 $  module with stabilized PCE as high as  $ 19.4\% $ , the highest-efficiency for a large-area module to date.

Organometallic halide perovskite has attracted extensive interest by virtue of its stunning defect tolerance, small exciton-binding energy,  $  ^{[1]}  $  high absorption coefficient,  $  ^{[2]}  $  extremely low trap-state density,  $  ^{[3]}  $  and long carrier diffusion length.  $  ^{[4]}  $  In just a few years, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of lab-scale perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has swiftly skyrocketed from 3.8% to 25.2%,  $  ^{[5]}  $  outperforming all other thin film solar cells. However, this acceleration is prone to getting out of control and easily fosters the formation of discrete grains, which must be compensated for by either employing an extremely slow coating speed or adding a surfactant to regulate the dynamics of fluid diffusion.  $  ^{[15]}  $   Additionally, although anti-solvent engineering was utilized for the champion PCE of 18.0% for the PSCs based on one-step slot-die coating, the fabrication process, which required immersing the wet films in the volatile anti-solvent, is unfavorable for large-scale manufacture.  $  ^{[16]}  $   Therefore, the universal gas treatment strategy possessing the same functions deserves investigation.  $  ^{[14b]}  $   Thus far, the highest PCE for slot-die coated PSCs whose formation was assisted by gas treatment reached only 15.57%,  $  ^{[17]}  $   which is unacceptable for large deployment. In addition, the perovskite material commonly utilized for the slot-die coating strategy as well as other scalable technologies is MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  , but MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   decomposes easily above 85 °C due to its low formation energy,  $  ^{[18]}  $   leading to poor device stability. Overall, the performance of slot-die coated PSCs is still low, lagging far behind those from the other large-scale technologies, especially the champion PCE of 21.9% from blade coating.  $  ^{[19]}  $   We attribute the unfavorable PCE to the complicated nature of perovskite crystallization from the precursor state and the complex processing parameters in the slot-die coating process, including substrate temperature, dynamic flow of inks, solvent density, substrate moving speed, etc. Thus, continuously optimizing the slot-die coating process and further improving the device performance are paramount for achieving large-scale, roll-to-roll production.

To address this, we demonstrate a high-pressure nitrogen-extraction (HPNE) strategy to assist crystallization and demonstrate a room-temperature, well-controlled, high-throughput printing process, which can deposit high-quality perovskite films, and, in parallel, we fabricate large area PSCs with outstanding performance. Strikingly, the stable intermediate phase enables this process to enjoy a wide processing window, leading the PCE to be independent of most printing parameters. In previous reports, the numerous undercoordinated atoms on the perovskite surface act as charged defects and generate deep-level trap states in the energy band.  $  ^{[20]}  $   Thus, surface passivation utilizing an ionic liquid was implemented to effectively neutralize the defects and deactivate deep-level traps, which can further improve the interface charge dynamics and restrain the perovskite degradation. As a result, the solar cell stabilized efficiency is increased to as high as 22.5% with reduced hysteresis, representing the best performance among PSCs based on scalable technologies. More noticeably, the PSC module with area   $  40 \times 40 \, mm^2  $   assembled by this controlled printing yields a PCE of 19.6%. Such high efficiency suggests that the slot-die coating in combination with HPNE is a valuable, well-controlled, and scalable technique for large area high-efficiency PSCs.

In this experiment, CsI and MACl were introduced into formamidinium-based lead triiodide (FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ) with the aim of shrinking the lattice and

It should be noted that high nitrogen pressure (  $  \approx1.0  $   MPa) was utilized in this study to achieve fast extraction comparing to low pressure (0.04–0.6 MPa)  $  ^{[22]}  $   used in literature. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was implemented to analyze the effect of HPNE on the slot-die coated perovskite films. As per Figure 1a, the diffraction peaks located at 13.9°, 19.8°, 24.3°, 28.1°, and 31.5° are assigned to the (101), (012), (021), (202), and (211) crystal planes of the cubic perovskite phase.  $  ^{[23]}  $   The main diffraction peaks in the HPNE-processed perovskite films have greater intensity than those of the control without HPNE, attesting to the enhanced texturing or crystallinity. Figure S2–S4, Supporting Information show the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images and elemental mappings of perovskite films with and without HPNE. There are many large grains in the pristine samples, leaving the underlayer exposed, whereas, as expected, the HPNE-processed films exhibit an extremely dense morphology composed of large grains with the average size of 580 nm coupled with uniform elemental dispersion. There are two possible reasons attributed to this enhancement. On one hand, the high speed nitrogen flow enables solvents to evaporate rapidly from the wet coating, quickly driving the system to a oversaturated state for homogeneous crystallization.  $  ^{[24]}  $   On the other hand, when the highly compressed nitrogen escapes from the outlet into the ambient, the quick gas expansion from high pressure to atmospheric pressure is expected to cause significant temperature drop (Equations (S1) and (S2), Supporting Information). As evidenced by Figure S5, Supporting Information, the low-temperature nitrogen flow is expected to effectively decrease the local temperature at the coating surface from 25.0 °C to 12.9 °C, leading to oversaturation for uniform crystallization. The diffraction peak located at 12.7° in XRD confirms the existence of the   $  PbI_{2}  $   phase in the HPNE-processed samples, possibly due to partial decomposition of perovskite, and the   $  PbI_{2}  $ , which accumulates at grain boundaries, is advantageous to the device performance by hindering charge recombination and forming a favorable energy band alignment.  $  ^{[25]}  $

It is clear that when as per Figure 1b, the liquid flow rate and coating speed have little effect on the photovoltaic performance. It is crucial to reveal the mechanism behind the wide processing window of our controlled printing technology, which must be associated with the HPNE process. In general, solution deposition of perovskites includes coating a wet precursor film onto the substrate, evaporating/drying the solvent and forming perovskite or an intermediate solid film, followed by thermal annealing to fully crystallize the film. Since the HPNE significantly affects the solvent evaporation and the local temperature at the wet film surface, the as-formed perovskite or the intermediate phase is carefully examined. The XRD was conducted to study the films prepared with various parameters before annealing. As per Figure S8, Supporting Information, the solidified film without any post treatments is approximately amorphous. In strong contrast, since the HPNE-processed films possess noteworthy crystallinity and are dominantly composed of the yellow non-perovskite (  $  \delta  $  ) phase with hexagonal symmetry (  $  P6_{3}/mmc  $  ), as attested to by the intense peak at 11.7°, and the black (  $  \alpha  $  ) phase perovskite with trigonal symmetry (  $  P3\overline{3}m  $  ). Surface defects, including undercoordinated ions, vacancies, Pb clusters, and antisite defects, are generally regarded as deep-level traps. They can affect the Fermi level splitting under irradiation conditions and restrain the interface charge transfer process, thereby deteriorating the photovoltaic performance of PSCs. To address this, the ionic liquid tetramethylammonium tetrafluoroborate (  $  [M_{4}N]BF_{4}  $  ) was cast onto the annealed perovskite films with the aim of passivating the surface defects. Upon surface passivation, the   $  BF_{4}^{-}  $  in   $  [M_{4}N]BF_{4}  $   could form strong ionic bonds with the unsaturated Pb. As such, the I vacancies were diminished accompanied by promoted interface performance. For further explanation, density functional theory calculations were carried out to adequately clarify   $  BF_{4}^{-}  $  interaction with halide vacancies. For reducing the calculation quantity, a   $  2 \times 2  $   supercell of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   was employed in the study. After constructing I vacancies, a trap state emerged in the forbidden band whilst it diminished upon binding   $  BF_{4}^{-}  $  ions, indicating the passivation effect. The binding energy   $  BF_{4}^{-}  $  and   $  I^{-}  $  with the vacancies are 7.34 and 3.74 eV, respectively. Meanwhile, despite the longer carrier lifetime in the   $  [M_{4}N]BF_{4}  $  -modified perovskite films without spiro-OMeTAD, the modified films display a similar or even shorted carrier lifetime compared with the control samples after depositing spiro-OMeTAD. For heterojunctions, the built-in potential is caused by carrier diffusion under thermal equilibrium and is a vital property for charge injection in solar cells. The application of an external bias can drive the junction from equilibrium to the flat band state, and results in capacitance variation. The capacitance–potential (C-V) relationship obeys the Mott–Schottky Equation (2) as follows.  $  ^{[37]}  $

$$
\frac {1}{C ^ {2}} = \frac {2}{e \varepsilon \varepsilon_ {0} N _ {\mathrm{D}}} \left(V _ {\mathrm{bi}} - V - \frac {k _ {\mathrm{B}} T}{e}\right)
$$

(2)

where  $ C $  refers to the capacitance of the space charge region,  $ V_{\mathrm{bi}} $  represents the built-in potential,  $ V $  is the applied potential,  $ k_{\mathrm{B}} $  is the Boltzmann constant,  $ T $  is the thermodynamic temperature, and  $ N_{\mathrm{D}} $  is the donor density. The built-in potential can thus be estimated from the intercept of a Mott-Schottky plot with the  $ x $ -axis. As per Figure 4d, the  $ V_{\mathrm{bi}} $  of devices with  $ [\mathbf{M}_4\mathbf{N}] $

$ \mathrm{BF}_4 $  is  $ \approx 1.30\mathrm{V} $ , outperforming the pristine device ( $ \approx 1.08\mathrm{V} $ ) and comparable with that reported in previous works of similar device structures.[44] Therefore, the  $ [\mathrm{M}_4\mathrm{N}]\mathrm{BF}_4 $  layer can enhance the driving force for carrier injection, which, coupled with the extended depletion region, leads to effective suppression of charge recombination.[45]

The   $  J_{SC}  $   and   $  V_{OC}  $   of PSCs with and without   $  [M_{4}N]BF_{4}  $   were measured under regulated light intensity (I) to estimate trap-assisted nonradiative recombination and bimolecular radiative recombination. The relationship between   $  J_{SC}  $   and I follows the power law   $  J_{SC} \propto I^{\alpha,[46]}  $   where   $  \alpha  $   is a factor dependent on the degree of bimolecular recombination. As illustrated in Figure 4e, the samples present similar   $  \alpha  $   values extremely close to 1, indicative of insignificant bimolecular recombination due to the high-quality perovskites. Furthermore, the   $  V_{OC}  $   ought to possess a liner correlation with the logarithm of light intensity according to Equation (3).  $  ^{[47]}  $

$$
\frac {d V _ {\mathrm{OC}}}{d l g I} = 2. 3 0 3 \frac {n k _ {\mathrm{B}} T}{q}
$$

(3)

where   $  k_{B}  $   is the Boltzmann constant, n is the ideality factor, T is the thermodynamic temperature, and q is the elementary charge. The deviation of the slope from   $  k_{B}T/q  $   is assigned to trap-assisted recombination. As described in Figure 4f, the device with   $  [M_{4}N]BF_{4}  $   enjoys a smaller slope of   $  1.09k_{B}T/q  $   compared with the   $  1.63k_{B}T/q  $   for the pristine sample, further validating that   $  [M_{4}N]BF_{4}  $   modification can effectively suppress the trap-assisted recombination.  $  ^{[48]}  $   Hence, it is   $  [M_{4}N]BF_{4}  $   that brings about the convergence of surface defect passivation, prolonged carrier lifetime, and accelerated interface charge transfer, thus reducing the energy loss and enhancing the device performance.

The uniform, pin-hole-free perovskite films prepared by this scalable controlled printing method exhibit potential for use in large-area PSC modules. A photograph of a PSC module fabricated on a   $  40 \times 40 \, mm^2  $   glass substrate is shown in Figure S19, Supporting Information. The width of each sub-cell and the nonworking regions are 0.6 and 0.1 cm, respectively; therefore, the geometrical fill factor is 85.7%, which can be dramatically enhanced by optimizing the etching technique. Because of this, we tested a module composed of the first four sub-cells (active area:   $  7.92 \, cm^2  $  ) and obtained the champion PCEs of 19.6% and 19.0% for the reverse and forward scans, respectively. In summary, we demonstrated a facile, yet compelling, strategy to deposit large-scale perovskite absorber layers by using a room-temperature, controlled printing process. By utilizing the HPNE strategy, we were surprised to find that our controlled printing process enjoys a wide processing window, almost independent of coating speed, liquid rate, and   $  N_{2}  $   flow pressure. Recent reports also confirmed that, for similar perovskite compositions, the blowing-  $  N_{2}  $   treating time after coating is longer than 10 min, and the time window from treating to annealing is wider than 100 s. Therefore, the HPNE technology is compatible with the slot-die coating process, meeting the requirements of high yield and easy fabrication for industrial

production. Noticeably,   $  Cs^{+}  $  and MACl play crucial roles in the wide processing window by stabilizing the intermediate phase. Furthermore, we used ionic liquid to passivate the surface defects, and the PCE was increased from 21.2% to 22.7% with ignorable hysteresis for the 0.09 cm  $  ^{2}  $   illuminated area. Meanwhile, the ionic liquid effectively prevents erosion by humidity and the diffusion of gold atoms, both of which are beneficial for enhancing stability. Eventually, the controlled printing was extended to assemble PSC modules of area 40 × 40 mm  $  ^{2}  $  , and the best PCE was 19.6% (18.6%) for an active area of 7.92 cm  $  ^{2}  $   (10.2 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ). We note that the device area indeed can be further increased, as it is now limited by the coating of spiro-OMeTAD and the illumination area of the solar simulator in our lab. Overall, these results suggest that this controlled printing with HPNE strategy is a highly efficient, wide-window, cost-effective, area-scalable technique for fabrication of PSCs, which will advance the development of slot-die coating as well as its application to high-throughput roll-to-roll deposition.
