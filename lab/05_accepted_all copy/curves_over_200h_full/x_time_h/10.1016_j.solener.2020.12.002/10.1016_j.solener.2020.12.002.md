# 1. Introduction

As a burgeoning photovoltaic technology, the organic-inorganic halide perovskite solar cell (PSC) is considered as one of the most promising alternative to Si-based solar cells, because of their numerous advantages, such as easy fabrication process, low production cost, mechanical flexibility and enhanced power conversion efficiency (PCE) (Jeon et al., 2014; Jeon et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2013; Yang et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2017). The PCE of the PSCs has rapidly increased within several years from  $ 3.8\% $  to  $ 25.2\% $ , exceeding that of the traditional polysilicon solar cells, thus exhibiting huge potential in future commercial applications (Chen et al., 2015; Im et al., 2011; Kojima et al., 2009; Sahli et al., 2018). There have been many attempts for improving PSCs, such as replacing Au electrode with C-based materials (Fagiolari et al., 2019), using Pb-free perovskite (Liu et al., 2018; Singh et al., 2020), adding dopants (Federico et al., 2018), adopting new interlayers (Yang et al., 2020), fabricating hybrid perovskite-polymer tandem cells (Liu et al., 2016) and semi-transparent PSCs (Pulli et al., 2020), and using two-dimensional perovskite (Tsai et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2020).

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) are considered among the most promising alternatives to Si-based solar cells, owing to the ongoing rise in their power conversion efficiencies (PCEs). While   $  TiO_{2}  $   has been widely used as an electron transport layer (ETL) in the n-i-p (regular) type PSCs, series of its disadvantages, including limited bulk electron mobility and photocatalytic character, restrict further improvement on the photovoltaic performance and environmental stability of devices. Therefore,   $  SnO_{2}  $   has attracted much attention as an alternative, because of its relatively low fabrication temperature, suitable energy alignment and high electron mobility. In this study, we effectively controlled the morphology of the   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL by adding an organic solvent (ethanol) into the commercialized colloidal   $  SnO_{2}  $   precursor. This approach improved not only the wettability of the precursor on the glass substrate, but also the stability of the   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloidal dispersion. Thus, an aggregation-free   $  SnO_{2}  $   film with homogenous morphology was obtained with the assistance of ethanol, which also contributed to the improved crystal quality of the perovskite. The PSCs fabricated using ethanol-processed   $  SnO_{2}  $   exhibited a high PCE of 18.84% and improved environmental stability. Thus, our investigation provides a simple and effective approach toward achieving high-performance   $  SnO_{2}  $ -based PSCs.

Huan and coworkers integrated a PSC with an electrolyzer cell for  $ \mathrm{CO}_{2} $  reduction to hydrocarbons (Huan et al., 2019).

The regular device architecture of the PSCs is described as follows: transparent conductive metal-oxide cathode (indium tin oxide or fluorine-doped tin oxide)/electron transport layer (ETL)/perovskite absorber/hole transport layer (HTL)/metal anode (Arora et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2019c; Liu et al., 2019d; Xu et al., 2018a; Xu et al., 2019). However, for perovskite materials with a greater hole diffusion length than the electron diffusion length, a well-developed ETL is crucial to obtain devices with high photovoltaic performance (Kim et al., 2013; Mohseni et al., 2021). Generally,  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  with planar or mesoporous structures is adopted as the ETL of the PSCs, which can effectively transfer the electrons and block the holes (Liu et al., 2014; Roldán-Carmona et al., 2015; Saliba et al., 2016). However, its preparation process generally requires high temperature or complex treatments that involve high production cost (Liu et al., 2019b). In addition, the photocatalytic properties of the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  film used in the PSCs reduces the device stability under illumination, thereby accelerating the degradation of the perovskite devices (Dong et al., 2019). Among these,   $  SnO_{2}  $   exhibits many outstanding advantages, including a simple fabrication process, high electron mobility, excellent optical transparency, suitable energy alignment and chemical stability (Jiang et al., 2016). Therefore,   $  SnO_{2}  $   has been widely studied as the ETL material for fabricating high-performance PSCs with a regular structure, resulting in significantly improved PCE—from the initial 7.43% to the recently certified 23.3% (Anaraki et al., 2016; Baena et al., 2015; Jiang et al., 2019; Ke et al., 2015; Song et al., 2015; Tavakoli et al., 2018). To achieve superior electronic properties, numerous approaches have been studied for the fabrication of   $  SnO_{2}  $ , such as solution-processed spin coating, e-beam evaporation, chemical bath deposition and atomic layer deposition (Ma et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2017; Xiong et al., 2018). Among these, the solution-processed spin-coating method is preferred because of the easy fabrication process, low-temperature treatment and economical production cost (Guo et al., 2018). Most solution deposition methods involve thermal decomposition by dissolving the Sn-based salt (  $  SnCl_{2}  $  ,   $  SnCl_{4}  $   or their aquo complex) in ethanol or other solvents to prepare the precursor solution, which is then spin coated onto a UV/ ozone-treated substrate (Jiang et al., 2018). Fang and coworkers obtained an average PCE of 17.21% by using the sol-gel method (Ke et al., 2015). However, as the annealing process requires a high temperature, i.e., 180 °C, obtaining a fully covered and homogenous film with uniform grain size is difficult. To overcome these problems, some efforts were made, such as adding chemicals to passivate the defects in   $  SnO_{2}  $   (Ai et al., 2019) and adopting the dual ETLs (Mohamadkhani et al., 2019; Otoufi et al., 2020). On the other hand, the commercial   $  SnO_{2}  $   nanocolloidal (15% in   $  H_{2}O  $  ) dispersion of fixed concentration and stable physicochemical property provides a relatively easier fabrication process and stable film quality, ensuring a controllable device performance and better repeatability. Similarly, You and his coworkers achieved a PCE of 20.9% by adopting the   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL deposited with commercialized   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloidal dispersion (Jiang et al., 2017). However, the deposited   $  SnO_{2}  $   film usually exhibited some aggregations, despite the dilution of the original colloidal solution with deionized (DI) water followed by filtering, which hinder further improvement in the photovoltaic performance.

In this study, we have utilized a solvent treatment engineering process to precisely control the  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  layer morphology and improve the PCE of the PSCs. The treatment involves the addition of different organic diluents (namely ethanol and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF)) to the  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  colloidal dispersion. This significantly improves not only the stability of the dispersion, but also the wettability to the substrate, which is required to achieve a high-quality ETL with a simple spin-coating process. Furthermore, we comprehensively investigated the morphology and contact angles of the ETL films fabricated using different solvents, and the results showed that the ethanol-diluted  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  precursor ensures a homogenous and aggregation-free ETL film with uniform grain size, compared with the ETL layer with severe aggregation and poor morphology prepared from the precursor samples diluted with DI water or DMF. The PSCs with the optimized  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  layer exhibited a superior PCE of  $ 18.84\% $ , which is  $ 15.2\% $  higher than that of the control PSCs  $ (16.35\%) $ . Besides, this method achieved a superior photovoltaic performance compared with that of the other physical treatment methods  $ (18.1\%) $  (Liu et al., 2019a). Moreover, the PSCs prepared using the ethanol-diluted  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  exhibited excellent stability in ambient environment, delivering suppressed device degradation with retained PCE from  $ 88\% $  to  $ 92\% $  for over  $ 300\mathrm{h} $ . Hence, this simple and low-cost solvent treatment engineering process holds high promise for the future industrial production of PSCs.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1. Chemicals and reagents

The   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloidal dispersion (15% in   $  H_{2}O  $  ) was purchased from Alfa Aesar (USA), while   $  PbI_{2}  $   and   $  PbBr_{2}  $   were obtained from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (Japan). Chlorobenzene (CB), dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO, anhydrous, 99.8%), ethanol, acetonitrile (99.9%), bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt (LiTFSI), 4-tert-butylpyridine (TBP, 96%) and DMF were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Formamidinium (iodide (FAI), methylammonium bromide (MABr) and 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis[N,N-di(4-methoxyphenyl)amino]-9,9'-spirobifluorene (spiro-OMeTAD) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. (China). The patterned indium tin oxide (ITO) glasses were purchased from Ying Kou You Xuan Trade Co., Ltd. (China).

# 2.2. Device fabrication

A planar device structure with the stacking sequence: glass/ITO/SnO $ _2 $ /perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD/Au was adopted for the PSCs, owing to its easy construction and solution processability, as shown in Fig. 1(a). The glass substrates with the ITO bar cathode were cleaned with detergent, DI water and isopropanol (IPA) in sequence and then treated with UV-ozone for 20 min. The diluted organic solvent (ethanol or DMF) was prepared by mixing the solvent with DI water in the volume ratio of 3:1, 1:1 or 1:3. The different SnO $ _2 $  precursors were prepared by diluting the SnO $ _2 $  colloidal dispersion to 2.67% (v/v) with DI water, diluted ethanol or DMF. The prepared SnO $ _2 $  precursor was spin coated on ITO glass at 4000 rpm for 30 s in ambient air, followed by thermal annealing at 150 °C for 25 min. The (FAPbI $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.85} $ (MAPbBr $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.15} $  perovskite was formed by a precursor solution prepared by dissolving PbI $ _2 $  (1.1 M), PbBr $ _2 $  (0.2 M), FAI (1 M) and MAI (0.2 M) in an anhydrous mixture of DMF and DMSO (4:1, v:v), which was then stirred for 8 h at 60 °C, where MA and FA stand for methylammonium and formamidinium, respectively. The perovskite precursor was deposited on top of the SnO $ _2 $  layer by spin coating, using a two-step procedure: at 1000 rpm for 10 s and then at 4000 rpm for 30 s in a nitrogen-filled glove box. During the second step, CB (300 μL) was quickly casted onto the central part of the samples at a time delay of 8 s. The samples were subsequently annealed on a hot plate at 100 °C for 45 min in a nitrogen-filled glove box. A compact and dense perovskite film of approximately 400 nm thickness was formed (Fig. S1). Next, the hole transport layer (HTL) was formed by spin coating at 3000 rpm for 30 s with the spiro-OMeTAD solution that was prepared by dissolving 72.3 mg spiro-OMeTAD, 28.8 μL TBP and 17.5 μL Li-TFSI solutions (520 mg mL $ ^{-1} $  in acetonitrile) in 1 mL CB. Finally, a 60 nm thick Au electrode was coated on top of the samples (working area: 0.1 cm $ ^2 $ ), using a thermal evaporator under high vacuum (<6.0 × 10 $ ^{-6} $  Torr).

# 3. Results and discussion

Fig. 1(b) shows that  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  exhibits deeper conduction and valence bands in the range of  $ -4.3\mathrm{eV} $  and  $ -7.5\mathrm{eV} $ , respectively, compared with  $ \mathrm{TiO}_2 $  (-3.7 eV and -7.0 eV, respectively), which benefits the electron transfer while efficiently blocking the holes (Jiang et al., 2016). Thus, replacing  $ \mathrm{TiO}_2 $  with  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  as ETL can decrease the recombination of the excitons in the PSCs (Jiang et al., 2016). The morphology and electronic property are generally optimized by reducing the concentration of the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  colloidal dispersion from its initial  $ 15\% $  to  $ 2.67\% $  (Jiang et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2018a; Wang et al., 2018b; Zhang et al., 2018). However, there was no obvious difference in the film morphology under various concentrations, as observed in the SEM images in Fig. S2(a-c). Therefore, for a comprehensive consideration of the film thickness and related spin-coating parameters, we applied the  $ 2.67\% $  concentration for our subsequent experiments. As shown in Fig. S2, the ETL films fabricated from the DI water diluted  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  precursor exhibited randomly distributed aggregations after the spin-coating process (regardless of concentration), which could result in poor interfacial contact. Moreover, the changed concentration of the initial  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  solution may affect the stability of dispersoid. To address these issues, the organic solvents were applied to improve the dispersibility as well as stability of the system. As shown in Fig. S3, direct addition of the pure organic solvent to dilute the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  colloidal dispersion strongly affects the solution environment, leading to the turbid precursors, which might be caused by the removal or dissolution of the surfactant surrounding the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  particles (Liu et al., 2019a). Therefore, to prepare a stable diluent, solutions of ethanol and DMF mixed with DI water at different ratios were prepared, as summarized in Table S1. Note that adding DMF to the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  precursor causes coagulation of the colloids despite the blending ratio, which may be due to the irreversible damage of the surfactant (Hoffmann et al., 1988; Wilkinson et al., 1997). Hence, the water-diluted organic solvent with a mixing ratio of 1:1 will be applied in the subsequent experiments if not specified otherwise. As shown in Fig. S4, after storage for  $ 500\mathrm{h} $ , the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  precursors diluted with ethanol (denoted as ethanol- $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $ ) and DI

water (denoted as  $ \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O - SnO}_2 $ ) maintain a transparent state unlike the diluent with DMF (denoted as DMF- $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $ ). The images of the water contact angles in Fig. 1(c-e) show that ethanol- $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  exhibits the lowest water contact angle of  $ 22^{\circ} $ , while the  $ \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O - SnO}_2 $  and DMF- $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  exhibit angles of  $ 44^{\circ} $  and  $ 30^{\circ} $ , respectively, which could be due to the altered surface tension. The reduced water contact angle of ethanol- $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  indicates improved wettability, which may contribute to a better film morphology (Dong et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2019b).

The initial   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloidal dispersion exhibited stability with an appropriate surfactant (Liu et al., 2019a), avoiding the massive agglomeration of the nanoparticles. However, during the spin-coating process, the concentration of the residual solution on substrate surface was altered due to the evaporation of the solvent, which could cause instability in the system (Wang et al., 2019). This in turn may result in an uncontrollable and rough ETL with dispersed agglomeration. However, as shown in Fig. 2(a), ethanol-  $  SnO_{2}  $   is relatively stable and effectively prevents the random aggregation during the spin-coating process. This is because the addition of ethanol improves the viscosity of the solution (1.4 mPa·s), which effectively controls the aggregation among the dispersed nanoparticles (Ferrini et al., 1979; Tambe et al., 1994); in contrast, mixing DI water reduces the system viscosity (1.2 mPa·s). The SEM images in Fig. 3(a-c) demonstrate the morphology differences between the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  layers prepared from the different types of precursors. Clearly, the ethanol-assisted colloidal dispersion effectively avoids the random aggregation of the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  nanoparticles during the deposition process ((Fig. 3(b)), which is consistent with our model in Fig. 2(a). In contrast, the control group mixed with  $ \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} $  exhibited some randomly distributed agglomerations on top of the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  layer, leading to reduced optical and electrical properties (Fig. 3(a)). Generally, the morphology of the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  film strongly influences the interfacial contact between the ETL and perovskite layer, thereby affecting the internal resistance of the perovskite devices. Therefore, the smooth surface morphology of the ethanol- $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  film is crucial to achieve high-performance PSCs. Furthermore, the water contact angles of the various types of  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  films, which reveal the difference in the wettability of the dripped solution, have also been investigated in Fig. 3(d-f). The lowest wettability with a water contact angle of  $ 35^{\circ} $  was obtained from the ethanol- $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  sample, owing to its uniform grain size and surface topography. On the other hand, the rough surface of the DMF- $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  sample resulted in an increased hydrophobicity with a water contact angle of  $ 56^{\circ} $ , which is higher than that of the control group ( $ 45^{\circ} $ ) (Liu et al., 2019a; Xin et al., 2019). Therefore, the enhanced wettability of

the ETL deposited with ethanol-  $  SnO_{2}  $  favors the formation of the perovskite layer. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 3(g) and 3(h), the perovskite deposited on top of the ethanol-  $  SnO_{2}  $  layer exhibits an enlarged grain size (average size: 400 nm) compared with the control group (average size: 300 nm), which is due to the enhanced wettability of the perovskite precursor on the ETL that contributes to the formation of the perovskite film with higher quality and fewer defects. However, the DMF-  $  SnO_{2}  $  film, which exhibits poor wettability for the perovskite precursor, forms an undesirable perovskite film with reduced quality, irregular grain size and random defects. The variations in the perovskite crystallinity was then characterized by XRD measurements. As shown in Fig. S5, there were two characteristic diffraction peaks at around 14.2° and 28.4° attributed to (110) and (220), respectively (Roldán-Carmona et al., 2015). The perovskite film deposited on the ethanol-  $  SnO_{2}  $  film exhibited the highest peaks, indicating a pure phase and improved crystallinity. Meanwhile, the DMF-  $  SnO_{2}  $  based samples exhibited declining diffraction peaks compared with the control group, confirming the reduced crystal quality.

To probe the electronic properties of the perovskite device fabricated using different types of   $  SnO_{2}  $   layers, the J-V characteristics of the PSCs were investigated. As shown in Fig. 4(a), the open circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ), short circuit current (  $  J_{sc}  $  ), and fill factor (FF) values of the PSC based on the ethanol-  $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL are 1.11 V, 21.76 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   and 0.78, respectively; moreover, the PSC exhibited an enhanced PCE of 18.84%, which is higher than that of the control group (16.35%)—an improvement by 15.2%. The improved PCE could be due to several reasons, including enhanced perovskite crystallinity, increased ETL conductivity and improved interfacial contact. However, the application of the DMF-  $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL reduced the photovoltaic performance resulting in PCE,   $  V_{oc}  $  ,   $  J_{sc}  $   and FF values of 15.27%, 1.08 V, 20.79 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   and 0.68, respectively; the reduced crystallinity and existing defects (pinholes) might explain the decreased PCE (Song et al., 2017). To explore the changes in the photoelectric properties, the PL spectra were examined based on the structure of the glass /ETL (ethanol-  $  SnO_{2}  $  or  $  H_{2}O-SnO_{2}  $  ) /perovskite (Fig. 5(a)). Samples with both types of  $  SnO_{2}  $  ETLs exhibited an obvious quenching effect compared with the samples without ETL, suggesting the effective charge transport property of the  $  SnO_{2}  $  film (Zhu et al., 2019). However, the ethanol-  $  SnO_{2}  $  based samples exhibited a slightly higher signal than the  $  H_{2}O-SnO_{2}  $  based samples, at  $  \sim770~nm  $ ; this is because of the better crystal quality of the perovskite film in the former. Notably, although the ETL formed by the ethanol-  $  SnO_{2}  $  exhibited a better charge transport property, the resulting perovskite film with higher crystallinity can generate more excitons (Wu et al.,

2017; Xin et al., 2019). Therefore, we speculate that the higher quantity of the generated excitons counteract quicker electron transfer, resulting in a slightly higher PL signal (Xu et al., 2018b).

The long-term stability of the PSCs, especially moisture tolerance in ambient air, is crucial for practical applications. Therefore, the stability of the PSCs was tested by continually measuring their PCE in ambient air (about 30% humidity) at 25 °C without encapsulation for 300 h, and the results were analyzed using statistical methods. As shown in the point plot of the normalized PCE degradation with respect to time (Fig. 5(b)), the devices based on the ethanol-  $  SnO_{2}  $  layer exhibited an enhanced environmental stability, maintaining ~92% of the initial PCE (18.19%), which is higher than that of the control group (88%); this is because of the uniform morphology of the ETL formed by ethanol-  $  SnO_{2}  $ , which provides a more compact interfacial contact that can effectively block the moisture from the ambient air. Moreover, the improved perovskite quality can also retard the device degradation rate. In contrast, a series of random aggregations in the ETL formed by  $  H_{2}O-SnO_{2}  $  results in poor interface contact with the perovskite layer, which reduces the moisture resistance. Meanwhile, the lower quality of the perovskite formed by  $  H_{2}O-SnO_{2}  $  involves more defects, which not only lead to more degradation centers, but also accelerate the degradation rate of the PSCs (Liu et al., 2019a; Xiong et al., 2018).
