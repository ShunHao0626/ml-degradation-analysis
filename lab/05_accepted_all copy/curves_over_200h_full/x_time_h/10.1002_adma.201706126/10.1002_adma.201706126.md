# Stable and Efficient Organo-Metal Halide Hybrid Perovskite Solar Cells via   $  \pi  $  -Conjugated Lewis Base Polymer Induced Trap Passivation and Charge Extraction

Ping-Li Qin, Guang Yang, Zhi-wei Ren, Sin Hang Cheung, Shu Kong So, Li Chen, Jianhua Hao, Jianhui Hou, and Gang Li*

High-quality pinhole-free perovskite film with optimal crystalline morphology is critical for achieving high-efficiency and high-stability perovskite solar cells (PSCs). In this study, a p-type  $ \pi $ -conjugated polymer poly[(2,6-(4,8-bis(5-(2-ethylhexyl) thiophen-2-yl)-benzo[1,2-b:4,5-b'] dithiophene)]-alt-(5,5-(1',3'-di-2-thienyl-5',7'-bis(2-ethylhexyl) benzo[1',2'-c:4',5'-c'] dithiophene-4,8-dione))] (PBDB-T) is introduced into chlorobenzene to form a facile and effective template-agent during the anti-solvent process of perovskite film formation. The  $ \pi $ -conjugated polymer PBDB-T is found to trigger a heterogeneous nucleation over the perovskite precursor film and passivate the trap states of the mixed perovskite film through the formation of Lewis adducts between lead and oxygen atom in PBDB-T. The p-type semiconducting and hydrophobic PBDB-T polymer fills in the perovskite grain boundaries to improve charge transfer for better conductivity and prevent moisture invasion into the perovskite active layers. Consequently, the PSCs with PBDB-T modified anti-solvent processing leads to a high-efficiency close to  $ 20\% $ , and the devices show excellent stability, retaining about  $ 90\% $  of the initial power conversion efficiency after 150 d storage in dry air.

Most commonly in the lab, the perovskite crystalline film is formed through solvent evaporation with high-speed spin coating the precursors onto the substrate. The fast solvent evaporation and rapid reaction of the lead source (e.g., lead (II) iodide   $  [PbI_{2}]  $  ) with halogenated amine (e.g.,   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $  ) make it hard to control the nucleation and growth of the perovskite crystal, inducing pinholes within the final perovskite film, and leading to the low coverage of the substrate with the poor morphology perovskite film.  $  ^{[19]}  $

In order to obtain high-quality perovskite, it is needed to slow down the crystal growth and induce rapid nucleation.  $  ^{[20,21]}  $   To retard perovskite crystallization, additives, such as dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 1, 8-diiodooctane, methylammonium acetate, and thiosemicarbazide have been employed to form intermediate adducts with   $  Pb^{2+}  $  .  $  ^{[22]}  $   To improve the nucleation process, anti-solvent pipetting has been proven to be an effective method to trigger a homogeneous nucleation at the surface of the perovskite layer.  $  ^{[20,21]}  $   However, no matter which preparation process or method is adopted, it is not possible to completely remove the GBs in perovskite film, and interface/GB trap passivation is still a significant issue. This measurement helps to reveal the relationship between traps and charge transfer around the GBs with foreign body doping (here Lewis base conjugated polymer). Although the individual grains are clearly distinguishable, the contrasts are not as much like in the morphology. With  $  C_{PBDB-T}  $  increasing further, much more PBDB-T film covers perovskite crystal to weaken the Pb—O stretching. Then the peak at 143 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  is leveled off. Therefore, it can be confirmed PBDB-T interacts with the Lewis acid  $  PbI_{2}  $  to form an intermediate PBDB-T–PbI  $  _{2}  $  adduct (Figure 4e), to weaken XRD peak of  $  PbI_{2}  $  (Figure 2c), which can retard the crystal growth and improve the crystallinity of the perovskite film. And the network structure PBDB-T provides much more channel for hole-transferring, which is benefit for suppressing the carrier trap recombination.

On investigation of device stability, we first conducted two types of study on the thermal stability of unencapsulated PSCs annealing at 85 °C in: (i) a glovebox (with oxygen and water less than 1 ppm) and (ii) ambient air (with ≈70% humidity), respectively. The J-V measurements were carried out under ambient conditions with ≈70% humidity at RT, as shown in Figure S9a,b in the Supporting Information. For the average PCEs of the devices, all of the PSCs present a decay with 85 °C annealing. In the glovebox case, the devices with PBDB-T decay to 80% of their initial PCEs at ca. 125 h, which is about twice as long as that for the control devices. The result indicates that the polymer PBDB-T possesses the function to suppress halide migration within the perovskite or lithium salts migrating though the perovskite layer, and thus better stability.[45,46] When annealed in ambient air with ≈70% humidity, the PBDB-T-coverage-based devices decay to 70% of their initial PCEs in ≈230 h, while the control devices decay to the same level in ≈74 h, i.e., the  $  T_{70}  $  lifetime under 85 °C thermal stress and 70% RH was improved by a factor of 3. The polymer-induced stability enhancements are further confirmed by the IPCE test (Figure S10a–f, Supporting Information). When the control and the PBDB-T-based unencapsulated devices were annealed at 85 °C in a glove box, they both showed a decrease in IPCE. However, the IPCE for the control devices decreased much more (≈85% to 65% peak value) than that for the PBDB-T based devices (85% to 75% peak value) after 264 h annealing. When the unencapsulated devices were annealed at 85 °C in ambient air with ≈70% humidity, i.e., under both thermal stress and humidity, the control devices decreased faster (to ≈60% after 264 h) than under glovebox annealing. With PBDB-T coverage, the decrease becomes much smaller, even in ambient annealing, retaining 70% IPCE after 264 h annealing. This clearly shows that a small amount of π-conjugated polymer PBDB-T can effectively suppress the reaction between moisture (or oxygen) and perovskite. For solar cells, photodegradation is another key parameter to investigate. In order to eliminate the effect of moisture, the encapsulated devices are put under a 150 W white-light-emitting diode (wLED) floodlight for testing, with 0.8 sun illumination (evaluated by a standard KG5 Si diode). The long-term PCE test result under continuous illumination is shown in Figure S9c in the Supporting Information, which shows that the average PCEs of these PSCs decay dramatically with white-light illumination, much faster than thermal-only (85 °C) degradation. The photostability of the PSCs clearly should be a future study focus, which needs to be further solved. The good news is that the devices with polymer passivation have significantly better lifetime. The control PSCs have average  $  T_{50}  $  (50% PCE decay) of 5 h, while the PBDB-T-based devices retain about 50% of their initial PCEs in 30 h. The  $  T_{40}  $  illuminated lifetime was significantly improved from ≈6 h to ≈270 h, a factor of 45× enhancement. Figure S11 in the Supporting Information shows the IPCE decay of the control and polymer-coated PSC devices. Figure S11a,b (Supporting Information) show the absolute IPCE measurement result, and photodegradation is clearly more harmful to the devices than thermal stress. The maximum of IPCE for the control device decreases from ≈80 to 10% with the wLED light soaking of 264 h (Figure S11a, Supporting Information), but, encouragingly, the IPCE spectra of the PBDB-T-based devices shows that a much slower decay is observed, and the maximum of the IPCE decreases from ≈80 to 40% for the same test period (Figure S11b, Supporting Information). The IPCE magnitude is not the whole story: from the IPCE data normalized at 100 nm (Figure S11c,d, Supporting Information), the control devices have significantly long wavelength (from ≈450 to 800 nm), but the PBDB-T-based devices have very little shape change in the long-wavelength region. This clearly shows the effectiveness of polymer PBDB-T coverage on perovskite stability. Clearly the PBDB-T coverage on the perovskite GB plays a significant role in stability enhancement, suppressing halide migration within the perovskite. Since the white-light-emitting diode floodlight has no UV component, we think that the decay trend comes from the interface between the perovskite and the hole-transfer-material layer.

It was recently reported that photon illumination may lead to decomposition of hybrid organo-metal halide perovskite, which may be transferred to and react with the gold anode;  $  ^{[48]}  $  or with the presence of the residual water in the devices, the illumination promotes the instability of spiro-OMeTAD. With the polymer PBDB-T coverage on the perovskite film, particularly the weak GBs, halide migration within the perovskite or lithium salts migrating through the perovskite layer could be suppressed; as such, the PBDB-T-based devices will be much more stable than the control devices under light illumination.

In summary, we introduce a facile and efficient anti-solvent engineering approach with small amount of p-type conjugated polymer PBDB-T dissolved in CB as anti-solvent during the process of perovskite film formation. Supported by SEM, EFM, FTIR, PL, FTIR, Raman, TRPL, and PDS techniques, we found that PBDB-T can trigger heterogeneous nucleation over the perovskite precursor film to improve film quality. # Experimental Section

Materials: FTO glass substrates with a sheet resistance of 14 Ω sq  $  ^{-1}  $  were supplied from Asahi Glass (Japan). MABr and FAI were purchased from Dyesol. PbBr  $  _{2}  $  (99.99%), CsI (99.99%), bis (trifluoromethane) sulfonimide lithium salt (Li-TFSI, 99%), and 4-tert-buty|pyridine (tBP, 96%) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. Tin(II) chloride dehydrate(SnCl  $  _{2}  $  ·2H  $  _{2}  $  O), thiourea, DMSO, DMF, acetonitrile, and CB were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. PbI  $  _{2}  $  (99.8%) were purchased from TCI while spiro-OMeTAD (purity. 99.5%) was purchased from Feiming Science and Technology Co., Ltd. All chemicals were used as received.

Devices Fabrications: The PSCs were prepared on FTO substrates with cleaned in detergent and then successively sonicated in deionized water, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol solution, then dried in an oven at 80 °C. Prior to the deposition of ETLs, FTO substrates were exposed to UV–ozone for 20 min. The SnO  $  _{2}  $  colloid precursor, obtained from Alfa Aesar (tin(IV) oxide, 15% in H  $  _{2}  $  O colloidal dispersion), was spin coated onto FTO substrates with a spin rate of 3000 rpm for 30 s as ETLs, and then were annealed in air at 200 °C for 1 h.

Perovskite photoactive layers were deposited on ETLs by an anti-solvent method described in the literature.  $  ^{[19,49]}  $   The   $  (\mathrm{CsPb}_{3})_{0.04}(\mathrm{FAPbI}_{3})_{0.82}(\mathrm{MAPbBr}_{3})_{0.14}  $   precursor solution was prepared in a glove-box from 1.35 M   $  Pb^{2+}(PbI_{2}  $   and   $  PbBr_{2})  $   in a mixed solvent of DMF and DMSO(4:1, v/v). The dissolved solution was then spin coated on FTO/  $  SnO_{2}  $   NP ETLs substrate at 4000 rpm for 30 s in a glove box; note that 150   $  \mu  $  L PBDB-T chlorobenzene solution with different concentrations (0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, and 5.0 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was pipetted onto the spinning film at 15 s before the end of this program. Thereafter, the as-cast perovskite films were annealed at 150 °C for 10 min. After cooling down to room temperature, spiro-OMeTAD solution (75 mg solved in 1 mL chlorobenzene) with 29   $  \mu  $  L of tBP and 17.5   $  \mu  $  L of Li-TFSI (520 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   in acetonitrile) was spin coated on the perovskite layer at 4000 rpm for 20 s in a glove box. Finally, 70–90 nm of gold was deposited by thermal evaporation on top of spiro-OMeTAD layer to complete the device, using a shadow mask to pattern the electrodes. The active area of the cells was 0.09 cm  $  ^{2}  $  , which was defined by the area of the Au electrode.

Characterization: The J–V characteristics of the devices were measured using a B1500 A semiconductor parameter analyzer under the calibrated ABET Technologies SUN 2000 solar simulator equipped with an AM 1.5 filter at   $  100 \, mW cm^{-2}  $  .

Scanning was done at a scan rate of   $  0.1 \, V s^{-1}  $  . And the scans started and finished under forward bias and had 2 s stabilization time at forward bias under illumination prior to scanning.

The corresponding IPCE spectrum was measured in air by a QE-R 3011 system from Enli Technology Co. Ltd. (Enli).

UV–vis spectroscopy was performed by using a UV–vis–NIR spectrophotometer (UV-2550 Shimadzu) at room temperature.

XRD patterns were determined by using a Rigaku SmartLab X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation.

The morphologies of PSCs were investigated by a high-resolution field emission SEM (JSM7100F).

Based on a tapping-mode AFM (Bruker NanoScope MultiMode 8) system with Pt/Ir coated Si tips with a quoted force constant of

0.4 N m  $  ^{-1}  $  , the EFM measures simultaneously CPD between the probe (Nanosenser PPP-EFM) and surface of a perovskite sample by constant probing. In the EFM measurement, a two-pass technique with phase mode was employed. The first pass was used to acquire the topographic height, and then the conductive probe was lifted with respect to the specimen surface with a constant separation, =10 nm here, and scanned to acquire the potential offset between the tip and the sample. The first resonant oscillation of the cantilever (52.590 kHz) was used for the noncontact AFM topographic imaging. The second resonant frequency (60 kHz) was used for the potential imaging. A scan rate of 0.977 Hz was chosen, and a DC bias of 1 V applied to the conductive probe. The sample was fixed in a custom-made vertical sample holder with electrical connections to both electrodes.

FTIR spectrum was recorded with a reflectance (ATR) instrument (PerkinElmer Spectrum 100, USA) from 4000 to 800 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  with a resolution of 2 cm  $  ^{-1}  $ .

Raman spectra were obtained by a Horiba HR800 Raman spectroscopy system with an external liner polarized diode pumped solid state laser of  $ 488~\mathrm{nm} $  and the resolution of  $ 0.6~\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $ .

The contact angle was measured using a standard automated contact angle goniometer (Rame-Hart, Model 200-F1) with deionized water.

For PDS measurement, the perovskite film was deposited on quartz substrate and then immersed into FC-72. A monochromatic light beam was modulated at  $ 13\mathrm{Hz} $  by a mechanical chopper and shined onto the sample. A laser was at the perpendicular side so that it was deflected periodically. A position detector, connected with a lock-in amplifier, was placed on the other side so that the deflection signal was measured.

PL spectrum and TRPL signals of perovskite film were recorded by using Edinburgh FLSP920 spectrophotometer equipped with the excitation source of 485 nm picosecond pulsed diode laser.
