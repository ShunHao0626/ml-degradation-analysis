# Polyethylene Glycol Polymer Scaffold Induced Intermolecular Interactions for Crystallization Regulation and Defect Passivation in FASnI  $  _{3}  $  Films

Bohong Chang, Bo Li, Lu Pan, Hui Li, Lian Wang, Lin Fu, Zihao Li, and Longwei Yin*

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Tin (Sn)-based perovskites as promising alternatives of Pb analogues have attracted increased attention for their ideal optical bandgap, low toxicity, and high carrier mobility but are still subject to an uncontrollable crystallization process and Sn(II)-induced serious defects and thus poor photovoltaic performance and unsatisfactory stability. Herein, we propose a strategy to construct a fluid flexible polymer scaffold by introducing an eco-friendly polyethylene glycol (PEG) polymer with plenty of ether bond groups (C–O–C) in an FASnI  $  _{3}  $  precursor to regulate the nucleation and growth of perovskite grains and fabricate uniform and full coverage perovskite films with lower defect density. We demonstrate that the hydrogen-bond interactions between FA  $  ^{+}  $  and C–O–C and Lewis acid–base complexation between uncoordinated Sn and C–O–C groups can effectively regulate film crystallization behavior, improve perovskite film coverage, and reduce defect state density. Importantly, PEG as a fluid

flexible polymer scaffold during the annealing process can cross-link the adjacent perovskite grains and relieve the stress between grains. With this method, we obtain high-quality PEG-modified FASnI  $  _{3}  $  perovskite films with larger grain size, higher coverage without pinholes, lower trap state density, and higher carrier lifetime. The optimized device achieves an optimal PCE of 7.53% under reverse scan and the unencapsulated device maintains more than 90% of the initial PCE after aging for 720 h in a N  $  _{2}  $  glovebox.

KEYWORDS: FASnI $ _{3} $ , polyethylene glycol, Lewis acid–base complexation, Sn(II) induced defects, photovoltaic

# INTRODUCTION

Organic–inorganic lead hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted increased attention due to their excellent photoelectric performance.  $  ^{1-3}  $   The inspiring breakthrough improvement of PCE from initial 3.8%  $  ^{4}  $   to more than 25%  $  ^{5}  $   in just ten-plus years indicates that halide perovskite is one of the most promising photovoltaic materials with potential applications in photovoltaic industry, whereas the toxicity of heavy metal Pb is the main obstacle for their commercial large-scale application.  $  ^{6}  $   Exploring environmentally friend elements to replace Pb is an effective route to solve the toxicity issue.  $  ^{7}  $   Previous studies have shown that low-toxicity element Sn is the closest same group element as Pb in the periodic table.  $  ^{8,9}  $   Compared with Pb analogues, Sn-based perovskites exhibit some more favorable photoelectrical properties such as narrower optical bandgaps for better near-infrared light absorption to get higher short-circuit current density of PSCs,  $  ^{10}  $   small exciton binding energies, and high charge carrier mobility in favor of the generation and transport of photogenerated carriers.  $  ^{11}  $   However, the easy oxidation of Sn  $  ^{2+}  $   to Sn  $  ^{4+}  $   and low formation energy of Sn vacancies (V  $  _{Sn}  $  ) lead to unwanted p-doping in final perovskite films, increasing the concentration of trap state and increasing the carrier

# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

We first investigated the effects of PEG on perovskite colloidal particle formation in a precursor solution by a dynamic laser scattering (DLS) technique. Using 99% purity   $  SnI_{2}  $   raw material, we adopted the tin source purification strategy to prepare a bright yellow   $  FASnI_{3}  $   precursor solution (Figure S1), which could reduce the content of unwanted   $  Sn^{4+}  $  , forming a Sn-rich environment to suppress the generation of   $  V_{Sn}  $   and enhance the film quality.  $  ^{34}  $   The schematic illustration of the

hydrogen-bond interactions and Lewis acid base complexation interactions between flexible PEG chains and FASnI $ _3 $  perovskite is depicted in Figure 1a. Large colloidal particle size is beneficial to increasing perovskite grain size in final films, but the wider size distribution of colloidal particles tends to result in the uneven grain size in the final films. $ ^{35} $  We speculate that small colloidal particles are coated by PEG and gradually merge with each other to grow into large colloidal particles, and the above conjectures indicate that there are some interaction between perovskite colloidal particles and PEG polymers (Figure S2). Using the purified precursor solution, we prepared   $  FASnI_{3}  $   perovskite films with different content of PEG by a simple one-step solution method with chlorobenzene as antisolvent and studied the influence of additive content on the film morphology by a scanning electron microscope (SEM). As shown in Figure 2a–d, the introduction of PEG obviously makes an obvious effect on the morphology and quality of   $  FASnI_{3}  $   films and appropriate addition can noticeably increase the grain size. In addition, when the amount of PEG added is less than 5 mg/mL, the average grain size has a roughly linear relationship with the average colloidal particle size (Figure S6). The pristine film exhibits poor morphology with obvious pinholes, blurred grain boundaries, and raised grains above the surface. A small amount of PEG (2 mg/mL) makes the grain boundary clearer and reduces the bumps of the surface grains. With the optimal PEG content (5 mg/mL), dense and flat films with larger grain size, lower surface roughness, and nonpinholes are obtained. However, excess PEG (10 mg/mL) causes the grains to separate from each other and the insulating PEG seems to be filled at the grain boundaries to cut the connection between the grains, which leads to pinholes at the junction of multiple grains. As is known, the pinholes will make the electron and hole transport layers contact with each other, which will lead to large leakage current and poor device performance.  $  ^{39}  $   The above SEM results indicate that appropriate amount of PEG helps to obtain high coverage and flat   $  FASnI_{3}  $   films and effectively solve the problem of poor film formation in tin-based perovskite. Moreover, the crystal structures of the   $  FASnI_{3}  $   perovskite films with different content of PEG have been characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD). As shown in Figure 2e, the positions of diffraction peaks at 14.02, 24.42, 28.14, 31.66, 40.38, and 42.90° correspond to (100), (102), (200), (122), (222), and (213) crystal faces of orthorhombic structure (Amm2), respectively.  $  ^{40}  $   The position of the diffraction peaks of the crystal plane does not change, which means the introduction of PEG has no effect on the crystal structure. As shown in Figure S7, the right amount of PEG helps to obtain higher peak intensity and narrower full width at half-maximum (fwhm) in (100), which indicates improved film crystallinity and optimized grain orientation of (h00) planes. However, superfluous PEG decreases the film crystallinity because of its amorphous property.

To further study the interactions between   $  FASnI_{3}  $   perovskite and PEG additive, we carried out FTIR measurement to reveal that except for hydrogen-bond interactions between   $  FA^{+}  $  and PEG, there is also Lewis acid–base interaction between   $  SnI_{2}  $   and PEG in solid films (Figure 2f). In the FAI/PEG mixed system, the hydrogen-bond interactions can induce a homogeneous electron cloud distribution of the corresponding groups, which reduces the stretching vibration frequency of C–O–C from 1103 to 1081 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  . In the   $  SnI_{2}/PEG  $   mixed system,   $  SnI_{2}  $   as a typical Lewis acid can form adducts with electron-donating oxygen atoms in PEG through weak chemical interaction, which reduces the force constant of C–O–C and redshifts the C–O–C stretching vibration frequency to 1084 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  . Previous studies have shown that the Lewis acid–base complexation between uncoordinated Sn and Lewis base can passivate trap states, which is beneficial to reducing nonradiation recombination and enhancing photovoltaic performance.  $  ^{41}  $   To characterize the position of PEG on the surface of perovskite film, we carried out nanoinfrared spectroscopy analysis based on photothermal induced resonance (PTIR) technology, and the PEG-modified

FASnI  $  _{3}  $  films were deposited on silicon substrates with flat and dense morphology (Figure S8). The atomic force microscope (AFM) was used to observe the surface topography of PEG-modified FASnI  $  _{3}  $  film and an adjustable pulse laser acted as infrared light source. The nanoscale two-dimensional infrared spectra was obtained by the AFM probe to detect the radiation absorption of perovskite films. The contact IR amplitude spectrum, AFM topography map, and PTIR map detected at 1462 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  corresponding to the shear vibration absorption peak of methylene group (CH  $  _{2}  $  ) in PEG are shown in Figure 2g–i. Comparing Figure 2h with Figure 2i, it is found that the PEG polymer mainly distributes at grain boundaries and on the surface of some sunken grains, which helps to passivate defects at corresponding locations. The reason for the absence of PEG on the surface of most grains is the extraction effect of the antisolvent on PEG, which is favorable for the charge carrier transport from perovskite layer to electron transport layer. Therefore, we can speculate that most PEG distributes at the grain boundaries of polycrystalline films and acts as a flexible polymer scaffold to modulate crystallization and passivate defects, which has been proven in Pb-based perovskites.  $  ^{33}  $

To further study the evolution process from colloidal particles in the precursor solution to final solid films, we obtained the SEM images of FASnI $ _3 $  films with and without PEG (Figure S9 and Figure 3a–d) at different annealing time (0, 30, 80, and 180 s), the remained solvent in the film was removed by volatilization at room temperature. The morphology evolution of these two films with annealing time increasing is broadly similar, and both have gone through the process of perovskite aggregation, grain growth and pinhole reduction, but the morphologies of the final films vary greatly. $ ^{42} $  In our opinion, the following two factors may be responsible for the poor film morphology. One is that the size and distribution of grains after dropping antisolvent can affect the interconnections between grains during the annealing process, and the other is that the stress of adjacent grains leads to uneven films with raised grains. Comparing the two unannealed films (0 s), the grains of the former are very small and scattered, which makes it difficult to eliminate pinholes by a single annealing process. Differently, the latter shows bigger grains due to the larger colloidal particles in precursor solution, which makes grains connect with each other easily in the subsequent annealing process. From Figure S9, it is shown that there are still many pinholes in the pristine film annealed for 180 s. It is hard for newly formed grains to fill the pinholes and the growth-restricted grains have to grow out of the surface to form bumps. Fortunately, the introduction of PEG (Mn = 10 000) with the viscous flow temperature of about 61  $ ^{\circ} $ C effectively eliminates the above issues by the hydrogen bond and Lewis acid–base interactions between flexible PEG chains and perovskite grains. At the annealing temperature of 70  $ ^{\circ} $ C, the PEG polymer chains are still in fluid state after the evaporation of most solvents and act as flexible polymer scaffold and buffer zone at grain boundaries, $ ^{33} $  alleviating the contact stress between adjacent grains and eliminating the raised grains as well as the pinholes. Through the above analysis on the crystallization process, we can establish the relationship between the precursor solution, the crystallization process, and the final film morphology. The enlarged colloidal particle size will increase the grain size of the unannealed film, which is conducive to improving the film coverage. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to study the surface composition of   $  FASnI_{3}  $   films with and without PEG.  $  ^{43}  $   As the PEG additive contains only C and O elements that can be detected, the type and position of peaks in XPS full spectrum (Figure S10) do not significantly change after PEG is added. The light absorption capacity, photogenerated carrier dynamics, and trap state density of the films are crucial standards to evaluate film quality. To alleviate the unwanted oxidation of the perovskite films during the following optical tests, we fabricated perovskite films on the quartz substrate and coated the film surface with poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) thin layer. In the UV–vis–NIR spectroscopy measurement results (Figure 4a), the absorption band edge of the FASnI  $  _{3}  $  film is located near 880 nm, and the calculation

semiconductor bandgap is about 1.4 eV, similar to previous studies (Figure S12), close to the optimal optical bandgap of 1.35 eV.  $  ^{46}  $   The PEG additive does not change the absorption band edge but helps to enhance light absorption especially in the visible light range, which is ascribed to the enlarged grain size enhancing the light capture by improving light scattering.  $  ^{47}  $   In addition, the steady-state photoluminescence (SSPL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) decay tests are employed to study the charge carrier recombination dynamics. As shown in Figure 4b, the PEG-modified FASnI  $  _{3}  $   film exhibits higher PL intensity than pristine films, indicating suppressed nonradiative recombination due to enhanced film quality with fewer carrier capture traps. Moreover, the energy level diagram of PEG and perovskite (Figure S13) indicates that PEG additive does not introduce new nonradiative recombination sites.  $  ^{48}  $   The TRPL results are fitted by a double exponent in Figure 4c, and the film PL lifetime increases from 0.74 to 1.9 ns after adding 5 mg/mL PEG. The extending carrier lifetime is attributed to the decrease of the carrier recombination traps inside the film and on the surface of grains. The PEG additive helps to regulate crystallization and slow down crystalline rate, which can reduce the trap states inside the films and the Lewis acid–base complexation between C–O–C with uncoordinated tin atoms will passivate the surface defects of perovskite grains. To further confirm the reduced carrier recombination in PEG-modified devices, we characterized the electrical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) of pristine and PEG-modified devices with a bias voltage of 0.3 V under dark conditions.  $  ^{49}  $   As shown in Figure S14, The semicircular curves represent the charge recombination resistance (  $  R_{rec}  $  ) and large   $  R_{rec}  $   indicates the more difficulties for carrier recombination. The PEG-modified device exhibiting a larger   $  R_{rec}  $   demonstrates that the PEG helps to passivate defects and decrease carrier recombination, which is favorable for higher   $  V_{oc}  $  . To further understand the effect of PEG-induced defect passivation on the carrier recombination mechanisms, the light-intensity-dependent   $  J_{sc}-V_{oc}  $   characteristics were carried out. As shown in the following equation,   $  V_{oc}  $   has a linear relationship with the natural logarithmic of light intensity (ln I).

$$
\frac {\partial \mathrm{V} _ {\mathrm{oc}}}{\partial (\ln \mathrm{I})} = \frac {n k T}{q}
$$

(2)

Where n is the ideality factor, k is the Boltzmann constant, T is the absolute temperature of test environment and q is the elemental charge. The closer the value of n is to 1, the more it tends to be a trap-free relationship.  $  ^{54}  $   As shown in Figure 4e, the PEG-modified device presents a lower value of n (n = 1.29) than that of pristine device (n = 1.61), which indicates that the effect of PEG-induced defect passivation helps to suppress the carrier trap-assisted recombination. Figure 4f demonstrates the linear relationship between the incident light intensity and   $  J_{sc}  $   based on the following direct ratio relationship.

$$
J _ {\mathrm{sc}} \propto I ^ {\alpha}
$$

(3)

Where   $  \alpha  $   represents an ideal exponential factor in relation to bimolecular radiative recombination and the value of   $  \alpha  $   close to 1 indicates weakened space-charge effects.  $  ^{55,56}  $   The PEG-modified device shows the   $  \alpha  $   value of 0.958 higher than that of the control device (  $  \alpha = 0.891  $  ), which proves that the introduction of PEG to decrease the defect states of perovskite films helps to suppress bimolecular recombination and reduce the space charge effect.

On the basis of the high-quality PEG-modified FASnI $ _3 $  perovskite films with enhanced crystallinity, higher coverage and lower defect density, we fabricated p-i-n structure devices with the architecture of ITO/PEDOT: PSS/perovskite/C $ _{60} $ /BCP/Ag (Figure 5a). Figure 5b shows the optimal  $ J-V $  curves of the FASnI $ _3 $  devices with different PEG content under AM 1.5 G illumination test condition. The introduction of PEG obviously helps to improve the device  $ V_{oc} $  from 0.381 to 0.525 V, which is the major factor for PCE improvement. As shown in Table S3, under reverse scan measurement, the pristine device only shows an optimal PCE of 3.90%, a  $ J_{sc} $  of 18.0 mA/cm $ ^2 $ , a  $ V_{oc} $  of 0.381 V, and an FF of 56.9%. By contrast, the optimal device with PEG additive exhibits a PCE up to 7.53% with a high  $ V_{oc} $  of 0.525 V, a  $ J_{sc} $  of 21.8 mA/cm $ ^2 $ , and a FF of 65.8% under the same test conditions. The enhanced photovoltaic performance is mainly attributed to the flat and full coverage films, higher crystallinity, longer carrier lifetime, and lower defect density. But the  $ J-V $  curve of device with 10 mg/mL PEG addition shows significantly reduced  $ J_{sc} $ , mainly due to the poor film morphology with separated grains caused by excess PEG and the insulating property of PEG impeding carrier transmission. In addition, as shown in Figure 5c and Table S4, the hysteresis phenomenon in the  $ J-V $  curve of PEG-modified FASnI $ _3 $  device is greatly improved than the control device, profiting from the reduced trap states and suppressed carrier recombination rate. $ ^{57} $  In the external

quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra (Figure 5d), the PEG-modified FASnI $ _3 $  device exhibits higher photon utilization and the integrated current density increases from 17.9 mA/cm $ ^2 $  of optimal control device to 21.8 mA/cm $ ^2 $ , which is basically consistent with the  $ J_{sc} $  in corresponding  $ J-V $  curves. To evaluate the reproducibility of device performance, we fabricated a batch of PSCs including pristine devices and PEG-modified FASnI $ _3 $  (5 mg/mL) devices under the same preparation conditions. As shown in Figure S15, the PCE distribution of control devices varied from 1.65 to 3.90% with an average value of 2.85%. In contrast, the PCE distribution of PEG-modified FASnI $ _3 $  devices locates in the region from 5.62 to 7.53% with an average value of 6.45% and the narrower PCE distribution indicates the improvement of device reproducibility. Finally, we characterized the storage stability of the unencapsulated device inside glovebox full of N $ _2 $  with both oxygen and water less than 0.1 ppm and in air conditions with a relative humidity (RH) of 50%, respectively. Figure 5e shows the efficiency change of pristine and PEG-modified FASnI $ _3 $  device in 720 h. The PCE of the control device decreases by 35% after aging for 720 h, whereas the PEG-modified FASnI $ _3 $  device maintains more than 90% of the initial PCE under the same storage conditions. As shown in Figure 5f, the pristine device lost efficiency within 18 h and the PEG-modified FASnI $ _3 $  device maintained about 60% of the original PCE after 48 h in air conditions. Because of the oxidation of Sn $ ^{2+} $  and the influence of moisture, the device efficiency decreases faster in air than inside the glovebox. Significantly, the PEG-modified devices have higher antioxidant capacity and moisture stability.

# CONCLUSIONS

In summary, we have demonstrated that flexible PEG additive can expand the colloidal particle size, enlarge the unannealed grain size, and regulate crystal growth to form uniform and pinhole-free   $  FASnI_{3}  $   films. The PEG-modified   $  FASnI_{3}  $   films exhibit better morphology, higher antioxidant stability, lower defects, increased carrier lifetime, and reduced nonradiative recombination. In this way, the PEG-modified   $  FASnI_{3}  $   devices achieve an optimal PCE of 7.53% with a   $  V_{oc}  $   of 0.525 V, a   $  J_{sc}  $   of 21.8 mA/cm², and an FF of 65.8% under reverse scan with enhanced reproducibility, and the unencapsulated devices retain greater than 90% of initial PCE after 720 h of storage in a   $  N_{2}  $   glovebox. It is found that the colloidal particle size in   $  FASnI_{3}  $   precursor solution has an important influence on the final film morphology and the fluid flexible PEG scaffold can regulate the crystallization behavior of   $  FASnI_{3}  $   films and passivate film defects to form high-quality films by Lewis acid–base complexation and hydrogen-bond interactions. We demonstrate that the hydrogen-bond interactions between   $  FA^{+}  $  and C–O–C, and Lewis acid–base complexation between uncoordinated Sn and C–O–C groups can effectively regulate film crystallization behavior, improve perovskite film coverage, and reduce defect state density. Importantly, PEG as a fluid flexible polymer scaffold during the annealing process can cross-link the adjacent perovskite grains and relieve the stress between grains. Such a strategy paves the way for optimizing film quality and enhancing the photovoltaic performance of Sn-based perovskites.

Film Preparation. The preparation of perovskite films was performed in a   $  N_{2}  $   glovebox with both   $  O_{2}  $   and   $  H_{2}O  $   concentrations below 0.1 ppm. The unpurified   $  FASnI_{3}  $   precursor solution was obtained by adding FAI (1 mmol),   $  SnI_{2}  $   (1 mmol), and   $  SnF_{2}  $   (0.1 mmol) into DMSO (200   $  \mu  $  L) and DMF (800   $  \mu  $  L) mixed solvents and stirring it on a 50 °C heating plate until dissolved. Then, Sn powder (0.2 g) was added into the above solution and kept stirring and heating for about 6 h until the color completely changed from dark red to yellow. After that, removing excess tin powder and impurities by 0.22 um polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) filter to obtain light yellow   $  FASnI_{3}  $   precursor solution. Then, the purified solution was mixed with PEG (0 mg as the control group, 2 mg, 5 mg, and 10 mg) and stirred at 50 °C for 1 h. The perovskite precursor solution was spin-coated on the PEDOT:PSS substrates at 4000 rpm for 30 s and chlorobenzene (100   $  \mu  $  L) as antisolvent was dripped onto the perovskite film at the 18th second. Finally, the resulting films were annealed at 70 °C for 10 min. During the process of film deposition, a flow of nitrogen gas was used to remove volatile solvents in time.

Device Fabrication. The patterned ITO glass substrates were ultrasonically cleaned with abstergent, deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol for 15 min, respectively, and then dried with   $  N_{2}  $   flow, followed by ultraviolet ozone treatment for 30 min. PEDOT:PSS aqueous solution was spin-coated on the ITO substrates at 4000 rpm for 30 s in the air, followed by 150 °C annealing for 15 min to prepare the hole transport layer. Then, transfer the ITO/PEDOT:PSS substrates to the   $  N_{2}  $   glovebox for depositing FASnI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite films. Then   $  C_{60}  $   (30 nm), BCP (8 nm), and Ag (100 nm) layers were sequentially evaporated on the ITO/PEDOT:PSS/perovskite substrates and the thickness of each layer was precisely controlled by crystal oscillator during the vapor deposition process. The area of the Ag electrode was determined by a metal mask and the active area of the device is about 0.09 cm  $  ^{2}  $  .
