# Abstract

Photovoltaic devices based on organic semiconductors and organo-metal halide perovskites have not yet reached the theoretically predicted power conversion efficiencies while they still exhibit poor environmental stability. Interfacial engineering using suitable materials has been recognized as an attractive approach to tackle the above issues. We introduce here a zinc porphyrin-triazine-bodipy donor-  $ \pi $  bridge-acceptor dye as a universal electron transfer mediator in both organic and perovskite solar cells. Thanks to its "push-pull" character, this dye enhances electron transfer from the absorber layer towards the electron selective contact thus improving the device photocurrent and efficiency. The direct result is a more than  $ 10\% $  average power conversion efficiency enhancement in both fullerene (from 8.65 to  $ 9.80\% $ ) and non-fullerene (from 7.71 to  $ 8.73\% $ ) based organic solar cells as well as in perovskite ones (from 14.56 to  $ 15.67\% $ ), proving the universality of our approach. # 1. Introduction

Organic solar cells (OSCs) have been thoroughly investigated in recent years as a promising solar thin-film technology due to their ease of fabrication even at large areas using low-cost solution processing methods.  $  ^{1-3}  $   Since the first report of a bilayer device by Tang in 1986,  $  ^{4}  $   OSCs based on blends of conjugated polymer donors with fullerene acceptors have exhibited striking improvements in the power conversion efficiency (PCE) values from 1% to above 12%.  $  ^{5-7}  $   However, it was the recent emergence of non-fullerene acceptors (NFAs) that has reinvigorated the field of OSCs with their certified PCEs to reach values over 17.3% in a tandem architecture.  $  ^{8-12}  $   Organic-inorganic perovskite solar cell (PSC) technology on the other hand, with a certified PCE of 25.2%, promises even higher solar power at potentially low-cost. This mainly stems from the long carrier diffusion length being of the order of mm in perovskite films in combination with very high absorption coefficients and large carrier mobility values.  $  ^{13-17}  $   However, further advances in the device performance and, importantly, in their long-term stability require except for improvements in the photoactive material the development of alternative hole transport (HTL) and electron transport (ETL) layers to successfully engineer the corresponding charge selective interfaces.  $  ^{18,19}  $   These transport layers, together with the photoactive materials, have advanced the performance of OSCs and PSCs by improving charge transport and collection.  $  ^{20,21}  $

Benefiting from their high transparency, adequate n-type conductivity, low-toxicity and ease of fabrication, zinc oxide (ZnO),  $  ^{22-25}  $   and titanium dioxide (TiO  $  _{2}  $  ),  $  ^{26-29}  $   are among the

They have shown to enable efficient electron transport/extraction towards the transparent bottom electron selective contact in the inverted OSC and conventional PSC architecture, respectively. $ ^{30,31} $  However, the electron injection process from the absorber layer (either organic or perovskite semiconductor) to ZnO/TiO $ _2 $  ETLs usually involves significant recombination losses of photogenerated charge carriers at trap states present on the oxides' surface. Herein, motivated by the successful implementation of donor-  $  \pi  $  bridge-acceptor (D-  $  \pi  $  -A) type of dyes in DSSCs to improve electron transfer towards  $  TiO_{2}  $ ,  $  ^{47-49}  $  and from the recent application of 4,4-difluoro-4-bora-3a,4a-diaza-s-indacene (bodipy, BDP) dyes as NFAs in OSCs,  $  ^{50,51}  $  we introduce a Zn porphyrin-bodipy dyad linked via a triazine moiety (hereafter termed as BZnTPP) as universal electron transfer mediator to modify the electron selective contacts in both OSCs and PSCs using FTO/ZnO and FTO/  $  TiO_{2}  $  bottom electrodes, respectively. The “push-pull” character of this porphyrin-bodipy dye enhances electron injection from the photoactive absorber to the metal oxide substrate, thus facilitating electron extraction and enhancing the device photocurrent. As a result, we obtained significant efficiency enhancement in OSCs and PSCs reaching values up to 9.80% for fullerene and up to 8.73% for non-fullerene OSC devices and up to 17.34% for the perovskite counterparts. Furthermore, the alteration of surface properties of metal oxides beneficially influences the nanomorphology of the photoactive overlayer and contributes to efficiency stabilization.

The synthesis of BZnTPP is given in details in the Experimental Section. We performed DFT calculations to obtain the gas-phase geometry optimized structure of BZnTPP which is shown in Figure 1b. The porphyrin and bodipy segments are oriented nearly orthogonal to each other. As a result a high molecular dipole moment of 6.8994 D is obtained which is generally beneficial for electron transfer. Regarding the energy levels of the D-  $  \pi  $  -A molecule, the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) is extended over the entire porphyrin moiety (Figure 1c). However, the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) is delocalized over the bodipy segment supporting its role as the electron acceptor as also predicted by theoretical calculations (Figure S1, Supporting Information). This result highlights the donor-acceptor (or else, "push-pull") character of this dye, which is expected to be beneficial for improving electron transfer from the photoactive layer towards the electron selective contact.

Figure 1d presents the UV-Vis absorption spectra of BZnTPP in methanol solution and in thin films. In both spectra, the appearance of the characteristic absorption features of the porphyrin segment is evident. The Soret band arising from the  $  \mathrm{S}_0 \rightarrow \mathrm{S}_2  $  transition appears at 422 nm whereas the two Q bands originating from the  $  \mathrm{S}_0 \rightarrow \mathrm{S}_1  $  transition appear at 558 and 598 nm. The absorption band of the bodipy unit at 500 nm (coming from the  $  \mathrm{S}_0 \rightarrow \mathrm{S}_1  $  transition) is evident in the spectrum of the dilute dye solution although of a smaller intensity compared to the porphyrin Soret band. This intensity difference is probably attributed to the fact that the porphyrin ring obeys the Hückel's rule as it is a planar conjugated 18 π-electron system whereas bodipy doesn't. In the absorption spectrum of the BZnTPP film, although the absorption bands of the porphyrin segment are quite distinct and appear at the same wavelengths as in the dilute solution, the band originating from the bodipy segment shows very low intensity and a pronounced red shift of 15 nm (from 500 to 515 nm, Figure S2).

UV-Vis absorption spectra of metal oxide films either pristine or covered with BZnTPP (Figure 1e) elaborated the successful incorporation of dye molecules on the metal oxide surface. The very low intensity of the Soret band (which appears markedly broadened due to the anchoring on metal oxide surfaces) combined with the negligible change in the bandgap (Figure S3) and the small decrease in the transmittance (Figure S4) of the metal oxides across the visible probably indicate the formation of a discontinuous film rather than a compact and solid layer. As both metal oxides exhibit a nanostructured surface, a large amount of dye molecules is probably deposited inside the nanovoids. This can explain the decrease in surface roughness of both ZnO (Figures S5 and S6) and TiO  $  _{2}  $   (Figures S7 and S8) upon BZnTPP coverage. Notably, the initial surface roughness and its reduction upon spin-coating the dye is much higher for ZnO compared to TiO  $  _{2}  $  . This clearly suggests that in ZnO most of the porphyrin molecules are gathered inside the voids of the nanosstructured surface whereas in the less rough TiO  $  _{2}  $   they rather form a more cohesive film atop the oxide surface. They also enhanced the hydrophobicity of the modified oxide substrates (Figure 1f) as a result of the reduction of surface roughness (that generally favors hydrophilicity) and of the hydrophobic nature of the dye (especially of the fluorine bearing bodipy segment). The different degree of the induced hydrophobicity in the two oxides again indicates the different coverage mode adopted by BZnTPP molecules on each oxide surface. The presence of BZnTPP on ZnO/TiO  $  _{2}  $   surfaces was also verified by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy.

In addition to the high efficiency, the stability of PSCs also represents an insurmountable challenge to be addressed.  $  ^{70}  $   We, therefore, investigated whether the porphyrin hydrophobic dye can enhance the device stability when inserted between the metal oxide and perovskite layer. To this end, after the initial device characterization, the unencapsulated PSCs were exposed in our lab environment (40% humidity and RT temperature) and were periodically measured for a time period of 650h. Figure 5 a-d show that the reference cell exhibited a sharp decrease in all device operational characteristics preserving the 61% of its PCE at the end of day 26 of the characterization period when the dye-containing cell exhibited a PCE equal to the 85% of the initial one. This mainly stems from a remarkable stability of nearly 100% in their   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF values. However, one should notice that the device photocurrent seems to degrade in a similar manner in both cases (Figure 5a) indicating a degradation mechanism independent of the interface quality such as the formation of light-activated metastable deep-level trap states within the perovskite material. The stability in   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF can be explained on the basis of enhanced perovskite film crystallinity and increased hydrophobicity of the metal oxide underlayer. The latter results in reduced amount of moisture -that causes the gradual hydrolysis of the perovskite layer- present on the   $  TiO_{2}  $   surface prior to absorber deposition. To unambiguously demonstrate the water resistance properties of our dye we kept two perovskite films (one deposited on pristine and one on dye-coated   $  TiO_{2}  $  ) exposed to ambient light and moisture for more than 8 months (about 6000 hours). The effects of quicker degradation of the reference film compared to that coated on dye were easily detectable by UV-Vis absorption measurements (Figures 5e and 5f) and simple visual inspection (Figures 5g and 5h). From Figures 5e and 5f it becomes evident that the perovskite film deposited on the   $  TiO_{2}/dye  $   substrate presents only a small decrease in the visible absorption after the period of 6000 hours indicating that it does not undergo severe degradation. Synthesis of BZnTPP. Starting with the condensation of benzaldehyde and pyrrole, we obtain directly, the tetra-phenyl-porphyrin. After the nitration of this porphyrin we obtained the TPP-NO₂ derivative, which is transformed to the amino compound after reduction with SnCl₂. Cyanuric chloride (cc) reacted with TPP-NH₂ in the presence of K₂CO₃ to form TPP-cc in 99% yield. Next the metallation of that porphyrin was carried out with Zn(CH₃COO)₂·2H₂O giving rise to ZnTPP-cc. The BDP-NH₂ molecule was synthesized from the condensation of 4-nitrobenzaldehyde and 2,4-dimethylpyrrole. This reaction yielded the precursor molecule BDP-NO₂, which transformed to the desired product through a reductive reaction with gas H₂ and Pd/C. TPP-cc and BDP-NH₂ were reacted for the formation of TPP-cc-BDP. Metallation with Zn(CH₃COO)₂·2H₂O gave rise to ZnTPP-cc-BDP (termed in the paper as BZnTPP). Every desired product was isolated via column chromatography with silica gel, the only exceptions being the TPP-NO₂ derivative being an intermediate for the synthesis of TPP-NH₂.
