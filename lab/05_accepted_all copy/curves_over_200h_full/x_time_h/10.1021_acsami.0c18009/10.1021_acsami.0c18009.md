# Construction of an lodine Diffusion Barrier Using Network Structure Silicone Resin for Stable Perovskite Solar Cells

Dongxu Lin,  $  ^{\#}  $   Xin Xu,  $  ^{\#}  $   Jiming Wang, Tiankai Zhang, Fangyan Xie, Li Gong, Jian Chen, Tingting Shi,  $  ^{*}  $   Jifu Shi,  $  ^{*}  $   Pengyi Liu,  $  ^{*}  $   and Weiguang Xie  $  ^{*}  $

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Long-term stability of organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs) is inhibited by ion diffusion. Herein, we introduce a thermally stable and hydrophobic silicone resin layer with a network structure as an interfacial layer between the perovskite and the hole-transporting layer (HTL). Experimental and theoretical investigations confirm that the small Si-O-Si unit in the network forms both Si-I and Pb-O bonds with the perovskite surface, which physically and chemically inhibit the diffusion and self-release of iodine. Besides, the silicone resin layer suppresses the thermal crystallization of spiro-OMeTAD and optimizes the interfacial energy level alignment for hole extraction. The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of a perovskite solar cell with a silicone resin layer is improved to 21.11%. The device maintains more than 90.1% of its original PCE after 1200 h under 85 °C thermal stress, 99.6% after 2000 h under RH ~55 ± 5%, and 83% of its original PCE after light soaking in air for 1037 h.

KEYWORDS: perovskite solar cell, passivation, iodine diffuse barrier, stability improvement, spiro-OMeTAD crystallization

# 1. INTRODUCTION

The excellent optoelectronic properties of organic–inorganic halide perovskites have drawn tremendous attention in the past 10 years, and the certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs has reached 25.5%.  $  ^{1-3}  $   To commercialize the PSCs, stability becomes an urgent problem to be solved.  $  ^{4-8}  $   Due to the flexible crystal lattice of perovskite, both halogen and organic components are easy to self-release from the device during long-term operation.  $  ^{9-11}  $   The self-release process induces lots of defects in the perovskite films, which serve as nonradiative recombination centers. The defects are also sensitive to moisture and oxygen that accelerate device decomposition.  $  ^{12}  $   Diffusion of the organic component can be suppressed by replacing it with more stable species. For example, MA  $  ^{+}  $   can be replaced with more thermally stable FA  $  ^{+}  $   without strongly affecting its band structure.  $  ^{13}  $   However, the release of I  $  ^{-}  $   ions is hard to be solved because there is no suitable alternative component. Therefore, construction of a diffusion barrier layer to prevent the release of I  $  ^{-}  $   ions is essential to improve the stability of PSCs.  $  ^{14,15}  $

The   $  I^{-}  $  ion diffusion can be physically blocked by a compact interfacial layer. Atomic layer deposition (ALD) is an effective method to fabricate compact inorganic layers such as   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   and   $  TiO_{2}  $ , which have been found to block the diffusion of ions in perovskite.  $  ^{16,17}  $   Chemically stable polymers, for example, chain-like PMMA, have also been applied to block the diffusion of   $  I^{-}  $  ions.  $  ^{18}  $   The formation of a polymer layer could wrap the boundaries, which retard the outward diffusion

Besides physical blocking, Wang et al. found that compared to the graphene oxide, a chlorinated graphene oxide layer was better in suppressing   $  I^{-}  $  ion diffusion because formation of strong Pb–Cl and Pb–O bonds reduced the I vacancy sites around uncoordinated Pb atoms.  $  ^{25}  $   This shows that chemical blocking is another key aspect. In addition to passivation of uncoordinated Pb atoms, the iodine atom itself should also be immobilized, especially under thermal stress. It is found that small metal ions, for example, Na, were able to immobilize   $  I^{-}  $  ions by increasing ionic charge on them.  $  ^{26}  $   Small molecules, for example, phenyl-  $  C_{61}  $  -butyric acid methylester (PCBM), can also induce electron-transfer reactions with ions in the perovskite. The reaction products chemically passivate perovskite crystals and strongly bind halogen atoms at grain boundaries.  $  ^{27}  $   Cross-linking of these small molecules to polymers could also play the same role.  $  ^{28}  $   However, small molecules are not thermally stable and the improvement of thermal stability is still not satisfied.  $  ^{27}  $

To suppress the thermal diffusion of   $  I^{-}  $   ions, the thermal stability of HTL is another problem that should be considered. Doped spiro-OMeTAD has been commonly used as an HTL in high-efficiency PSCs for its matchable energy level and good conductivity.  $  ^{29}  $   However, in the thermal stability test, spiro-OMeTAD is always replaced by other HTLs.  $  ^{26,30}  $   It has been demonstrated that amorphous spiro-OMeTAD will crystallize under thermal or light aging.  $  ^{31,32}  $   Pinholes generated during crystallization serve as pathways for   $  I^{-}  $   diffusion and moisture infiltration, which accelerate device degradation. Furthermore, dopants such as bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonamide lithium salt (LiTFSI) and cobalt complex will be precipitated from spiro-OMeTAD films.  $  ^{33}  $   Therefore, to comprehensively improve the device stability, especially using spiro-OMeTAD as HTL, not only the physical and chemical blocking should be taken into consideration but also the stability of spiro-OMeTAD should be re-enforced.

Here, we report the physical and chemical suppression of I  $  ^{-}  $  diffusion using a hydrophobic and thermally stable silicone resin layer. It is inserted as an interfacial layer between spiro-

OMeTAD and the perovskite thin film. The silicone resin layer is found to possess a small network structure to prevent I  $  ^{-}  $  diffusion. The Si–O–Si bonding in the network of silicone resin is found to form Si–I and Pb–O bonds with the perovskite and thus both defect passivation and iodine immobilization are realized. Besides, it suppresses the thermal crystallization of spiro-OMeTAD and offers effective waterproof performance to the device. As a result, PSCs with a PCE of 21.11% are developed. The device maintains 90.1% of its original PCE after 1200 h under 85 °C thermal stress, 99.6% after 2000 h under RH ~55 ± 5%, and 83% of its original PCE after light soaking in air for 1037 h.

# 2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

2.1. Materials. Materials used in experiments include PbI  $  _{2}  $  (99.99%, TCI), CsI (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), MACl (>98%, TCI), spiro-OMeTAD (Advanced Election Technology Co., Ltd., 99.8%), and SnO  $  _{2}  $  colloid precursor (Alfa Aesar, tin(IV) oxide, 15% in H  $  _{2}  $  O colloidal dispersion). FAI, MAI, and MABr were purchased from Greatcell. N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF), dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO), isopropanol (IPA), and chlorobenzene (CB) were all purchased from Acros. Silicone resin was purchased from Mansite Chemical Co., Ltd.

2.2. Device Fabrication. ITO glass was cleaned using detergent, deionized water, acetone, and IPA successively with each for 30 min. After 5 min O₂ plasma treatment, SnO₂ nanocrystal suspension (the volume ratio of the SnO₂ colloid precursor and water is 1:5) was spin-coated onto the ITO glass at 3000 rpm for 30 s and then annealed in ambient air at 150 °C for 30 min. The SnO₂ substrate was then treated with O₂ plasma for 3 min. After that, the PbI₂ thin film was prepared by spin-coating 37 μL of PbI₂ precursor solution (1.3 M, dissolved in DMF/DMSO (9:1, v/v) and mixed with 5% CsI) onto the SnO₂ substrate at 2300 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 70 °C for 1 min. The perovskite thin film was prepared by spin-coating 50 μL of organic cation solution (40 mg of FAI, 19 mg of MAI, 6 mg of MABr, and 5 mg of MACl were dissolved in 1 mL of IPA) onto the PbI₂ thin film at 2000 rpm for 30 s and then annealed at 135 °C for 20 min in a covered Petri dish in a nitrogen glovebox. To prepare the silicone layer, silicone resin was dissolved in IPA with 3, 5, and 7 wt %. The solution was then spin-coated on the perovskite thin film with a speed of 5000 rpm for 30 s without annealing. The relative humidity in the chamber was controlled at 55 ± 5%. For the thermal stability test, the unencapsulated perovskite devices were stored in a closed oven with a fixed temperature of 85 °C. For the illumination stability test, the encapsulation perovskite devices were illuminated using a LED source (CREE J SERIES* 5050, 6500K) with a power of 100 mW/cm $  ^{2}  $ . $  ^{34}  $

2.5. Theoretical Calculation. In this paper, we performed all calculations based on density function (DFT) theory. We utilized the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) $ ^{35} $  with the projector augmented wave (PAW) method. $ ^{36} $  DFT calculations were performed using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation function of generalized gradient approximation (GGA). $ ^{37} $  A plane wave basis with a cutoff energy of 400 eV was set to solve the Kohn-Sham equation. The Brillouin zone sampled a  $ 2 \times 2 \times 1 $  mesh centered at  $ \Gamma $  point. The atoms of all structural configurations are completely relaxed until the Hellmann-Feynman forces on them are within 0.05 eV/A. The absorption energies of silicone resin on the surface were calculated by the equation below.

$$
E _ {\mathrm{ads}} (\mathrm{sr}) = E \left(\mathrm{MAPbI} _ {3} (\mathrm{sr})\right) - \left(E \left(\mathrm{MAPbI} _ {3}\right) + E (\mathrm{sr})\right)
$$

Here,   $  E_{\mathrm{ads}}(\mathrm{sr})  $   is the absorption energy.   $  E(\mathrm{MAPbI}_{3}(\mathrm{sr}))  $   and   $  E(\mathrm{MAPbI}_{3})  $   are the total energies of the supercell with and without silicone resin, respectively.   $  E(\mathrm{sr})  $   is the total energy of the silicone resin.

# 3. RESULTS

3.1. Impact of Silicone Resin on the Surface of the Perovskite. Figure 1a,b shows the molecular structure of silicone resin and its preparation process on the surface of the perovskite. The basic unit of silicone resin is a quadrilateral composed of Si–O–Si bonds. The connected units form a quasi-periodic network structure that could extend in a two-dimensional plane. The Si–O–Si bond length of 0.33 nm is smaller than the diameters of an   $  I^{-}  $  ion (0.412 nm)  $  ^{14,38}  $   and MA  $  ^{+}  $  ion (0.434 nm),  $  ^{39,40}  $   which is suitable for physical blocking. Furthermore, the silicone resin is a nonconductive thin film that could withstand long-term thermal aging above 200 °C. It also possesses hydrophobic properties, as shown in Figure S1a. The formation of a continuous network is expected to package the surface. Different weight ratios (wt %) of silicone resin were spin-coated onto the surface of as-prepared perovskite thin films without annealing. After spin coating, the Si 2p peak located at 102.48 eV can be detected using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), as shown in Figure 1c. SEM images in Figure S1d–g show that the spin-coated silicone resin does not change the morphology and grain size of the perovskite films. Meanwhile, XRD patterns in Figure S1b show that there is no notable change after introduction of the silicone resin layer. UV–vis absorption spectra show the same absorption onsets near 790 nm (Figure S1c). These results confirm that the introduction of silicone resin only forms an ultrathin surface layer on the perovskite thin film without changing its crystallinity and optical properties.

To investigate the effect of the silicone resin surface layer, we first conduct in situ atomic force microscopy (AFM) to observe the morphological change of the perovskite thin film under 100 °C thermal stress and a relative humidity of 50%. Figure 1d shows that the control perovskite thin film decomposes in 120 min. The decomposition process can be divided into three stages. In the first stage, small grains decompose into smaller particles as demonstrated in a previous study.  $  ^{41}  $   It shows rougher morphology in the red dashed line area at 30 min. 3.2. Iodine Blocking Effect. To illustrate the effect of silicone resin on the improvement of stability, we first explore the effect of coverage of silicone on the perovskite thin film. Figure S3a,b shows the current images of perovskite thin films using conductive atomic force microscopy (C-AFM). The current distribution on the perovskite control thin film is not uniform. The boundaries are more conductive, implying that the carrier transport depends strongly on the boundary defect.  $  ^{42,43}  $   When the surface is covered by a silicone resin layer, the current is significantly suppressed and becomes more uniform. This is consistent with the insulating nature of silicone resin and can be further confirmed by the dark J-V curves in Figure S3c. It means that the silicone resin covers the entire perovskite surface instead of locating only at the grain boundaries. The uniform silicone resin is beneficial to block the diffusion of   $  I^{-}  $   ions.

As the previous report has shown that interdiffusion and reaction of Ag and I  $  ^{-}  $  at the interface of Ag/perovskite were strong,  $  ^{44}  $  Ag/perovskite is chosen for demonstration of the blocking effect of the silicone resin layer. Figure 2a shows the full XPS depth profile of the as-prepared 100 nm Ag/550 nm perovskite structure. The XPS intensity of the Ag 3d peak decreases slowly after 500 s etching time. At the same time, the intensity at I 3d increases, which reflects the interface of Ag/perovskite. To investigate the interdiffusion, the sample was aged under 85 °C for 48 h. Figure 2b shows that after thermal aging, the iodine band in the XPS profile moves to the Ag

electrode, especially the intensity of iodine increases inside the Ag film. Meanwhile, the Ag signal can be detected even at an etching time of 1500 s. It confirms that the released iodine component permeates into the Ag electrode, while Ag diffuses deeply into the perovskite thin film. The great change of SEM morphology shown in Figure S4 further confirms the interdiffusion due to thermal aging. In sharp contrast, when the silicone resin layer was coated between Ag and perovskite, the surface morphology kept unchanged after the same thermal aging. Element profiles of Ag and I in Figure 2c show that the Ag signal decreases rapidly at the interface of Ag/silicone resin/perovskite. The I profile in the perovskite thin film is more uniform than the above two cases without the interfacial layer. This suggests that the silicone resin interface not only prevents the diffusion of Ag atoms into the perovskite films during electrode deposition but also effectively suppresses the interdiffusion of Ag and I during high-temperature thermal aging. Even after annealing the perovskite thin film with the silicone resin surface layer at an elevated temperature of 200 °C for 20 min, there are no obvious decomposition products as compared to that without the layer (Figure S5). This result confirms that coating of the silicone resin layer can effectively prevent the movement of iodine by forming a diffusion barrier, which significantly improves the thermal stability of perovskite.

Besides suppression of thermal release of the iodine component, light-induced iodine component release can also be suppressed, as shown in Figure 2d,e. Perovskite films with or without silicone resin are immersed in chlorobenzene (CB), respectively, and are illuminated continuously under a light intensity of   $  100 \, mW/cm^{2}  $  . Figure 2d shows the comparison of optical images of perovskite films after illumination for 48 h. The purple color of the CB with silicone resin is lighter than that without silicone resin. Furthermore, the corresponding UV–vis absorption spectra of CB in Figure 2e show lower absorption at the iodine peak at 500 nm.  $  ^{95}  $

3.3. Interaction between Silicone Resin and Perovskite. To further elucidate the mechanism of stability enhancement by the silicone resin layer, the absorption energy changes are calculated using VASP based on the density functional theory. Figure 3a,b compares the absorption when an O atom is on a Pb atom and on an I atom, respectively. The relaxed absorption energies are given in Figure 3e. It is found that the O atom was prone to stay with the uncoordinated Pb atom than stay with the I atom. The passivation effect of the uncoordinated Pb atom by the O atom is consistent with the XPS result in Figure 3f, which is similar to previous publications.  $  ^{25,46}  $   This passivation effect not only reduces the trap states but also forms a p-type doping surface that facilitates hole extraction and electron blocking, as shown in Figures S6 and S7. Furthermore, we calculate the absorption of the Si atom on Pb and on I atoms in Figure 3c,d respectively. The absorption energy of the Si atom on the I atom is smaller, implying that the Si atom preferentially absorbed on the I atom than absorbed on the Pb atom. The bonding of Si–I is also supported by XPS. Figure 3g,h shows that after treated with silicon resin, the I 3d peak shifts toward lower binding energy by 0.47 eV. Meanwhile, the Si 2p peak on the perovskite film shifts toward higher binding energy by 0.46 eV. The opposite shifting of Si and I peaks by similar binding energy indicates formation of Si–I bonds between silicone resin and perovskite. Here, a minimum absorption energy of -0.522 eV is found at the configuration that the Si atom is close to the I atom and the O atom is close to the uncoordinated Pb atom in Figure 3e. With the repeating of this quadrilateral Si–O–Si unit, the silicone resin forms a continuous network that is able to cover the surface of perovskite. Both XPS data and theoretical calculation results demonstrate that the silicon resin layer can passivate the potential surface defects and forms Si–I bonds, which could immobilize I  $  ^{-}  $   ions and thus support the improvement of thermal and illumination stability.

As the crystallization of spiro-OMeTAD is suppressed, an unencapsulated device with an architecture of ITO/SnO  $  _{2}  $  /perovskite/silicone/spiro-OMeTAD/Au was chosen for the thermal aging test at 85 °C in air. It is found that after 1200 h, the control device only maintained 13.7% of its original PCE, whereas the device with the silicone resin layer maintained 90.1% of its original PCE (Figure 5c). The inset photographs (photos from the ITO side) in Figure 5c show that the device with the silicone resin interlayer keeps unchanged, while that without the interlayer becomes transparent.

The storage lifetime of the devices without encapsulation was investigated under humidity of   $  55 \pm 5\%  $   in air at 22–29°C. Figure 5d shows that the PCE of the control sample decreases to 78.2% compared with its original PCE after 1400 h storage, while the device with the silicone resin layer retains 99.6% of its original PCE even after 2000 h storage. If an additional silicone resin layer was spin-coated on the Au

surface in Figure S12a, the device can even work in water with an output voltage of around 1 V, as shown in Figure S12c. Besides, the device with silicone resin encapsulation can stay in water for more than 3 min but the reference device completely degrades after being placed in water for 3 min, as shown in Figure S12b. Finally, we test the photostability of the silicone device and control device in Figure 5e. The silicone device maintains 83% of its original PCE after 1037 h of 100 mW/cm  $  ^{2}  $  irradiation, while the control device just maintains 18% of its original PCE after 240 h.

# 4. CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, we have fabricated a highly efficient perovskite solar cell with a PCE of 21.11% by inserting an interfacial silicone resin layer between the perovskite and the HTL. The device maintains 90.1% of its original PCE after 1200 h under 85 °C thermal stress and 99.6% after 2000 h under RH ~55 ± 5%. It is found that the Si and O atoms in the silicone resin passivated the perovskite film by formation of Pb–O and Si–I bonds, respectively. The polymer with a network structure shows the benefit of packaging the surface. The Si–I bond directly immobilizes I  $  ^{-}  $  ions and the uniform network structure of the silicone resin is able to realize high-stability PSCs by suppressing iodine diffusion. Besides, the appearance of the silicone resin layer suppresses the thermal crystallization of spiro-OMeTAD. This work provides a new perspective for highly stable PSCs.
