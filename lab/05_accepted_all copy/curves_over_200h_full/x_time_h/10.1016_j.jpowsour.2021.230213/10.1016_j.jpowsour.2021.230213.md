# 1. Introduction

The metal halide perovskite materials, which possess the advantages of high light-absorption coefficient, high carrier mobility, long exciton diffusion lengths, tunable bandgaps, low exciton binding energy, low cost, and solution processability   $  [1]  $  , have been applied into various optoelectronic devices, such as perovskite solar cells (PSCs)   $  [2]  $  , light-emitting diodes (LEDs)   $  [3]  $  , photodetectors   $  [4]  $  , etc. Among them, PSCs have captured extensive attention and witnessed great progresses in power conversion efficiency (PCE) and stability. To date, three-dimensional (3D) PSCs have achieved a record certified PCE of up to 25.5%   $  [5]  $  . However, the practical application of 3D PSCs is challenged by the poor environmental stability of 3D perovskite materials. It has been widely demonstrated that two-dimensional (2D) or quasi 2D PSCs exhibits much more excellent environmental stability as compared to its 3D counterpart   $  [6]  $  . In the past few years, huge research advancements have been obtained on 2D PSCs   $  [6,7]  $  . Nevertheless, there is still large room in PCE enhancement for 2D PSCs in comparison with 3D analogue.

It is well known that two types of 2D perovskites are used for PSCs, namely Ruddlesden-Popper (RP) and Dion-Jacobson (DJ) perovskites. RP and DJ 2D layered perovskites adopt the general structural formula of A'  $  _{2}  $  A  $  _{n-1}  $  B  $  _{n}  $  X  $  _{3n+1}  $   and A''A  $  _{n-1}  $  B  $  _{n}  $  X  $  _{3n+1}  $  , respectively, where A' is large-size monovalent organic cations (such as phenethylammonium (PEA  $  ^{+}  $  ) and butylammonium (BA  $  ^{+}  $  )), A'' is large-size divalent organic cation (such as propane-1,3-diammonium (PDA  $  ^{2+}  $  ), 1,4-butanediammonium (BDA  $  ^{2+}  $  ), and 1,4-phenylenedimethanammonium (PDMA  $  ^{2+}  $  )), A is small-size monovalent organic or inorganic cation (such as methylammonium (MA  $  ^{+}  $  ), formamidinium (FA  $  ^{+}  $  ), Cs  $  ^{+}  $   and Rb  $  ^{+}  $  ), B is divalent metal cation (such as Pb  $  ^{2+}  $   and Sn  $  ^{2+}  $  ), X is monovalent halide anion (such as I  $  ^{-}  $  , Br  $  ^{-}  $  , Cl  $  ^{-}  $   and BF  $  _{4}  $  ), n is the layer number of the inorganic [BX  $  _{6}  $  ]  $  ^{4-}  $   octahedral inorganic slabs [8]. The introduction of large organic cations into perovskites can form 2D perovskites accompanying by the formation of natural quantum wells (QW), where the inorganic [BX  $  _{6}  $  ]  $  ^{4-}  $   octahedral layers act as wells and the bulky spacer cation layers serve as barriers [9]. Therefore, the incorporation of large-size spacer cation layer is not conducive to carrier transport. The two insulating specer cation layers are inserted between neighbouring inorganic corner-sharing [PbI  $  _{6}  $  ]  $  ^{4-}  $   sheet RP 2D perovskites while only one insulating specer cation layer in DJ 2D perovskites. Consequently, smaller barrier and thus better carrier transport are expected in DJ 2D perovskites relative to RP 2D perovskites. The neighbouring inorganic cornersharing [PbI  $  _{6}  $  ]  $  ^{4-}  $   layers in RP 2D perovskites are connected by the weak van der Waals interaction due to the existence of van der Waals gap whereas the neighbouring inorganic layers in DJ 2D perovskites are connected via the hydrogen bond interaction due to no van der Waals gap. Since the hydrogen bond interaction is stronger than weak van der Waals interaction, the DJ 2D perovskites are expected to have better structural stability compared with RP 2D perovskites [10]. In a word, the DJ 2D perovskites have advantages over RP 2D perovskites regarding carrier transport and structural stability. In other words, the DJ 2D PSCs have a potential in realizing higher PCE and better stability compared to the RP 2D counterpart. Actually, higher PCE and better stability have been testified for DJ 2D PSCs than RP 2D analogue [11,12]. However, presently reported highest PCE (up to 18.6%) for the DJ 2D PSCs is much lower than 25.5% of 3D counterpart [13]. Consequently, it is urgent to further increase the PCE of DJ 2D PSCs.

Currently, most reported 2D perovskites possess much enlarged

bandgaps because of the incorporation of large-size organic cations, which is not beneficial for light harvesting and achieving high current density. Therefore, it is necessary to narrow bandgap through composition modulation. For this purpose,   $  FA^{+}  $   cation is suggested to substitute   $  MA^{+}  $   cation in 2D perovskites in light of the successful use of   $  FA^{+}  $   in 3D PSCs [14,15]. In addition, most reported 2D perovskites contain volatile   $  MA^{+}  $   cation, which is easy to decompose under heating and light illumination [16,17]. The thermal and light stability of the PSCs has been demonstrated to be able to be significantly improved by the replacement of   $  MA^{+}  $   cation with   $  FA^{+}  $   cation [18–20]. Consequently, the incorporation of   $  FA^{+}  $   cation in perovskite composition can not only increase light harvesting by reducing bandgap but also improve the thermal and light stability. In spite of its wide application in 3D perovskites,   $  FA^{+}  $   cation is rarely employed in 2D perovskites for solar cells [10,13]. It is highly desirable to fabricate DJ FA-based 2D PSCs in order to simultaneously realize efficient and stable PSCs.

Serious bulk and interfacial carrier nonradiative recombination hinders the further improvement of efficiency and stability of DJ FA-based 2D PSCs. The poor carrier extraction and collection should be responsible for the bulk and interfacial charge nonradiative recombination. Up to now, various strategies have been developed to improve carrier transport, extraction and collection of DJ 2D perovskites, such as development of novel large-size organic cations [8,21,22], additive engineering [23], procedure optimization [13], etc. Although interface engineering has been extensively demonstrated to be very effective in modulating energy band alignment for the sake of reducing interfacial nonradiative recombination losses in the field of 3D PSCs [20,24–26], it is rarely reported in 2D PSCs especially DJ 2D PSCs. It was well documented that gradient energy band alignment is conducive to carrier extraction and transfer along with suppressing carrier nonradiative recombination [24–26]. In the past, constructing gradient energy level alignment is usually achieved by gradient 2D distribution in 3D perovskite films or gradient Br doping. The gradient Br doping is difficult to achieve directly by modulating perovskite precursor compositions. Fortunately, the gradient Br doping can be realized through either the reaction between organic Br salts (such as FABr and MABr) and excess PbI $ _{2} $ , or the ion exchange reaction between Br $ ^{-} $  and I $ ^{-} $ . The latter could be more controllable as compared to the former. The ion exchange reaction strategy has been investigated extensively for modulating perovskite compositions [27,28] or engineering interface [29] in the field of 3D PSCs. Therefore, it is highly expected to modulate the energy band alignment of DJ FA-based 2D PSCs via in situ ion exchange reaction in order to minimize the bulk and interfacial nonradiative recombination losses.

In this work, we successfully constructs gradient energy band alignment by achieving gradient Br doping (GBD) via the in situ ion exchange reaction between   $  I^{-}  $  and   $  Br^{-}  $  in DJ FA-based 2D PSCs. In addition, the perovskite film quality is improved due to reduced defect density and enhanced crystallinity. The gradient energy band alignment can facilitate carrier transport, extraction and transfer, finally leading to suppressing bulk and interfacial nonradiative recombination. As a result, the PCE and stability are simultaneously increased. The device with GBD achieves a much higher PCE of 16.75% than the control device (13.78%), which is mainly as a consequence of a significantly boosted open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) from 0.970 V to 1.107 V. The unencapsulated device with GBD maintains 93% of its initial PCE after aging under the relative humidity range of 15–20% for 1600 h, 91% after aging at 60 °C for 400 h, and 66% after aging under one sun illumination for 400 h.

# 2. Experimental section

Materials: The  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  colloid precursor was obtained from Alfa Aesar (tin (IV) oxide,  $ 15\% $  in  $ \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} $  colloidal dispersion). Ammonium chloride  $ (\mathrm{NH_4Cl}, 99.99\%) $  were purchased from Aladdin. Lead (II) iodide  $ (\mathrm{PbI}_2, 99.99\%) $  and 1,4-diaminobutane dihydroiodide (BDADI,  $ 99.5\% $  ) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. Spiro-OMeTAD  $ (99.86\%) $  were purchased from Advanced Election Technology Co., Ltd. Formamidine hydroiodide (FAI) and formamidine hydrobromide (FABr) was purchased from Great Cell Solar.  $ N,N $ -dimethylformamide (DMF,  $ 99.8\% $ ), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO,  $ 99.9\% $ ), and chlorobenzene (CB,  $ 99.8\% $  ) were bought from Sigma Aldrich. All chemical reagents were used as received without further purification.

Device Fabrication: ITO-coated glass substrates were etched by laser. The etched ITO glass was ultrasonically cleaned for 20 min using detergent, deionized water, and ethanol, sequentially. The   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloidal solutions were prepared by diluting   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloidal solution of 15 wt% to 1.875 wt% with deionized water. Subsequently, KCl was added into the above colloidal solution where the concentration of KCl was 1 mg/mL. After the ITO substrates were treated by UV ozone (UVO) for 20 min, the colloidal solution of   $  SnO_{2}  $   was spin-coated on the ITO substrates at 3000 rpm for 30 s and then as-prepared   $  SnO_{2}  $   films were annealed at 150 °C for 30 min. After the films cooled down to room temperature,   $  SnO_{2}  $   films were exposed to UVO for 20 min. The 0.6 M perovskite precursor solution (nominal perovskite composition was (BDA)FA4Pb5I16) was prepared by dissolving BDADI of 41.28 mg, FAI of 82.54 mg,   $  NH_{4}Cl  $   of 10.70 mg, and   $  PbI_{2}  $   of 276.61 mg in the mixed solvents of DMF and DMSO (  $  V_{DMF}: V_{DMSO} = 9 : 1  $  ). The perovskite films were prepared through spin-coating the above as-prepared perovskite precursor solution on the   $  SnO_{2}  $   films preheated at 70 °C at 5000 rpm for 25 s where 100 μL CB antisolvent was dripped on perovskite films at 7 s before ending the program. The wet perovskite films were annealed at 150 °C for 15 min. In case of modified device, 30 μL different concentrations of FABr solutions in IPA (0.5 mg/mL, 1 mg/mL, 2.5 mg/mL, 5 mg/mL and 10 mg/mL) were spin-coated on the as-prepared perovskite films at 5000 rpm for 25 s. The Spiro-OMeTAD solution was prepared by dissolving 72.3 mg Spiro-OMeTAD in 1 mL chlorobenzene, which was followed by adding 28.8 μL of 4-tert-butyl pyridine (tBP) and 17.5 μL of lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (Li-TFSI) stock solution (520 mg Li-TSFI in 1 mL acetonitrile) into the above solution. Finally, a 80 nm-thick gold counter electrode was thermally evaporated on the top of Spiro-OMeTAD film under a vacuum of   $  3 \times 10^{-4}  $   Pa by using a shadow mask.

Characterizations:  $ J-V $  curves were measured using a solar simulator equipped with 150 W Xenon lamp (150 W, SolarIV-150A) and a Keithley 2400 source meter. Light intensity was calibrated to AM 1.5G one sun  $ (100\mathrm{mW/cm}^2) $  with a NIM calibrated standard Si solar cell (QE-B1). The effective active area of the device was defined to be  $ 0.07~\mathrm{cm}^2 $  by using a black metal mask.  $ J-V $  curves were measured from  $ -0.1\mathrm{V} $  to  $ 1.2\mathrm{V} $  (forward scan) or from  $ 1.2\mathrm{V} $  to  $ -0.1\mathrm{V} $  (reverse scan) with a scan rate of  $ 100\mathrm{mV/s} $ . The incident photon-to-electron conversion efficiency (IPCE) measurement was performed on an IPCE measurement system. Impedance spectroscopy was measured by an Electrochemical Workstation (China) and the data were analyzed by the Z-View program. UV-vis absorption spectra measurement was carried out on a UV-vis spectrometer (Shimadzu UV-1800, Japan). The SEM images were measured by using a field-emission scanning electron microscopy (JSM-7800F, Japan). Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) measurement was carried out using a dual-beam TOF-SIMS IV (IONTOF) spectrometer equipped with a  $ 30\mathrm{keV} $ ,  $ 1\mathrm{pA}\mathrm{Bi}^{3+} $  beam for the analysis and a  $ 1\mathrm{KeV} $ ,  $ 1\mathrm{nA}\mathrm{O}^{2-} $  ion beam for the sputtering operated in non-interlaced mode. Steady-state and time-resolved photoluminescence spectra were measured using Edinburgh FLS1000 (UK), where the excitation wavelength of TRPL was provided by a  $ 450\mathrm{nm} $  laser. XPS and UPS spectra were obtained using a Thermo-Fisher ESCALAB 250Xi system with a monochromatized Al Kα (for XPS mode) under the

pressure of   $  5.0 \times 10^{-7}  $  Pa. X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra were recorded by a PANalytical Empyrean (Netherlands). Transient photocurrent (TPC) and transient photovoltage (TPV) decay measurements were performed as follows: the devices were illuminated by laser pulses (532 nm, 100 mJ, 6 ns width from an Nd:YAG laser), and then the decay of signals was recorded by a 1 GHz Agilent digital oscilloscope (DSO-X3102A) with input impedance of 1 MΩ/50 Ω. Two-dimensional grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) images were collected on BL1W1A at Beijing Synchrotron Radiation Facility (BSRF) (  $  \lambda = 1.54 \AA  $  ). The incidence angle is   $  0.16^{\circ}  $   and the exposure time is 100 s.

# 3. Results and discussion

In order to construct gradient band alignment, a certain concentration of FABr in isopropanol (IPA) was spin-coated onto the prepared DJ FA-based quasi-2D perovskite films (nominally described as (BDA)  $ \mathrm{FA_4Pb_5I_{16}} $ ), and new perovskite composition (BDA)  $ \mathrm{FA_4Pb_5I_{16 - x}Br_x} $  with GBD was formed through the ion exchange reaction between  $ \mathrm{I}^{-} $ and  $ \mathrm{Br}^{-} $ , as schematically illustrated in Fig. 1a. For clarity of presentation, the device (perovskite film) without FABr treatment was denoted as the control device (perovskite film) while the device treated by the optimal concentration (2.5 mg/mL) of FABr was denoted as the target device (perovskite film). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurement was carried out to confirmed the existence of FABr in the final perovskite film and the chemical interaction between FABr and (BDA)  $ \mathrm{FA_4Pb_5I_{16}} $ . As shown in Fig. S1a, we detected the peak at of Br 3d in the target perovskite film whereas not in the control film. This suggested that FABr existed in the final perovskite film. The peaks of Pb 4f in the target perovskite film (Pb  $ 4f_{7/2} $ : 138.53 eV and Pb  $ 4f_{5/2} $ : 143.33 eV) exhibited a increased binding energy than those in the control perovskite film (Pb  $ 4f_{7/2} $ : 138.00 eV and Pb  $ 4f_{5/2} $ : 142.90 eV) (Fig. S1b), which was ascribed to the higher electronegativity of Br than I. This indicated that Br could be incorporated into perovskite crystal lattices through ion exchange reaction.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurement was performed to study the effect of FABr posttreatment on the crystal structure and crystallinity of perovskite films. As illustrated in Fig. 1b, the control perovskite film exhibited main characteristic diffraction peak at  $ 2\theta $  values of  $ 14.39^{\circ} $  and  $ 28.52^{\circ} $  corresponding to the (111) and (202) crystal planes of cubic perovskite, respectively, which was shifted to higher diffraction angle of  $ 14.84^{\circ} $  and  $ 28.97^{\circ} $  in the target perovskite film due to lattice contraction resulting from the smaller ionic radius of  $ \mathrm{Br}^{-} $  than  $ \mathrm{I}^{-} $ , suggesting the successful incorporation of Br into the perovskite crystal lattices via ion exchange reaction. In addition, a much increased peak intensity and much reduced Full width at half maximum (FWHM) from 0.346 to 0.118 regarding (111) diffraction peak were observed in the target perovskite film as compared to the control film (Table S1), which was indicative of much improved crystallinity after FABr treatment. This showed that recrystallization process occurred when perovskite films were post-treated by FABr. Actually, similar recrystallization process has been well confirmed in the previous studies [27,30]. The above results implied that FABr posttreatment can not only construct gradient energy band alignment but also promote the recrystallization of the perovskite films. Grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) patterns were obtained to gain insights into the orientation of the control and target perovskite films, as depicted in Fig. S2. The target perovskite film exhibited slightly brighter scattering rings in comparison with the control film, suggesting slightly improved crystallinity and crystal orientation which was consistent with the above XRD results. UV-vis absorption spectra were collected to further investigate the effect of FABr treatment on the light harvesting property and bandgap of the perovskite films. As presented in Fig. 1c and d, the blue-shifted UV-vis absorption peak corresponding to an enlarged bandgap from  $ 1.54\mathrm{eV} $  to  $ 1.59\mathrm{eV} $  was found after FABr treatment, indicating the successful incorporatin of  $ \mathrm{Br}^{-} $ into the perovskite crystal lattices, which was in good agreement with the XRD results. It was found obviously that the amount of Br⁻ was significantly smaller than that of I⁻. This strongly showed that a tiny amount of I⁻ was substituted by Br⁻ even if at the top of perovskite films. It was concluded that the gradient band alignment was constructed while the bandgap increased slightly. Finally, the long-term operational stability of the control and target devices was evaluated systematically. Fig. 5a and Fig. S10 show the ambient stability of the unencapsulated control and target devices aged under a relative humidity of 15–20% at room temperature in the dark. The PCE of the control device was degraded by 46% after aging for 400 h while the PCE of the target device was degraded only by 34%. It can be concluded that the improved stability was put down to the Br doping, improved crystallinity, and reduced defect density [31,33,35,36]. This indicated that GBD can not only improve the PCE but also increase operational stability.

# 4. Conclusions

In summary, we have successfully demonstrated a strategy for constructing gradient energy band alignment by achieving gradient Br doping (GBD) via the in situ ion exchange reaction between  $ \mathrm{I}^{-} $  and  $ \mathrm{Br}^{-} $  in DJ FA-based quasi-2D PSCs. First, the gradient energy band alignment due to GBD can facilitate carrier transport, extraction and transfer. Second, improved crystallinity and reduced defect density owing to recrystallization process are realized after FABr treatment. Finally, the incorporation of Br mainly contributes to increased device stability. As a result, the PCE and stability are simultaneously increased. The device with GBD achieves a much higher PCE of  $ 16.75\% $  as compared to the control device  $ (13.78\%) $ , which is mainly as a consequence of a significantly boosted  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  from  $ 0.970\mathrm{V} $  to  $ 1.107\mathrm{V} $  because of suppressed bulk and interfacial nonradiative recombination.
