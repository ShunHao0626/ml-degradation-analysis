Abstract: Surface passivation of perovskite thin films has been established as a promising approach to improving both the efficiency and stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). While remarkable progress has been achieved with various surface passivating agents, long-term stability of PSCs has not been fully addressed. Here, we report a new strategy for surface passivation of PSCs using a low cost industrial organic pigment quinacridone (QA). The procedure involves solution processing a soluble derivative of QA, N,N-bis(tert-butyloxycarbonyl)-quinacridone (TBOC-QA), followed by facile thermal annealing to convert TBOC-QA to insoluble QA. With the halide perovskite thin films coated by QA, PSCs based on methylammonium lead lodide (MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ) showed significantly improved performance with remarkable stability. A PCE of 21.1% was achieved, which is much higher than 18.9% recorded for the unmodified devices. Density functional theory calculations revealed strong interactions between QA molecules and metal halides, which effectively reduced the surface defects of halide perovskite thin films and suppressed the nonradiative recombination. The QA coating with exceptional insolubility and hydrophobicity also led to remarkably enhanced contact angle from 35.6° for the pristine MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   thin films to 77.2° for QA coated MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   thin films. As a result, the stabilities of QA passivated MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite thin films and PSCs were greatly enhanced, retaining about 90 % of the initial efficiencies after more than 1000 hours storage under ambient conditions. The universality of this passivation strategy was evaluated by extending MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   systems to triple-cation mixed-halide perovskite systems, which further confirm the effectiveness of industrial pigment passivation of PSCs with enhanced efficiency and stability.

# Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have received tremendous research attention as new paradigm in photovoltaic technologies.  $  ^{[1, 2]}  $   Thanks to relentless efforts in materials development and devices engineering, remarkable progress has been achieved over the last decade with the power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) improved from around 4% to more than 25% for single junction devices,  $  ^{[3, 4]}  $  . However, long-term stability is widely considered to be one of the major challenges that must be addressed before large scale commercialization of PSCs.  $  ^{[5, 6]}  $   Many approaches

have been developed during last few years in improving the stability of PSCs, including surface passivation of halide perovskite thin films using various types of materials, such as organic halide salts,  $  ^{[2,7,8]}  $  polymers,  $  ^{[9-11]}  $  organic small molecules,  $  ^{[5,12-14]}  $  low-dimensional perovskite hybrids,  $  ^{[15,16]}  $  and inorganic compounds,  $  ^{[17,18]}  $  and more.  $  ^{[19,20]}  $  The function of surface passivation is twofold. The first is in suppressing charge recombination at the interfaces between perovskite and charge transport layers. The second is in increasing device stability by preventing the penetration of degrading agents (i.e.  $  H_{2}O  $  and  $  O_{2}  $ ) into the perovskite layer. While various types of materials have been developed as surface passivation agents, the long-term stability requirements for the commercialization of PSCs is yet to be met by these materials. An ideal surface passivation agent should be easy to process, low cost, and highly stable.  $  ^{[5,13]}  $

Industrial organic pigments are insoluble organic compounds of high sunshine-resistant coloring strength. Insolubility in this context means that they have extremely low solubilities in water, organic solvents, and other kinds of media. These unique properties of industrial organic pigments make them intriguing candidates as surface passivation agents for PSCs but, to date, have been relatively unexplored. Quinacridone (QA) is five-ring planar structure (Figure 1) that is widely used as an industrial organic pigment for red paints and cosmetics, because it is air stable, nontoxic, and low-cost ( $ \sim $  \$0.50/kg). $ ^{[21, 22]} $  Many properties of QA originate from the synergistic effects of intermolecular hydrogen-bonding between carbonyl and amine groups, as well as  $ \pi $ - $ \pi $  stacking with adjacent molecules, which consequently leads to the formation of supramolecular structures. $ ^{[23]} $  These characteristics afford QA impressive physicochemical properties, including high stabilities versus light and heat (sublimation temperature  $ >500^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ ) and excellent charge carrier mobility (0.2 cm $ ^2 $  V $ ^{-1} $  s $ ^{-1} $ ) in electronic devices. $ ^{[21, 24]} $  In addition to industrial colorant applications, QA has been explored for use in organic field-effect transistors, $ ^{[21]} $  light-emitting diodes, $ ^{[25]} $  photovoltaic devices $ ^{[26-28]} $ , as well as in carbon dioxide and lithium-ion capture, because of its outstanding electrochemical properties. $ ^{[24, 29]} $

The effects of QA coating on the device performance of PSCs were then investigated. By controlling the amount of TBOC-QA in the mixed IPA/CB solvents, the optimal concentration for achieving the highest PCE was found to be 2 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  (Figure S2 and Table S1). More than thirty PSCs were fabricated and tested for both pristine and QA coated  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  thin films to probe the reproducibility, with the results shown in Figure 1e. The average performance characteristics of these PSCs are summarized in Table S3. The devices based on pristine  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  thin films gave an average  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  of  $ 1.10\mathrm{V} $ , a  $ J_{SC} $  of  $ 22.10\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $ , a FF of 0.74, and a PCE of  $ 17.92\% $ . When treated with QA, the parameters were clearly enhanced to an averaged  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  of  $ 1.12\mathrm{V} $ , a  $ J_{SC} $  of  $ 22.74\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $ ,

The incident photon conversion efficiencies (IPCEs) of the pristine and QA coated devices were also recorded and the results are shown in Figure 1f. The QA coated devices exhibited better IPCEs than the pristine devices across the entire wavelength range (350-800 nm).

To gain fundamental understanding of the impacts of QA coating on the device performance, the morphological, electronic, and photophysical properties of QA coated  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  thin films were characterized. The same X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of pristine and QA coated  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  thin films suggest that QA coating does not change the tetragonal phase of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  (Figure S4a). UV-Vis absorption spectra of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  thin films also display little-to-no change after QA coating, implying the same light absorption behavior of these samples (Figure S4b). To further characterize the effects of QA coating on the film morphology, SEM images of the corresponding  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  thin films were recorded and the results are shown in Figure 2a and 2b. While perovskite crystallites with the size of a few hundred nanometers are clearly observed in both samples, irregularly shaped small spots appeared after QA coating (shown in the white oval of Figure 2b), indicating the formation of new species on the surfaces of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  thin films.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) and Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) measurements were performed under a nitrogen atmosphere (Figure 2c-f) and the results are in good agreement with the observations by SEM measurement. For the QA coated sample, nanoparticles of tens of nanometers covered the surface of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  thin film (white oval of Figure 2b and 2d), further confirming surface coating by QA. The QA coated MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  thin film also exhibited a higher surface potential than that of pristine MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  thin film (Figure 2e and 2f), which is mainly attributed to the reduction of surface defects caused by dangling bonds.  $  ^{[35]}  $  It is well known that the presence of these dangling bonds/defects increases the rate of nonradiative recombination of photogenerated charge carriers.  $  ^{[36, 37]}  $  Thus the reduction of surface defects by the QA coating contributes to the improved the photovoltaic performances shown in Figure 1d-f.  $  ^{[13, 35]}  $

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted to probe the resistance and charge transfer kinetics of the PSCs, and the results are shown in Figure 3a with fitting paramteres summarized in Table S4. In the equivalent circuit (Figure S5), the   $  R_{s}  $   calculated from the intersection of high frequency imaginary impedance on the X-axis is the series resistance of a PSC, while the   $  R_{ct}  $   and   $  R_{rec}  $   ascribed to the high frequency and low frequency elements are the charge transport resistance and recombination resistance, respectively.  $  ^{[38-40]}  $   The QA coated device has a   $  R_{s}  $   of 14.1   $  \Omega  $  , lower than that of the pristine device at 18.6   $  \Omega  $  , suggesting more efficient charge transfer in the device. This is not surprising, as HOMO energy of QA is appropriate to form a cascade with MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   and spiro-OMeTAD, resulting in the improvement of FF from 0.76 to 0.80, as shown in Figure 1c.  $  ^{[32]}  $   The smaller   $  R_{ct}  $   (56.5   $  \Omega  $  ) and larger   $  R_{rec}  $   (925.8   $  \Omega  $  ) of the QA coated device, as compared to those of the pristine device with values of 79.9   $  \Omega  $   and 489.1   $  \Omega  $  , suggest more efficient hole extraction and suppressed charge recombination at the interfaces.

We then preformed the light intensity dependent   $  J_{SC}  $   and   $  V_{OC}  $   measurements to ascertain the charge recombination kinetics of the PSCs. From a linear fit of the   $  J_{SC}  $   versus intensity data (Figure 3b), the QA coating shows a greater slope (0.99) than that of the pristine device (0.96), signifying the enhanced charge extraction in the former likely due to decreased trap density at the passivated interfaces.  $  ^{[32]}  $   The   $  V_{OC}  $   versus light intensity is shown in Figure 3c. The ideality factor (n) can be calculated from the   $  V_{OC}  $   and light intensity (  $  P_{light}  $  ) using the equation (1):

$$
n = \frac {q}{k _ {B} T} \frac {\mathrm{d} V _ {O C}}{\mathrm{dln} (P _ {l i g h t})}
$$

(1)

where   $  k_{B}  $   is the Boltzmann constant, q is elementary charge of the electron, and the n is representative of the charge carrier recombination process. In general, if the n value approaches unity, the device performance is dominated by bimolecular recombination, i.e. the recombination of free electrons and holes in the perovskite layer. When n approaches 2, the trap-assisted Shockley-Read-Hall (SRH) charge carrier recombination is dominant.  $  ^{[32, 41]}  $   We determined n values of 1.96 and 1.45 for the pristine and QA coated devices, respectively, indicating that a trap-assisted SRH recombination process is occurring at the charge collection interfaces. The decreased n value for the device with QA coating suggests the suppression of trap-assisted SRH recombination due to reduced trap-state density, which manifests in the increased FF and PCE (Figure 1e).

The reduction of trap-state density in QA coated perovskite thin films was also confirmed by the quantitative characterization of hole-only devices via a space-charge-limited current (SCLC) method Figure 3d. At the region of low bias voltage, the linear relation indicates an ohmic response of the device, while the current increased quickly as the voltage went up and exceeded the kink point, signifying the trap-states were fully filled.  $  ^{[39]}  $   The trap-state density   $  (n_{i})  $   can be calculated from the trap-filled limit voltage   $  (V_{\mathrm{TFL}})  $   using the equation (2):  $  ^{[42]}  $

$$
n _ {t} = \frac {2 \varepsilon \varepsilon_ {0} V _ {\mathrm{TFL}}}{e L ^ {2}}
$$

(2)

where  $ \varepsilon $  represents the relative dielectric constant of perovskite ( $ \varepsilon = 28.8 $  for MAPbI $ _3 $ ), $ ^{[39]} $   $ \varepsilon_0 $  is the vacuum permittivity, and  $ L $  is the thickness of the perovskite layer. From the cross-section SEM image (Figure 1b) the perovskite thickness was determined to be  $ \sim 480 $  nm. As shown in Figure 3d, the  $ V_{\text{TFL}} $  of pristine and QA coated thin films are 0.39 and 0.26 V giving  $ n_t $  values of  $ 5.39 \times 10^{15} $  and  $ 3.60 \times 10^{15} $  cm $ ^{-3} $ , respectively. The lower value for the device with QA coating again supports the conclusion that the hole trap-state density of perovskite thin films was greatly reduced by QA passivation, resulting in lower charge recombination and increased fill factor, as shown in Figure 1e. $ ^{[43]} $

To further probe the effect of QA coating on the charge transfer dynamics between perovskite layer and HTM, steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved PL (TRPL) spectra were recorded for glass/perovskite/HTM devices. The QA coated MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  thin film exhibited stronger PL quenching (Figure 3e) and decreased excited state lifetime of 8.8 ns (Figure 3f, Table S5) relative to the pristine film (13.8 ns). This emission quenching is consistent with more rapid and efficient hole extraction at the perovskite/HTM interfaces  $  ^{[44]}  $  and the increased FF and reduced hysteresis index.  $  ^{[45]}  $

In comparison to the pristine film, several new absorption features are observed for the QA treated MAPbI $ _3 $  thin films (dotted oval of Figure 3g) that are consistent with the presence of QA. It was found that the shift of the C=O vibration in QA treated PbI $ _2 $  follows the same trend as that of QA coated MAPbI $ _3 $  thin films. To gain a better understanding of the interactions between QA and MAPbI $ _3 $ , density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed. In the calculations, a PbI $ _2 $  terminated symmetric slab was constructed from the partially relaxed tetragonal MAPbI $ _3 $ . The slab consisted of 2 × 2 periodicity in the a-b plane and three layers of PbI $ _2 $  layers in the c axis. A 4 × 4 × 1 k-point mesh was adopted for Brillouin-zone sampling. Afterward, the QA molecule was placed on the (001) PbI $ _2 $  terminated perovskite surface orthogonally. Detailed calculation parameters can be found in the Supporting Information. The optimized difference charge density distribution of QA and MAPbI $ _3 $  is presented in Figure 4. To investigate the effects of QA coating on the device stability, we then examined the change of PCEs for the pristine and QA passivated  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  thin films under long-term storage and continuous illumination in ambient conditions. The device based on pristine  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  showed a significant reduction of PCEs with  $ 80.4\% $  performance loss after storing in  $ 50 - 60\% $  relative humidity (RH) at room temperature for 1000 hours (Figure 5e). These results strongly indicate that the hydrophobic and insoluble QA layer served as a protective coating against degradation of the perovskite layer by moisture.

# Conclusion

In summary, we have demonstrated a new passivation strategy by using a low-cost industrial organic pigment, quinacridone (QA), as multifunctional passivation agent for halide perovskite thin films to achieve highly efficient and stable PSCs. In a first of its kind, an insoluble QA passivation layer was prepared by thermal annealing of a solution processed thin film. The energy levels of the QA layer enabled cascading, directional hole transfer from the perovskite to hole transport layers, while inhibiting electron transfer, which increased hole extraction efficiencies and decreased charge recombination, respectively. Moreover, the strong interactions between QA and perovskite via C=O…Pb Lewis acid/base coordination effectively reduce the surface defects of perovskite thin films and suppressed the trap-assisted nonradiative recombination. PSCs based on halide perovskite thin films with QA coating achieved high PCEs of up to 21% with significantly suppressed hysteresis. The hydrophobicity of QA coating layers greatly increased the water contact angle and improve the stability of the passivated perovskite thin films and PSCs. This QA passivation strategy could also be applied on triple-cation mixed-halide perovskite systems to achieve efficient and stable PSCs. This work suggests surface passivation of halide perovskite thin films by low solubility industrial pigments is a promising strategy to address the long-term stability issues of PSCs. Combining low cost existing organic pigments and derivatives with emerging halide perovskites paves a new route towards high performance hybrid material systems.
