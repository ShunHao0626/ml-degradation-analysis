# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic halide perovskite materials have attracted much attention because of their outstanding optoelectronic properties, such as strong light absorption and long carrier transport lengths   $  [1-4]  $  . In the past decade, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has increased rapidly from 3.8% to 25.6% since 2009, demonstrating their significant potential in the photovoltaic application   $  [5]  $  . However, it is still a challenge to further increase the efficiency and stability of PSCs by the reported methods   $  [6,7]  $  . The commonly employed spin-coating creates the polycrystalline perovskite films with a large number of grain boundaries (GBs) due to the presence of uncoordinated octahedrons in the precursor solution   $  [8,9]  $  , which reduces the charge transfer rate and induces the non-radiative charge recombination   $  [10]  $  . High crystallization quality and low defect state density of perovskite films are essential for high-performance devices. Herein, we develop a facile and effective method using potassium hexafluorophosphate (KPF $ _6 $ ) to fabricate perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with highly improved power conversion efficiency (PCE) and outstanding stability. The corresponding mechanisms of the improved crystallization process, grain boundaries (GBs) passivation, and internal ionic defect passivation of the perovskite films are systematically studied. The results indicate that the introduced PF $ _6 $  can promote crystallization by increasing fully coordinated intermediate groups in the perovskite solution and compensate for halide vacancies, which can modify ionic point defects in the perovskite films, resulting in reduced non-radiative recombination. Meanwhile, K $ ^+ $  ions are predominantly distributed at the GBs, which not only benefits the device efficiency but also significantly reduces the hysteresis due to the passivation effect. The champion passivated device presents a PCE of 22.04%, with a significantly improved PCE of around 16% compared with that of the control device (18.99%), retaining 80% of its initial PCE after 1000 h of light soaking without any encapsulation. Our work provides a defect passivation method for further improving both the PCE and stability of PSCs and can also be extended to other devices.

Additive engineering has been considered to be a simple and effective method to improve the crystal quality and reduce the defect state density of perovskite films, thus boosting the overall device performance [13-16]. In 2014, Jen et al. firstly introduced 1, 8-diiodooctane (DIO) to improve the crystallization of perovskite thin films by controlling the rate of perovskite crystallization, leading to an efficient improvement of PCE from  $ 9.0\% $  up to  $ 11.8\% $  for PSCs [17]. Following this work, different organic additives, including organic halide salts, Lewis acid-base adduct, ionic liquids, fullerenes, and polymers, have been widely employed [18-25]. # 2. Results and discussion

Fig. 1a depicts the detailed fabrication process. Firstly, the  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  films were fabricated by spin coating on ITO substrates, and then the perovskite precursor solution mixed with different molar ratios of  $ \mathrm{KPF_6} $  as the additive were dropped on the ITO/ $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  substrate. Then,  $ 150~\mu \mathrm{L} $  of chlorobenzene anti-solvent was slowly dropped onto it at a right time. Finally, the prepared films were annealed at  $ 150^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 15\mathrm{min} $ . For comparison, the controlled devices were also fabricated similarly by the above procedure without adding the  $ \mathrm{KPF_6} $  in the perovskite precursor solution. Fig. 1b displays that device structure with a planar N-I-P PSCs of ITO/ $ \mathrm{SnO_2 / Cs_{0.05}(FA_{0.83}MA_{0.17})_{0.95}Pb(I_{0.83}Br_{0.17})_3} $  with  $ \mathrm{KPF_6 / Spiro - OMeTAD / Ag} $ . The cross-sectional scanning electron microscope (SEM) image of the complete device including the ITO,  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ ,  $ \mathrm{Cs_{0.05}(FA_{0.83}MA_{0.17}^{*}{}_{0.95}Pb(I_{0.83}Br_{0.17})_3} $  with  $ \mathrm{KPF_6} $ , Spiro-OMeTAD and Ag layer is shown in Fig. 1c. Notably, the perovskite film modified with  $ \mathrm{KPF_6} $  has larger grain sizes comparing to that in the controlled device (Fig. S1), leading to the formation of perovskite-type monolayer grains, which is considered as the ideal structure for the process of rapid charge transfer in the vertical direction in the previous investigations [35,36]. Although the GBs exist in the films, they are vertical to the substrate, thus leading to a minor influence on the charge transfer [37-40]. Fig. 1d-f show the full XPS spectrum and the narrow scan of the F 1s and K 2p of the perovskite film without and with  $ \mathrm{KPF_6} $ , which demonstrates that the perovskite film modified with  $ \mathrm{KPF_6} $  exhibits F 1s and K 2p peaks, indicating the successful incorporation of  $ \mathrm{KPF_6} $  into the perovskite film while the perovskite film without  $ \mathrm{KPF_6} $  shows no corresponding peaks. Fig. S2 show the full XPS spectrum of the perovskite film with  $ \mathrm{KPF_6} $  and the narrow scan of the C 1s and K 2p to better display the K 2p peak.

To clarify the effects of   $  KPF_{6}  $   on the morphology and the crystallinity of the perovskite films, the corresponding SEM images and the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were studied. Fig. 2a-d present the top-view SEM images of perovskite films without and with different   $  KPF_{6}  $   molar ratios, and statistical particle size distributions are also shown in Fig. 2e. The perovskite film without   $  KPF_{6}  $   exhibits some voids and pinholes (Fig. 2a) and displays the average grain size of   $  \sim240  $   nm (Fig. 2e). As the molar ratios of   $  KPF_{6}  $   increase from 0.3% to 0.5%, the perovskite average grain size significantly increases from   $  \sim350  $   nm to   $  \sim460  $   nm and the perovskite film becomes denser and more uniform as shown in Fig. 2b, c, and e, showing that the introduction of   $  KPF_{6}  $   can promote the perovskite film quality. The perovskite average grain size decreases to   $  \sim400  $   nm and pinholes emerge again as the molar ratio of   $  KPF_{6}  $   further increases to 0.7% (Fig. 2d and e). The decreased grain sizes are mainly attributed to excess   $  KPF_{6}  $   additives in the perovskite film disturbing perovskite growth, leading to the increase of GBs [41]. Thus, the optimized molar ratio (0.5%) of   $  KPF_{6}  $   is important to obtain a perovskite film with better film quality. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements also show

that the perovskite film with 0.5% molar ratio of KPF $ _6 $  has smoother surface and larger grain sizes compared with the film without KPF $ _6 $  additive (Fig. S3). According to the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns showed Fig. 2f, the perovskite films without and with KPF $ _6 $  additive exhibit similar diffraction peaks at 14.15°, 28.46°, and 31.81°, corresponding to the (110), (220), and (310) crystal planes of perovskite phase, respectively [42], which indicates that the introduction of KPF $ _6 $  does not change crystal structure [43]. However, the (110) diffraction peak shifts to the small-angle side for the perovskite film with KPF $ _6 $  additive (Fig. S4), demonstrating the increase of lattice constants, which is most probably resulted from the partial substitution of small radius of halide ion by the large PF $ _6 $ . In addition, the increased diffraction peak intensities from 9629 of the control sample to 14193 of perovskite film prepared with KPF $ _6 $  molar ratios of 0.5%, and the decreased full width at half-maximum (FWHM) of the corresponding (110) peak are observed, which indicates the improved crystallinity of perovskite films, and this is consistent with the SEM measurements (Fig. 2g). To further reveal the reasons for improving the crystallinity of perovskite films, the UV–vis spectra of precursor solutions without and with KPF $ _6 $  are used to demonstrate the various intermediate groups including the uncoordinated intermediate groups and fully coordinated intermediate groups. From the previous investigations, only the fully-coordinated intermediate groups are beneficial to crystallization [44], while the uncoordinated intermediate groups lead to the formation of polycrystalline films [9]. In Fig. S5, the concentrations of uncoordinated intermediate groups including [Pb(I + PF $ _6 $ )L $ _5 $ ] $ ^{+} $ , [Pb(I + PF $ _6 $ ) $ _2 $ L $ _4 $ ], [Pb(I + PF $ _6 $ ) $ _3 $ L $ _3 $ ] $ ^{-} $  and [Pb(I + PF $ _6 $ ) $ _4 $ L $ _2 $ ] $ ^{2-} $  decrease for the film with KPF $ _6 $  due to the replacement of the organic ligands by PF $ _6 $ , leading to increased the number of fully coordinated [Pb(I + PF $ _6 $ ) $ _6 $ ] $ ^{4-} $ .

We calculate the ratios of   $  (\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{Br}+\mathrm{P})  $   to Pb defined as   $  (\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{Br}+\mathrm{P})/\mathrm{Pb}  $   in the films displayed in Fig. 3a, and atomic ratios of   $  (\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{Br}+\mathrm{P})  $   and Pb obtained from the energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) data with different   $  KPF_{6}  $   molar ratios are shown in Table S1. The   $  (\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{Br}+\mathrm{P})/\mathrm{Pb}  $   is 2.81 for perovskite film without   $  KPF_{6}  $ , while the   $  (\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{Br}+\mathrm{P})/\mathrm{Pb}  $   increases to 2.98 for the perovskite film with 0.7%   $  KPF_{6}  $ , which is quite close to the ideal atomic ratio (3.00). The increased ratio from 2.81 to 2.98 is due to the introduction of   $  PF_{6}^{-}  $  which can compensate for halogen vacancies. The XPS spectra of perovskite films without and with   $  KPF_{6}  $   are further studied (Fig. 3b), from which the peaks ascribed to Pb   $  4f_{7/2}  $ , Pb   $  4f_{5/2}  $ , and Pb 0 are detected in the perovskite film without   $  KPF_{6}  $ , while the signals of Pb 0 disappear in the perovskite film with   $  KPF_{6}  $   [12], indicating the reduction of halide vacancies. The modulation of states of the perovskite film is mainly contributed to the compensation of halide vacancies by incorporation of   $  PF_{6}  $   [43]. From the above results, the schematic of the perovskite film fabrication is shown in Fig. 4d and Fig. S8. The uncoordinated octahedrons in the perovskite precursor solution will accumulate with each other during the nucleation and growth of the film, thus hindering the growth of crystal grains (Fig. S8). After introducing the  $ \mathrm{PF}_6 $ , the number of fully coordinated octahedrons increases and further promote the growth of perovskite film, leading to larger grain sizes. Meanwhile,  $ \mathrm{K}^+ $  ions are located at the GBs to passivate the GB defects of perovskite film by coordinating with bromide ions. Therefore, the introduction of  $ \mathrm{KPF}_6 $  into the perovskite solution can not only increase the number of fully-coordinated intermediate groups, which is beneficial for crystallization but also passivate the internal defects and GBs of perovskite films. This double passivation is beneficial for improving the performance of PSCs.

The stability of PSCs without and with   $  KPF_{6}  $   is displayed in Fig. 7. We study the long-term stability of the PSCs influenced by   $  KPF_{6}  $   under ambient conditions (40–50% humidity) after 4400 h in Fig. 7a. The PCE of the modified devices retains 80% of its original PCE value whereas the PCE of the control devices decreases to 40% of its original PCE value after 2000 h under ambient conditions. The devices with   $  KPF_{6}  $   show better stability than the control devices under ambient air. Fig. 7b displays the results of the photo-stability of the devices in nitrogen atmosphere (20 °C–25 °C) under 1 sun continuous light illumination. The devices performance was observed regularly in 1000 h. In Fig. 7b, the PCE of the device with   $  KPF_{6}  $   maintains more than 80% of its initial efficiency after 1000 h. However, the control device can only maintain ~15% of its original PCE value after 500 h. This shows the device modified with   $  KPF_{6}  $   obviously improves the photo-stability of the PSCs comparing with control device. To further prove improving the photostability of the devices by the introduction of   $  KPF_{6}  $ , the evolution of XRD for perovskite films without and with   $  KPF_{6}  $   as a function of light irradiation time is shown in Fig. 7c. It is observed that the intensity of the (110) diffraction peak of the control perovskite film decreases, and an additional XRD diffraction peak at 12.49° attributed to the (101) diffraction peak of   $  PbI_{2}  $ , is observed after 1000 h of continuous light. However, the (110) peak intensity of the perovskite film with   $  KPF_{6}  $   is only slightly reduced and the (101) peak intensity related to   $  PbI_{2}  $   is obviously lower than that in control film after 1000 h of continuous light. This also indicates that the incorporation of   $  KPF_{6}  $   can significantly improve the perovskite structure stability. The improvement of device long-term stability and light stability can be mainly attributed to two aspects. On the one hand, higher crystallinity is obtained by introducing   $  PF_{6}  $   (Fig. 2), which can resist moisture penetration during aging. On the other hand, the defect states in the perovskite films are reduced, because

$ \mathrm{PF}_6^- $  compensates for the halogen vacancies in the perovskite film and the  $ \mathbf{K}^{+} $  ions passivate the GBs, thereby inhibiting ion migration and enhancing stability.

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, we have introduced   $  KPF_{6}  $   as a novel additive into perovskite films. Benefiting from the   $  KPF_{6}  $   passivation at the internal defects and GBs of perovskite films, trap densities significantly decrease from   $  5.78 \times 10^{15}  $   to   $  4.05 \times 10^{15}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  . Meanwhile, the grain sizes and crystallinity of the perovskite films are improved by introducing   $  PF_{6}  $   ions. Subsequently, this method is successful in enhancing the PCE and stability of PSCs. The optimized PSC with   $  KPF_{6}  $   presents a large Voc of 1.167 V, a Jsc of 24.09 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   and a FF of 78.26%, yielding a champion PCE of 22.04%, which is an increase of   $  \sim16\%  $   than that of the control device (18.99%). The optimized PSC shows negligible hysteresis and remarkable stability. This work not only illustrates an effective method to promote crystallization by adding fully coordinated intermediate groups but also displays a way to heal the ionic defects achieved by balancing halide ions and passivating the GB defects, which can be widely extended to other optoelectronic devices.
