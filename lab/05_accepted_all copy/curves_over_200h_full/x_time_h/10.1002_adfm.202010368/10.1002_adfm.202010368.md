# Two-Dimensional Metal–Organic Frameworks-Based Grain Termination Strategy Enables High-Efficiency Perovskite Photovoltaics with Enhanced Moisture and Thermal Stability

Lele Qiu, Kai Xing, Jian Zhang,  $  ^{*}  $   Yulin Yang,  $  ^{*}  $   Wei Cao, Xuesong Zhou, Ke Zhu, Debin Xia, and Ruiqing Fan  $  ^{*}  $

Perovskite degradation induced by surface defects and imperfect grain boundaries of films seriously damages the performance of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Meanwhile, conventional organic molecules cannot maintain the long-time passivation effects under the stimulation of external environmental factors. Here, efficient and stable grain passivation in perovskite films is realized by preparing formic acid-functionalized 2D metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) as the terminated agent. Through robust interactions between exposed active sites and   $  PbI_{2}  $  , the 2D MOFs tightly caps the surface of   $  PbI_{2}  $  -terminated perovskite grains to stabilize the perovskite phases and aids the adhesion of adjacent grains. The MOFs mainly distributed at the grain boundaries of the perovskite film is directly observed at the microscopic scale. The modified perovskite films have regular morphology, lower defect density, and superior optoelectronic properties. Benefiting from the suppressed charge recombination and faster charge extraction, a power conversion efficiency of 21.28% is achieved for the best-performing PSC device. The unencapsulated PSCs with the MOFs modification maintain 88% and 81% of their initial efficiency after 750 h heating at 85 °C under   $  N_{2}  $   atmosphere and more than 1000 h storage in ambient environment (25 °C, RH ≈ 40%), respectively.

# 1. Introduction

Since the advent of all-solid-state organic metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) in 2012, the certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of the lab-prepared devices has exceeded  $ 25\% $  in a short time, leveraging the exceptional optoelectronic properties of perovskite materials.[1-4] Compared with market-dominant silicon solar cells, the advantages of low cost and easy preparation seem to provide tremendous potential for the commercialization of PSCs. However, the long-term stability of the PSCs in the realistic

Perovskite films are usually prepared through a low temperature solution-based process, which causes numerous defects at the surface and grain boundaries of the polycrystalline films.  $  ^{[16,17]}  $   This situation not only leads to serious recombination behaviors and deteriorates photovoltaic performance of the PSCs, but also induces the initial decomposition of perovskite films.  $  ^{[7,18,19]}  $   Additive engineering and surface treatment strategies were developed to address this issue by increasing grain size or passivating surface and grain boundaries to enhance the PCE and stability of PSCs. For instance, organic molecules or polymers containing Lewis base functional groups are utilized as passivation reagents to interact with undercoordinated lead ions, which can effectively reduce the electronic defects with relatively low formation energy and improve the overall performance of the PSCs.  $  ^{[20-22]}  $   Tailoring the molecule structures containing amine or/and carboxyl groups makes the molecular passivation effects optimal, thereby maximizing device performance.  $  ^{[23-25]}  $   However, the effects of many passivation reagents cannot be maintained for a long time under the stimulation of environmental stresses for the inherent migration and decomposition. Furthermore, excessive   $  PbI_{2}  $   in precursor solutions is often adopted to generate   $  PbI_{2}  $  -terminated perovskite or form

operation processes is greatly subject to external factors for the functional layers are sensitive to moisture, heat, light, etc.  $  ^{[5-7]}  $   This has become the critical obstacle for photovoltaic devices based on metal halide perovskite materials to seize market share. Over the years, the phase separation problems of mixed halide perovskite under light irradiation have been recognized and substantially suppressed by compositional tuning or additive engineering.  $  ^{[8-10]}  $   In addition, interface engineering and ultraviolet light filtration are also efficient solutions to relieve the light-induced decomposition of perovskite devices.  $  ^{[11,12]}  $   Various encapsulation schemes have also been developed to isolate the perovskite photovoltaics from corrosive environments or make even the methylammonium (MA)-containing perovskite devices withstand the heat test.  $  ^{[13-15]}  $   However, the packaging materials face the problems of breakage and increased manufacturing cost. Therefore, it is still very essential to fundamentally understand and improve the stability of PSCs against moisture and heat.

However, it is difficult to precisely control the residual amount of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  in the final perovskite films and the accumulation of large amounts of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  is not conducive to charge transfer and long-term stability of PSCs.[26,27]

Due to the high specific surface area and stable ordered structure, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) were widely utilized in optoelectronic and energy conversion fields.  $  ^{[28,29]}  $   Although perovskite nanocrystals or quantum dots grown in MOFs channels possess high stability and superior performance, it is unrealistic to incorporate MOFs with a few hundred nanometers or even microns to prepare perovskite films with regular morphology.  $  ^{[30-32]}  $   Therefore, MOF materials were usually treated as an interlayer or a scaffold for the most successful cases in the perovskite photovoltaic field.  $  ^{[33-35]}  $   But the tunable crystallographic orientation and functionalized framework of MOF materials have seldom been considered for regulating perovskite photovoltaics. Herein, we designed and prepared a formic acid-functionalized zinc-based MOFs Zn-cbpp (  $  [Zn(cbpp)(HCOO)]n  $  , Hcbpp = 1-[4-carboxylbenzyl]-3-[pyrzin-2-yl]pyrazole) through the molecular self-assembly strategy to modify the state-of-the-art triple mixed cation perovskite films. The as-prepared Zn-cbpp exhibited a 2D ordered structure with plenty of nitrogen and oxygen active sites along the extension, suggesting a promising terminated agent. Based on the experimental results, we confirmed that Zn-cbpp mainly existing at the grain boundaries could produce robust interactions with perovskite grains through coordination bonds and hydrogen bonds, and simultaneously act as a cross-linking agent to anchor adjacent perovskite grains, which eliminated pinholes and cracks in the light absorption films. Due to the suppressed charge recombination and faster charge transport, the PCE of the corresponding device is increased from 19.59% to 21.28% compared with the control device. Therefore, this grain termination strategy based on the 2D MOFs effectively reduces the defect density and improves the optoelectronic properties of perovskite films. More importantly, the moisture and thermal stability of Zn-cbpp-modified PSCs are significantly improved. This work presents an efficient approach by employing MOF materials as a terminated agent to realize high efficiency and stable PSCs, which will further encourage researchers to explore the application of MOFs in the field of photoelectronic devices.

# 2. Results and Discussion

The organic ligand Hcbpp containing one carboxyl group and two nitrogen-atom heterocyclic rings was chosen and reacted with low-cost zinc metal salt to construct 2D-functionalized MOFs through a simple solvothermal method. Formic acids produced by the hydrolysis of the solvent N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) under acidic condition were cleverly used to crosslink zinc ions to generate the 2D framework and simultaneously create more active sites. Acting as a terminal agent, as-prepared Zn-cbpp incorporated in the perovskite precursor solution was expected to react with   $  PbI_{2}  $   to form Lewis acid-base adducts. During the spin-coating and calcination of the precursor films, perovskite continuously grew along

$ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ -Zn-cbpp adducts, and resulted in Zn-cbpp-terminated perovskite grains. The 2D Zn-cbpp also facilitated the connection of adjacent perovskite grains, eliminating the pinholes or cracks that may exist between the perovskite grains and avoiding the accumulation of excessive unreacted  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  at the grain boundaries (Figure 1a). Figure 1b,c shows the crystalline structure of as-prepared Zn-cbpp, which was identified by single crystal X-ray diffraction (Details see Tables S1 and S2, Supporting Information, CCDC: 2040061). From the  $ a $ -axis perspective, large numbers of oxygen atoms and nitrogen atoms containing lone pairs of electrons are exposed, which provides enough active sites for the interactions with perovskite grains. The ordered structure of Zn-cbpp provides convenience for precise regulation. The layers are stacked by hydrogen bonds between pyrazines in the ligands to produce an ordered supramolecular structure (Figure S1, Supporting Information). From the Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra, it can be seen that no stretching vibration peak of the carboxyl group  $ (1708~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}) $  in ligands is found in Zn-cbpp, and instead there are two stretching vibration peaks at 1556 and  $ 1402~\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $  attributed to the carboxylate group, suggesting the successful formation of Zn-cbpp (Figure S2a, Supporting Information). The powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern further confirmed the phase-pure of Zn-cbpp based on the simulated diffraction pattern (Figure S2b, Supporting Information). The weak light absorption in the visible light region and high decomposition temperature of Zn-cbpp both suggest its potential as an additive for perovskite films (Figure S2c,d, Supporting Information). In order to further investigate the stability of Zn-cbpp under the conditions of device application, the XRD measurements were performed on samples before and after soaking in different solvents after  $ 24\mathrm{h} $  under air conditions. As shown in Figure S3, Supporting Information, the diffraction peaks matched well with the pristine sample and most of main peaks are maintained, indicating the excellent stability of as-prepared Zn-cbpp. Electrostatic potential (ESP) was used to analyze the electronic structure of Zn-cbpp asymmetrical cell and it can be found that the highest electron density is mainly distributed on the carboxylate groups, followed by the undercoordinated nitrogen atoms on the pyrazine at both ends (Figure 1d). These electron-rich atoms or groups can form Lewis acid-base adducts through strong coordination bonds with undercoordinated lead ions.[36,37] Furthermore, the main diffraction peaks of (200) and (300) planes of Zn-cbpp powder suggest that the exposed crystal planes in the synthesized sample are mainly along  $ a $ -axis (Figure 1b), which is beneficial to the interactions with perovskite precursors due to the small steric hindrance (Figure S4, Supporting Information). Hence, we proposed that exposed active sites in the Zn-cbpp framework may form strong interactions with  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and thereby passivate the surface and grain boundaries of perovskite films.

The photovoltaic performance of PSCs modified with different concentration of Zn-cbpp was studied and photovoltaic parameters statistical distribution box diagrams were given in Figure 5. The PCE of PSCs increased with the added concentration of Zn-cbpp and obtained optimum at  $ 2.0\mathrm{mg}\cdot \mathrm{mL}^{-1} $ . The change of open-circuit voltage  $ (V_{\mathrm{oc}}) $  was mainly responsible for the improvement of PCE due to the excellent crystal quality and low defect density of perovskite films. Excessive accumulation of Zn-cbpp at the grain boundaries would cause the fill factor of PSCs decrease under high doping concentration, resulting in relatively poor performance. Figure 6a shows the  $ J - V $  curves of the best-performing PSCs with and without Zn-cbpp modification under reverse scan direction. The control device (without Zn-cbpp modification) exhibited a  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  of  $ 1.108\mathrm{V} $ , a short-circuit current density  $ (J_{\mathrm{sc}}) $  of  $ 22.90\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $ , and a fill factor of  $ 77.2\% $ , for a PCE of  $ 19.59\% $  whereas the Zn-cbpp-modified device showed a significantly improved PCE of  $ 21.28\% $ , corresponding to a  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  of  $ 1.155\mathrm{V} $ , a  $ J_{\mathrm{sc}} $  of  $ 23.32\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $ , and a fill factor of  $ 79.0\% $ . The integrated  $ J_{\mathrm{sc}} $  was obtained from the incident photon to current conversion efficiency (IPCE) spectra (Figure 6b) and the values for PSCs with and without Zn-cbpp modification were 22.79 and  $ 22.27\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $ , respectively, which matched well with the results from the  $ J - V $  curves ( $ <3\% $  discrepancy). Specifically, Zn-cbpp modified PSC exhibits higher IPCE value in the range of 650 to  $ 750~\mathrm{nm} $ , which may be due to the changes in the composition of the perovskite film at the grain boundaries. In order to further prove the feasibility of Zn-cbpp in improving the properties of perovskite films, the application of Zn-cbpp in another representative perovskite material,  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ , was also investigated. As shown in Figure S14, Supporting Information, the performance of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -based PSCs with Zn-cbpp modification was also significantly improved, which indicated the

universality of this material. In order to further explain why the Zn-cbpp modification can improve the performance of PSCs, transient photovoltage (TPV) and transient photocurrent (TPC) decay were used to analyze the charge transfer and recombination behaviors in the devices. As shown in Figure 6c, the photovoltage decay time for the PSC with Zn-cbpp modification was 56.18   $  \mu  $  s while the control device was 35.88   $  \mu  $  s. The prolonged decay time indicates that the charge recombination can be efficiently suppressed, which coincides with the higher   $  V_{oc}  $   and fill factor of Zn-cbpp-modified PSCs. The photocurrent decay time was reduced from 6.39 to 4.73   $  \mu  $  s for the PSCs without and with Zn-cbpp modification (Figure 6d). The decrease in photocurrent decay time may be due to the passivation of defects and elimination of voids at grain boundaries, which makes the extraction of charge carriers more favorable. The faster charge carrier extraction and suppressed charge recombination in Zn-cbpp-modified PSCs also help to alleviate the hysteresis behavior, and the hysteresis factor is reduced from 10.6% to 7.2% for Zn-cbpp-modified PSCs (Figure S15, Supporting Information). This simple grain termination strategy does not require secondary operations and can also greatly improve the stability of the perovskite films against moisture and heat, which will be deeply discussed below.

The moisture stability of perovskite films was first investigated and Figure 7a displayed the photos of the perovskite films with and without Zn-cbpp modification before and after storage at room temperature with a humidity of  $ \approx 85\% $  for 7 days. It can be clearly seen that the Zn-cbpp-modified film still retained the original black-brown appearance after 7 days of high humidity aging whereas most of the untreated perovskite film turned yellow, indicating that the pristine perovskite structure has changed significantly. However, as the heating time extended, XRD results indicated the intensification of   $  PbI_{2}  $  -associated diffraction peaks. The deterioration of performance may be rationalized by continuous decomposition of the surface and grain boundaries of perovskite films into   $  PbI_{2}  $   upon heating. During this process, the more unstable MA in the system decreased and caused local lattice distortion of the CsFAMA perovskite, which partially converted into   $  \delta  $  -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  . This process resulted in significant changes in the morphology of the perovskite films and continuously decreased the performance (Figure 7c,d). As expected, the modification of Zn-cbpp alleviated the thermal decomposition of perovskite films and restrained the occurrence of phase transition (Figure S18c,d, Supporting Information).
