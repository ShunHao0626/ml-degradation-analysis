# Atomic Layer Deposition of an Effective Interface Layer of TiN for Efficient and Hysteresis-Free Mesoscopic Perovskite Solar Cells

Rohit D. Chavan, Mohammad Mahdi Tavakoli, Daniel Prochowicz, Pankaj Yadav, Shivani S. Lote, Sangram P. Bhoite, Ajaysing Nimbalkar, and Chang Kook Hong  $  ^{*}  $

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have experienced outstanding advances in power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) by employing new electron transport layers (ETLs), interface engineering, optimizing perovskite morphology, and improving charge collection efficiency. In this work, we study the role of a new ultrathin interface layer of titanium nitride (TiN) conformally deposited on a mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   (mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $  ) scaffold using the atomic layer deposition method. Our characterization results revealed that the presence of TiN at the ETL/perovskite interface improves the charge collection as well as reduces the interface recombination. We find that the morphology (grain size) and optical properties of the perovskite film deposited on the optimized mp-  $  TiO_{2}/TiN  $   ETL are improved drastically, leading

to devices with a maximum PCE of 19.38% and a high open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ) of 1.148 V with negligible hysteresis and improved environmental (  $  \sim  $  40% RH) and thermal (80 °C) stabilities.

KEYWORDS: perovskite solar cell, interface layer, recombination, hysteresis free, stability

# INTRODUCTION

Fabrication of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has emerged as a new photovoltaic (PV) technology by virtue of their high power conversion efficiency (PCE).  $  ^{1-5}  $   Since the first reported PCE of 3.8% in 2009,  $  ^{6}  $   the recently achieved PCE has exceeded 25%  $  ^{7}  $   owing to their simple and scalable manufacturing techniques as well as the excellent optoelectronic properties of metal-halide perovskites, including high absorption coefficient, long carrier diffusion length, and low trap state.  $  ^{3,5,8-13}  $   These semiconducting materials can be deposited in the form of a thin film through a variety of techniques such as evaporation technique,  $  ^{14}  $   chemical vapor deposition,  $  ^{15}  $   ink-jet printing,  $  ^{16}  $   flash infrared annealing,  $  ^{17}  $   one or two-step solution deposition process,  $  ^{18,19}  $   antisolvent treatment,  $  ^{20}  $   spray pyrolysis,  $  ^{21}  $   and dip coating.  $  ^{22}  $   However, the low reproducibility and long-term stability issues are still the main obstacles for future commercialization of the PSCs.  $  ^{23}  $

Electron transport layer (ETL) is an important layer in PSCs, which plays many critical functions including the separation and transfer of photo-induced carriers. The most widely used inorganic ETLs in the PSCs are   $  TiO_{2}  $  , ZnO, and   $  SnO_{2}  $   because of their excellent electronic and optical properties.  $  ^{24-26}  $   Among them,   $  TiO_{2}  $  -based PSCs currently have the highest PCE and stability.  $  ^{27-29}  $   However,   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETL suffers from low conductivity, poor ultraviolet (UV) stability, and high surface defects. Recent works revealed that N-doped   $  TiO_{2}  $   can enhance the efficiency of PSCs because of the reduction in the charge-transfer resistance.  $  ^{46}  $   Because titanium nitride (TiN) has a wide band gap of 3.4 eV, excellent electrical conductivity, chemical stability, and good thermal and mechanical properties, it could be a good alternative as an interface layer in the PSCs.  $  ^{47,48}  $

Hence, in this work, we introduce an ultrathin layer of TiN as an effective interface layer on the mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $  surface using the atomic layer deposition (ALD) technique and investigate its effect on the PV performance of complete PSC devices. The perovskite film deposited on the optimized mp-  $  TiO_{2}/TiN  $  ETL shows superior optoelectronic properties, band energy alignment, and morphological properties as compared to the perovskite film deposited on pristine mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $ . We also demonstrate that the PSCs based on mp-  $  TiO_{2}/TiN  $  ETL show lower interfacial charge recombination and better charge carrier transfer, resulting in higher  $  V_{oc}  $ . As a result, our champion device yields a high PCE of 19.38% with negligible hysteresis and improved environmental [  $  \sim  $  40% relative humidity (RH)] and thermal (80 °C) stabilities compared to the pristine mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $ -based PSCs. This study provides a simple and effective approach for the passivation of mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $  defects and improvement of optoelectronic and morphological properties in the PSC field.

The observed lower amount of Cl atoms on the surface of mp- $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / TiN} $  ETL indicates that the reaction of ALD is completed (Figure S1c,d and Table S1). To further probe the presence of TiN on the surface of mp- $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  ETL, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed. Figure S2 shows the TEM images of the mp- $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / TiN} $  sample prepared by focused ion beam (FIB). We found that a thin layer of amorphous TiN is coated on the edge of the sample with an average thickness of  $ 1.9\mathrm{nm} $ . We also performed surface analysis of our samples using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements, as shown in Figures 2 and S3. The detailed XPS elemental analysis of both mp- $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  and mp- $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / TiN} $  films is shown in Tables S2 and S3. In the pristine mp- $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  film, the doublet peaks of octahedral  $ \mathrm{Ti^{4 + }} $ , i.e., Ti  $ 2\mathrm{p}_{3/2} $  and Ti  $ 2\mathrm{p}_{1/2} $ , are located at 458.2 and  $ 463.98\mathrm{eV} $ , which correlated well with the values reported for anatase  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  (Figure 2a). In turn, the doublet peaks of Ti  $ 2\mathrm{p}_{3/2}\mathrm{A} $  and Ti  $ 2\mathrm{p}_{1/2}\mathrm{A} $  for the mp- $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / TiN} $  film are located at 457.28 and  $ 463.43\mathrm{eV} $  (Figure 2d). Furthermore, the additional Ti  $ 2\mathrm{p}_{3/2}\mathrm{B} $  and Ti  $ 2\mathrm{p}_{1/2}\mathrm{B} $  peaks shifted toward lower binding energies, which are located at 456.04 and  $ 461.38\mathrm{eV} $ , respectively, and represent the formation of Ti-N bonding. The difference between the peaks of  $ \mathrm{Ti^{4 + }} $  are mainly due to the binding of Ti to lower electronegative species as compared to oxygen, such as the formation of Ti-N bonds. The O 1s XPS spectrum of mp- $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  was deconvoluted into three peaks with energies of 529.7, 531.5, and  $ 532.5\mathrm{eV} $ , which can be assigned to the lattice oxygen in the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  structure (scan A), the oxygen deficiency (scan B, and the hydroxyl groups on the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  surface (scan C), respectively (Figure 2b,e). The intensity of the  $ 531.5\mathrm{eV} $  peak has been shown to decrease after deposition of an ultrathin TiN layer because of passivation of oxygen defect at the surface of  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  by N atoms (Tables S2 and S3). The N 1s spectrum of the mp- $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / TiN} $  film shows a sharp peak centered at  $ 396.2\mathrm{eV} $  (scan A) and a shoulder peak at 398 eV (scan B) attributed to Ti-N and O-Ti-N bonds, respectively (Figure 2g). The O-Ti-N bonds on the surface of  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  were found due to interstitial incorporation of nitrogen atoms that passivates the oxygen defects on the surface of  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ . The decrease of the oxygen defects upon TiN passivation is beneficial for improvement of the conductivity of the mp- $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  film as discussed below. The C 1s (reflecting C-C/C-H and C-O bonding) and Cl 2p XPS spectra can be assigned to the traces of solvent molecules adsorbed onto the surface and the contaminants after the TiN deposition process, respectively (Figure 2f,h). Recently, Tan et al. demonstrated that Cl-doped  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  can improve the performance and stability of the PSCs, and they found that the interfacial Cl ions lead to strong electronic coupling and chemical binding at the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2}/ $  perovskite junction.[29]

To investigate the reason behind PCE enhancement, low hysteresis, and high stability, we further studied the morphology and crystallinity of the perovskite films deposited on mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $   and mp-  $  TiO_{2}/TiN  $   ETLs. Figure 4a shows the powder X-Ray Diffraction patterns of the perovskite films deposited on the ETLs with and without TiN. These results indicate the presence of similar patterns, with the main perovskite peaks located at   $  \sim14.0  $   and   $  \sim28.0^{\circ}  $   originating from the (101) and (202) planes of the three-dimensional perovskite phase, respectively. However, the intensity of diffraction peaks for the perovskite film deposited on mp-  $  TiO_{2}/TiN  $   is much stronger than that of the reference film, indicating its improved crystallinity. Top-view SEM images of both perovskite films show a compact and pinhole-free morphology (Figure 4b,c). We observed that the perovskite film deposited on mp-  $  TiO_{2}/TiN  $   ETL has larger grain size (  $  \sim800  $   nm) than the reference film (  $  \sim600  $   nm), which can explain the higher value of   $  V_{oc}  $   and lower hysteresis in   $  TiO_{2}/TiN  $ -based PSCs.  $  ^{59,60}  $   Figure 4d,e shows the atomic force microscopy (AFM) images of the perovskite films deposited on both ETLs, which are in good agreement with SEM results in terms of morphology and crystal size. The root-mean-square surface roughness values for perovskite films deposited on mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $   and mp-  $  TiO_{2}/TiN  $   ETLs were estimated to be 28.30 and 17.74 nm, respectively (Figure S8a,b). These results highlight the high quality of the perovskite film deposited on mp-  $  TiO_{2}/TiN  $   with lower roughness, which is beneficial for reducing the charge recombination at the ETL/perovskite interface.  $  ^{61}  $   TiN has a cubic crystal structure of NaCl-type with nonbonding lone pair of electrons at N atoms. A plausible explanation to the improved crystallinity and grain size of perovskite films deposited on mp-  $  TiO_{2}/TiN  $   is that the uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ions of the perovskite precursor may coordinate with the lone pair electrons of the N atoms, which is expected to retard the perovskite crystal growth (Figure 3a).

In general, the   $  V_{oc}  $   of a solar cell is affected by the recombination at the interfaces. In order to understand the role of TiN on   $  V_{oc}  $   and PCE enhancement, we study the potential reasons for the interface recombination in the representative PSCs using UV–visible, photoluminescence (PL), and time-resolved PL (TRPL) spectroscopies as well as UV photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) and Mott Schottky measurements. Figure 5a shows the UV–visible spectra of the perovskite films on mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $   and mp-  $  TiO_{2}/TiN  $   ETLs, which indicate the presence of similar absorption with an optical band gap of 1.59 eV. The PL spectra of the corresponding perovskite films show an emission peak at 780 nm confirming the optical band gap of the perovskite film.

# CONCLUSIONS

In summary, we have employed the ALD technique to conformally deposit an ultrathin TiN layer on the surface of mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $  ETL, which significantly improved the PV parameters of the mesoscopic PSCs. We found that the TiN functionalization increases the PCE from 17.1 to 19.38%, mainly due to enhancement of  $  V_{oc}  $  from 1.1 to 1.148 V. Our characterization results revealed that the observed PV enhancement is due to lower interfacial recombination, better charge transfer, and improved band alignment. Notably, the TiN-modified PSCs showed a negligible hysteresis with a great shelf-life and thermal stability as compared to the reference PSCs. These results demonstrate that the interface engineering at the ETL/perovskite interface using large band gap materials such as TiN can open new opportunities toward reproducible, efficient, and stable PSCs.

# EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

Materials.   $  TiO_{2}  $   paste (18 NRD) was purchased from Dyesol. Formamidinium iodide and methyl ammonium bromide were purchased from Greatcell Solar. Titanium(IV) isopropoxide (99.9%), 4-tert-butylpyridine (TBP, 96%), lithium bis(trifluoromethylsulphonyl)imide (95%), anhydrous ethanol (99.9%), chlorobenzene (95%), anhydrous N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%), acetonitrile (99.8%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.9%), lead iodide (  $  PbI_{2}  $  , 99.9%), lead bromide (  $  PbBr_{2}  $  , 99.99%), and PTAA were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Device Measurement. All devices were measured under a solar simulator (AM 1.5G) with a light intensity of   $  100 \, mW cm^{-2}  $  , calibrated by a silicon solar cell and a KG5 filter. The   $  J_{sc}  $   of PSCs was verified using the EQE technique using a Spectral Products DK240 monochromator and by scanning the wavelength from 300 to 800 nm.

Film Characterization. The quality (phase and crystallinity) of the perovskite films was investigated by an X-ray diffraction spectrometer (XRD, Rigaku, Japan) equipped with a Cu Kα line of 1.5410 Å. The morphology of the perovskite films was studied by a field emission scanning electron microscope (S-4700, Hitachi). AFM (XE-100 Advanced Scanning Probe Microscope, Park System) was employed to study the surface roughness of perovskite films. The composition of the films was analyzed by XPS (model name: MultiLab2000). Hall measurements were performed for all thin films (optimized thickness of mp-TiO₂ 200 nm) at a temperature of 300 K to measure the electrical properties of mp-TiO₂ and other films. An FIB electron microscope device with an operating voltage of 300 kV was used for fabrication and making field emission TEM cross-sectional observation samples (model name: FEI, Helios G3 CX). The optical properties of the perovskite films were analyzed by PL spectroscopy (f = 0.5 m, Acton Research Co., Spectrograph 500i. USA) equipped with an intensified CCD(PI-MAX3) camera (Princeton Instrument Co., IRY124, USA) and UV-visible measurement (model name: Cary 300 UV-vis). The lifetime of the perovskite films was characterized by TRPL spectroscopy using an inverted-type scanning confocal microscope (MicroTime-200, Picoquant, Germany). The excitation source in this measurement was a single-mode pulsed diode laser (470 nm with a ~30 pulse width). For TRPL measurement, a long-pass filter (HQ500lp, AHF) and a dichroic mirror (490 DCXR, AHF) were used. The pinhole for the laser was 75 μm, and a 700 nm long-pass filter (FEL0700, Thorlabs) and a single photon avalanche diode (PDM series, MPD) were employed to record the TRPL spectra. The band alignment of the PSCs was studied by UPS (model name: Axis-Supra).

Device Fabrication. The etched fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) substrates were cleaned using soap solution, deionized water, ethanol, and isopropanol. The c-  $  TiO_{2}  $  film (30 nm) was coated on cleaned FTO by spin-coating.  $  ^{66}  $  The mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $  films were deposited from a diluted solution of  $  TiO_{2}  $  paste in ethanol (1:6 weight ratio paste: ethanol). The mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $  films were spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 20 s on a c-  $  TiO_{2}  $  film, followed by sintering at 450 °C for 30 min in air.

The ALD TiN buffer layer was deposited on mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $  using an ALD equipment (NCD Lucida-D100, Korea). The reaction chamber contains titanium tetrachloride (  $  TiCl_{4}  $  ) and ammonia (  $  NH_{3}  $  ) precursor sources, where nitrogen (  $  N_{2}  $  ) was used as a carrier gas for both precursor sources. The working pressure of the reaction chamber was fixed at 0.6 Torr with an optimized temperature of 350°C. The deposition rate of the TiN film was estimated to be 0.13 nm/cycle under this condition, where the thickness of the TiN film was analyzed by in situ spectroscopic ellipsometry. During each cycle of TiN deposition, gases were flowed into the chamber in the sequence of  $  TiCl_{4}  $ ,  $  N_{2}  $ ,  $  NH_{3}  $ , and  $  N_{2}  $ .

A perovskite composition of   $  MA_{0.17}FA_{0.83}Pb(I_{0.83}Br_{0.17})_{3}  $   dissolved in a mixed solvent of DMF/DMSO (4:1 volume ratio) was used as an absorber layer.  $  ^{50}  $   The perovskite precursor solution was spin-coated in two-steps, 1000 and 6000 rpm for 10 and 30 s, respectively. In the second step, 110   $  \mu  $  L of chlorobenzene was dropped onto the perovskite layer, followed by annealing at 110 °C for 60 min in a nitrogen-filled glovebox. After cooling down the perovskite film, a solution of PTAA was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 30 s on top of the perovskite film. The solution of PTAA was prepared by dissolving PTAA in toluene (15 mg/1.5 mL) and adding 15   $  \mu  $  L solution of lithium bistrifluoromethane sulphonimidate in acetonitrile (170 mg/mL) and 7.5   $  \mu  $  L of TBP.  $  ^{20}  $   Finally, an 80 nm thick gold electrode was thermally evaporated to complete the device structure. The active area of this electrode was fixed at 0.09 cm  $  ^{2}  $  .
