# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic halide perovskites have attracted global interest owing to their unique optoelectronic properties such as high absorption coefficient, ambipolar charge transport, long carrier diffusion length, and low recombination rate   $  [1-7]  $  . Moreover, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of organic-inorganic halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has been boosted from 3.8% to 25.2% in a short period of time   $  [8-10]  $  , challenging commercial silicon-based solar cells. Despite these significant achievements, PSCs still suffer from severe stability issues   $  [11-13]  $  , which are a major drawback for immediate commercialization.

# ABSTRACT

For the global commercialization of highly efficient and stable perovskite solar cells (PSCs), it is necessary to effectively suppress the formation of various defects acting as nonradiative recombination sources in perovskite light-harvesting materials. Interfacial defects between the charge-selective layer and the perovskite are easily formed in the solution process used to fabricate perovskite films. In addition, owing to the difference in thermal expansion coefficients between the substrate and the perovskite film, internal residual tensile stress inevitably occurs, resulting in increased nonradiative recombination. Herein, a simple compositional engineering scheme for realizing efficient and stable PSCs, which incorporates acetamidinium bromide (AABr) as an additive into the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  lattice, is proposed. As an additive, AABr has been found to provide synergistic multiple passivation for both internal and interfacial defects. AABr was found to effectively release the tensile strain of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  film by forming a structure stabilized by NH-I hydrogen bonds, as evidenced by calculations based on density functional theory (DFT). Furthermore, the incorporated AABr additives created a charge carrier recombination barrier to enhance charge collection capability by reducing interfacial defects. Accordingly, a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 20.18\% $  was achieved using a planar device employing AABr-incorporated  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ . This was substantially higher than the  $ 18.32\% $  PCE of a pristine  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -based device. Notably, unencapsulated PSCs using AABr-incorporated  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  absorbers exhibited excellent long-term stability, maintaining  $ >95\% $  of initial PCE up to 1200 hours in ambient air.

The inevitably generated strain concentrated on the perovskite films is reported as one of the major nonradiative recombination sources that increases interfacial defects and decreases charge carrier lifetime   $  [16,17]  $  . Recently, additive engineering has been reported as a method that can effectively eliminate strain in perovskite films   $  [18]  $  . For example, Snaith et al. incorporated n-butylammonium (BA) into perovskite lattices, thereby reducing the strain in perovskite films and simultaneously realizing high stability and efficiency   $  [19]  $  . Defect concentration can also be controlled by interfacial engineering, in which an additional layer is inserted to passivate interfacial defects at the interfaces between electron selective layers (ESLs) and perovskite layers or between perovskite layers and hole-selective layers (HSLs). For example, additional layers such as FABr, DABr, and  $ \mathrm{MAPbBr}_3 $  quantum dot on perovskite film have been effective in passivating surface traps [24-26]. Although the insertion of an additional layer has been successful in improving PSC performance by suppressing defect states, this insertion process is time consuming, expensive, and complex, and potentially limits its practical applications [27]. To achieve high-efficiency PSCs, a synergistic multiple-passivation strategy including interface trap passivation and residual stress removal is essential.

Herein, we propose a new approach to simultaneously solve the problems of residual strain and interfacial defect passivation by incorporating a “synergistic additive” into the perovskite lattice without an additional layer. The incorporation of acetamidinium bromide (AABr) into   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   not only reduces the residual tensile strain in   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   but also decreases charge carrier recombination at the interface between perovskite and HSL. Furthermore, by applying calculations based on density functional theory (DFT), we show that AABr-incorporated   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   perovskite has lower formation energy than pristine   $  MAPbI_{3}  $ . Synergistic multiple passivation enables a PCE of 20.18%, which is substantially higher than that (18.30%) of a device based on pristine   $  MAPbI_{3}  $ . Furthermore, unencapsulated devices with AABr-incorporated   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   absorbers indicate excellent long-term stability in an ambient air atmosphere, maintaining more than 95% of initial PCE for durations up to 1200 hours.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1. Preparation of Li–SnO  $  _{2}  $  ESLs

PSCs were fabricated on FTO-coated glass substrates (TEC-8; Pilkington), which were patterned using Zn powder and dilute HCl solution, followed by cleaning with diluted Hellmanex, acetone, ethanol, and deionized water. Li–SnO  $  _{2}  $  was prepared as an ESL on the FTO glass by spin-coating an ethanol solution of 0.1 M tin(II) chloride dihydrate and 1.7 mM lithium bis(trifluoromethane sulfonyl)imide (Li–TSFI) (Sigma–Aldrich) at 3000 rpm for 30 s [28]. After drying at 100 °C for 1 min, the sample was annealed at 185 °C for 3 h on a hotplate. The Li–SnO  $  _{2}  $  films were subsequently treated with UVO for 25 min.

# 2.2. Synthesis of AABr-based  $ \mathsf{MAPbI}_3 $  and  $ \mathsf{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite

To synthesize MAPbI $ _3 $  and MAPbI $ _3 $  with AABr perovskite, an MAPbI $ _3 $  precursor solution (50 wt%) was prepared by dissolving lead(II) iodide (PbI $ _2 $ ; TCI) and methylammonium iodide (MAI; GreatcellSolar) in 0.635 mL of DMF (Sigma-Aldrich) and then 70  $ \mu $ L of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich) was added [29]. To form MA $ _{1-x} $ AA $ _x $ PbI $ _{3-x} $ Br $ _x $  ( $ x = 1 $  mol%, 3 mol%, 5 mol%, or 7 mol%), various concentrations (1 mol%–7 mol%) of acetaminidinium bromide (AABr; Greatcellsolar) were incorporated into the MAPbI $ _3 $  precursor solution. The completely dissolved solution was spin-coated onto a Li–SnO $ _2 $  ESL layer at 4000 rpm for 25 s, 0.5 mL of diethyl ether (Sigma-Aldrich) was slowly dripped onto the rotating substrate over a period of 10 s before the surface became turbid because of the rapid vaporization of DMF. The sample was subsequently annealed at 65  $ ^{\circ} $ C for 1 min and then at 100  $ ^{\circ} $ C for 2 min. A hole-selective layer was then coated onto the perovskite layer by spin-coating 20  $ \mu $ L of a spiro-MeOTAD solution composed of 80 mg spiro-MeOTAD (1-Material), 28.5  $ \mu $ L of 4-tert-butyl pyridine, 17.5  $ \mu $ L of Li–TSFI (Sigma-Aldrich) solution (520 mg

of Li–TSFI in 1 mL of acetonitrile), and 1 mL of chlorobenzene (Sigma–Aldrich) at 4000 rpm for 30 s. An Au electrode was evaporated under   $  10^{-6}  $   Torr using a thermal evaporation system.

# 2.3. Characterization

The crystal structures of the perovskite films were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and grazing-incidence XRD (GIXRD) (Rigaku Smartlab) analysis using Cu K $ _{\alpha} $  radiation. The 2d–sin $ ^{2} $   $ \psi $  function was also performed using high-resolution XRD (HR-XRD, X'Pert PRO-MRD, KBSI) over a range of  $ \psi $  values. The morphology and elemental distribution of the synthesized films were examined using field-emission scanning electron microscopy and Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectrometer (FE-SEM, EDS, SU8010, Hitachi) with a resolution of 15 kV. Fluorescence lifetime imaging (FLIM) was performed using an inverted-type scanning confocal microscope (MicroTime 200, Picoquant, Germany) with a 100×(oil-immersion) objective. A single-mode pulsed diode laser (470 nm with ~30 ps pulse width, ~0.1  $ \mu $ W average power, and 0.5 MHz repetition rate) was used as an excitation source. A dichroic mirror (490 DCXR, AHF), a 700 nm long-pass filter (HQ500lp, AHF), a 100  $ \mu $ m pinhole, and a single-photon avalanche diode (PDM series, MPD) were used to collect the emissions from the samples. The time-correlated single-photon counting technique was used to count the fluorescence photons. 200 × 200 pixel FLIM images were recorded using the time-tagged time-resolved (TTTR) data acquisition method. The acquisition time of each pixel was 2 ms. Exponential fittings for the obtained fluorescence decays, which were extracted from the FLIM images, were performed using SymPhoTime-64 software by employing the exponential decay model  $ I(t) = \sum A_i e^{-t/\tau_i} $ , where  $ I(t) $  is the time-dependent photoluminescence (PL) intensity,  $ A $  is the amplitude,  $ \tau $  is the PL lifetime, and  $ i $  takes integral values from 1 to 3. The steady-state photoluminescence (SSPL) of the samples at a focal volume were measured by guiding the fluorescence signal to an external spectrometer (F-7000, Hitachi). The photovoltaic (PV) properties of the assembled PSCs were determined using a solar simulator (Polaronix K201, McScience, Korea) equipped with a 150 W Xe lamp and a Keithley 2401 source meter under AM 1.5 simulated sunlight with a power density of 100 mW cm $ ^{-2} $ . After the intensity was calibrated using a KIER-calibrated KG5-filtered Si reference cell, all measurements were performed by masking the cells with a metal aperture to define an active area of 0.09 cm $ ^{2} $ . An incident photon-to-current conversion efficiency (IPCE, HS Tech, Korea) measurement system was used to evaluate the EQE. Electrochemical impedance (EIS) and Mott-Schottky (M-S) (Biologic SP-240) measurements were conducted in the dark. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) were conducted using an AXIS ultra-delay line detector (DLD) (Kratos, UK) equipped with monochromatic Al K $ _{\alpha} $  (1486.6 eV) radiation as the X-ray source or He I gas (21.22 eV) as the UV source.

# 2.4. Computational methods

All DFT calculations on bulk phases of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  and  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  with AABr were performed using the Quantum Espresso package [30] with Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE)-based generalized gradient approximation [31]. The core electrons of each atom were described by ultrasoft pseudopotential, and the respective energy cutoff values of 1020 and  $ 1088\mathrm{eV} $  for  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  and  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  with AABr were used to achieve reliable predictions of electronic structures. During the DFT-based geometry optimization, all atoms were allowed to fully relax until the forces on all atoms were below  $ 0.01\mathrm{eV} / \AA $ . A  $ 10\times 10\times 10 $  Monkhorst-Pack k-sampling was used to perform the DFT calculations. To determine the relative thermody

# 3. Results and discussion

Fig. 1(a) shows a schematic of the procedure for incorporating AABr into the MAPbI $ _3 $  lattice using compositional engineering. Here a controlled amount of AABr additive was simply added to the MAPbI $ _3 $  precursor (50 wt% concentration) to form MA $ _{1-x} $ AA $ _x $ -PbI $ _{3-x} $ Br $ _x $  ( $ x = 1 $  mol%, 3 mol%, 5 mol%, or 7 mol%). The detailed experimental process can be found in the Experimental section. Hereafter, we denote MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskites with and without AABr as ABP and PMP, respectively. As a first step, the Goldschmidt tolerance factor  $ t $  can be used to predict whether the AABr additive can be incorporated into the MAPbI $ _3 $  lattice. It is calculated from the ionic radii of constituent atoms as  $ t = r_{\text{A}} + r_{\text{x}}/\sqrt{2}(r_{\text{B}} + r_{\text{x}}) $ , where  $ r_{\text{A}} $  and  $ r_{\text{B}} $  are the ionic radii of the A and B site cations, respectively, and  $ r_{\text{x}} $  is the ionic radius of the halide anion [34]. The calculated tolerance factor for ABP is 0.886, which is included in the ideal cubic phase range (0.8–1.0) of stable perovskite [35]. Therefore, AABr additives can be successfully incorporated into the MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite.

To investigate the enhanced PV properties obtained through the incorporation AABr into  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ , we performed EIS analysis to further investigate the interface charge transport and recombination dynamics [45]. Fig. 4(a) shows the Nyquist plots of the ABP and PMP based devices operated at  $ 1.0\mathrm{V} $  in the dark using equivalent

circuits (inset of Fig. 4a). The Nyquist plots exhibit two semicircles or arcs. The high-frequency arc is ascribed to the charge-transfer resistance  $  R_{\mathrm{tr}}  $ , while the low-frequency arc is ascribed to the recombination resistance  $  R_{\mathrm{rec}}  $  [46]. The smaller  $  R_{\mathrm{tr}}  $  value for the ABP-based device than for the PMP-based device indicated greater charge extraction (Fig. 4b). This difference is attributed to the Br ions acting as charge carrier recombination barriers at the interface between the MAPbI3 and HSL layers, as in a previous report [47]. In addition, the larger  $  R_{\mathrm{rec}}  $  value for the ABP based device than for the PMP based device indicated lower charge recombination (Fig. 4c), which was responsible for the increased  $  V_{\mathrm{oc}}  $  value. Generally,  $  V_{\mathrm{oc}}  $  is correlated with the energy loss ( $  E_{\mathrm{loss}}  $ ) in PSCs, resulting from defect states acting as nonradiative energy loss channels [48,49]. Apparently, the incorporation of AABr drastically reduced the nonradiative recombination of perovskite at the contact interfaces. To further investigate charge carrier behavior, we conducted M-S analysis. Fig. 4(d) shows M-S plots of the devices based on ABP and PMP measured at 25 kHz. From these plots, we obtained built-in potentials ( $  V_{\mathrm{bi}}  $ ) of 1.091 V for ABP and 1.047 V for PMP. Using the slope of each M-S plot, the carrier densities of ABP and PMP were calculated to be  $  9.7 \times 10^{16}  $  and  $  13.16 \times 10^{16} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}  $ , respectively. These M-S analysis results reveal that ABP has the improved charge collection and decreased carrier accumulation at the interface [50,51]. From the EIS results, it was confirmed that the ABP device had less charge accumulation and rapid charge transport owing to the structural stability and reduced tensile strain of ABP and the role of Br ion acting to prevent charge transport recombination at the interface.

# 4. Conclusions

We demonstrated that compositional engineering incorporating AABr into the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  lattice reduced the defect states via synergistic passivation effects. First, we confirmed that AABr can incorporate into the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  lattice and stabilize the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  lattice by hydrogen bond. Second, in the incorporation of AABr into perovskite, ABP expanded the perovskite lattice, reducing the residual strain in the perovskite. Third,  $ \mathrm{Br}^- $  ion acted as a charge carrier recombination suppressor at the interface between perovskite and HSL, leading to enhanced charge collection. Lastly, AABr can modulate the band offset of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  according to the existence of AABr. Using these effects, we developed a perovskite solar cell with a  $ 20.18\% $  PCE that maintained high stability for 1200 hours.
