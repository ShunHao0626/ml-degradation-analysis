# Cost effective perovskite solar cells with a high efficiency and open-circuit voltage based on the perovskite-friendly carbon electrode

Qian-Qian Chu, $ ^{\dagger} $  Bin Ding, $ ^{\dagger} $  Qi Qiu, $ ^{\ddagger} $  Yan Liu, $ ^{\dagger} $  Cheng-Xin Li, $ ^{\dagger} $  Chang-Jiu Li, $ ^{\dagger} $  Guan-Jun Yang $ ^{*} $ , $ ^{\dagger} $  and Baizeng Fang $ ^{*} $ ,§

For low-temperature paintable carbon based perovskite solar cells (PSCs), the commercial carbon paste utilized to prepare carbon electrode damages the well-fabricated perovskite films, resulting in a bad photoelectric property. Herein, we have successfully prepared a low temperature perovskite-friendly and environment-benign carbon paste using propylene glycol monomethyl ether acetate as a solvent, which has shown a high electrical conductivity. More importantly, with the exclusive carbon paste, the perovskite film keeps a dense and uniform morphology. The as-developed PSCs do not involve the fabrication of organic hole transport materials (HTMs) which are generally expensive and air-sensitive, greatly limiting the long-term stability. The cost-effective HTM-free carbon-based PSCs demonstrate a champion power conversion efficiency of up to  $ 13.5\% $  and a champion open-circuit voltage of up to  $ 1.07\mathrm{V} $  with the active area of  $ 0.1\mathrm{cm}^2 $ , the highest value reported so far for carbon-based PSCs with  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  as light harvester. Furthermore, the long-time stability test shows that the solar cells with the as-prepared carbon electrode retain more than  $ 90\% $  of its initial power conversion efficiency after  $ 960\mathrm{h} $ .

# Introduction

Hybrid organic-inorganic solar cells, utilizing the organometal halide perovskite materials as the light harvesters have attracted much attention due to their low-cost solution processability and high power conversion efficiency (PCE).  $  ^{1-6}  $   Recently, the record efficiency of 22.7% has been certified, comparable to that of single-crystal Si solar cells.  $  ^{7-9}  $   However, the commercialization of the perovskite solar cells (PSCs) is mostly hindered by organic hole transportation materials (HTMs) and noble metal electrodes (e.g., Au, Ag).  $  ^{10-14}  $   Moreover, in most of the state-of-the-art PSCs the fabrication of conventional HTM usually involves the utilization of spiro-OMETAD,  $  ^{12, 14-16}  $   which is costly and unstable in air and the metal electrode has to be conducted in a vacuum environment.  $  ^{14, 16-18}  $   Fortunately, perovskite possesses an ambipolar property, and can be served not only as the light harvester but

also as the hole conductor.  $  ^{19}  $   Furthermore, carbon-based materials are abundant with a similar work function to Au electrode (i.e.,   $  \sim 5.0  $   eV), and show a great potential as an alternative for practical application.  $  ^{18,20}  $

The absence of HTM causes the direct contact between the commercial carbon paste to the perovskite film,  $  ^{21-23}  $  resulting in that the perovskite films are easily affected by the solvents in the carbon electrode. Therefore, many research groups have made great efforts to solve the problem by prefabricating carbon electrode. Han et al. prefabricated a scaffold of mesoscopic  $  TiO_{2}/ZrO_{2}/carbon  $  electrode triple layer, followed by hosting the perovskite absorber, resulting in a PCE of 12.8%.  $  ^{24-27}  $  Yang et al. prefabricated  $  PbI_{2}  $  and carbon electrode, which was then subjected to a  $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $  bath, avoiding the damage of the solvents in the carbon electrode and achieving a PCE of 11.02%.  $  ^{28}  $  Meng et al. improved PCE up to 13.53% by hot pressing the separately prepared carbon film onto the perovskite film.  $  ^{29}  $  Compared to the complex preparation processes, many research groups directly coated the carbon paste on the perovskite film.  $  ^{30-32}  $  For example, Ma et al. changed the solvents of the carbon paste to chorobenzene to avoid the damage of solvents in the commercial carbon paste. Although the low temperature carbon-based PSC simplifies the fabrication process, a PCE of only ca. 9% was achieved with the carbon paste in above efforts.  $  ^{22, 23}  $  For the simplest carbon-based PSC, whereas there still exists no exclusive carbon paste for PSC to reach a high efficiency.

In this study, a low-temperature perovskite-friendly carbon paste was designed and prepared for paintable PSCs. The solvents in the as-prepared carbon paste barely dissolve the perovskite film, therefore keeping the morphological homogeneity and exhibiting good light absorption. The as-developed carbon based PSCs could achieve a champion PCE of  $ 13.5\% $  and a champion open-circle voltage of 1.07 V. The resultant PSC displays a great potential for economical applications based on the advantages of negligible dissolution of perovskite, low electrode fabrication cost, simple and scalable low-temperature electrode preparation process.

# Experimental

# Materials

Dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.5%) was purchased from Aladdin Regents. Methylammonium iodide (CH₃NH₃I, MAI, 99.5%) and lead iodide (PbI₂, 99.999%) were purchased from Polymer Light Technology Inc. 40 wt % MAPbI₃ precursor solution was prepared by mixing MAI and PbI₂ in a molar ratio of 1:1 in DMF. N, N-dimethylformamide, propylene glycol monomethyl ether acetate (PGMEA), propylene glycol monomethyl ether (PGME), diethylene glycol butyl ether acetate (EBAC), ethylene glycol monomethyl ether (EGME), isopropanol, terpineol, isophorone, cyclohexanone, cyclohexane and hexane were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (China). All of the reagents were analytical grade and used without any further purification. Carbon black was purchased from CARBOT. Graphite was purchased from Qingdao Jiuyi Graphite Inc.

# Fabrication of perovskite-friendly carbon paste

Carbon pastes with different kind of solvents were prepared. First, polyacrylic resin (10 g) acting as a binder of the carbon

paste was dissolved in the solvent (60 g) with a vigorous stirring at 100 °C and kept for 2 h. Upon the complete dissolution of the binder, Carbon materials including carbon black (Vulan-XC72, 30 nm, 15 g) and graphite (5000 mesh, 45 g) were added into the solution immediately, and heated. After that, the mixture was ball milled for 12 h at a rotational speed of 250 rpm.

# Fabrication of perovskite solar cell

Patterned fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass substrates with a sheet resistance of 15 Ω sq-1 were cleaned by ultrasonic washing with acetone, ethanol, and deionized water in sequence for 10 min each and then exposed in a UVO cleaner for 15 min. Afterwards a TiO₂ sol (0.15 M titanium diisopropoxide bis in 1-butanol) was spin coated on a clean substrate at 3000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 450 °C for 30 min to obtain a compact TiO₂ film with a thickness of about 60 nm. A TiO₂ paste (Dyesol, 18NR-T), diluted with ethanol with a mass ratio of 1:7, was spin coated on the compact TiO₂ at 3000 rpm for 30 s, with subsequent annealing at 500 °C for 30 min. After that, the MAPbI₃ precursor was spin coated on the TiO₂ layer at 3000 rpm for 10 s. Then the sample was immediately transferred to a sample chamber connected with the vacuum buffer tank by a valve. When the valve was turned on, the air pressure in the sample swiftly decreased to 10 Pa to form a brown, mirror-like perovskite film in 5 s. Then the film was annealed at 100 °C for 15 min with the gas pump method. After the carbon paste was coated on the perovskite film by doctor-blade method, the sample was annealed at 100 °C to dry the carbon paste.

# Measurements and Characterization

The resistance of the carbon films was measured by a four-point probe resistivity measurement system (Keithley 4200A-SCS). The surface morphology of the perovskite films and the cross-sectional morphologies of PSCs with different carbon electrodes were analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, TESCAN MIRA 3 LMH). The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were measured by a D8 ADVANCE X-ray from Bruker at a scanning range of 10–70° and a scanning speed of 0.05 °/s. The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra were obtained by a compact steady-state spectrophotometer (Fluoromax-4, Horiba Jobin Yvon) with an excitation wavelength of 532 nm to explore the trap states and recombination of light-excited charge in semiconductors. The time resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectra were obtained by PicoQuant Fluotime 300, equipped with the excitation source of PDL 820 with the pulse with/duration of 25 ps. For the MAPbI3 perovskite film, the excitation wavelength is 508.6 nm and emission wavelength is 768 nm. The current-voltage (J-V) curves were recorded by a Keithley 2400 source-meter with an AM 1.5G filter (Sol3A, Oriel) equipped on a 450 W Class AAA solar simulator under the illumination of 100 mW/cm². The curves were measured with the reverse scanning voltage of 1.2 V to -0.2 V and the forward scanning voltage of -0.2 V to 1.2 V at a scan step of 23.7 mV and a delay time of 100 ms. # Results and discussion

# Screening of Solvents

Esters, alcohols, ketones, ethers, aromatic hydrocarbons are usually used as solvents in the carbon paste, so a series of 10 solvents were employed to investigate the dissolution of perovskite, including A: propylene glycol monomethyl ether acetate (PGMEA), B: propylene glycol monomethyl ether (PGME), C: diethylene glycol butyl ether acetate (EBAC), D: ethylene glycol monomethyl ether (EGME), E: isopropanol, F: terpineol, G: isophorone, H: cyclohexanone, I: cyclohexane and J: hexane. The dissolution was tested by dropping each solvent on a perovskite film. From Figure 2, the well-fabricated perovskite films could be evidently damaged by the solvents of B (PGME), D (EGME), G (isophorone), and H (cyclohexanone) after  $ 5\mathrm{min} $ . When the solvents volatilized completely, the four perovskite films became almost bare and the substrates were exposed. It is predicable that the four solvents cannot play a positive role in the carbon paste for PSCs. However, the perovskite films treated by the other 6 solvents were dissolved slightly. Especially the perovskite films treated by A (PGMEA), C (EBAC), and J (hexane) kept almost the same appearance as those before the solvent treatment. Therefore the remained six solvents (i.e., A, C, E, F, I, J) have the potential to prepare perovskite-friendly carbon pastes.

In order for carbon paste to have a high homogeneity, solvents should dissolve the binder well. To examine if a solvent can dissolve the binder, the binder was added into the selected solvents in mass ratio of 1:6, followed by heating at  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . After heating for  $ 2\mathrm{h} $ , the solutions with the solvents of A (PGMEA), C (EBAC) and F (terpineol) (Figure S1, Supporting Information) are clear without any sediments. In contrast, the solvents of E (isopropanol), I (cyclohexane) and J (hexane) cannot dissolve the binder well.

Further, the morphologies of the perovskite films (Figure 3a) treated by PGMEA, EBAC, and terpineol were examined by SEM to analyze the damage caused by the above three solvents. It is obvious that the perovskite films treated by PGMEA and EBAC still keep dense, uniform, and full coverage morphologies. However, the perovskite film treated by terpineol produces a rough morphology with flakes on the film surface. The XRD patterns shown in Figure 3b reveal that the perovskite films treated by PGMEA and EBAC have the diffraction peaks of the perovskite phase of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $ . However, the phase of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  was observed for the samples treated by terpineol.[21] Also, in Figure 3c, the absorption spectra of the perovskite films treated by PGMEA and EBAC on the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / FTO} $  substrate show stronger absorption compared to that treated by terpineol when the wavelength was shorter than  $ 590~\mathrm{nm} $ . At a longer wavelength (greater than  $ 590~\mathrm{nm} $ ), the slightly stronger intensity of the perovskite film treated by terpineol might be attributed to the scattering caused by the rough surface. The steady-state PL spectra of the perovskite layers on the FTO substrate (Figure 3d) reveal an enhanced PL intensity for the perovskite film treated by PGMEA and EBAC compared with that treated by terpineol. Time-resolved PL spectra in Figure 3e demonstrate significantly lower quenching rates in the perovskite layers treated by PGMEA and EBAC than that treated by terpineol. The life times of the perovskite films treated by PGMEA, EBAC and Terpineol were 62.5, 54.8 and 27.4 ns, respectively by double exponential fitting. The perovskite film treated by PGMEA has a longer life time, promoting the charge transport efficiently, avoiding the charge accumulation. The stronger PL intensity and the slower decay of the photoexcited carriers for the perovskite layers obtained from PGMEA and EBAC underpin the more suppressed trap mediated nonradiative recombination and thus the lower trap state density,[22] resulting from the higher quality of the perovskite films. Therefore, the carbon pastes with solvents of PGMEA and EBAC were supposed to have the potential prepare perovskite-friendly carbon pastes.

However, for the devices based on PF-1 and PF-2, the   $  V_{oc}  $   and   $  J_{sc}  $   show a tiny increase at the beginning followed by a slow decrease after about 1000 h of test, the PSC based on PF-1 maintains more than 90% of the initial PCE and the   $  V_{oc}  $   declines slightly from 1.05 V to 1.03 V. The perovskite-friendly carbon paste (PF-1 carbon paste) evidently reduces the damage to the perovskite film, and maintains the uniform morphology of the perovskite film, resulting in more stable photovoltaic behavior for the as-fabricated carbon based PSCs.
