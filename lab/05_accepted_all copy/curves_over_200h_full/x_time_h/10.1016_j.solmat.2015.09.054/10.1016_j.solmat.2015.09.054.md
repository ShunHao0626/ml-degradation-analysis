# 1. Introduction

As an emerging technology for next generation photovoltaics,   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbX_{3}  $   (X=Cl, Br, I)-based perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attained great success by boosting their power conversion efficiency (PCE) to over 20%, a figure that is highly competitive compared with that of other solar cell technologies developed for decades, merely within few years [1–6]. An important merit of PSCs compared with the commercially available Si-based photovoltaics is that PSCs can give high voltage output at weak illumination of light [7–8]. Therefore, PSCs are particularly useful as an energy supply for wearable and flexible electronics. The high PCE of PSCs is closely associated with the excellent photoelectrical properties of perovskite light absorbers including the appropriate band gap, the small exciton binding energy, the long and balanced ambipolar charge transport property [9–13]. Electron collection layer (ECL) is one of the most important fundamentals to determine the power conversion efficiency (PCE) in organometal halide-based perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Herein, we prepared   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   nanocomposites with different Zn/Sn ratios at low temperature as ECLs for   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $  -based planar-structured PSCs.   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   nanocomposite with the optimal   $  \sim89  $   mol% of the ZnO content gives higher PCE than the ZnO for the best fabricated PSC. The photoluminescence spectroscopies measured in both steady and transient states and the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy were carried out to characterize the interface of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   and different ECLs, namely ZnO,   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   composite, and   $  SnO_{2}  $  . The high PCE of PSCs based on the   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   nanocomposite ECL was thus attributed to joint contributions of the high charge extraction efficiency and large charge recombination resistance both on the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}/ECL  $   interface. The thermal stability of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   absorber and the device stability of the corresponding PSC are both dependent on the ECLs in the order:   $  SnO_{2}>ZnO-SnO_{2}>ZnO  $  , suggesting that the hydroxyl-induced degradation of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   may be predominant in the ambient air environment in the initial   $  \sim700  $   h. The PCE of the optimized device was further improved to 15.2% by introducing the low-temperature processable   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   as a capping layer to the   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   composite.

In the PSC device architectures, an electron collection layer (ECL) plays a significant role in suppressing recombination and rectifying photocurrent generated in the perovskite bulk films   $  [21-30]  $  . To date, some inorganic n-type semiconductors, such as   $  TiO_{2}  $  , ZnO,   $  SnO_{2}  $  ,   $  Nb_{2}O_{5}  $  , and   $  WO_{x}  $  , have been studied as the ECLs in PSCs   $  [21-22,25-27,30]  $  . Nevertheless, in order to fabricate PSCs on flexible substrates for potential applications in flexible and wearable electronics, the ECL must be processed at a low temperature. In view of this, many investigations into low-temperature processed PSCs with   $  TiO_{2}  $   or ZnO as ECL have been carried out, and have shown high PCEs of 14~19%   $  [18,23]  $  . Moreover, PSCs based on  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  show excellent durability in the ambient environment [29]. On the other hand, the PCE of  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $ -based PSCs is lower than that of ZnO-based PSCs, owing to inherent lower conduction band edge and low current rectification property of  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  compared with that of ZnO resulting in more severe charge recombination between the ECL and perovskite in the former than the latter [27,33]. Thus, the insufficiency of each ECL material investigated in PSCs so far strongly encourages further development of ECL by either exploring other n-type semiconductor that outperforms current ECL semiconductors or by combining two semiconductors into one so that they can compensate each other.

Semiconductor nanocomposites are a large group of nanomaterials that their chemical, physical and opto-electrical properties are tuned by varying the composition of each semiconductor in a mixture to fulfill some particular requirements of semiconductor devices. The nanocomposites of   $  ZnO-TiO_{2}  $  ,   $  SnO_{2}-MgO  $  ,   $  TiO_{2}-Nb_{2}O_{3}  $  ,   $  SnO_{2}-TiO_{2}  $   and   $  SnO_{2}-ZnO  $   under this concept have been very successfully applied in dye-sensitized solar cells to result in enhanced photovoltaic performance [35–37]. Similarly, the use of nanocomposite ECLs in PSCs will also be of considerable interests and provide an important piece of information in further development of this photovoltaic field. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no report on inorganic semiconductors nanocomposite as ECL for PSCs so far.

In this investigation, we prepared   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   nanocomposite thin films by spin-coating of mixed nanoparticles as ECLs to fabricate low-temperature-processed PSCs. We employed a previously reported two-step sequential deposition technique to grow   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   active layer [29]. We observed clear dependence of short-circuit current (  $  J_{sc}  $  ) and fill factor (FF) on the component content in the composite films despite almost unchanged open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ). With planar layered structures, the PSCs exhibited relatively high PCE of   $  14.0 \pm 0.3\%  $   under the standard AM 1.5 G (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) simulated sunlight illumination with an optimal weight ratio of 2:1 for   $  ZnO/SnO_{2}  $   in solution. Most importantly, the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   layer and device based on   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   nanocomposite thin film exhibited improved thermal stability and device stability in comparison with that based on ZnO, respectively. This study will contribute to both the progress of the low-temperature planar-heterojunction PSCs and the real-world applications of the PSC technology.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1. Materials

The ZnO (ZnO-350, Sumitomo Osaka Cement Co.) [38] and   $  SnO_{2}  $   nanoparticles (ca. 22–43 nm, Wako chemicals) were used as received.   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   paste was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The lead iodide (beads, –10 mesh, 99.999% trace metals basis) and lithium salts (>99.0%) were purchased from Aldrich. The   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $   (>99.0%) was purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Co. (PLT). The Spiro-OMeTAD (2,2',7,7'-tetrakis-(N,N-di-4-methoxyphenylamino)-9,9'-spirobifluorene, >99.0%) was purchased from Merck. Ultradry solvent of N, N-dimethyl formamide (DMF, >99.9%) and isopropanol (>99.9%) were obtained from J&K and Acros, respectively. All the chemicals and solvents were kept in the glove-box before starting our experiment.

# 2.2. Device fabrication

The pre-patterned ITO substrates were cleaned with detergent, deionized water, acetone, and 2-propanol in sequence. The ZnO

and   $  SnO_{2}  $   nanoparticles were dispersed in butanol in different blending proportion with a total concentration of 6 mg/ml and stirred for 3 h before use. The solution was filtered with a PVDF hydrophobic 0.45   $  \mu  $  m filter and spin-coated on ITO substrates at 2500 rpm for 30 s to form a relatively compact   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   thin layer, and followed by short baking at 150 °C for 5 min. This procedure was repeated for three times before finally baking at 200 °C for 1 h. A 460 mg/mL solution of   $  PbI_{2}  $   in DMF was then spin-coated on the top of the   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   layer at 3000 rpm for 30 s in the glove-box. The   $  PbI_{2}  $ -coated   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   thin film was kept in a Petri dish for about 5 min allowing a slow growth of   $  PbI_{2}  $   nanocrystals, and was followed by annealing at 70 °C for 30 min. After cooling to room temperature, the films was dipped into a   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $   2-propanol solution (10 mg/mL) for 50 s, and was followed by annealing at 70 °C for 20 min to give a desired crystallite formation. As hole-transporting material (HTM), a Spiro-OMeTAD solution (80 mg of Spiro-OMeTAD, 10.5   $  \mu  $  L of 4-tert-butylpyridine (tBP), and 46.5   $  \mu  $  L of a lithium-bis(tri-fluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (Li-TFSI) solution (170 mg Li-TFSI/1 mL acetonitrile) in 1 mL chlorobenzene) was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 30 s on the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $ . The substrates with HTM were left overnight in the dry air in dark at room temperature. Finally, 60 nm thick Ag electrode was thermally evaporated on the top of HTM to produce a completed PSC device.

# 2.3. Device characterization

The current–voltage characteristics of solar cells were measured by a computer-controlled Keithley 2400 source meter measurement system with an AM1.5 G filter at a calibrated intensity   $  100 \, mW cm^{-2}  $   illumination, as determined by a standard silicon reference cell (91150 V Oriel Instruments). The effective area of the cell was defined to be   $  0.04 \, cm^{2}  $   using a non-reflective metal mask. IPCE spectra was measured in air under short-circuit conditions using a commercial IPCE setup (Crowntech QTest Station 1000AD), which was equipped with a 100 W Xe arc lamp, filter wheel, and monochromator. Monochromated light was chopped at a frequency of 80 Hz and photocurrents were measured using a lock-in amplifier. The setup was calibrated against a certified silicon reference diode. The impedance spectroscopy measurement was carried out by a VSP multichannel potentiostatic-galvanostatic system (Biologic, France), under dark conditions by applying a 5 mV voltage perturbation with the frequency ranging between 1 MHz and 1 Hz. Z-View Analyst software was used to model the Nyquist plots obtained from the impedance measurements.

# 2.4. Thin film characterization

Film thicknesses were measured using a Veeco Dektak 150 surface profilometer. Steady-state fluorescence spectra were measured on a Shimadzu RF-5301PC spectrophotometer. Time-resolved photoluminescence (TPL) spectra were obtained on a PL spectrometer (Edinburgh Instruments, FLS 980), excited with a picosecond pulsed diode laser (EPL-375). A Hamamatsu C5680–04 streak camera was used for TPL. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded on Rigaku SmartLab X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (  $  \lambda=1.5418\ \AA  $  ) at 25 °C. The data were collected with a 0.01° step size (2θ). A field emission scanning electron microscope (Hitachi SU8020) was used to acquire SEM images. The energy band values were measured with an integrated ultrahigh vacuum system equipped with multi-technique surface analysis system (VG Scienta R3000) UPS.

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Sequential deposition of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   on ZnO–SnO  $  _{2}  $   ECL

The   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   nanocomposite films were prepared by spin coating the mixed nanoparticles through a PVDF filter with average pore size of 0.45   $  \mu  $  m. The nanoparticles are both commercially available. We simply confirmed the nanoparticles by measuring their X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns. Fig. S1 in the Supplementary Material shows the XRD patterns of both ZnO and   $  SnO_{2}  $   nanoparticles used in this investigation. Each of these diffraction lines is commonly assigned to the hexagonal crystalline phases of ZnO and to the tetragonal rutile crystalline phases of   $  SnO_{2}  $   as reported before [29]. Fig. S2 shows the high magnification scanning electron microscope (SEM) surface images of the   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   thin film prepared by spin-coating the   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   nanoparticle solution containing ZnO and   $  SnO_{2}  $   nanoparticles with different weight ratios of 5:1, 2:1, 1:1 and 1:2. The morphologies for the   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   composite thin films are basically identical with the SEM images taken for each ZnO and   $  SnO_{2}  $   in our previous investigations [26,29]. In addition, these composite films are very flat suggesting the corresponding PSCs are likely based on the planar-heterojunction configuration. Given that the mixture of nanoparticles was spin coated from a PVDF filter, the exact ratios of ZnO and   $  SnO_{2}  $   on the resultant films must be different from their mixed ratios. We performed energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) to analyze the real mole ratio of ZnO (Zn mol%) in the composite films as a function of the mixed weight ratio in the solution. As shown in Fig. 1, the exact amount of ZnO in the composite films is basically dependent on the amount of ZnO in the mixed solution. The Zn mol% of 97%, 89%, 77%, and 60% in the composite films corresponds to the mixed weight ratios of   $  ZnO/SnO_{2}  $   of 5:1, 2:1, 1:1 and 1:2 in the mixed solution, respectively. In addition, the weight ratios for ZnO in the composite films calculated from the mole ratios are also provided in Fig. 1. The corresponding ZnO weight ratios are 95%, 81%, 64% and 45%. For the convenience of discussion, however, we simply refer to the mixed ratios in the following investigation.

A previously reported two-step sequential deposition method [29] was employed to prepare  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  films on  $ \mathrm{ZnO - SnO_2} $  thin films as detailed in the experimental section. With this method,  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  with high crystallinity remains in the perovskite layer to give a passivation effect [29]. The XRD pattern of the perovskite films in Fig. S3a is basically consistent with previous results [29]. The XRD signals appearing at each  $ 14.0^{\circ} $ ,  $ 24.3^{\circ} $ ,  $ 28.4^{\circ} $ ,  $ 31.7^{\circ} $  and  $ 50.3^{\circ} $  correspond to the (110), (202), (220), (310) and (404) planes of the tetragonal phase of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $ , respectively [40]. Fig. S3b also shows the SEM surface image of the perovskite films used in this investigation.

# 3.2. J-V characteristics of the solar cells

PSCs were then fabricated with the above perovskite layer as the absorber and with ZnO,   $  SnO_{2}  $  , and   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   (5:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2) nanocomposite as the ECLs. In each PSC, an   $  \sim18  $   nm thick ECL and a   $  \sim210  $   nm thick perovskite layer was commonly employed. Fig. 2(a) shows the PSC device configuration based on the   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   ECL. Fig. 2(b) shows the energy level diagram of the PSC devices based on ZnO or   $  SnO_{2}  $   ECL [23,29]. The conduction band edge (CBE) of either ZnO (-4.2 V) or   $  SnO_{2}  $   (-4.5 V) is sufficiently lower than the lowest occupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   perovskite absorber ensuring efficient extraction (or dissociation) of free charge carriers (or excitons) at the ECL/   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   interface [23]. The spectra of the ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) and UV-vis absorption spectroscopy of the   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   nanocomposite with the best blend ratio (2:1 in solution) are shown in Fig. S4. The band edges of the valence and conduction bands were thus estimated to be -7.55 eV and -4.25 eV, respectively with these UPS and absorption spectra, and are shown in Fig. 2(b). For the   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   nanocomposites, their CBEs are located between that of ZnO and   $  SnO_{2}  $  , as widely studied in the previous investigations [35,39]. Noticeably, in the heterojunction structure, there may be some interfacial trap states between   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   and   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   interface owing to the imperfection from both the materials and from lattice mismatch-induced defects between them. The poor screening of the surface charge-induced electric field by the low concentration of free carrier density in the semiconductor can lead to the band bending that has an effect on the charge transfer [44]. In the energy level diagram of Fig. 2(b), the interface state effect is put aside temporarily and we regard the heterojunction as an ideal condition.

Fig. 3 shows the current–voltage (J-V) characteristics measured from the reversed voltage scan of PSCs with each ZnO, SnO  $  _{2}  $  , and ZnO–SnO  $  _{2}  $   nanocomposites (5:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2) as ECL, and the detailed parameters are summarized in Table 1. Interestingly, no significant change in the   $  V_{oc}  $   value was observed, despite the CBE of SnO  $  _{2}  $   is significantly below that of ZnO. This insensitivity of the   $  V_{oc}  $   value to the CBE level of the ECL could be attributed to both the passivation effect of the remnant PbI  $  _{2}  $   and the effective charge collection by both ZnO and SnO  $  _{2}  $   ECL layers [29]. For the FF and   $  J_{sc}  $   values, however, they exhibit clear dependence on the ZnO/SnO  $  _{2}  $   ratio, i.e., with increased SnO  $  _{2}  $   component, the   $  J_{sc}  $   value increases whereas the FF value decreases. For the SnO  $  _{2}  $ -based device, it gives the highest   $  J_{sc}  $ , which may be due to the most efficient interfacial charge-transfer, the smallest current loss derived from the wide band gap, and the best antireflection effect [28,41]. In contrast, the highest FF value obtained for the ZnO-based device is the consequence of the higher electron mobility and carrier selectivity (rectification) by ZnO [23]. As the result of the varied   $  J_{sc}  $   and FF values, the highest PCE of 14.3%, determined by a   $  J_{sc}=19.6\ mA\ cm^{-2}  $ , a   $  V_{oc}=1.06\ V  $ , and a FF=0.688, was achieved for the best fabricated device with ZnO/SnO  $  _{2}  $ =2:1. Fig. S5 in the Supplementary material shows the dependence of each photovoltaic parameter on the ZnO/SnO  $  _{2}  $   ratios.

# 3.4. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy and electron mobility measurements

Although the charge extraction efficiency and   $  J_{sc}  $   of   $  SnO_{2}  $   is higher than those of ZnO and their nanocomposite, the FF value of the   $  SnO_{2}  $  -based PSC is lower than that of other ECL-based PSCs. This means the charge collection and current rectifying efficiency, determined by the interfacial charge-transfer, the charge-transport and the recombination dynamics, is the lowest for   $  SnO_{2}  $   among all investigated ECLs. Thus, we carried out electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements in the dark at 0 V relative to the open circuit potential on the devices with the configuration of ITO/ECL/  $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $  /Spiro-OMeTAD/Ag, in which ECL is   $  SnO_{2}  $ , ZnO–  $  SnO_{2}  $ , and ZnO, respectively. Fig. 6(a) shows the Nyquist plots of PSC devices with these ECLs. The Nyquist plot is composed of two irregular arcs, i.e., a small arc at high frequency and a large arc at low frequency [30]. We fitted the experimental data with a relatively simple equivalent circuit model from the previous investigation [42] as shown in Fig. 6(b). # 4. Conclusions

In summary, we successfully developed   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   nanocomposites as electron collection layer for   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $  -based perovskite solar cells. The composition of the nanocomposites was optimized by adjusting ZnO and   $  SnO_{2}  $   nanoparticles in the mixed solution. The relatively high power conversion efficiency of   $  14.0\pm0.3\%  $   was achieved with   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   (2:1 weight ratio in solution) nanocomposite as the ECL. This high efficiency was originated from an excellent charge extraction efficiency of the   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   composite and a large recombination resistance between   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   and   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $ , as revealed by the steady state photoluminescence spectroscopy, the time-resolved photoluminescence spectroscopy and the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy measurements. Moreover, the   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   nanocomposite thin films exhibit better thermal stability of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   and resultant PSC device stability, as compared to ZnO. The development of   $  ZnO-SnO_{2}  $   nanocomposite as electron collection layer for perovskite solar cells provides a new choice for the further advances of the novel photovoltaic technology for perovskite.
