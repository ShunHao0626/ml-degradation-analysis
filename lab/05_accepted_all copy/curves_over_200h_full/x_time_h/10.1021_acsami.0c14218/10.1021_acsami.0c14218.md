# Incorporation of Lithium Fluoride Restraining Thermal Degradation and Photodegradation of Organometal Halide Perovskite Solar Cells

Alan Jiwan Yun,  $  ^{\dagger}  $   Jinhyun Kim,  $  ^{\dagger}  $   Bumjin Gil, Hyungsub Woo, Kimin Park, Jaemin Cho, and Byungwoo Park  $  ^{*}  $

Cite This: https://dx.doi.org/10.1021/acsami.0c14218

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Because of the facile formation of defects in organometal halide perovskites, the defect passivation has become an important prerequisite for the stable and efficient perovskite solar cell (PSC). Regarding that ionic defects of the perovskites play a significant role on the performance and stability of PSCs, we introduce lithium fluorides as effective passivators based on their strong ionic characteristics and small ionic radii. Both Li $  ^{+}  $  and F $  ^{-}  $  are observed to successfully incorporate within the perovskite layer, improving the device performances with the best efficiency over 20%, while the hysteresis effects are significantly reduced, confirming the passivation of perovskite defects. Moreover, LiF restrains both thermal degradation and photodegradation of PSCs, where over 90% of the initial efficiencies have been retained by LiF-incorporated devices for more than 1000 h under either 1 sun illumination or 85 °C thermal condition. As the trap density of states is analyzed before and after the thermal stress, not only the mitigation of electronic traps as fabricated but also the dramatic relaxation of traps during the postannealing step is observed with the LiF incorporation. From this work, LiF has shown its potential as a promising ionic

passivator, and the phenomenal achievement of device stability by LiF provides a clear insight to overcome the stability issues of PSCs, a key to the commercialization of next-generation photovoltaics.

KEYWORDS: perovskite solar cell, 85 °C stability, photodegradation, ionic passivator, defect analysis

# INTRODUCTION

With interest and demands in renewable energy-conversion systems, intensive efforts are recently being devoted to reveal the dominant degradation mechanisms of perovskite solar cells (PSCs).  $  ^{1-12}  $   Though numerous reports attributed the low operational stability of PSC to fragility of the organic hole-transporting material, spiro-OMeTAD, several studies have shown that room for the perovskite modification still remains to achieve stable PSCs under thermal and illumination conditions.  $  ^{13,14}  $   Specifically, because the solution-processed perovskite possesses uncountable defects including ionic vacancies and terminations at the surfaces, the passivation of traps in perovskite is reported to bring significant improvements in both performance and stability of the perovskite photovoltaics.  $  ^{15-21}  $

In order to passivate the defects in the perovskite or at the interfaces, various materials such as ionic compounds, polymers, and organic molecules with functional groups have been utilized as additives or interlayers.  $  ^{14,22-27}  $   Among the candidates, metal halides are regarded to have great advantages in their small size and tendency to form ionic bonds within the perovskite layer.  $  ^{28-33}  $   Especially, fluoride ions with high electron affinity are reported to help forming hydrogen bonds with organic cations or substitute halide vacancies, resulting in the strong passivation effects.  $  ^{13,34}  $   However, the

In this work, LiF is introduced as a straightforward additive into the perovskite to carry a role as a successful ionic passivator. The   $  Li^{+}  $  and   $  F^{-}  $  ions are observed to be incorporated in perovskite near the interfaces with the carrier transport layers, possibly interacting with fragile ions such as halides and organic cations. Upon the incorporation of   $  Li^{+}  $  and   $  F^{-}  $ , their effects on the defect states have been indirectly explored through the analyses on the perovskite films and solar cells. With the photovoltaic devices having been tested, significant changes in the performance and stabilities for more than 1000 h under either 1 sun illumination or 85 °C thermal condition are scrutinized. Interestingly, anomalous transition of interfacial traps has been noticed under thermal stress, and the impacts of   $  Li^{+}  $  and   $  F^{-}  $  ions on the degradation processes in solar cells are further discussed, focusing on the defects of materials and devices.

# EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

Perovskite Preparation. Cesium iodide (0.065 M, CsI, TCI Chemicals), 1.045 M formamidinium iodide (FAI, GreatCell Solar), 0.19 M methylammonium bromide (MABr, GreatCell Solar), 1.1 M lead iodide (PbI $ _2 $ , TCI Chemicals), and 0.2 M lead bromide (PbBr $ _2 $ , TCI Chemicals) are dissolved in a mixture of  $ N,N $ -dimethylformamide (DMF, Sigma-Aldrich) and dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich) with a volume ratio of 4:1. LiF additive solution (0.05 M) was prepared by dissolving lithium fluoride (LiF, 99.99% trace-metal basis, Sigma-Aldrich) powder in the DMF/DMSO solvent. Both perovskite precursor solution and LiF solution had been stirred for 1 h before mixing. Afterward, LiF solution and the DMF/DMSO solvent were added to the precursor solution for the desired LiF concentration and stirred for additional 30 min. All the preparation procedures were conducted in a N $ _2 $ -filled glovebox.

Solar Cell Fabrication. The patterned ITO substrate (2.5 × 2.5 cm², AMG) had been sonicated in acetone, isopropanol (IPA), and deionized water for 30 min each. The UV–ozone treatment was performed on the substrate for 15 min. SnO₂ nanoparticle dispersion in H₂O (Alfa Aesar) was used for the SnO₂ film deposition, with the addition of tetramethylammonium hydroxide (TMAH, Sigma-Aldrich) as ligands. The SnO₂ solution was spin-coated on ITO glass at 3000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 120 °C for 30 min, followed by 15 min of UV–ozone treatment. The perovskite film was deposited by spin-coating the precursor solution on the SnO₂ substrate in N₂, with the following steps: 1000 rpm for 10 s for the first step with 1 s ramp and 5000 rpm for 20 s for the second step with 2 s ramp. Chlorobenzene (400 μL, anhydrous, Sigma-Aldrich) was used as the secondary solvent and added dropwise 3 s before the second spin-step ended. The substrate was then annealed at 100 °C for 30 min on a hot plate. The PSC was fabricated by the solution process of spiro-OMeTAD (Lumtec), dissolved in chlorobenzene (72.3 mg mL⁻¹), with 28.8 μL of 4-tert-butylpyridine (tBP, Sigma-Aldrich), 17.5 μL of bis(trifluoromethane) sulfonimide lithium salt (Li-TFSI, Sigma-Aldrich) in acetonitrile (520 mg mL⁻¹), and 21.9 μL of Co(II)-TFSI (FK209, Sigma-Aldrich) solution as additives. The spiro-OMeTAD solution was spin-coated with a spin-rate of 3000 rpm for 30 s. For the device stability tests, PTAA was used for HTL instead of spiro-OMeTAD. PTAA (20 mg, 47 kDa, MS Solutions)

was dissolved in 1 mL of chlorobenzene with 6   $  \mu  $  L of tBP and 4   $  \mu  $  L of Li-TFSI solution as dopants. Last, the Au electrode was thermally evaporated onto the device. The solar cell was glass-encapsulated by using UV curable epoxy resin (XNR-570, NAGASE), and an additional getter (AD Tech) was attached inside the capping glass.

Characterization. The film morphology and cross-sectional image of perovskite films and devices were observed using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (MERLIN Compact, ZEISS). To observe the crystallinity and impurity of the perovskite films, an X-ray diffractometer (new D-8 ADVANCE, Bruker) was used. Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) of the perovskite films was analyzed by LabRAM HR Evolution (Horiba) and time-resolved PL by FlouTime 300 (PicoQuant). The perovskite sample for the PL measurements was deposited on a glass substrate. For the depth profile of elemental distribution, a time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometer (ToF-SIMS, ION-TOF) was utilized. Bi was used as a primary source, and Cs and oxygen were used as etching sources. Areas of the analysis and etching were  $ 100 \times 100 \mu \mathrm{m}^2 $  and  $ 500 \times 500 \mu \mathrm{m}^2 $ , respectively.

The  $ J - V $  characteristics of solar cells were measured using a solar cell measurement system (K3000, McScience) with an active area of  $ 0.09~\mathrm{cm}^2 $ . The voltage was swept from 1.2 to  $ -0.1\mathrm{V} $  for reverse scan and from  $ -0.1 $  to  $ 1.2\mathrm{V} $  for forward scan, with a scan rate of  $ 100\mathrm{mV} $   $ \mathrm{s}^{-1} $ . 1 sun illumination (AM 1.5G) was used by a solar simulator (xenon lamp, McScience). Every device was preilluminated for more than  $ 30\mathrm{s} $  before the measurement. The external quantum efficiency of the solar cell was measured by the incident photon-to-current efficiency measurement system (QEX7, CEXCI). The thermal stability was tested for the encapsulated devices using thermostat in ambient air. The thermally stressed device had been cooled down for  $ 15\mathrm{min} $  before every  $ J - V $  measurement. For the capacitance analysis, a potentiostat (Zive SP-1, WonATech) was adopted, and the impedance of the device was measured under zero bias and  $ 10\mathrm{mV} $  AC perturbation with varying frequency from  $ 100\mathrm{kHz} $  to  $ 10\mathrm{mHz} $  in dark. Before every measurement, the device had been relaxed in dark to lower the potential below  $ 5\mathrm{mV} $ . For the photostability test, the solar cell reliability test system (K3600, McScience) was used with 1 sun light source (PLS300, McScience). The maximum-power-voltage had been tracked and biased on the solar cell throughout the test.

# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

In order to avoid any formation of crystalline disorders or undesirable phases, the amount of LiF addition has been carefully optimized, and microstructures of the perovskite films are examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and X-ray diffraction (XRD). The perovskite films of various LiF concentrations (0.1, 0.2, and 0.5 mol %) show an indistinguishable morphology and crystallinity with the bare triple-cation perovskite [Cs-doped (FA $  _{0.83}  $ MA $  _{0.17}  $ )Pb-(I $  _{0.83}  $ Br $  _{0.17}  $ ) $  _{3}  $ , CsFAMA] film as intended, which are shown in Figures 1a,b and S1. In spite of the low solubility of LiF in nonaqueous solvents, no signs of impurities such as undissolved LiF nanoparticles are observed for the LiF-incorporated CsFAMA (CsFAMA:LiF), where LiF is expected to be incorporated as lithium and fluoride ions within the perovskite layer. $  ^{35}  $

With   $  Li^{+}  $  and   $  F^{-}  $  ions present, the charge dynamics and optoelectronic properties of materials are likely to be affected. The PL analyses of LiF-added perovskites are demonstrated in Figure 1c,d. Despite the similarities in crystallinity and morphology, the PL characteristics of LiF-added perovskites are highly distinctive, which are observed from both steady-state and time-resolved PL spectra. As LiF is properly added into the perovskite, the PL intensity is considerably increased and the PL decay occurs more slowly. Increases in the PL intensity and lifetime are reported to indirectly imply the suppression of nonradiative recombination and the reduction of electronic traps in perovskites,  $  ^{36,37}  $   and LiF is expected to be incorporated as ionic species in the perovskite playing an important role as defect passivators. Because the optoelectronic properties of perovskite may guarantee its potential for the photovoltaic application, the solar cell devices have been fabricated with the LiF-incorporated perovskites for further analyses.

In order to scrutinize the distributions of   $  Li^{+}  $  and   $  F^{-}  $  in solar cells, the ITO/  $  SnO_{2}  $  /CsFAMA:LiF/spiro-OMeTAD device

has been analyzed via ToF-SIMS. With the device architecture shown by cross-sectional SEM (Figure 2a), the major elemental depth profiles are exhibited in Figure 2b. Unlikely to the general preparation method for the spiro-OMeTAD hole-transport layer (HTL), the HTL has been fabricated without the Li-TFSI dopant, leaving the LiF additives in perovskite as the only source of  $ \mathrm{Li^{+}} $  and  $ \mathrm{F^{-}} $ . Both ions are dominantly observed at the  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ /perovskite interface, while  $ \mathrm{Li^{+}} $  is barely detected elsewhere possibly because of its low yield. Still,  $ \mathrm{F^{-}} $  ions are well dispersed in perovskite near the perovskite/HTL junction, by which the passivation effects are expected not only at two interfaces but also at the grain boundaries. The schematics of  $ \mathrm{Li^{+}} $  and  $ \mathrm{F^{-}} $  incorporation in the device is shown in Figure 2c.

The solar cell exhibits the best performance with the addition of 0.1% LiF (Figure 2d), and the solar cell parameters are compared in Figure S2. In detail, the open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) is improved with LiF incorporation regardless of its concentration, and the short-circuit current density (  $  J_{SC}  $  ) is slightly increased by adding proper amount of LiF, which is closely compared in Figure S3. Considering that   $  V_{OC}  $   is highly dependent on the trap states, the improvement of   $  V_{OC}  $   is attributed to the passivation of defects by LiF incorporation.  $  ^{20,38,39}  $   Hence,   $  Li^{+}  $  and   $  F^{-}  $  ions are likely to have strong interactions with halides and organic cations in the perovskite, possibly at the vacancy sites or terminations near the grain boundaries and interfaces. The repressed J-V hysteresis of PSCs with LiF (PSCs–LiF) shown in Figure 2e also supports this hypothesis that   $  Li^{+}  $  and   $  F^{-}  $  mitigate the ionic defects and forbid potential ion migration in perovskite, which is regarded as a dominant origin of the hysteresis phenomena.  $  ^{19,40}  $   The 0.1 mol % addition of LiF is regarded as the optimum remedy in the aspect of the photovoltaic performance, and the 0.1% LiF device has achieved the highest power-conversion efficiency (PCE) of 20.1%, as shown in Figure 2f. Insights from the preliminary research studies on the operational stability of solar cells suggest its strong dependence on the defect states of materials and devices.  $  ^{14,41-43}  $   With indications of the trap passivation effects by   $  Li^{+}  $  and   $  F^{-}  $  above, it is convincing that their incorporation should boost the thermal stability and photostability of PSCs–LiF as well. Because spiro-OMeTAD is generally reported to be fragile under heat/light conditions, PTAA instead has been utilized as a hole-transporting material for the stability tests, in order to avoid the degradation of HTL being a determining factor of the device stability.  $  ^{44,45}  $   The photovoltaic performances of ITO/  $  SnO_{2}  $  /CsFAMA:LiF/PTAA/Au devices with various LiF concentrations are included in Figure S4, and the devices have been stored under thermal stress at 85 °C to compare the thermal stabilities. The J-V measurement has been made with extensive intervals of 100–200 h in order to avoid immoderate relaxation because of frequent cooldowns. Changes in PCE along the stability test are exhibited in Figure 3a. As expected, the PSCs–LiF show better thermal stabilities compared to the

reference device, and the 0.1% LiF device retains 92% of its initial PCE after 1119 h storage at 85 °C. However, when 0.5% of LiF is added, the solar cell performance degrades faster than the pristine device. Coherently to the results from various analyses in Figures 1 and 2, excessive Li $  ^{+}  $  and F $  ^{-}  $  in perovskites result in extra mobile ions, which can be detrimental factors causing the thermal degradation even with the reduced interfacial defects, as suggested in other studies. $ ^{46-50} $  Interestingly, when each cell parameter is compared,  $  J_{\text{SC}}  $  of devices does not show notable degradations nor differences by the LiF concentrations (Figure 3c). However,  $  V_{\text{OC}}  $  shows distinctive degradation trends as LiF concentration changes, where the LiF incorporation suppresses the degradation of  $  V_{\text{OC}}  $ , which is the parameter closely related to the trap states of devices as mentioned. Regarding that the fill factor ( $  FF  $ ) is usually determined by the quality of films and junctions, Li $  ^{+}  $  and F $  ^{-}  $  bonded within perovskite and interfaces are suspected to restrain the compositional loss by potential atomic diffusion and evaporation, especially of the facile organic cations (Figure 3d). $ ^{51,52} $

The capacitance analysis (or the admittance spectroscopy) is a common method used to understand the distribution of deep traps as reported.  $  ^{13,14,53}  $   Trapping/detrapping of the charge carriers induced by AC perturbation reveals the trap density of states (tDOS) in solar cells, and further details of the analysis are embedded in our previous work.  $  ^{20,38}  $   The trap distribution spectra for the pristine devices are compared in Figure 3e, where the reduced trap densities and trap shifts toward the shallow level are observed by LiF incorporation. These results in the tDOS well explain the trends of PL spectra and device performances in Figures 1 and 2, which are both highly dependent on the defect characteristics of the device. Also, it is noteworthy that the trap density is not further reduced as the LiF concentration exceeds 0.2%, at which the passivation effect by LiF appears nearly saturated. This observation supports that when LiF is added excessively beyond the critical concentration, the negative effects of extra mobile ions in the perovskite can overwhelm the positive effects of the defect passivation by LiF.

More interestingly, as tDOS of the devices are analyzed after annealing at 85 °C for 100 h, the traps are reduced in all the devices, with the formation of additional trap states near   $  \sim  $  0.5 eV (Figure 3f). Though the origin of this thermal transition of electronic traps is unclear, the relaxation and formation of different trap states are considered as reflections of the degradation processes occurring in the devices.  $  ^{54}  $   As shown in Figure 3f, the relaxation of deep traps (>0.6 eV) is observed to be more drastic, while the formation of secondary traps (more clear in the insets) is to be mitigated by LiF incorporation. Consequently, the 0.1% LiF device exhibits the best passivated-trap states even after the postannealing step. Based on the trap analyses of pristine and postannealed devices, LiF not only passivates the electronic traps during the fabrication steps but also restrains further degradation mechanisms at high temperature, resulting in less-defective and stable PSCs.

# CONCLUSIONS

We introduce LiF as a facile additive in the perovskite to passivate defects, enhancing both device performance and stabilities of the PSCs.   $  Li^{+}  $   and   $  F^{-}  $   ions are expected to form strong bonds with halides and organic cations mostly at the perovskite terminations and grain boundaries, passivating undesirable ionic defects. The incorporation of these ionic passivators improves the performance of PSCs, enabling high efficiency over 20%, and the hysteresis effect is largely reduced. The LiF incorporation restrains both 85 °C and 1 sun degradations securing over 90% of the initial PCEs for more than 1000 h. Furthermore, the temporal evolutions of trap states and photovoltaic parameters are correlated with the device stabilities, exploring the impact of   $  Li^{+}  $   and   $  F^{-}  $   ions on the defect characteristics of devices. Outstanding thermal stability of the PSC–LiF has also been certified with large-area flexible devices, and LiF has demonstrated its potential as promising ionic passivators for advanced applications of large-area and flexible solar cells by securing both thermal stability and photostability.
