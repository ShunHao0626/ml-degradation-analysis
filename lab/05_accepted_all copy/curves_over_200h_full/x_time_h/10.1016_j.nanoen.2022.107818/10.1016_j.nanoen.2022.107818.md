# 1. Introduction

Metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted tremendous interest due to the unique properties of perovskite materials, such as long carrier diffusion length, low exciton binding energy, tunable direct bandgap, and so on [1-3]. The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of lead (Pb)-based PSCs has been boosted to  $ 25.7\% $ , comparable with that of the

Tin (Sn) perovskites as photovoltaic materials show great promise due to their suitable bandgaps and lower toxicity. However, various defects in Sn perovskites induce significant losses in devices. In this work, we report a strategy of dual-site passivation of Sn-related defects in lead-free Sn perovskite solar cells. We adopt ethylenediammonium halide salts (i.e.,   $  EDAI_{2}  $   and   $  EDABr_{2}  $  ) as additives in Sn perovskite and find that both   $  EDAI_{2}  $   and   $  EDABr_{2}  $   can suppress Sn oxidation and passivate trap states, however,   $  EDABr_{2}  $   works better than   $  EDAI_{2}  $   in terms of passivating undesired grain boundaries and surface Sn vacancies, and reducing background hole density, due to the synergistic roles of   $  EDA^{2+}  $   cation and Br anion. Moreover,   $  EDABr_{2}  $   exhibits more consummate passivation effect on Sn1 antisite defects as deep-level traps due to its larger electrostatic potential and shorter bonding length between -Br and -Sn. These enable great suppression of non-radiative recombination and enhancement of charge carrier transport. As a result, the best-performing   $  EDABr_{2}  $ -modified device achieves a power conversion efficiency of 14.23% with long-term durability of keeping ~93% of its initial efficiency after storing for ~4000 h and improved operational stability. Our work provides a promising approach to choose satisfactory passivators and fabricate efficient Sn perovskite solar cells.

Although the PCE of Sn PSCs has remarkably increased to over 14% in the past decade, their performance still lags far behind their Pb counterparts due to severe non-radiative recombination losses in Sn perovskites and solar cells   $  [16–18]  $  . Large number of deep-level defects in Sn perovskites cause this problem, which mainly originate from: (1) easy oxidation of   $  Sn^{2+}  $   to   $  Sn^{4+}  $  , especially at the surface of perovskite film   $  [19]  $  ; (2) intrinsic point defects, such as antisite substitutions (e.g.,   $  Sn_{I}  $   antisite) and undercoordinated   $  Sn^{2+}  $   (e.g.,   $  Sn_{I}  $   interstitial)   $  [20,21]  $  ; (3) grain boundaries (GBs) caused by the small grains during fast crystallization   $  [22]  $  . These deep-level defects act as primary non-radiative recombination centers, leading to significant efficiency loss   $  [23,24]  $  . On the other hand, the low formation energy of Sn vacancies induces intrinsic p-type doping in the bulk Sn perovskites, leading to high background carrier density   $  [25]  $  , which are classified as shallow-level defects   $  [20]  $  .

Additive engineering is an effective approach to suppress both intrinsic defects (e.g., Sn vacancies and   $  Sn_{1}  $   antisite defects) and extrinsic defects (e.g., GBs) [26–28]. Tin fluoride (  $  SnF_{2}  $  ) works as the most commonly used and indispensable additive for Sn-based perovskites to mitigate oxidation of   $  Sn^{2+}  $   to   $  Sn^{4+}  $ , but excessive   $  SnF_{2}  $   may accumulate at the GBs and result in phase segregation [29]. Moreover, merely   $  SnF_{2}  $   itself is usually not enough to prevent the Sn oxidation of   $  Sn^{2+}  $   to   $  Sn^{4+}  $ . Thus, another one or more additives, such as pyrazine [29,30], germanium iodide (  $  GeI_{2}  $  ) [31,32], phenethylammonium iodide (PEAI) [33,34], ethylenediammonium diiodide (  $  EDAI_{2}  $  ) [35–40], etc., are usually introduced to make efficient Sn PSCs. Among these additives,   $  EDAI_{2}  $   has been proved effective to improve film quality and device performance along with   $  SnF_{2}  $ . W. Ke et al. have firstly introduced   $  EDAI_{2}  $   into FA- and MA-based Sn PSCs and formed so-called “hollow” perovskites to tune the bandgap and improve the film morphology [35–37]. In 2018, Joker et al. have added trace amount of   $  EDAI_{2}  $   into FASnI  $  _{3}  $   PSCs to strengthen their long-term stability and the efficiency increased continuously to 8.9% after storage over 1400 h due to slow relaxation of crystal structure [38]. Hayase and coworkers have incorporated ethylammonium iodide (EAI) with 1 mol%   $  EDAI_{2}  $   to regulate the tolerance factor to nearly 1 and achieved an enhanced PCE of 13.24% but compromised short-circuit current density (  $  J_{SC}  $  ) [41]. Sandhu et al. have studied the synergistic effects of   $  EDAI_{2}  $   and 2-phenylethylammonium iodide (PEAI) on the performance of FASnI  $  _{3}  $   PSCs [40]. While, so far most of the reports focus on the A site cation (  $  EDA^{2+}  $ ), the roles of X site anion in EDA-based additives of Sn PSCs have not been fully understood yet. We also notice that some intrinsic point defects, for example,   $  Sn_{1}  $   antisite defects in Sn perovskites, which have relatively higher formation energy than Sn vacancies [25], and have been paid less attention.

Herein, we report a facile dual-site defect passivator of ethylenediammonium dibromide (EDABr $ _2 $ ) as multiple roles. We find that the incorporation of Br $ ^- $  in EDABr $ _2 $  better passivates the increased GBs caused by EDA $ ^{2+} $  cation compared with that of I $ ^- $  in EDAI $ _2 $ . More interestingly, EDABr $ _2 $  exhibits more excellent passivation effects than EDAI $ _2 $  for the Sn-related deep-level traps (e.g., Sn $ _1 $  antisite defects) due to larger electrostatic potential (ESP) and shorter bonding length between -Br and -Sn. EDABr $ _2 $  also effectively prevents Sn $ ^{2+} $  oxidation under coordinated effects of EDA $ ^{2+} $  and Br $ ^- $ . These effective passivations enable significant suppressed non-radiative recombination by the decreased deep-level defects and enhanced carrier transport by reduced background carrier density, leading to largely improved FF of 77.40% and open-circuit voltage ( $ V_{OC} $ ) of 0.817 V for the EDABr $ _2 $ -based devices, among the best values of Sn PSCs to date. As a result, our champion EDABr $ _2 $  modified FASnI $ _3 $  PSC obtains a greatly enhanced PCE of 14.23% with outstanding long-term stability of maintaining almost 93% of its initial efficiency after storing for ~4000 h in N $ _2 $  atmosphere and improved operational stability. Our work paves a route to understand the defects in Sn perovskites and offers a promising defect passivation

strategy to further improve efficiency and stability of Sn PSCs.

# 2. Experimental section

# 2.1.Materials

All chemicals were used as received without further purification, including ethylenediammonium dibromide (EDABr₂, ≥99.5%, Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp.), ethylenediammonium diiodide (EDAI₂, >98.0%, Sigma Aldrich), tin (II) iodide (SnI₂, 99.99%, Sigma Aldrich), tin (II) fluorine (SnF₂, 99%, Sigma Aldrich), germanium (II) iodide (GeI₂, >99.8%, Sigma Aldrich), formamidinium iodide (FAI, >98%, Greatcell), poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) polystyrenesulfonate (PEDOT:PSS, Clevious PVP AI 4083) and 2,20,7,70-tetrakis (N,N0-di-p-methoxyphenylamine)-9,90-spirobifluorene (SpiroOMeTAD, ≥99.8%, Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp.). Dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, ≥99.9%), and chlorobenzene (CB, 99.8%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich.

# 2.2. Device fabrication

The glass substrates coated with indium tin oxide (ITO) were cleaned with detergent, deionized water and ethanol by ultrasonication for 15 min each. After drying with nitrogen flow, the ITO substrates were treated with ultraviolet-ozone for 15 min. PEDOT:PSS, a hole transport layer, was then spin-coated onto the ITO at 500 rpm for 10 s followed by 5000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 150 °C for 20 min. After that, the substrates were transferred into a glove box filled with nitrogen. 0.9 M control perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving SnF₂, GeI₂, FAI and SnI₂ at a molar ratio of 0.10:0.05:1.00:1.00 in mixed solvent of DMF (800 μL) and DMSO (200 μL). 0.9 M EDAI₂-modified precursor solution was prepared by adding 2.8 mg EDAI₂, 14.1 mg SnF₂, 14.7 mg GeI₂, 151.7 mg FAI and 335.3 mg SnI₂ into 1 mL mixed solvent (DMF:DMSO = 4:1). And 0.9 M EDABr₂-modified precursor solution was prepared by adding 2.0 mg EDABr₂, 14.1 mg SnF₂, 14.7 mg GeI₂, 151.7 mg FAI and 335.3 mg SnI₂ into 1 mL mixed solvent (DMF:DMSO = 4:1). The perovskite precursor was filtered with 0.22 μm polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) before spin coating. 50 μL precursor was spin-coated onto the substrate at 5000 rpm for 50 s with 600 μL CB dripped onto the perovskite film at 10 s. Then the as-prepared films were annealed at 70 °C for 20 min. Afterwards, C₆₀ (25 nm), BCP (6 nm), and Ag (100 nm) were sequentially deposited via thermal evaporation under 5 × 10⁻⁴ Pa, resulting in an active area of 0.09 cm². Devices were encapsulated with cover glass and ultraviolet-curable epoxy. The hole only devices for space charge limited current (SCLC) measurement were fabricated with the structure of ITO/PEDOT:PSS/Perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD/Au.

# 2.3. Film characterization

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained using a Shimazu XRD-6100 diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiation (  $  \lambda = 1.5406 \AA  $  ) at 40 kV and 30 mA. Ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorbance spectra of the perovskite films were measured using a Lambda 950 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer Inc.). The top-view scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were taken by FE-SEM, Regulus-8230, Hitachi. The cross-sectional SEM images of inverted PSCs were taken with Hitachi S-4800. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) and Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) images were performed using Bruker Nano Inc DI Multi Mode 8. AFM and KPFM with commercially available MESP conductive probe coated with Co/Cr. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) results were carried out using a photoelectron spectrometer (ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermal Fisher Scientific). Photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved PL (TRPL) measurements were obtained with FLS980 (Edinburgh Inc.). And they were both conducted using a supercontinuum pulsed laser (Wuhan Yangtze Soton Laser Co. Ltd.). Ultraviolet

# 2.4. Device characterization

Current density-voltage (J-V) curves were collected using a Keysight Technologies B2901A source meter under simulated AM 1.5 G solar illumination (Enlitech, SS-F5-3A) at 100 mW/cm² (1 sun) in an N₂-filled glove box, as calibrated by NREL reference silicon solar cells. The measured area is fixed to be 0.0784 cm² with a non-reflective black shadow mask. Unless otherwise stated, all J-V results were measured in a nitrogen-filled glovebox with a scanning speed of 100 mV/s. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra was tested under monochromatic light from 300 nm to 1000 nm (QEX10, PV Measurements, Inc.). The steady-state PCEs was conducted by setting the bias voltage at the voltage of maximum power point and then tracking the current density for 600 s. The maximum power point (MPP) tests of the devices with encapsulation were conducted by monitoring the variation of voltage and current density at the maximum power point over time under continuous illumination using a LED light in ambient condition with ~50% relative humidity at room temperature. The long-term shelf stability of VOC and PCE was tracked for the devices encapsulated by UV curable adhesive and stored in a filled nitrogen glove box. The light intensity dependence of VOC and JSC were performed by setting a series of light intensity and then measured the J-V characteristics. The SCLC measurements were carried with a Keysight Technologies B2901A source meter under dark condition. The dark J-V characteristics and Mott-Schottky measurements were recorded by Agilent 4155 C and 4284A with a test fixture of Agilent 16442A. According to the Mott-Schottky equation:   $  \frac{A^{2}}{C^{2}} = \frac{2}{\epsilon\nu_{0}N_{A}}(V_{bl} - V)  $  , the   $  V_{bl}  $   and   $  N_{A}  $   can be sequentially calculated from the x axis intercept and slope obtained by linear fitting of the curve [42]. The transient photovoltaic (TPV), transient photocurrent (TPC) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were carried out by the all-in-one characterization platform (PAIOS, Fluxim AG, Switzerland). Thermal admittance spectroscopy (TAS) measurements were performed at a frequency from 0.1 Hz to 1 MHz with 20 mV of AC sinusoidal in dark. To convert x-axis from angular frequency to   $  E_{\omega} = k_{B}T\ln(\frac{\omega_{0}}{\omega})  $  , the   $  \omega_{0}  $   was calculated from frequency-dependent capacitance plot via relaxation process instead of Arrhenius plot of characteristic frequency.

# 2.5. Density functional theory calculations

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed utilizing the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP) [43] code with the projector augmented wave (PAW) method [44–46]. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) [47] was chosen for electron exchange and correlation. Energy cutoff, energy and force convergence criteria were set at 450 eV,   $  10^{-5}  $   eV and 0.05 eV Å  $  ^{-1}  $  , respectively, in all calculations. We used a   $  2 \times 2 \times 1  $   gamma centered k-point grid generated by the Monkhorst-Pack scheme for FASnI  $  _{3}  $   (001) surface structures and two passivation models with EDABr  $  _{2}  $   and EDAI  $  _{2}  $   molecules.

We calculated the ESP of two molecules using Gaussian 09 program in which B3PW91 functional and all-electron double-  $  \xi  $  valence basis sets of 3–21 G* were employed. Subsequently, we built the FASnI  $  _{3}  $  (001) surface structure with Sn  $  _{1}  $  antisite defects according to the experimental observation. The EDABr  $  _{2}  $ /EDAI  $  _{2}  $  additive molecules were also considered to bond with the defect in our passivation models. All crystal structures were built by Materials Studio and Vesta [48]. Geometry optimizations of EDAI  $  _{2}  $ /EDABr  $  _{2}  $  were performed under default mode.

# 3. Results and discussion

We first examined the effects of   $  EDA^{2+}  $   in   $  EDAI_{2}  $   and   $  EDABr_{2}  $   additives on   $  FASnI_{3}  $   perovskite structure by performing XRD measurements for the control,   $  EDAI_{2}  $ , and   $  EDABr_{2}  $   modified films, which henceforth are referred as to control-Sn,   $  EDAI_{2}-Sn  $ , and   $  EDABr_{2}-Sn  $   films, respectively. As shown in Fig. 1(a), the six dominant diffraction peaks assigned to (100), (120), (200), (122), (222), and (300) crystal planes indicate that all films adopt a 3D orthorhombic (Amm2)   $  FASnI_{3}  $   structure [34,49]. Compared with the control-Sn film, the perovskite peaks in both   $  EDAI_{2}-Sn  $   and   $  EDABr_{2}-Sn  $   films shifted towards lower angle (Fig. 1(b)), suggesting that the larger   $  EDA^{2+}  $   cation was incorporated into the lattice, causing the lattice to expand and likely forming a 3D hollow structure [35–37,50]. In addition, the intensity of (100) and (200) diffraction peaks increase remarkably with the incorporation of   $  EDAI_{2}  $   and   $  EDABr_{2}  $ , which manifests a highly preferred orientation and enhanced crystallinity. Moreover, the full width at half maximum (FWHM) values of (100) and (200) crystal planes of both   $  EDAI_{2}-Sn  $   and   $  EDABr_{2}-Sn  $   films are higher than that of control-Sn film, suggesting the decreased grain size with the incorporation of   $  EDA^{2+}  $   (Fig. 1(c) and Table S1).

We further investigated the morphology of the control-Sn,   $  EDAI_{2}-Sn  $  , and   $  EDABr_{2}-Sn  $   perovskite films via SEM measurements (Fig. 1(d-f)). It is interesting that, both   $  EDAI_{2}-Sn  $   and   $  EDABr_{2}-Sn  $   perovskites show decreased average grain size of   $  \sim250  $   nm, compared with that (  $  \sim350  $   nm) of the control one (insets of Fig. 1(d-f)), in accordance with the FWHM values and previous reports [38,50]. Smaller grains are usually detrimental for the perovskite film quality due to increased GBs that act as deep-level defects [51,52]. EDA-based salts could likely form a 3D hollow structure and reduce the perovskite grain size [35], however in our case, they could additionally passivate the GBs and improve the Sn perovskite film quality and device performance, which will be analyzed in details later.

To further investigate the effects of I and Br anions of   $  EDA^{2+}  $   salts on morphology evolution and defect passivation of   $  FASnI_{3}  $   perovskite film, AFM and KPFM measurements were performed. The control-Sn and   $  EDAI_{2}-Sn  $   films exhibit similar root-mean-square (RMS) roughness values of 18.0 and 17.2 nm, respectively, while, the   $  EDABr_{2}-Sn  $   film has a reduced RMS roughness of 13.3 nm (Fig. 2(a-c)). Reflected by the AFM line scans (Fig. 2(g-i)), the control-Sn and   $  EDAI_{2}-Sn  $   films show large peak valley fluctuation between grain interiors (Gls) and GBs, indicative of rough surface. While, the   $  EDABr_{2}-Sn  $   film has smaller and shrunk fluctuation and shrinks. This implies that   $  EDA^{2+}  $   incorporation could effectively smoothen the perovskite surface in the presence of Br'. A smooth surface is beneficial for the contact between perovskite film and electron transport layer (ETL) to improve electron extraction [53]. However, the surface roughness of   $  EDAI_{2}-Sn  $   film is very close to that of control-Sn film due to the increased GBs. Br anion in   $  EDABr_{2}-Sn  $   film could segregate to GBs and slowly release the strain stress due to its higher volatility [54], leading to reduced surface roughness.

Meanwhile, the potential contrast between GBs and GIs in both control-Sn and   $  EDAI_{2}-Sn  $   films is significantly larger than that in   $  EDABr_{2}-Sn  $   film (Fig. 2(g-i)), indicating that the former two films have a deeper Fermi level at GBs, which likely results from a higher density of defects [55]. We then infer that the incorporation of   $  Br^{-}  $  in   $  EDABr_{2}  $   enables significant reduction of surface roughness and more effective passivation of deep-level defects at GBs due to   $  EDA^{2+}  $  -induced smaller grains, compared with that of   $  I^{-}  $  in   $  EDAI_{2}  $  .

Surface Sn vacancies originated from the oxidation of   $  Sn^{2+}  $   to   $  Sn^{4+}  $ , are one of the most notorious defects in Sn-based perovskites, which can induce deep-level defects as dominant non-radiative recombination centers and severely impair the   $  V_{OC}  $   and FF of Sn PSCs [18,19]. We then performed XPS measurements to evaluate the   $  Sn^{4+}/Sn^{2+}  $   ratio on the surface of perovskite films. We then measured steady-state PL and TRPL to investigate the charge dynamics in all films. We deposited the Sn perovskite films with two different configurations as glass/FASnI $ _3 $  and glass/FASnI $ _3 $ /C $ _{60} $ . The slight increase in bandgap is reflected by the blue shift of PL peak position from 910 nm for control-Sn film to around 897 nm for both EDAl $ _2 $ -Sn and EDABr $ _2 $ -Sn films (Figs. 3(e) and S1(a)), which is consistent with the trend of their absorption band edge change (Fig. S2). The PL intensity of the EDABr $ _2 $ -Sn film is obviously higher than that of the control-Sn and EDAl $ _2 $ -Sn films regardless of the existence of electron transport layer (ETL), indicating reduced non-radiative recombination and thus decreased density of trap states in perovskite layer. Figs. 3(f) and S1b shows the TRPL plot fitted with a biexponential decay function defined as  $ I = A_1exp(-t/\tau_1) + A_2exp(-t/\tau_2) $ , and the detailed trace fit data listed in Tables S3 and S4. In general, two components are usually assigned to trap-assisted non-radiative recombination for the faster decay  $ \tau_1 $  and radiative recombination process of free carriers in the perovskite bulk for the slower decay  $ \tau_2 $  [19,56]. For the configuration of glass/FASnI $ _3 $ , the lifetime constant  $ \tau_1 $  is elongated from 4.58 ns for the control-Sn film and 6.55 ns for the EDAl $ _2 $ -Sn film to 8.10 ns for the EDABr $ _2 $ -Sn film, indicating that the non-radiative recombination at GBs and surface Sn vacancies has been greatly suppressed upon incorporation of EDABr $ _2 $ , which is consistent with the KPFM and XPS results. Moreover, the longest  $ \tau_2 $  value (21.53 ns) for the EDABr $ _2 $ -Sn film implies

an improved quality of bulk perovskite seemingly due to decreased background carrier density. Consequently, the average carrier lifetimes are 6.32, 12.07, and 16.50 ns for the control-Sn,   $  EDAI_{2}-Sn  $  , and   $  EDABr_{2}-Sn  $   films, respectively. The TRPL decays of these three perovskite films capped with   $  C_{60}  $   display the similar trend with those without   $  C_{60}  $   ETL. The average carrier lifetime of the   $  EDABr_{2}-Sn  $   (7.83 ns) film is longer than that of the   $  EDAI_{2}-Sn  $   (4.70 ns) and control-Sn (2.87 ns) films, indicating suppressed non-radiative recombination and thus improved contact at the Sn perovskite/  $  C_{60}  $   interface. These results suggest that the   $  EDA^{2+}  $   cation and halide anions (I, Br) could synergistically passivate the defects at GBs and inhibit   $  Sn^{2+}  $   oxidation with improved film quality, but   $  EDABr_{2}  $   performs better than   $  EDAI_{2}  $  , as illustrated in Fig. 4(a).

To further investigate the effect of EDAX $ _2 $  on the energy levels of these three Sn perovskite films, UPS measurements were conducted as shown in Fig. 3(g-h). It is widely accepted that the Sn vacancies in bulk perovskite tend to induce highly p-type doping characteristic [60], which increases the background carrier (hole) density and thus the undesired recombination process in the bulk [41]. Considering the existence of Br' in EDABr2, we conjecture that EDABr2 enables to inhibit the p-type doping and lowers background hole density [30]. To verify this hypothesis, the capacitance-voltage (C-V) measurements were conducted for the corresponding Sn-based PSCs. According to the Mott-Schottky equation:   $  \frac{A^{2}}{C^{2}} = \frac{2}{\varepsilon\varepsilon_{0}N_{A}}(V_{bi} - V)  $  , where A, e,   $  \varepsilon  $  ,   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $  ,   $  N_{A}  $  , and   $  V_{bi}  $   are the active area of device, elementary charge, relative permittivity of the perovskite, vacuum permittivity, hole density, and built-in potential, respectively [61]. It is clear that, the control-Sn and EDAI2-Sn devices have a relatively high hole density   $  N_{A}  $   of   $  1.19 \times 10^{16}  $   and   $  8.26 \times 10^{15}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  , respectively, however, the EDABr2-Sn device has a reduced hole density   $  N_{A}  $   of   $  5.76 \times 10^{15}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  , confirming that EDABr2 helps reduce background carrier density. Meanwhile, as shown in Fig. 6(a), the   $  V_{bi}  $   value of the

EDABr $ _2 $ -Sn device is enhanced to 0.55 V, compared with that of 0.42 V for control-Sn device and 0.50 V for EDAI $ _2 $ -Sn device. This indicates that the photo-generated charge carriers are more efficiently driven to charge transport layer in EDABr $ _2 $ -Sn device [62], which is in good agreement with the UPS results and contributes to the enhanced  $ V_{OC} $  and PCE.

The  $ J_0 $  for the control-Sn device is  $ 1.82 \times 10^{-3} \mathrm{~mA/cm^2} $ , indicating a large defect state and leakage current [41]. With the incorporation of additives, the  $ J_0 $  for the  $ \mathrm{EDAl}_{2}-\mathrm{Sn} $  and  $ \mathrm{EDABr}_{2}-\mathrm{Sn} $  devices drops to  $ 5.66 \times 10^{-6} $  and  $ 5.02 \times 10^{-7} \mathrm{~mA/cm^2} $ , respectively. This indicates that the Br-contained additive, i.e.,  $ \mathrm{EDABr}_{2} $ , has more outstanding capability to lower the background carrier density than the I-contained additive, i.e.,  $ \mathrm{EDAl}_{2} $ , evidencing the stronger ability of  $ \mathrm{EDABr}_{2} $  to restrict the formation of Sn vacancies during the fabrication process, which in turn improves the resultant FF and  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  [54].

We further performed TAS measurements to probe the trap-state distributions and energies in FASnI $ _3 $  films. The energetic profile of trap density of states (tDOS) can be derived from the angular frequency dependent capacitance using the equation [64],  $ N_T(E_\omega) = -\frac{V_{bi}}{qW}\frac{dC}{d\omega}\frac{\omega}{k_B P} $ , where q is the elementary charge,  $ \omega $  is the angular frequency,  $ C $  is the capacitance,  $ k_B $  is the Boltzmann's constant, and  $ T $  is the temperature.  $ V_{bi} $  and  $ W $  (depletion width) were extracted from the Mott-Schottky analysis. The applied angular frequency  $ \omega $  defines an energetic demarcation:  $ E_\omega = k_B T \ln \left( \frac{\omega_0}{\omega} \right) $ , where  $ \omega_0 $  is the attempt-to-escape frequency. The trap states below the energy demarcation can capture or emit charges with the given  $ \omega $  and contribute to the capacitance [64]. As

shown in Fig. 6(d), the tDOS sharply decreases with the incorporation of EDA-based additives for both shallow-level (<0.4 eV) and deep-level defects (>0.4 eV) [65]. Moreover, the EDAI₂-Sn film displays almost 10-fold greater trap density than that of the EDABr₂-Sn film at lower energy region, indicating that the background hole density in the bulk was more efficiently reduced by the incorporation of EDABr₂, in consistent with C-V and dark J-V results. Meanwhile, lower trap density at the deep-level proves superior passivation effect of EDABr₂ on defects at GBs, surface Sn vacancies, and Sn₁ antisite defects to that of EDAI₂.

# 4. Conclusion

We have demonstrated that the ethylenediammonium halide salts (i.e.   $  EDAl_{2}  $   and   $  EDABr_{2}  $  ) can passivate trap states and prevent   $  Sn^{2+}  $   from being oxidized, however,   $  EDABr_{2}  $   possesses a more splendid capability on passivation of deep-level defects, such as undesired GBs, surface Sn vacancies, and   $  Sn_{1}  $   antisite defects owing to larger ESP and shorter bonding length between -Br and -Sn, as well as reduction of background hole density in perovskite bulk, leading to remarkably suppressed nonradiative recombination and enhanced carrier transport. As a result, our best-performing   $  EDABr_{2}-Sn  $   device achieves a high PCE up to 14.23% with extremely superior long-term stability of keeping almost 93% of its initial efficiency for   $  \sim4000  $   h storage in   $  N_{2}  $   atmosphere and enhanced operational stability. Our work paves a new way for further understanding of the defects in Sn perovskites and development of Sn-based PSCs with both excellent efficiency and stability.
