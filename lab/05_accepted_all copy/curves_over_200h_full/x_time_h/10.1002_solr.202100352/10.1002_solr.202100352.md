Herein, a new paradigm toward accelerating the development of efficient and stable PSCs is opened up.

# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have had a lasting impression on the scientific community because of their fast progress as high efficient and low-cost technology.  $  ^{[1]}  $   Starting from Kojima et al., in 2009, maximum power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) exceeding 25% have already been certified, resulting from crystallinity

Moreover, it is urgently needed to develop efficient ILs that play complementary roles in additive engineering to hinder phase separation, bulk vacancy defects, surface/interface defects, negative ion migration, and moisture sensitivity in PSCs.  $  ^{[29]}  $

To date, the research works concentrated mainly on the interaction between the anions and perovskite.  $  ^{[30]}  $   A question naturally arises about the possible utilization of cations of ILs to make up for the defects of A-site cations in PSCs. The organic cations in organic and inorganic perovskite are more sensitive and vulnerable to the ambient environment.  $  ^{[31]}  $   Especially under high temperature and continuous illumination conditions, the hydrogen bonding energy between organic cations and Pb–I framework would be weakened, resulting in the migrated organic cations, which has tremendous impacts on the formation of ionic displacements or charged defects and further speed up the negative ion migration and perovskite decomposition.  $  ^{[32,33]}  $   Hence, it is imperative to immobilize the organic cations to improve the stability and optoelectronic properties of perovskite.  $  ^{[32]}  $   More specifically, A-site cation compositional engineering is crucial to modulate the perovskite crystallization.  $  ^{[34]}  $   Nevertheless, due to the limited tolerance space of the 3D perovskite, only fewer organic cations other than   $  FA^{+}  $  have been recently exploited to tune the A-site formulation. Guanidinium (GA),  $  ^{[35]}  $   and hydrazinium  $  ^{[36]}  $   cations were developed to passivate the crystallographic defects through coordination/bonding with perovskite to achieve enhanced crystallinity and inhibited ion migration. Developing new organic cations to enhance crystallinity, reduce the charge trap density, elongates the carrier lifetime, and inhibit the ionic migration is urgently needed. Meanwhile, a clear understanding of the role of organic cations in ILs on the photophysical process and their relationship with photovoltaic performance is still lacking.

Herein, we introduce 4-fluoro-phenylammonium tetrafluoroborate-based zwitterionic ionic liquid (ZIL) with rationally designed cations for reducing defects, suppressing ion migration, improving crystallinity, and enhancing carrier transporting of the polycrystalline perovskite. The 4-fluoro-phenylammonium tetrafluoroborate contains both an electron donor and a hydrophobic functional group to improve photovoltaic performance and stability simultaneously. First, the framework  $ \pi $ -conjugated benzene structure is conducive to electron transport, and it can

donate electrons to the iodide compounds (such as   $  I_{2}  $  ,   $  CH_{3}I  $  , etc.) in the perovskite film.  $  ^{[37]}  $   Second, the   $  -NH_{3}  $   structure attached to the benzene ring can effectively fill the A-site vacancies. Third, the fluorination of phenyl ammonium can enhance the moisture barrier through the molecular accumulation of C–F bonds. Moreover, incorporating   $  BF_{4}^{-}  $  into the perovskite could help exhibit a longer photoluminescence lifetime and lower trap density. In short, 4-fluoro-phenylammonium tetrafluoroborate can also achieve crystal growth regulation and defect passivation. Photophysical characterization and electrical analysis reveal that the ZIL-modified hysteresis-free PSCs could exhibit the best   $  V_{OC}  $   and PCE of 1.16 V and 22.5% while maintaining more than 80% of the initial efficiency after 2000 h testing.

# 2. Results and Discussion

The chemical structure of the multifunctional 4-fluorophenylammonium tetrafluoroborate-based ZIL, possessing 4-fluoro-phenylammonium (4FB $  ^{+}  $ ) as cations and tetrafluoroborate (BF $  _{4}^{-}  $ ) as anions, is shown in Figure 1a. The preparing process of ZIL-modified (FAPbI $  _{3}  $ ) $  _{1-x}  $ (MAPbBr $  _{3}  $ ) $  _{x}  $  perovskite film by a two-step sequential spin-coating process is shown in Figure 1b. Briefly, a mixed solution of organic components FAI, MABr, MACl, and different concentrations of ZIL (0, 0.1, 0.5, and 1 mg/mL) were spin coated onto the PbI $  _{2}  $  film. The ZIL-modified perovskite film was obtained by further annealing at 150 °C to achieve complete crystallization. Figure 1c shows the possible passivation mechanism of the defect by the ZIL. As we discussed in Section 1, first, the intrinsic organic anionic and cationic defects should be passivated by the 4FB $  ^{+}  $ cation and BF $  _{4}^{-}  $ anion, respectively, as shown in Figure 1c. Second, ZIL was expected to be capable of improving perovskite crystallization through the chemical interaction (like hydrogen bond) between the ZIL and perovskite components in the precursor solution. Previous work has shown that hydrogen bonding can effectively prevent the diffusion and degradation of cations, and more importantly, it can passivate shallow defects and improve the crystallization of perovskite films. $ ^{[38,39]} $  The incorporation of fluorine-substituted benzene ring was expected to not only improve the moisture stability of perovskite film but also improves the electronic environment. $ ^{[40,41]} $  Specifically, the benzene ring could act as the electron donor for more conducive interaction between the ZIL containing benzene ring and perovskite, and reduce the channel of nonideal carrier recombination, and ultimately realize efficient electron transfer. $ ^{[40-42]} $  In a word, multiple functions were expected to be achieved after the incorporation of ZIL. Subsequently, we investigated the chemical interaction between ZIL and perovskites by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurement (Figure 1d–g and Figure S1, Supporting Information). The characteristic peak of B 1s and F 1s at 185.0 and 687.7 eV, respectively, confirm the incorporated ZIL in the modified perovskite films. As the introduction of ZIL, we can confirm the chemical interaction between ZIL and the perovskite film by a shift to the reduced binding energy in the peaks of Pb $  ^{2+}  $  in Figure 1g, revealing that amino-containing 4FB-BF $  _{4}  $  can bind to the uncoordinated Pb $  ^{2+}  $  and fill the organic vacancy. Generally, the photovoltaic performance of PSCs is affected by carrier transport and recombination. Carrier density can be obtained by classical Mott–Schottky via capacitance–voltage measurements.  $  ^{[52]}  $   Usually, the interfacial carrier density is inversely proportional to the slope of the Mott–Schottky plot. As shown in Figure 5a, the ZIL-modified device exhibited a slightly increased slope compared with the control device, indicating a decrease in carrier recombination.  $  ^{[53]}  $   As the built-in potential (  $  V_{bi}  $  ) is closely associated with the   $  V_{OC}  $  ,  $  ^{[54]}  $   the   $  V_{bi}  $   of the control and target devices was determined by measuring capacitance–voltage curves. The target device showed a   $  V_{bi}  $   of 1.08 V, which was much higher than 0.97 V of the control device. This indicated that carrier separation and extraction were improved after ZIL modification. Accordingly, the deviation of the slope from unity   $  k_{B}T/q  $   is closely related to the trap-assisted recombination in PSCs.  $  ^{[55]}  $   A slope of 2.04   $  k_{B}T/q  $   was obtained for the control device (Figure 5b), whereas it reduces to 1.42   $  k_{B}T/q  $   for the ZIL-modified device. These results confirmed that a relatively severe   $  V_{OC}  $   loss was suppressed mainly as a result of carrier recombination.

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, we developed a ZIL, including 4-fluorophenylammonium (4FB  $  ^{+}  $  ) as cations and tetrafluoroborate (BF  $  _{4}^{-}  $  ) as anions to achieve highly efficient and stable (FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  )  $  _{1-x}  $  (MAPbBr  $  _{3}  $  )  $  _{x}  $  -based PSCs. ZIL modification in perovskite can effectively reduce the trap states density and improve the photovoltaic performance of the PSCs. Experimental results from the point view of KPFM, SSPL, TRPL, SCLC, Mott–Schottky, IS measurements proved that the ZIL could effectively reduce the trap state density, thereby inhibiting the nonradiative recombination and achieving a high PCE exceeding 22% with a high V  $  _{OC}  $  of 1.16 V. The ZIL-modified PSCs also exhibit excellent long-term stability and maintain over 80% of the initial efficiency after being placed in ambient condition for 2000 h. The combination of defects engineering and carrier promotion by a ZIL can help to obtain high-performance and stable perovskite photovoltaics for future commercial production.

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials: The   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloid precursor (15% in   $  H_{2}O  $   colloidal dispersion) was obtained from Alfa Aesar (tin (IV) oxide), isopropanol (IPA, 99.9%), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.9%), and dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO, 99.8%) were purchased from Macklin. Formamidinium iodide (FAI, 99.9%), methylammonium bromide (MABr, 99.9%), and 4-fluoro-phenylammonium tetrafluoroborate (ZIL, 99.99%) were purchased from Greatcell Solar Materials. Methylammonium chloride (MACl, 99.9%) and   $  PbI_{2}  $   (99.99%) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. Spiro-OMeTAD (99.5%) was purchased from Advanced Election Technology Co., Ltd. 4-Tert-butylpyridine (t-BuPy), acetonitrile (ACN) (>99.9%), and lithiumbis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (Li-TFSI) were obtained from Aladdin. All chemicals were used without further purification.

Device Fabrication: ITO glass was cleaned by sequentially washing with detergent, deionized water, and absolute ethanol. Before using, the ITO was cleaned by UVO for 10 min. Then, the substrate was spin coated with a thin layer of SnO₂ nanoparticle film (2.67 wt% in H₂O) at 4000 rpm for 30 s and annealed in ambient air at 150 °C for 30 min. After that, 1.3 M of PbI₂ in a mixed solvent (DMF:DMSO = 9.5:0.5) was spin coated on ITO/SnO₂ substrate at 1500 rpm for 30 s and then annealed at 70 °C for 1 min. After that, the mixture solution of FAI:MABr:MACl (60 mg:6 mg:6 mg dissolved in 1 mL IPA) with different ZIL was spin coated onto the PbI₂ at 1300 rpm for 30 s and then annealed at 150 °C for 15 min in ambient condition. The hole transporting layer was deposited on top of the perovskite layer by 3000 rpm for 30 s using a spiro-OMeTAD solution, which consisted of 72.3 mg Spiro-OMeTAD, 17.5 μL bis(trifluoromethane) sulfonamide lithium salt stock solution (520 mg predissolved in 1 mL acetonitrile), 28.8 μL 4-tertbutylpyridine, and 1 mL chlorobenzene. An evaporator was used to deposit 80 nm Au electrodes at an evaporation rate of 0.3 nm s⁻¹ to complete the preparation of the entire device.

Materials and Devices Characterization: A nitrogen gun was used to clean the surfaces before carrying out the measurements. The SEM

images were acquired using a field-emission SEM (JEOL 7610 F). Agilent 5500 scanning probe microscopy was utilized for AFM topography and KPFM in tapping mode, whereas conductive atomic force microscope (CS-AFM) in contact mode with 1 V applied bias. The XRD patterns of the perovskite films (2θ scans) were carried out on a Rigaku SmartLab using Cu Kα radiation (  $  \lambda=1.5405  $   Å) as the X-ray source at a scanning rate of 8° min  $  ^{-1}  $  . The PL and SSPL measurements were carried out by a fluorescence luminescence spectrometer (FLS980). UV–vis–NIR absorption spectra were collected with a Shimadzu UV-1800 in the range from 300 to 900 nm. All measurements were carried out at room temperature in ambient conditions. XPS measurements were carried out using a ESCALAB 250Xi. EQE was measured by QE-IRIS of Enlitech. IS measurement was carried out in the dark by AMETEK VersaSTAT 3 F at a frequency from 0.1 Hz to 1 MHz. Mott–Schottky measurement was carried out using the same instrument as used in IS but at a fixed frequency of 1 kHz. The applied bias voltage range is 0–1.5 V. For the stability test, the unencapsulated PSCs devices were maintained in ambient conditions. The devices were measured using a digital source meter under simulated AM 1.5G solar illumination of 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  . The light intensity of the solar simulator was calibrated by a standard silicon solar cell provided by PV measurements Inc. The J-V measurements of all PSC devices were carried out using a Keithley 2400 as a source meter. The light intensity was calibrated using a certified reference cell. The J-V scan test was carried out at a certain time. The devices were measured both in a RS (1.2 V→0 V, step 0.05 V) and a FS (0 V→1.2 V, step 0.05 V).
