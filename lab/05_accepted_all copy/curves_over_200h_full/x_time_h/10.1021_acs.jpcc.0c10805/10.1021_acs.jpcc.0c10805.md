# Tuning the Interactions of Methylammonium Acetate with Acetonitrile to Create Efficient Perovskite Solar Cells

Bo Sun, Weiwei Wang, Hui Lu, Lingfeng Chao, Hao Gu, Lei Tao, Jianfei Hu, Bixin Li, Xinrong Zong, Wei Shi, Xueqin Ran, Hui Zhang, Yingdong Xia, Ping Li,  $  ^{*}  $   and Yonghua Chen  $  ^{*}  $

Cite This: J. Phys. Chem. C 2021, 125, 6555–6563

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: The elucidation of intermolecular interactions for the systematic control of nucleation and crystallization is key to produce perovskite photovoltaics with superior performance. In this study, we added a weak coordination solvent acetonitrile (ACN) to an ionic liquid precursor solution and studied the effect of ACN on the coordination environment of  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  in the precursor solution and determined the role of ACN in perovskite nucleation and the crystallization process. The addition of ACN was found to reduce the coordination between the solvent and perovskite solutes ( $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{C} = \mathrm{OO}^{-}\dots \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $ ), which facilitated the formation of large micelles by sharing halogens. More importantly, the strong hydrogen bond interaction between organic cations and ACN ( $ \mathrm{CH}_3 - \mathrm{NH}_3^+\dots $   $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{C}\equiv \mathrm{N} $ ) slowed the growth of perovskite crystals to produce a compact and smooth thin film.  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_{2.8}\mathrm{Br}_{0.2} $ -based solar cells based on intermolecular interaction tuning achieved a high-power conversion efficiency of over  $ 21\% $  with long-term stability for over  $ 1500\mathrm{h} $ . The results of this study demonstrate that the

performance of perovskite photovoltaic devices can effectively be improved by adjusting the chemical environment of various components of the precursor solution.

# INTRODUCTION

Lead halide perovskite-based photovoltaics have been at the forefront of emerging solar technologies because of a low manufacturing cost and a high power conversion efficiency (PCE).  $  ^{1-3}  $   These photovoltaics exhibit exceptional optical–electrical characteristics,  $  ^{4}  $   including a high carrier mobility,  $  ^{5}  $   long carrier diffusion length,  $  ^{6,7}  $   tunable band gap,  $  ^{8}  $   and high tolerance of defects  $  ^{9,10}  $   based on a unique crystal structure and electronic configurations, resulting in a remarkable increase in the record PCE from 3.8% to a certified 25.5% over the last several years.  $  ^{11,12}  $   The solution process is one of the most economical and simple strategies available and offers unparalleled advantages for fabricating high-quality perovskite films, as has been reported in a considerable number of studies.  $  ^{13-20}  $   A solvent provides a liquid environment for precursor-coordination molecule interactions and therefore plays a crucial role in perovskite nucleation and crystallization and ultimately influences the photovoltaic performance of perovskite solar cells (PSCs).  $  ^{21-26}  $

The Lewis bases, dimethyl sulfoxide, N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), and   $  \gamma  $  -butylolactone (GBL), have superior solvation capability for PbI  $  _{2}  $   and have been extensively used as precursor solutions.  $  ^{19,27-29}  $   However, numerous limitations of these organic solvents, such as high toxicity, a high boiling point, and excessive interactions strongly dependent on their molecular structures, have impeded the development of the

# EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

Materials. ACN (99.8%), DMF (99.8%), chlorobenzene (CB, 99.8%), methylamine solution (MA, 33% in   $  H_{2}O  $  ), ethylic acid (HAc, 99%), and tin (IV) oxide (  $  SnO_{2}  $  , 15% in   $  H_{2}O  $   colloidal dispersion) were purchased from Alfa Aesar. Methylammonium iodide (MAI), methylammonium bromide (MABr), spiro-OMeTAD, and butylamine hydrochloride (BACl, 99.99%) were purchased from Youxuan Tech. Lead iodide (  $  PbI_{2}  $  , >98.0%) and   $  PC_{61}BM  $   were purchased from TCI and Nano-C, respectively. All the materials were used as received without further purification.

Device Fabrication. The precursor solutions were prepared by mixing a 1:0.8:0.2 stoichiometric ratio of   $  PbI_{2}  $  , MAI and MABr in methylamine acetate or the mixture of methylamine and ACN. The mixed solution was stirred at 60 °C for 6 h. The ITO substrates were cleaned using a cleaning agent in deionized water, ultrapure water, acetone, and ethanol and placed in an ultrasonic instrument, followed by drying with   $  N_{2}  $   flow, and then dried at 120 °C overnight.   $  SnO_{2}  $   solutions (  $  V_{SnO_{2}}:V_{H_{2}O}=1:5  $  ) were spin-cast onto ITO electrodes at 4000 rpm for 30 s, followed by heating at 150 °C for 30 min. BACl (25 mg/mL) in DMF solution was spin-coated on   $  SnO_{2}  $  . Perovskite precursor solution in the mixture of MAAc and ACN (or MAAc) was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 20 s on a

hot substrate at 60 °C (80 °C) throughout the entire spin-coating process and then annealed at 80 °C (100 °C) for 3 min (5 min). The entire preparation process was performed under ambient conditions. Spiro-OMeTAD in CB solution (72.6 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) with 17.5 μL of lithium bistrifluoromethylsulfonate (LiTFSI) and 29 μL of 4-tert-butylpyridine (TBP) was deposited onto the perovskite layer by spin-coating at 3000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, the device was transferred to the evaporator for thermal evaporation of MoO  $  _{3}  $  (5 nm) and Au (100 nm) at a pressure of 1 × 10  $  ^{-4}  $  Pa. The area of each device is 0.05 cm  $  ^{2}  $ , as defined by the overlap of the ITO and the thermally evaporated Au.

Device Characterization. All the devices were tested in a   $  N_{2}  $  -filled glovebox using a Keithley 2400 source meter and an Enlitech SS-F5-3A solar simulator (Enli Tech, Taiwan). The light intensity was calibrated to be 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $   using a NIST-certified monocrystalline Si solar cell (Newport 532 ISO1599). The light current density–voltage (J-V) curve was obtained using a Keithley 2400 source meter unit at room temperature by scanning the voltage from -0.2 to 1.2 V with a step of 0.02 V and 5 ms dwell time.

Chemical, Optical, and Morphology Characterization. Thermal decomposition profiles of MAAc were recorded by a thermogravimetric analyzer TA Q500 with a flow rate of 20 ml min  $  ^{-1}  $  . The temperature varied from 25 to 300 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C min  $  ^{-1}  $  . Dynamic light scattering (DLS) experiments were performed using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano instrument. The UV–vis absorbance spectra of all samples were measured using an Agilent Cary 5000 UV–vis spectrophotometer with an integrating sphere and a Hitachi F-4600 fluorescent spectrophotometer. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) images were taken using a Park XE7 atomic force microscope in noncontact mode. The morphologies of the perovskite films were obtained using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi S–3400N). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were measured using a Japan's Smart Lab diffractometer. # RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

To analyze the   $  PbI_{2}  $   solvation capability in precursor solutions, the ACN concentration in the MAAc solution was optimized by performing experiments on MAAc/ACN volume ratios of 1:0, 9:1, 4:1, 7:3, 3:2, and 1:1 (denoted as the control, 9:1, 4:1, 7:3, 3:2, and 1:1, respectively). A uniformly dispersed yellow colloidal solution was formed after 6 h of stirring using MAAc as the main solvent with a small quantity of added ACN (Figure S1). However, insoluble substances appeared in the 3:2 sample that were identified as insoluble   $  PbI_{2}  $ , which were produced because ACN is a weakly coordinating molecule (Figures S1 and S2). DLS was employed to characterize the size distribution of the dispersoids in the precursor solutions. Figure 1a shows that the micellar size in the as-prepared solutions uniformly increases with the ACN concentration from 570 nm for 1:0 to 1024 nm for 7:3. A further increase in the ACN concentration in the 3:2 and 1:1 samples produces even larger micellar sizes of 8000 and 12,000 nm, respectively, that are significantly beyond the colloidal range. These large sizes hinder the homogenization of perovskite layers. Thus, the 3:2 and 1:1 samples were excluded from subsequent characterization. Many octahedral soft frameworks uniformly aggregate into large micelles shared and surrounded by halogen ions (Figure S3). Large micelles are prone to form

large perovskite crystals by cyclic arrangement, which provide unblocked channels for charge transport and significantly enhance device performance. The UV–vis spectra of the precursor solutions provide structural information associated with the micellar size effect (Figure 1b). A remarkable red shift in the excitonic absorption plateau edge from 399 to 411 nm occurs as the micellar size increases, which probably results from the favorable coordination environment for   $  Pb^{2+}  $  . Our experiment was performed without changing the stoichiometric ratio to ensure the micelles in the precursor solutions had identical elemental compositions.

To verify the effect of the ACN additive on the formative capacity of flat devices during the fabrication process, the viscosity of precursor solutions was determined and is listed in Table S1. The results show that the addition of ACN considerably reduced the precursor viscosity, which increases the coverage on the substrate during spin coating. This result is in accordance with contact angle measurement that was carried out to determine the wettability of the precursor solution on the electron transport layer. The contact angle was significantly reduced from   $  43.9^{\circ}  $   (control) to   $  22.1^{\circ}  $   (7:3) as the quantity of ACN increased, which favored the formation of perovskite layers on the substrate (Figure 1c). Thermodynamic characterization analysis was used to determine the state of the precursor solutions at various temperatures. The TGA spectra show that adding ACN to the precursor shifts the inflection points to a lower temperature because of partial volatilization of ACN, which is particularly noticeable for the 7:3 sample at 60.81 °C (Figures 1d and S4). The as-prepared control sample (in which only MAAc is used as the solvent) evaporates completely when the temperature is increased to approximately 193.28 °C. The evaporation temperature is 184.77 °C for the 7:3 sample, corresponding to a difference of 11.5 °C from the evaporation temperature of the control sample, which is attributed to the lower boiling point of ACN (81 °C) compared with MAAc (164 °C).  $  ^{34}  $   By comparison, the perovskite layer is prepared in the mixed solvent and crystallizes at a lower annealing temperature than the control. The DTG spectra show a faster instantaneous reaction rate from 30 to 180 °C for samples with added ACN compared to the control (Figure 1e), which is consistent with the results of our previous study.  $  ^{34}  $   The slower decay rate of the sample above 180 °C based on the mixed solvent compared to the control show that the solvent system effectively inhibits perovskite decomposition. These thermodynamic results indicate that using ACN as an addictive in the precursor solution decreases the energy consumption for fabricating perovskite layers, which is attributed to the interaction between solvent molecules and PbI  $  _{2}  $  .

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) measurements are widely used to study the crystallization kinetics of PSCs on the precursor solutions and were employed in this study to identify the molecular structure of the solvent and the chemical environment of the precursor solutions. The   $  {}^{1}  $  H spectrum shown in Figure 2a exhibits an invariant chemical shift position on methyl hydrogens (hydrogen bonded to carbon, MA  $  ^{+}  $  /CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}  $    $  ^{+}  $  , CH  $  _{3}  $  COO  $  ^{-}  $  , and CH  $  _{3}  $  CN, corresponding to the b, c, and d peaks). However, the active hydrogens (hydrogen

bonded to nitrogen,   $  -NH_{3}^{+}  $  ) exhibit an apparent chemical shift to higher fields with increasing added ACN. The chemical shift peaks are located at 8.425, 8.384, 8.348, and 8.261 ppm for the control, 9:1, 4:1 and 7:3 samples, respectively, where the maximum difference between the peak locations is 0.164 ppm. This noteworthy shift is attributed to the enhancement of the electrophilic addition reactivity by the change in the chemical environment after ACN addition. The strong electronegativity of the nitrogen atom to ACN causes the chemical shift of   $  MA^{+}  $  via hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions (  $  CH_{3}NH_{3}^{+}\cdots N\equiv C-CH_{3}  $  ). This strong chemical interaction probably inhibits the entry of   $  MA^{+}  $  into the perovskite soft framework during the annealing process. The nitrogen–hydrogen bond between ACN and   $  MA^{+}  $  is confirmed by the NMR spectra of the pristine MAAc and the mixed solution, in which the   $  {}^{1}H  $   chemical shift moves to higher fields (Figure S5). The Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (Figures 2b and S6) shows that the N–H stretching vibration of   $  MA^{+}  $  shifts from 3419 to 3399 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   after ACN addition, indicating strengthening of the nitrogen–hydrogen bond interaction. We analyzed XRD patterns at different substrate temperatures (Figures 2c,d and S7) to confirm the effect of hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions on the formation of the perovskite crystal phase. All the samples for XRD were prepared on a constant temperature substrate under identical conditions without annealing treatment. The perovskite phase transformation proceeds by thermal transformation of the amorphous phase to a nonperovskite phase, probably with the simultaneous generation of an intermediate phase and finally to the perovskite phase. Under a low substrate temperature (  $  \leq55^{\circ}C  $  ), the film appears amorphous for all the as-prepared samples with a large quantity of the residual solvent. As the substrate temperature increases to 60 °C, the control sample with coexisting nonperovskite and perovskite phases exhibits higher crystallinity than the other samples, whereas the 7:3 sample is in the amorphous phase. Figure 3 graphically illustrates the four steps of the perovskite crystallization process in the precursor solutions. (i) Soft frameworks are formed by  $ \mathrm{X}^{-} $  ( $ \mathrm{X} = \mathrm{Cl} $ , Br, I) and  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  resulting from the dissolution of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and MAX in MAAc. These soft framework units are classified as coordination compounds containing  $ [\mathrm{PbX}_3]^- $ ,  $ [\mathrm{PbX}_4]^{2-} $ ,  $ [\mathrm{PbX}_5]^{3-} $ , and  $ [\mathrm{PbX}_6]^{4-} $  with different coordination numbers. (ii) The saturation of coordination compounds increases from  $ [\mathrm{PbX}_3]^- $  to  $ [\mathrm{PbX}_6]^{4-} $  with the increase in added ACN. ACN improves the transference of  $ [\mathrm{PbX}_6]^{4-} $  for large micelles by reducing the coordination. The doubling of the micellar size is closely correlated with the coordination environment of the precursor solution system, which depends on the added ACN content. Lead polyhalides are coordination complexes of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  in the precursor solution that act as initial nucleation centers in the formation of perovskite thin films. Complexes with lower coordination generally tend to share halogen ions for full coordination with lower surface energy and higher symmetry by corner sharing. ACN effectively weakens the coordination between the solvent and lead ions ( $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{C}=\mathrm{OO}^{-}\cdots\mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $ ), demonstrating that the number of nuclear Pb atoms increases for halogen-ion corner sharing. (iii) The amorphous phase of perovskite (perovskite sol) forms on the ITO substrate after spin coating. Meanwhile,  $ \mathrm{MA}^+ $  is prepared for entering the perovskite sol in the form of an intermediate phase ( $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{C}\equiv\mathrm{N}\cdots\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3^+ $ ). (iv) A perovskite film forms from an

amorphous phase bridged by a nonperovskite phase after the annealing process. During the annealing process, the coexistence of the nonperovskite phase and   $  MA^{+}  $  promotes the crystallization of perovskite films spatially and temporally. The stable existence of a nonperovskite phase significantly slows the entry of   $  MA^{+}  $  into the octahedral framework. The growth rate of perovskite crystals is considerably inhibited by hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions between A-site cations and ACN (  $  CH_{3}-NH_{3}^{+}\cdots CH_{3}C\equiv N  $  ), resulting in a compact and smooth thin film.

The ultimate objective of this study was to obtain uniform, compact, and smooth perovskite films via regulated nucleation and crystallization for solar cell applications. The surficial morphology was characterized by top-view SEM and AFM. We compared the control sample with the sample with the best photovoltaic device performance and device parameters, that is, the 7:3 sample (Figures S8, S9 and Table S2). Uniform and smooth perovskite films were obtained without clear grain boundaries and pinholes using the 7:3 sample (Figure 4b). However, the films based on the control sample were rough with extensive grain boundaries (Figure 4a). The surface roughness of the control sample of 35.858 nm was higher than that of the 7:3 ratio sample (15.129 nm) (Figure 4c,d). We attribute this difference to the strong interaction between the A-site cation and ACN, which decrease the growth rate of perovskite crystals. Poor film morphology results in a leakage current, while impeding effective carrier transport between the perovskite active layers and the transport layers, which eventually deteriorates the device performance. This result is confirmed by the XRD patterns shown in Figure 4e. The fwhm (Table S3) shows that ACN addition improves the crystallinity of perovskite films. Furthermore, residual MAAc was detected on the perovskite film by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), showing that the O (1 s) peak (Figure S10) of MAAc was located at 530 eV. MAAc is advantageous for defect modification and perovskite film density improvement, presenting in the form of ionic salts in the perovskite films.  $  ^{41}  $

Uniform, dense, smooth, and high-crystallinity perovskite films were formed for the preparation of photovoltaic devices with excellent performance and long-term stability. The improved stability of the PSCs based on the 7:3 system is attributed to the superior quality of the perovskite film with few trap states, as demonstrated by the previous experimental results.

# CONCLUSIONS

In summary, we propose an effective strategy for tuning intermolecular interactions by adding ACN to a MAAc precursor solution. A simple one-step approach in ambient air is used to fabricate highly efficient and stable perovskite devices based on   $  MAPbI_{x}Br_{3-x}  $   with an optimum PCE of 21.20%. Hydrogen bonding between organic cations and ACN (  $  CH_{3}-NH_{3}^{+}\cdots CH_{3}C\equiv N  $  ) is observed, resulting in high-quality perovskite films with a low trap density and thus a high device performance. Reducing the coordination between the solvent and perovskite solutes (  $  CH_{3}C=OO^{-}\cdots Pb^{2+}  $  ) increases the   $  I^{-}  $  coordination with   $  Pb^{2+}  $   to produce larger micelles in the precursor solution. The intermolecular interaction between the A-site and ACN provides an alternative strategy in solvent engineering for modulating the crystallization process.
