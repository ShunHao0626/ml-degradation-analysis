# Ligand-Modulated Excess   $  PbI_{2}  $   Nanosheets for Highly Efficient and Stable Perovskite Solar Cells

Huanhuan Wang, Zaiwei Wang, Zhen Yang, Yuzeng Xu, Yi Ding, Liguo Tan, Chenyi Yi, Zhuang Zhang, Ke Meng, Gang Chen, Ying Zhao, Yongsong Luo, Xiaodan Zhang, Anders Hagfeldt, and Jingshan Luo*

Excess lead iodide (PbI $ _2 $ ), as a defect passivation material in perovskite films, contributes to the longer carrier lifetime and reduced halide vacancies for high-efficiency perovskite solar cells. However, the random distribution of excess PbI $ _2 $  also leads to accelerated degradation of the perovskite layer. Inspired by nanocrystal synthesis, here, a universal ligand-modulation technology is developed to modulate the shape and distribution of excess PbI $ _2 $  in perovskite films. By adding certain ligands, perovskite films with vertically distributed PbI $ _2 $  nanosheets between the grain boundaries are successfully achieved, which reduces the nonradiative recombination and trap density of the perovskite layer. Thus, the power conversion efficiency of the modulated device increases from 20% to 22% compared to the control device. In addition, benefiting from the vertical distribution of excess PbI $ _2 $  and the hydrophobic nature of the surface ligands, the modulated devices exhibit much longer stability, retaining 72% of their initial efficiency after 360 h constant illumination under maximum power point tracking measurement.

Usually, the excess   $  PbI_{2}  $   is randomly distributed in the perovskite films or on the perovskite upper and lower sur-

faces.  $  ^{[16,21,22]}  $   It would be ideal if the excess   $  PbI_{2}  $   could be distributed as nanosheets between the grain boundaries of the perovskite film, exploiting the advantage and avoiding the disadvantage of excess   $  PbI_{2}  $   to the maximum.  $  ^{[24]}  $   There are many ways to tune the morphology of a material. In nanocrystal synthesis, surface ligands could effectively modulate the structure

Lead halide perovskites attract great interest for optoelectronic applications because of their long charge carrier diffusion lengths, high light harvesting efficiency and bandgap (  $  E_{g}  $  ) tunability.  $  ^{[1-7]}  $   Since its advent in 2009 and with extensive efforts in the past decade, perovskite solar cell (PSC) has reached a certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 25.2%.  $  ^{[2,8-18]}  $

State-of-the-art PSCs generally consist of an excess of lead iodide (PbI $ _2 $ ) for both one-step and two-step fabricated devices to enhance their efficiencies. $ ^{[16,19-22]} $  The benefits of excess PbI $ _2 $  in perovskite films are mainly assigned to its passivation effect, leading to longer carrier lifetime and reduced halide vacancy concentration. $ ^{[19,23-25]} $  However, excess PbI $ _2 $  is a double-edged sword. $ ^{[24]} $  It also results in the inhibited charge transport and accelerated degradation of perovskite layer likely due to the photodecomposition of PbI $ _2 $  and the increasing ionic movement. $ ^{[24,26-28]} $  Therefore, it is crucial to modulate the excess PbI $ _2 $  in perovskite films to achieve high efficiency and stable PSCs.

Furthermore, benefiting from the vertical distribution of excess   $  PbI_{2}  $   and the hydrophobic nature of the surface ligands, the modulated perovskite films and PSCs showed enhanced stability.

As shown in Figure 1b, excess PbI $ _2 $  in the control film without modulation is randomly distributed in the form of nanoparticles in the perovskite film, which is consistent with the previous reports. $ ^{[16,21]} $  The morphology of excess PbI $ _2 $  in the CTAB-modulated perovskite film (Figure 1c) is a vertical nanosheet structure at the grain boundary. It is worth noting that the CTAC modulated film with the same cation as CTAB, also exhibits the vertical PbI $ _2 $  nanosheets. In contrast, the formamidinium bromide (FABr) and methylaminium bromide (MABr) modulated films do not show the generation of PbI $ _2 $  nanosheets, although they have the same halide anion as CTAB (Figure S1, Supporting Information). These indicate that the morphology modulation is mainly ascribed to the cation ligands rather than the halide anions. Besides CTAB, other common ligands, such as OTAB, DTAB and CDAB, with different cations as CTAB are investigated and these ligands modulated perovskite films all contain the vertical PbI $ _2 $  nanosheets, indicating the universality of the ligand-modulation technology. Other ligands that have been used for the preparation of 2D materials might also be applicable here. In addition, the ligands might also need to fulfill certain lengths to tune the morphology. Based on the relatively smooth surface of CTAB-modulated film and the good photovoltaic performance of the associated devices (Figure S2 and Table S1, Supporting Information), CTAB is chosen as the representative modulator for the following study.

The concentration of ligands also plays an important role in tuning the size and nanostructure of the materials.  $  ^{[29,30]}  $   As shown in Figure S3 (Supporting Information), with increasing the concentration of CTAB from 0.01 to 0.1 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  , PbI  $  _{2}  $   changes its shape from nanoparticles to nanosheets. When the concentration of CTAB is further increased to 0.5 and 1 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  , PbI  $  _{2}  $   maintains the nanosheet structure though it becomes slightly thinner. However, the perovskite grain size reduces significantly, which may introduce more defects due to the increase of the grain boundaries in these perovskite films. When the concentration of CTAB reaches 2 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  , both PbI  $  _{2}  $   and perovskite become nanoparticles. The formation of PbI  $  _{2}  $   nanosheets might benefit from the anisotropic interaction between CTAB and PbI  $  _{2}  $   crystal facets.  $  ^{[36]}  $   At the appropriate concentration range (0.1–1 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ), CTAB selectively adheres to particular crystal facet which inhibits the growth of this facet and leads to the formation of PbI  $  _{2}  $   nanosheets. When little CTAB (0.01 and 0.05 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) is added, the interaction is not strong enough to obviously limit the facet growth. Both control and CTAB-modulated perovskite materials showed similar Bragg reflections.  $  ^{[37]}  $   In the CTAB-modulated perovskite, the narrower diffraction ring of   $  PbI_{2}  $   compared to that of the control film indicates the higher grade orientation of excess   $  PbI_{2}  $  .  $  ^{[38]}  $   We also prepared   $  PbI_{2}  $  -CTAB composite films, and   $  PbI_{2}  $   film post-treated with CTAB. This indirectly proves that the nanosheet is   $  PbI_{2}  $   rather than some new materials formed by the reaction between CTAB and   $  PbI_{2}  $  .

The distribution of   $  PbI_{2}  $   in the films was further characterized by the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) coupled-cathodoluminescence (CL) mapping (Figure 2d,e and Figure S7, Supporting Information). The false color images are divided into green and red using different spectral window from 470 to 530 nm and from 750 to 850 nm respectively, corresponding to the emission of   $  PbI_{2}  $   and perovskite respectively. As shown by the CL mapping images (Figure 2d,e), the excess   $  PbI_{2}  $   in the control film is randomly distributed on the surface in the form of nanoparticles. In contrast, the excess   $  PbI_{2}  $   in the CTAB modulated film vertically stuck at the grain boundary in the form of nanosheets, which were further confirmed by the cross-section SEM images, Figure S8 (Supporting Information).

The absorption edges of the control and CTAB-modulated films are nearly the same (Figure S9, Supporting Information), indicating that there was negligible amount of bromine (Br) incorporated into the perovskite film. This was further confirmed by the negligible shift of the perovskite diffraction peaks. Furthermore, the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectroscopy (ToF-SIMS) depth profiles (Figures S10–S12, Supporting Information) have not detected the existence of Br in the CTAB-modulated film. This might be due to the low Br  $  ^{-}  $  concentration (<0.048% of that of I  $  ^{-}  $  ) in the organic salt precursor solution. In contrast, the ToF-SIMS depth profiles showed that the CTA  $  ^{+}  $  was distributed across the CTAB-modulated film (Figure S12, Supporting Information). As CTA  $  ^{+}  $  is too large in size to enter into the perovskite and PbI  $  _{2}  $  crystal lattice;  $  ^{[22]}  $  it most likely locates at the surface and at the grain boundaries of perovskite and PbI  $  _{2}  $ .

To characterize the optoelectronic properties of as prepared films, steady state photoluminescence (PL), time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL), space-charge-limited-current (SCLC), electrical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements and dark current density–voltage (J–V) measurement were carried out. The intensity of the steady state PL of the CTAB-modulated perovskite film was stronger than that of control film (Figure 3a), indicating the reduced nonradiative recombination of the modulated film. As shown in the TRPL decay spectra (Figure 3b and Table S3, Supporting Information), the

carrier lifetime of the CTAB-modulated film increased to 2046 from 1485 ns of that of control film. The SCLC measurement based on the electron-only structure device of ITO/SnO $ _2 $ /perovskite/PCBM/Au was carried out to determine the electron-trap densities ( $ n_t $ ) and the electron mobility ( $ \mu $ ) in the dark. $ ^{[4,39,40]} $  The density of trap states of CTAB-modulated device reduced to  $ 5.768 \times 10^{15} $  from  $ 1.002 \times 10^{16} $  cm $ ^{-3} $  of that of control device. Furthermore, the electron mobility of the modulated device increased to  $ 1.93 \times 10^{-2} $  from  $ 1.09 \times 10^{-2} $  cm $ ^2 $  V $ ^{-1} $  s $ ^{-1} $  of that of control device in Figure S13 (Supporting Information). In addition, the EIS spectra in Figure 3d and Table S4 (Supporting Information) showed that the recombination resistance ( $ R_{rec} $ ) of CTAB-modulated PSC increased to 3129 from 1889  $ \Omega $  of that of control PSC, which means reduced nonradiative recombination and traps for CTAB-modulated PSC. $ ^{[41]} $  Furthermore, the dark current density–voltage (J–V) curve in Figure S14 (Supporting Information) showed the lower current density of CTAB-modulated device in comparison with that of control device, indicating the suppressed charge-carrier recombination and increased charge collection. $ ^{[19,42-44]} $

The planar devices with a configuration of indium tin oxide (ITO)/compact tin oxide (c-SnO $ _2 $ )/perovskite/2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-di-p-methoxyphenyl-amine)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD)/Au were fabricated. The champion device based on CTAB-modulated perovskite film showed a PCE of 22.03% with an open circuit voltage ( $ V_{oc} $ ) of 1.14 V, short circuit current density ( $ J_{sc} $ ) of 23.82 mA cm $ ^{-2} $  and a fill factor (FF) of 81.14%, demonstrating obvious improvement in comparison with the PCE of 20.58% for the control device (Figure 4a). The enhanced performance is mainly due to the enhanced  $ V_{oc} $  and FF, in accordance with the reduced trap density and enhanced carrier mobility. $ ^{[42,45]} $  The stabilized power output (SPO) of control and CTAB-modulated champion cells were 19.14% and 21.20%, shown in Figure S15 (Supporting Information), respectively. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra (Figure 4b) resulted in an integrated current density of 23.81 and 23.75 mA cm $ ^{-2} $  for the modulated and control device respectively, which agree with the current density–voltage (J–V) scanning result. In addition, the device of CTAB-modulated showed a lower hysteresis factor of 0.012, comparing to 0.032 for control device (Figure S16 and Table S5, Supporting Information). A statistical analysis of the photovoltaic parameters of 69 devices demonstrates the good reproducibility of high-performance ligand-modulated devices (Figure S17, Supporting Information).

The commercialization of PSCs has been restricted by its instability, and one of the main reasons is the moisture-sensitive nature of perovskite films. The contact angle tests in Figure 4c showed that CTAB-modulated film was more hydrophobic in comparison with the control perovskite film. When the control and CTAB-modulated films were exposed to ambient air at room temperature with a relative humidity around  $ 60\% $  for  $ 216\mathrm{h} $ , an obvious degradation was observed for the control film, as revealed by the UV-vis spectra (Figure S18, Supporting Information), which was accompanied by the phase transformation from the perovskite black phase to non-perovskite yellow phase (Figure 4d). In contrast, the CTAB-modulated film did not show obvious degradation at the same condition, and the perovskite black phase was maintained.

The stability monitoring results of unencapsulated devices in a relative humidity of 66% (Figure S19, Supporting Information) showed that the CTAB-modulated perovskite device retained 78% of its initial PCE and maintained the black color after 110 h, whereas the control device decayed to 40% of the original performance and the film became yellow. Furthermore, the unencapsulated devices were stored in nitrogen under dark condition to evaluate the shelf lifetime in Figure 4e, and the CTAB-modulated devices showed a higher stability than the control device. To check the photostability, the maximum power point (MPP) of unencapsulated control and CTAB-modulated devices were tracked under a constant white light-emitting diode irradiation with an intensity equivalent to 1 sun in   $  N_{2}  $   atmosphere (Figure 4f). The control device showed a rapid loss of PCE and only retained 52% of its initial value, while the CTAB-modulated device maintained 72% PCE after 360 h of continuous light soaking.

Multiple factors might be responsible for the improved PCE and stability of devices based on CTAB-modulated films. On one hand, the addition of CTAB induces the formation of

vertically distributed   $  PbI_{2}  $   nanosheets, which enhances the passivation effects of   $  PbI_{2}  $   and improves the hole transport from perovskite to the hole transport material. On the other hand, the CTA  $  ^{+}  $  ligand further passivates the defects and increases the hydrophobicity of perovskite films, enhancing the carrier lifetime and the humidity stability.

In summary, a ligand-modulation technology is demonstrated to fabricate the vertically aligned   $  PbI_{2}  $   nanosheets at the grain boundary of perovskite films, which reduces the trap density, increases the carrier lifetime and enhances the charge mobility of perovskite layer. The efficiency of CTAB-modulated PSC reached 22%. Because of the regulated distribution of excess   $  PbI_{2}  $   and the hydrophobic nature of the surface ligands, the stability of modulated perovskite device showed obvious increase in comparison with that of control device in ambient air, in a nitrogen glove box and under continuous illumination with MPP tracking. In addition, a wide range of ligands are effective for the modulation, which provides opportunities to use ligands with special functional groups to further enhance the optoelectronic property of the films.
