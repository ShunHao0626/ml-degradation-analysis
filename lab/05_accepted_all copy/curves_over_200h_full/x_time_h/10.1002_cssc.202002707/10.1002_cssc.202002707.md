# Abstract

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) are displaying auspicious candidacy towards photovoltaic technology based on their features including low-cost assembling, exceptional performance, and low-temperature solution processing. However, the progression of PSCs in the direction of commercialized upgrade desires high efficiency and long-term stability. The surface and grain boundaries of perovskite layer, as well as interfaces are critical factors intended for the performance of the assembled cells. The defects mainly located in the perovskite surfaces can trigger the hysteresis, carrier recombination, and degradation, which obstacle the progress in the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of the ensuing cells. Herein, we propose to stabilize the  $ \alpha $ -FAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite phase without scarifying the spectral features by taking the benefits of 2,3,4,5,6-pentafluorobenzyl phosphonic acid (PFBPA) molecule as a passivation agent for the first time. Accordingly, high-quality PSCs with improved efficiency of  $ 22.25\% $  was attained with respectable cell parameters compared to the pristine cells without passivation layer. Remarkably, the PFBPA passivated ultrathin layer effectively prevents the perovskite layer from moisture, resulting in better long-term stability for unsealed PSCs maintain  $ >90\% $  of the original efficiency under different humidity levels (40 to  $ 75\% $ ) after  $ 600\mathrm{h} $ . This work finds out that the PFBPA passivation has a considerable impact in obtaining high-quality and stable FAPbI $ _3 $  films for extra beneficent both the efficiency and stability of PSCs.

Keywords:  $ \alpha $ -FAPbI $ _3 $ , Moisture stability, Perovskite passivation, Perovskite solar cells, Phosphonic acid.

# Introduction

The scientific community and photovoltaic industry give huge consideration to perovskite solar cells (PSCs) on account of their easiness of assembly as well as the high power conversion efficiency (PCE) overriding 25%.  $  ^{[1]}  $   The sensitizer perovskite material is the main compound in the configuration of the fabricated devices thanks to its auspicious electronic and optical characteristics including low-temperature solution treatment, low excitons binding energy, great carrier movement, long charge-carrier diffusion length, and strong band-edge absorption.  $  ^{[2]}  $   It is perceived that the PCE of the devices depending on perovskite materials is very close to the champion PCE of conventional silicon solar cells. However, methylammonium (MA  $  ^{+}  $  ) cation, which is considered as the main component in most of the utilized perovskite materials, is unstable and degrades to methylamine when affected by heat, moisture, and light.  $  ^{[3]}  $   The decomposition of these cations may influence the stability and the upscaling trend of the devices towards commercialization.  $  ^{[4]}  $   To overcome that drawback, the formamidinium (FA  $  ^{+}  $  ) cation is considered as possible substitutional to MA  $  ^{+}  $   as pristine FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   characteristics lower volatility and close to optimal Goldschmidt tolerance factor,  $  ^{[5]}  $   in addition to an absorption edge extending into the near IR to around 840 nm, displaying more effectual solar light compared to MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  .  $  ^{[6]}  $   Nonetheless, non-perovskite yellow phase (δ-FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ) can be formed while exposed to heat or moisture that is considered as a serious quandary.  $  ^{[7]}  $   Different instructions become compulsory to dispose of the creation of unnecessary phase including the introducing of different ions such as Cs  $  ^{+}  $  , or MA  $  ^{+}  $   to the FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   lattice.  $  ^{[8]}  $   Notwithstanding, the integration of these ions influences both the spectral response as well as stability of the fabricated PSCs upon exposure to heat and in humidity circumstances.  $  ^{[9]}  $   Another direction was elaborated to beat the snag of FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   decomposition via surface post-treatments by deposition of passivation layer above the FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite film. Furthermore, this trend not only passivate the assembled perovskite film but also advance the film crystallinity, heal grain boundaries, and dominate the surface defects through the combination with uncoordinated Pb  $  ^{2+}  $   ions.  $  ^{[10]}  $

Besides, applying passivation layers to the perovskite films can lessen the trap-assisted nonradiative recombination and keep the films from the moisture.  $  ^{[11]}  $   Presently, the surface treatment methodologies to attain passivation contain creating coordinate covalent or ionic bonding with defective perovskites or transforming the structure into 2D perovskites.  $  ^{[12]}  $   As example, the uncoordinated halide ions were passivated using iodopentafluorobenzene molecules through halogen bonding, decreasing the ability of charge trapping at the surface defects, thus growing carrier lifetime and improving charge transportation.  $  ^{[13]}  $   Correspondingly,

Herein, we introduce 2,3,4,5,6-pentafluorobenzyl phosphonic acid (PFBPA) molecule as a passivation agent for the first time above the perovskite layer to protect the surface from ambient moisture while mitigating ionic diffusion in the device. The P=O groups of the PFBPA show an important role in the combination between PFBPA and uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ions, improving the PCE of the treated devices up to 22.25%. Moreover, because of the mitigated dispersion forces related to the high electronegativity of fluorine (F), this fluorinated compound is hydrophobic and hardly wetted by water. As a result, undesired   $  \delta  $  -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   can be eliminated. The fabricated cells prove a noticeable moisture stability through the 40–75% relative humidity (RH) and maintain >90% of the preliminary efficiency after 600 h, compared to the control device that drops sharply to <50% of the preliminary PCE, which may be because of ion migration, poor film quality, and phase instability under ambient conditions.

# Substrate preparation

The laser-patterned FTO substrates were consecutively cleaned with 2% Hellmanex aqueous solution, deionized water, acetone and ethanol by sonicating for 15 min for each solvent. After drying the substrates with compressed air, the substrates were treated by ultraviolet ozone for 15 mins for further cleaning of organic residuals.

# ETL preparation

A 20 to 30 nm-thick compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   (c-  $  TiO_{2}  $  ) film was deposited on top of clean FTO using spray pyrolysis method at 450 °C with a precursor solution of titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetylacetonate) (75 wt. % in isopropanol) in EtOH (1:9, v:v). The mesoscopic   $  TiO_{2}  $   (mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $  ) film of ~200-nm thickness was deposited onto compact underlayer via spin-coating of a diluted solution of 30 NR-D paste in EtOH (1:5.5, w:w) at 5000 rpm for 15 s. Subsequently, the substrates were sintered at 450 °C for 45 min. After cooling down to 150 °C, all substrates were transferred to nitrogen-filled glovebox for perovskite deposition.

# Perovskite layer preparation

FAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite precursor solution in the presence of MACl as crystallization aid was prepared according to our previous study, $ ^{[2b]} $  by dissolving a mixture of equimolar PbI $ _2 $  and FAI with MACl in mixed solution of DMF/DMSO (4:1, v:v) under mild heating condition at  $ \sim 70^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  to assist dissolving. The spinning procedure was carried out in a two-step program at  $ 1000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 10~\mathrm{s} $  and  $ 4000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 20~\mathrm{s} $ , respectively.  $ 200~\mu \mathrm{L} $  of CB as antisolvent was poured at the 10 s prior to the end of the last step. Then, the substrates were sequentially annealed at  $ 80^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 2\mathrm{min} $ ,  $ 110^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 3\mathrm{min} $ , and  $ 150^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 15\mathrm{min} $ .

# Passivation layer preparation

The passivation process was employed by dipping treatment of the perovskite films with a solution of the PFBPA (10, 20, and 30 mM) and BPA (20 mM) precursors in IPA for 15 s. The residue passivation solution was removed by spin coating, followed by annealing treatment at 100 °C for 10 min. The whole procedure is performed in a nitrogen-filled glovebox.

# HTL preparation

Spiro-OMeTAD as HTL was deposited on the top of the passivation layer by spin-coating at 4000 rpm for 20 s. ~70 mM spiro-OMeTAD was dissolved in CB and doped by Li-TFSI and tBP, and the molar ratios were 33 and 330%, respectively.

# Gold electrode preparation

Finally,  $ \sim 80 $  nm of gold (Au) back electrode was evaporated using thermal evaporation under high vacuum.

# Characterizations

# Film characterizations

The crystal structures of pristine and passivated perovskite films were examined using Bruker D8 Advance model X-Ray diffraction (XRD) spectroscopy. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) of perovskite films was performed on a photoelectron spectrometer (VersaProbe II, PHI). Optical absorbance analyses of different perovskite films were performed using a spectrophotometer (LAMBDA 1050, Perkin Elmer). Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra of perovskite films deposited on  $ \mathrm{FTO / Al_2O_3} $  substrates were recorded with a spectrometer (FLS920P, Edinburgh Instruments). The time-resolved PL (TRPL) spectra of different perovskite films were recorded by exciting the samples with picosecond pulsed diode laser (EPL-405) which emits 49 ps duration pulses at  $ 405\mathrm{nm} $ . Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, S-5500, Hitachi) was employed to examine surface morphology and cross-section of perovskite films and full devices. The hydrophilicity of surfaces as a function of time was assessed by the contact angle measurements (CAMs) with a Krüss DSA100 drop shape analyzer at ambient conditions. The energetic profile of trap density of state (tDOS) was assessed through interrogating the frequency dependent capacitance by thermal admittance spectroscopy (TAS) measurements.

# Results and Discussion

$ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  perovskite precursor solution in the presence of methylammonium chloride (MACl) as crystallization aid was prepared as described in the experimental part. The passivation process was employed by dipping treatment of the perovskite films with a solution of the PFBPA (10, 20, and  $ 30~\mathrm{mM} $ ) and BPA  $ (20~\mathrm{mM}) $  precursors in isopropanol (IPA) solvent. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were checked to investigate the impact of PFBPA passivation on the structure and crystallinity of  $ \alpha $ - $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  phase and the results are presented in Figure 1a. The XRD patterns of pristine  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  film on FTO/ETL substrate designed without PFBPA passivation layer exhibit a peak at  $ 2\theta = 12.6^{\circ} $ , indicating the unreacted  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  peak. This peak may be accredited to the decomposition of perovskite coming from the escape of organic cations and halides all through the annealing process.[18] Conversely, insertion of PFBPA passivation layer on top of the  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  sample displays abatement in the intensity of the gained diffraction peaks with a weak feature, indexable to  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  that can confirm the respectable passivation of the fabricated perovskite layer. PFBPA materials can overcome the decomposition of perovskite through the passivation of uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  ions on the surface and grain boundaries of perovskite films.[19] Furthermore, the optical features of both pristine and passivated layers were examined and displayed in Figure 1b. The results indicated that no noteworthy alteration in the absorption edge is detected. Besides, the corresponding band gap of pristine and passivated layers were calculated through Tauc plots and founded to be 1.503 and  $ 1.505\mathrm{eV} $  for pristine and passivated perovskite layers, respectively (Figure 1c). The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectrum (Figure 1d) of passivated perovskite layer demonstrated an enhancement in the peak intensity compared to the pristine sample, indicating the inhibited recombination because of the surface passivation effect and the upgraded film quality in the presence of PFBPA.[20] Furthermore, the perovskite film passivated with PFBPA material exhibited a slight blue-shift in the PL peak associated with that of pristine film supposing a substantial passivation in the trap states near

Subsequently, trap density of state (tDOS), time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL), and transient photovoltage decay as a function of incident light intensity were accomplished to check and study thoroughly the charge-transport/recombination kinetics in the fabricated cells. The PFBPA passivated device produces an obvious passivation effect in the trap density almost through the whole energy range, compared to the device employing pristine perovskite film (the peak value decreased from  $ \sim 1.7 \times 10^{18} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3} \mathrm{eV}^{-1} $  to  $ \sim 0.9 \times 10^{18} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3} \mathrm{eV}^{-1} $ ), confirming a thriving passivation to reduce the deep trap-states at the interface/grain boundaries of perovskite. The trap density profile of control sample also demonstrated additional two peaks located at 0.12 and  $ 0.33 \mathrm{eV} $ , respectively, which correspond to shallow traps whereas these peaks are almost diminished in the passivated sample, further endorsing a successful healing strategy to reduce the shallow trap-states. The reason behind the high density of trap states in control samples is coming from the enhancement in defects, which attributed to interstitial iodide with reasonably low formation energy on the surfaces or at grain boundaries. # Conclusions

In conclusion, we successfully inserted a hydrophobic PFBPA passivation layer on the surface of  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  perovskite film to improve the efficiency and environmental stability. The formation of PFBPA passivation layer above the perovskite surface can overcome the grain boundary defects, reduce the charge recombination, support the interfacial carrier transportation, and shield the perovskite structure from moisture issue. SEM results show a flat surface with less  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  flacks for the passivated film that can limit the existence of trap recombination sites. PL spectrum proves an enhancement in the peak intensity for the films with PFBPA, indicating less recombination due to the surface passivation effects. Eventually, the fabricated cells employing PFBPA passivation layer achieved a high efficiency exceeding  $ 22\% $  and long-term stability in the ambient environment compared to the pristine devices. Accordingly, this work delivers an efficient direction to develop both the efficiency and the stability of  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $ -based perovskites, revealing a great application potential for the industrialization of PSCs.
