# 1. Introduction

The outstanding optoelectronic characteristics of hybrid organic-inorganic metal halide perovskites such as the relatively long diffusion length of charge carriers, tunable bandgap, and excellent light absorption capability have led to the rapid development of the perovskite solar cells (PSCs) in the last decade (Yang et al., 2018; Wehrenfennig et al., 2014; Saliba, 2019; Saliba, 2018; Turren-Cruz et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2017). In recent years the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs has been soared from  $ 3.8\% $  to  $ 25.2\% $  for single-junction, and  $ 29.15\% $  for silicon-perovskite tandem solar cells, however, long term stability remains as a validating factor for PSCs to become competitive (Bush et al., 2016; Deretzis et al., 2018; Leijtens et al., 2013; Grancini et al., 2017; Domanski et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2015; Han et al., 2015). The long term stability of PSCs can be affected by intrinsic and extrinsic (environmental) features such as moisture (Hou et al., 2019), oxygen (Pont et al., 2017), temperature, and UV irradiation (Wei et al., 2019). Until now diverse approaches have been utilized to hinder the intrinsic instability issues and enhance PSCs' long term stability such as electron

A key direction toward enhancing the long term and outdoor stability of the perovskite solar cells is encapsulation. As a result, a suitable encapsulation package is required to prevent moisture and oxygen penetration toward the perovskite solar cells. In this work, a low-cost commercially available bilayer structure of poly(methyl methacrylate)/room-temperature vulcanizing silicone rubber (RTV) encapsulation package for enhancing the long term stability of the perovskite solar cells has been investigated. Encapsulated cells retained more than  $ 80\% $  of the initial efficiency at the environmental condition of  $ 50\% $  moisture, and room temperature after  $ 1000\mathrm{h} $ , however reference cell efficiency decrease severely. The origin of enhanced stability in encapsulated cells under harsh environmental condition was investigated and explained by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy measurements. Our study, in general, introduces a novel encapsulating layer which is feasible to apply without any needs for further common post heat-treatments or UV curing.

Right after application, the material starts crosslinking in contact with moisture in the air which is favorable (Wu et al., 2017; Parsae and Shokrieh, 2018). Silicone elastomers are generally expected to be heat resistant and flexible at both high and low temperatures keep their excellent mechanical properties over a very wide temperature range (Standards, 1968). Outstanding long-term thermal resistance from  $ -50^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  to  $ 180^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ ; low elasticity modulus, electrically insulating, high weathering, UV and radiation resistance, very good chemical resistance, and simple processing are the main advantages of using RTV as an encapsulation layer for perovskite solar cells (Song et al., 2017; Du, 2017; McKeen, 2017). Depending on the nature of the cross linker, a small amount of acetic acid, amine, or neutral byproducts, such as alcohol, is released during vulcanization (Li et al., 2017; Dunn, 2003). As a result, an interlayer between perovskite solar cells and RTV is essential to prevent further possible reactions of byproducts with the perovskite layer. To this purpose, we have used a thin film of poly methyl methacrylate (PMMA) between perovskite active area and RTV layer. Besides as an interlayer, as a result of good adhesion properties, PMMA can decrease the roughness of the perovskite active area leads to a better RTV coating.

The vapor permeability of silicone rubber is an important factor for

stability applications and can very drastically from  $ 63\mathrm{g}\mathrm{m}^{-2}\mathrm{day}^{-1} $  to  $ 1.0\times 10^{3}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{m}^{-2}\mathrm{day}^{-1} $  due to several factors, including physical structure, temperature, additives and fillers (Hara et al., 2016).

Here we are investigating the efficacy of PMMA/RTV layers as a perovskite solar cell encapsulation package which is inexpensive, feasible to apply even on a large scale using blading, without any needs for further post-treatment such as heat treatment or UV curing.

Therefore as a result of encapsulation process no loss in efficiency of the reference cell was seen and led to the higher or comparable long term stabilities in comparison to conventional encapsulating layers (Castro-Hermosa et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2019; Li et al., 2018; Idigoras et al., 2018; Ramos et al., 2018). PMMA/RTV layers deposition lead to an excellent hydrophobic coated surface with a contact angle of  $ 117.8^{\circ} $ . Encapsulated cells using PMMA/RTV bilayer retained  $ 80\% $  of their PCE for more than  $ 1000\mathrm{h} $  which is higher than ultra-high permeation barrier films (UHPBFs) which retained  $ 76.6\% $  of their PCE for more than  $ 840\mathrm{h} $  being exposed to the environmental condition of  $ 50\% $  moisture at room temperature (Castro-Hermosa et al., 2019).

# 2. Experimental procedures

# 2.1. Fabrication of PSCs

Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)-coated glass (Sigma-Aldrich, TEC-7  $ \Omega $  sq $ ^{-1} $ ) were chemically etched with zinc powder and 3 M HCl solution and cleaned, followed by 15 min sonication in deionized water, ethanol and isopropyl alcohol respectively. The substrate were heated at  $ 500^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for 30 min. All substrates were further treated by UV-ozone for 10 min before the deposition of the electron transport layer. A 50 nm thick compact TiO $ _2 $  layer was subsequently spin-coated at 2000 rpm, for 30 s on the pre-treated substrates. A 0.15 M solution of TTIP (titanium tetraisopropoxide) in anhydrous ethanol was normally utilized in this deposition stage and layers were annealed at  $ 450^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for 30 min. Then, a mesoporous TiO $ _2 $  layer was deposited by spin coating for 20 s at 4000 rpm with a ramp of  $ 4000~\mathrm{rpm~s^{-1}} $ , using 30 nm particle paste (IRASOL) diluted in ethanol  $ (150~\mathrm{mg~mL^{-1}}) $ . After the spin coating, the substrates were immediately dried at  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for 10 min and then sintered again at  $ 450^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for 30 min under dry airflow. For the deposition of the  $ \mathrm{Cs}_{0.05}(\mathrm{MA}_{0.17}\mathrm{FA}_{0.83})_{0.95}\mathrm{Pb(I_{0.83}Br_{0.17})_3} $  perovskite layer a solution containing FAI (1 M), CsI (0.05 M),  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  (1.1 M), MABr (0.2 M), and  $ \mathrm{PbBr}_2 $  (0.22 M) in anhydrous DMF: DMSO solvent (4:1 vol ratio) was prepared in the first stage. Then it was coated on FTO/compact TiO $ _2/ $  Mesoporous TiO $ _2 $  by spin coating approach through a two-step procedure at 1000 rpm, 10 s, and 6000 rpm for the 20 s. During the second step,  $ 250~\mu\mathrm{L} $  of CB was poured on the spinning substrate 5 s before the end of the program. The substrates were then annealed at  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for 1 h in a glove box. The CIGS (copper indium gallium disulfide) HTL layers were spin-coated from its  $ 20~\mathrm{mg/mL} $  solution in chloroform at 3000 rpm for 30 s and then heated at  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for 20 min (Khorasani et al., 2019). Finally, the metal contact (Au) was evaporated on the HTL.

# 2.2. Encapsulation process

In order to track the device photovoltaic performance after deposition of the encapsulation layer, a commercial conductive copper (Cu) tape (R < 1 Ω) with a thickness of 10 μm was used as an external contact. The Cu tape was stuck from the backside on the evaporated Au contact. PMMA solution was prepared by dissolving 0.22 gr of PMMA (Sigma Aldrich, MW ~ 120,000) in 1 mL of CB. 15 μL of PMMA solution was spin coated on PSC at 6000 rpm for 90 s in a dynamic mode and finally RTV (Wacker, N 553 POWERSIL) was coated using conventional doctor blade method with no specific deposition parameters, after PMMA layer deposition. Here RTV is a one-component rubber which doesn't need further specific curing, and dries after 10–15 min at room temperature (RT). Here all the encapsulation stages were performed in the air.

# 2.3. UV-Vis, PL, damp heat test, and EIS measurement

The absorption and photoluminescence spectra of the perovskite film were analyzed by UV–Vis spectroscopy using a PerkinElmer Lambda 25 spectrometer and an Avaspec 2048 TEC spectrophotometer using a blue LCMDTL-374QT laser (405 nm, 1000 Hz, 2 mJ per pulse), illuminated to the top of the films, respectively. For damp heat test, the performance of encapsulated devices was measured repeatedly after exposure to 85% moisture and 85 °C temperature using a solar simulator (IRASOL SIM-1000 system) under standard AM 1.5 light irradiance of 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  . J–V curves were recorded by swiping the voltage between 1.1 to -0.01 V with a Keithley 2400 digital source meter while the cells were masked during the measurement. The active area of cells was 0.09 cm  $  ^{2}  $  . Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were conducted using Sharif Solar PGE-18 potentiostat/galvanostat in a frequency range between 1 Hz and 1 MHz with an AC amplitude of 20 mV.

The surface morphology of the perovskite layer can be seen in Fig. 1a) with an average perovskite grain size of 0.5  $ \mu \mathrm{m} $ . Photoluminescence and absorption Spectra of perovskite layer can be seen in Fig. 1b) verifying the suitable absorption edge at  $ 760~\mathrm{nm} $  for the mentioned perovskite layer.

Fig. 1c) reveals the XRD pattern of the perovskite layer. The diffraction peaks at 14.11, 19.99, 24.54, 28.39, 31.79, 34.91, 40.68, and  $ 43.23^{\circ} $  are related to the crystalline planes of the photoactive black phase of  $ \mathrm{Cs}_{0.05}(\mathrm{MA}_{0.17}\mathrm{FA}_{0.83})_{0.95}\mathrm{Pb(I_{0.83}Br_{0.17})_3} $  perovskite layer (Saliba et al., 2016). Meanwhile, there is no diffraction peak at  $ 11.61^{\circ} $  from the delta phase of  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  which is a not preferred photo inactive side structure in three cationic perovskite layers. In addition to the FTO/TiO $ _2 $  diffraction patterns, cubic  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  peaks were determined, which could be from the incomplete conversion of precursors to the black perovskite phase. It is known that  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  could passivate the defects and enhance the cells performance, however, excessive  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  adversely distress the device performance through its poor optoelectronic properties (Chen et al., 2019). For further investigation on encapsulated cell efficiency change being exposed to high temperature and content of the moisture, EIS test was conducted and the capacitive behavior of cells was explored at different frequencies. Fig. 5 presents the capacitance – frequency (C – f) plot of reference and encapsulated cells (a rephrasing of the impedance data) before and after exposure to moisture. Complex capacitance is defined as   $  C(\omega) = (\mathrm{i}\omega Z(\omega))^{-1}  $  , and   $  \omega  $   is the angular frequency. Fig. 5 indicates the real part of the complex capacitance.

According to the provided figure, the high-frequency capacitance values of the reference cell is exponentially decayed after exposure to the moisture. It is known that the capacitive behavior in high-frequency ranges is originated by the geometric capacitance of the perovskite in between the contact layers (Guerrero et al., 2016). Consequently, the mentioned decrement of the reference cell's capacitance explains a clear degradation of the perovskite layer after moisture. In stark contrast, the high-frequency capacitive behavior of the encapsulated cells remained similar after exposure to the moisture.

These results are in good agreement with considerably higher long term stabilities of encapsulated cells as explained before.

On the other hand, the behavior of capacitance in lower frequencies is influenced by ionic double-layer capacitance and additional electronic capacitance representing surface states and the accumulation of carriers at the contacts (Guerrero et al., 2016). Consequently the higher capacitance of encapsulated cells in lower frequency domain could originate by partial degradation of HTL layer by chlorobenzene during the PMMA deposition (Kim et al., 2019; Corsini and Griffini, 2020), which could enhance the trap states and consequently charge accumulation at interfaces. The accumulated charge could soar the capacitance in a clear way (Guerrero et al., 2016).

Concerning the long term stability, the efficiency change of reference and encapsulated cells was explored in larger time scales of 1000 h in RT and 50% moisture represented in Fig. 6. According to Fig. 6 (a), reference cells experienced an efficiency drop of more than 40% of the initial efficiency. However encapsulated cells retained 80% of their PCE for more than 1000 h which is higher than ultra-high permeation barrier films which retained 76.6% of their PCE for more than 840 h (Castro-Hermosa et al., 2019). # 4. Conclusion

Long term stability remains as a validating factor for PSCs to become truly competitive as a result using a suitable encapsulation package to enhance perovskite solar cells long term stability seems to be inevitable. In this work, we have investigated the efficacy of low-cost commercially available bilayer structure of PMMA/RTV as perovskite solar cells encapsulation package. The encapsulated cell efficiency drop was less than  $ 20\% $  of the initial efficiency at the environmental condition of  $ 50\% $  moisture at room temperature after  $ 1000\mathrm{h} $  while reference cell efficiency suffers from severe decrement. The effective properties of the proposed PMMA/RTV protective layer in harsh environmental condition was also explained by EIS method. In general, here we have explained an effective encapsulating layer without any needs for further common post-treatments, which makes it desirable for commercial applications.
