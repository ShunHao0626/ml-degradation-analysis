# SUMMARY

It is challenging to realize high efficiency and stability in perovskite photovoltaics simultaneously. Here, we show supramolecular chemistry using macrocyclic crown ether to prepare high-performance metal-halide perovskite films. Multiple cooperative supramolecular interactions between perovskites and crown ether are built, and superior defect passivation is achieved. A thin crystalline capping layer on top of perovskites is constructed to synergistically passivate the perovskite surface defects and protect the underlying films from environmental damage. The capping layer is confirmed to be a unique two-dimensional (2D) crystal with a highly ordered, high-crystallinity lamellae structure and face-sharing lead-halide octahedral organization, providing surface protection on the perovskite matrix. Lead-halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with supramolecular passivation and a 2D crystal interlayer demonstrated remarkable photovoltaic efficiency and stability improvements, offering a  $ 21.5\% $  efficiency and improved environmental stabilities under moisture, ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, and thermal stress. Our work provides a strategy to achieve efficient and stable lead-halide perovskite-based devices by crown ether-induced supramolecular passivation and 2D crystal interlayer protection.

# INTRODUCTION

Metal-halide perovskite as an emerging photovoltaic semiconductor has attracted enormous attention due to its remarkable structural and electronic properties such as high defect tolerance,  $  ^{1-3}  $   tunable band gap,  $  ^{4}  $   low exciton binding energy,  $  ^{5,6}  $   and prominent charge carrier properties (high diffusion length, long lifetime, and high mobility).  $  ^{7-10}  $   A large leap in power conversion efficiency (PCE) of single-junction perovskite solar cells (PSCs) from 3.8%  $  ^{11}  $   to 25.5%  $  ^{12}  $   was recorded in the past 10 years. Despite this progress, cautious attention has been paid to the defects in crystal and at interfaces that lead to operational instabilities.  $  ^{13-15}  $   Defect passivation methods have been developed to conquer this issue, and establishing robust interfaces between the perovskite light-absorbing layer and the charge-transporting layers (CTLs) is of importance.  $  ^{16-18}  $   Thus far, efforts to overcome the defects include chemical doping,  $  ^{19}  $   additives,  $  ^{20}  $   ligands,  $  ^{21}  $   interfacial engineering,  $  ^{22}  $   and dimensional tailoring.  $  ^{23}  $   Seldom has success been achieved in gaining quantitative and robust interfaces that can undergo complicated stability challenges as well as maintain excellent device performance. An ideal surface protection should be a thin layer that can effectively reduce crystal defects and protect against environmental damage, which at the same time should not impede the charge carrier transport. Crown ether is our preferred candidate to overcome the challenges and enhance perovskite-based device performance. Some recent similar work shows that crown ether molecules were used to improve perovskite device.  $  ^{24,25}  $   However, there have been fewer concerns about exploring crown ether passivating perovskite defects and forming effective crystalline interlayer.

Here, we reveal that the supramolecular chemistry of crown ether offers superior molecular passivation to the vacancy and antisite defects of perovskites. A strong equilibrium of crown ether coordination sequesters lead-halide octahedrons and forces them to form 1-dimensional (1D) frameworks through a face-sharing scheme; then, they combine organic cations and extend into 2D assemblies via inorganic-organic bridges. Finally, they coordinate with crown ether-hydrated proton assemblies to form a new type of layered 2D crystal by charge compensation. Vacancy and antisite defects and the accompanying trap-assisted carrier recombination of perovskite film can be effectively suppressed, which thus elevate the photovoltaic performance of PSCs. A thin higher-band gap crystalline interlayer between perovskite and the hole-transporting layer (HTL) enhances the perovskite Fermi level and surface potential to establish a higher built-in electric field in the heterojunction. This favors charge separation and decreases nonradiative carrier recombination. The crown ether supramolecular interactions and the interfacial 2D crystal in PSCs enabled a recorded PCE of  $ 21.5\% $  and improved stabilities under moisture, ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, and heat. Our methodology in PSC fabrication provides a significant strategy that simultaneously enhances device performance and environmental stabilities via supramolecular passivation, heterojunction modulation, and 2D crystal interlayer protection.

Furthermore, we implemented first-principles calculations to quantify these chemical interactions in terms of specific lattice planes, defect sites, and energy landscaping. Simplified FAPbI $ _3 $  (the major composition of the perovskite formula) perovskite model was used. The binding energies of 15C5 adsorbing onto the perfect perovskite FA-terminated and Pb-terminated lattice planes under three possible adsorption situations are considered. The calculation results (Figures S6 and S7) showed that the binding energies of 15C5 adsorbing onto Pb-terminated lattice plane are  $ -2.05 $  to  $ -1.87 $  eV, which indicates that strong metal-organic coordination bonding between 15C5 and Pb is built; the binding energies of 15C5 adsorbing onto FA-terminated lattice plane are  $ -0.16 $  to  $ -0.11 $  eV, which implies hydrogen bonding between 15C5 and organic cation. It is known that the perovskite surfaces and grain boundaries are prone to defect accumulation, which leads to material degradation and reduced device performance. $ ^{13,14} $  This recipe of incorporating crown ether in perovskite film fabrication that has strong and specific molecular interactions could thus enhance passivating perovskite defects. The formation energies for typical defects $ ^{33} $  of perovskite were then investigated. The perovskite crystal model with iodine vacancy (V $ _i $ ), Pb-I antisite (Pb $ _i $ ), FA-I antisite (FA $ _i $ ), and FA vacancy (V $ _{FA} $ ) defects, respectively, was constructed. Such surface defects generally manifest as the mismatching and disorder of metal (Pb $ ^{2+} $ ) and organic cations in perovskites. Then, 15C5 adsorption onto specific defect sites was calculated, with the results shown in Figures 1E–1G and S8. The formation energies of the above-mentioned defects elevated from 0.39, 1.34, 1.38, and 2.73 eV to 13.09, 3.24, 1.50, and 5.99 eV, respectively, upon 15C5 passivation. The higher formation energies lead to a lower probability of defect generation. This reveals that crown ether (15C5) anchoring onto the perovskite surfaces and grain boundaries by supramolecular interactions, which include metal-organic coordination bonding and hydrogen bonding, could effectively passivate such serious surface defects in perovskites. Therefore, here, we propose a new concept: supramolecular passivation.

# Surface passivation and device performance

The surface and bulk optoelectronic properties of perovskite thin films were studied. UV-visible (UV-vis) absorption and steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra are shown in Figures 3A and S18, the two films demonstrating a typical perovskite absorption characteristic, and the films with 15C5 showing obvious enhanced PL, which indicated that the incorporation of 15C5 could effectively suppress the nonradiative recombination of a pristine perovskite film. Time-resolved PL (TRPL) was conducted to evaluate the charge carrier lifetime. The perovskite film with 15C5 exhibited a remarkably longer lifetime than that of the control film (Figure 3B). A typical dual-exponential decay trace was recorded that the fast decay was due to the nonradiative trap-assisted recombination, and the slow decay process came from naturally radiative recombination. To summarize, here, we report supramolecular chemistry using crown ether to induce supramolecular passivation and a unique 2D crystal interlayer on perovskite surfaces and at perovskite-CTL interfaces. # Preparation of perovskite precursor solution,  $ \mathrm{TiO_x} $  precursor and spiro-OMeTAD solution

Mixed-ion lead-halide perovskite (FA $ _{0.85} $ MA $ _{0.15} $ Pb(I $ _{0.85} $ Br $ _{0.15} $ ) $ _3 $ ) precursor solution was prepared by dissolving FAI (1.02 mol/L), PbI $ _2 $  (1.05 mol/L), MABr (0.18 mol/L), and PbBr $ _2 $  (0.18 mol/L) in a mixed solvent of DMF and DMSO (4:1, in volume). The perovskite precursor solution incorporating crown ethers was prepared by adding crown ethers (including 15C5, 12C4, 18C6, DB21C7, and DB24C8, respectively) with different concentrations into a pristine perovskite precursor solution. Finally, a perovskite precursor solution without and with crown ether was stirred and then filtered by 0.45  $ \mu $ m polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) filter for further use.

TiO  $  _{x}  $  precursor solution was prepared for the ETL. A total of 380  $  \mu  $  L titanium(IV) isopropoxide was added dropwise into 2.53 mL IPA while stirring. Then, a 35-  $  \mu  $  L diluted HCl solution (  $  \sim  $  6%) was slowly added into the as-prepared Ti-containing IPA solution. The obtained solution was stirred for hours and filtered by a 0.45-  $  \mu  $  m PTFE filter for subsequent use.

The spiro-OMeTAD solution was prepared for the HTL. Spiro-OMeTAD (70 mM in chlorobenzene) was doped with Li-TFSI, FK 209 Co(III)-TFSI salt, and tBP according to their molar ratio, which is 1:0.5:0.03:3.3. The doped spiro-OMeTAD solution was stirred for hours and filtered by a 0.45-  $  \mu  $  m PTFE filter for subsequent use.

# Synthesis of 2D monocrystal

We synthesized the 2D monocrystal by using the antisolvent vapor-assisted crystallization (AVC) method,  $  ^{38}  $  which takes advantage of the different solubility of perovskites in different solvents to achieve crystallization. First, the perovskite precursor component FAI (1.02 mol/L),  $  PbI_{2}  $  (1.05 mol/L), MABr (0.18 mol/L), and  $  PbBr_{2}  $  (0.18 mol/L) were dissolved in GBL, and fully stirred and filtered. Then, 15C5 was added dropwise into the perovskite precursor solution by a molar ratio of 1:2 and stirred continuously. These were sealed in a reagent bottle, while a syringe needle was inserted into the bottle cap. Second, the mixed solvent (  $  \sim70\%  $  DCM and  $  \sim30\%  $  IPA in volume percentage) was added to a wide-mouth reagent container with a grinding mouth. Finally, the reagent bottle containing the mixture of perovskite precursor solution and 15C5 was placed into the wide-mouth container and sealed by vacuum grease and then kept static standing for >20 days. After the above operations, the new orange single crystal grew in the reagent bottle. All of the procedures were performed in a glovebox.

# First-principles calculations

All first-principles calculations were implemented in the software Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP). The type of generalized-gradient-approximation was set to the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof formula. Meanwhile, the DFT-D3 method with Becke-Jonson damping was adopted to correct the potential weak van der Waals interaction between the small organic molecules and the perovskites.  $  ^{41}  $   As for the electronic self-consistent calculations, the cutoff energy for the planewave basis set was set to 400 eV and the Gaussian smearing method was used to determine the electronic occupation at ground state with a smearing width of 0.05 eV. The thresholds of energy and force during geometric optimization were   $  10^{-4}  $   eV and 0.05 Å/eV, respectively.

Then,   $  3 \times 3  $   supercells of Pb-terminated and FA-terminated (100) surfaces were cleaved from the bulk counterpart and optimized with a   $  2 \times 2 \times 1  $   Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh accordingly.  $  ^{42}  $   The chemical interaction (  $  E_{a}  $  ) of small organic molecules and the perovskite surface was defined by

$$
E _ {a} = E (* A) - E (*) - E (A)
$$

where the   $  E(\star)  $   and   $  E(A)  $   were the total energy of the bare perovskite surface and the organic molecule, and the   $  E(\star A)  $   was the total energy of surface after adsorption. The formation energy of the possible defects (  $  E_{i}  $  ) was calculated by

$$
E _ {f} = E (\text { defect }) - E (\text { perfect }) + \sum_ {i} n _ {i} \mu_ {i}
$$

where  $  E(\text{defect})  $  and  $  E(\text{perfect})  $  were the total energy of the perovskite surfaces with and without defects.  $  \mu_i  $  was the corresponding potentials of the elemental species, with the number  $  n_i  $  of atoms added or removed from the pure host materials. The standard chemical potentials of FA molecules and I/Pb atoms were referenced to the total energy of single molecules and their bulk structures.
