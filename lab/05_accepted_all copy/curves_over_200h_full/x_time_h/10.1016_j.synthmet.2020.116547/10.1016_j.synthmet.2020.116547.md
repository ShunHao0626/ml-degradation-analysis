# 1. Introduction

Over the past years, perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted a growing interest due to exceptionally high power conversion efficiencies (PCE), currently reaching 25.2% [1], combined with the cost-effective device fabrication methods [2]. PSCs, however, suffer from poor operational stability [3], causing skepticism about the real value of this technology. At the initial stages of development, perovskite solar cells degraded within days even in an inert atmosphere [4]. Now, PSCs became much more robust, achieving stable operation for 1000 h, mainly via interface stabilization using tailored transport layers [5–7]. There is a growing confidence that processes at interfaces control the operational stability of PSCs [8,9].

Among n-type oxide materials, zinc oxide offers multiple advantages as an electron transport layer (ETL): excellent optoelectronic properties, high electron mobility, absence of photo-induced degradation (due to a large bandgap) and technological effectiveness [10]. However, the perovskite/ZnO interface suffers from chemical instability caused by the deprotonation of the methylammonium cation by the weakly alkaline zinc oxide surface [11,12]. Therefore, passivation of the oxide surface or introducing additional interlayers between the perovskite and ZnO films largely improves the stability of the PSCs. When ZnO is modified with ethanolamine [13], zinc sulphide [14] or prepared using an acetate-free method [15], the stability of perovskite films increases. Despite the success of surface modification, the stability of PSCs with ZnO ETL is inferior as compared to the devices utilizing  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  or  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  ETLs [5,6]. Therefore, more research should be focused on stabilizing the zinc oxide/perovskite interface.

Unlike zinc oxide, the tungsten oxide   $  WO_{3}  $   is much less studied as an electron transport layer for PSCs. Wang et al. used solution-processed   $  WO_{3}  $   as a replacement for   $  TiO_{2}  $ , reaching PCE of 8.5% [20]. The authors concluded that   $  WO_{3}  $   has an unfavorable band alignment with the perovskite absorber which makes it nonideal ETL for PSCs. The e-beam processed   $  WO_{3}  $   ETL films delivered higher PCE of 10% [21]. Recently, the device performance has been improved by applying a thin layer of   $  C_{60}  $   between   $  WO_{3}  $   and perovskite, which increased the efficiency to 16% [22]. Tungsten oxide was also used as an interlayer between ITO and   $  SnO_{2}  $ , which improved the device performance [23]. Despite the aforementioned progress in that field, the stability of the   $  WO_{3}  $ /perovskite interface remains largely unexplored.

In this work, we explored the effect of a thin   $  WO_{3}  $   interlayer placed between ZnO ETL and perovskite absorber in n-i-p perovskite solar cells on their operational stability. The ZnO, ZnO/  $  WO_{3}  $   and   $  WO_{3}  $   ETLs were compared. The changes in the crystal structure and optical absorption of absorber thin films and photovoltaic performance of solar cells were investigated. # 2. Results and discussion

In this work, we utilized sol-gel processing of ZnO films widely used in organic photovoltaics   $  [24,25]  $  . To protect perovskite from the alkaline surface of ZnO we used an interlayer of amorphous   $  WO_{3}  $   deposited by thermal evaporation. This method allowed us to achieve a uniform coating of tungsten oxide and to avoid any side reactions that may occur during a solution based deposition. Fig. 1A shows surface topography images of pristine ZnO, ZnO covered with 10 nm   $  WO_{3}  $   layer, and   $  WO_{3}  $   deposited on an ITO substrate. The surface of ZnO has “wrinkled” morphology due to a mismatch between the thermal expansion coefficients of ITO and ZnO, leading to surface deformation during the annealing of the film   $  [26]  $  . The   $  WO_{3}  $   uniformly covered both ZnO and ITO substrates without significantly changing the topography or transparency (figure S1, in the SI) of the samples.

To probe the electron extraction on the ETL/perovskite interface we measured the steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra of   $  Cs_{0.12}FA_{0.88}PbI_{3}  $   perovskite films deposited on ITO/ETL substrates. Fig. 1B, C show the PL spectra of the substrates measured with 532 nm laser through the perovskite side and normalized PL spectra, measured through the ITO side. It is worth noting that the relative intensity of the PL was similar when measured from either side. The intensity of perovskite emission centered at 800 nm showed the strongest quenching for the perovskite deposited on the   $  WO_{3}  $   layer, followed by the ZnO/  $  WO_{3}  $   and the ZnO layers, which indicated more favorable electron extraction by the   $  WO_{3}  $ , probably due to its lower-lying conduction band. The spectral shape of the PL from ZnO/perovskite, when measured through the ITO side, revealed a shoulder at longer wavelengths, which showed the presence of defects at the perovskite/ZnO interface [27]. On the contrary, no shoulder in the PL spectrum was observed for perovskite deposited on   $  ZnO/WO_{3}  $   and   $  WO_{3}  $   ETLs.

To confirm this assumption, we deposited the perovskite films on ZnO,   $  ZnO/WO_{3}  $   and   $  WO_{3}  $   ETLs and then aged the stacks in inert atmosphere for 200 h under 80 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $   and 65 °C. The crystal structure of the absorber films coated on   $  WO_{3}  $   and   $  ZnO/WO_{3}  $   ETLs did not change significantly after aging, while their absorbance slightly increased, possibly due to a minor recrystallization of the perovskite, which was observed before [28]. On the contrary, we did not observe any perovskite remaining on ZnO after 200 h of aging. The absorbance spectrum of the degraded sample was featureless, while the XRD patterns consisted of multiple peaks, which corresponded to a mixture of perovskite decomposition products. The peaks at 12.6°, 25.4°, 34.0° 2Theta matched the characteristic signals of   $  PbI_{2}  $  ; the peak at 11.8° 2Theta can be assigned to β-FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   (100) reflection, while the peaks at 8.4° and 16.7° 2Theta most likely correspond to a formamidine - lead iodide adduct [29], which could have formed if ZnO catalyzed FAI decomposition to HI and   $  NH_{2}CH = NH  $  . While the identification of perovskite degradation products lies beyond the scope of this paper, we believe that the deprotonation of FAI by ZnO may occur similarly to the MAI deprotonation [30], though being slower. Thus, we concluded that the   $  WO_{3}  $   coating on ZnO largely increased the perovskite film stability as compared to the reference ZnO/perovskite samples.

Since the   $  WO_{3}  $   buffer layer stabilized the   $  Cs_{0.12}FA_{0.88}PbI_{3}  $   perovskite absorber films, we evaluated the operational stability of PSCs fabricated using ZnO,   $  WO_{3}  $   and   $  ZnO/WO_{3}  $   ETLs. The back contact was composed of   $  MoO_{3}/Al  $   hole-selective electrode [31] and a dopant-free polytriarylamine (PTA) [32] HTL. The devices were aged while being under open circuit conditions at 80 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $   light at 65 °C in nitrogen. Such settings were selected in order to simulate realistic operational conditions [33], since in commercial solar cells any moisture or oxygen-related effects are suppressed using encapsulation, while the heat and light play the role of major stress factors.

# 3. Conclusion

In this work, we investigated an impact of interfacial modification of ZnO with a thin   $  WO_{3}  $   layer on the photothermal stability of hybrid lead halide perovskite. We show that the tungsten oxide significantly improved the photostability of   $  Cs_{0.12}FA_{0.88}PbI_{3}  $   perovskite films, which was attributed to blocking of ZnO contact with the perovskite. The devices with   $  ZnO/WO_{3}  $   binary ETL performed better as compared to PSCs with ZnO and   $  WO_{3}  $   ETLs. Moreover, the operational stability of the solar cells improved dramatically, reaching T75% of 2000 h under 65 °C and 80 mW/cm². It is clear that the binary   $  ZnO/WO_{3}  $   ETL provides a promising solution to stabilize the perovskite cells, which is required for practical implementation of this technology.

# 4. Materials and methods

# 4.1. Solar cell fabrication

The device fabrication procedure was similar to the previously published ones   $  [32,35]  $   Glass/ITO substrates (Kintec, 15 Ω/sq.) were sequentially cleaned with toluene, acetone, and isopropanol. Then, the ITO slides were air-plasma (100 W, Femto Diener) treated for 5 min and immediately coated with ZnO layer. The precursor solution for zinc oxide was prepared by dissolving 100 mg of zinc acetate in a mixture of 1 mL of 2-methoxyethanol and 33 μL of monoethanolamine. The solution was magnetically stirred for 24 h, filtered through 0.45 μm PTFE syringe filter, and spin-coated on ITO at 6000 rpm. The films were annealed in air at 200 °C for 1 h. All subsequent steps were performed under inert atmosphere inside a nitrogen-filled glove box. The 40 nm   $  WO_{3}  $   layer was thermally evaporated under 9*10  $  ^{-6}  $   Torr pressure and 0.2–0.4 A s  $  ^{-1}  $   rate The 1.4 M   $  Cs_{0.12}FA_{0.88}PbI_{3}  $   ink obtained by dissolution of 99 mg CsI, 435 mg FAI and 1474 mg PbI  $  _{2}  $   in 1.7 mL of DMF and 0.3 mL of DMSO, was spin-coated at 4000 rpm (80 μL) and quenched with 180 μL of ethyl acetate dropped 15 s later. The deposited films were annealed for 10 min at 100 °C on a hotplate. To deposit the HTL, 4 mg of PTA (poly[bis(4-phenyl)(4-methylphenyl)amine]  $  [32,35]  $  ) in 1 mL of toluene was spin-coated at 1000 rpm on top of the perovskite films. Then, a solution of 0.4 mg/mL of polystyrene was spincoated at 3000 rpm. Molybdenum oxide (15 nm)/Al (100 nm) electrodes were evaporated through a shadow mask. The device active area was 0.08 cm  $  ^{2}  $   as defined by a shadow mask.

# 4.2. Characterization techniques [32,34]

The current-voltage characteristics of the devices were measured under nitrogen atmosphere using the simulated AM1.5 G illumination (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) provided by Newport Verasol AAA class solar simulator. The intensity of the illumination was checked before each measurement using a calibrated silicon diode with a known spectral response. J–V curves were recorded using Advantest 6240A source-measurement units. The EQE spectra were measured using a PV Instruments system integrated with MBraun glove box.

The X-ray diffraction patterns were collected using a Bruker diffractometer with   $  CuK_{\alpha}  $   source. Measurements were carried out in a fixed illumination mode with no sample rotation. The absorption spectra were obtained in an inert atmosphere using AvaSpec-2048–2 fiber spectrometer integrated with MBraun glove box. Atomic force microscopy measurements were performed in a semi-contact mode using NTMDT NTEGRA atomic force microscope. PL spectra were measured under nitrogen atmosphere using a Horiba spectrometer and 532 nm laser as an excitation source.
