As is commonly known, the weak stability of perovskite originates mainly from the high density (  $  10^{15}-10^{16}  $   cm  $  ^{3}  $  ) of defects such as Pb interstitials, halide vacancies, and point defects, which exist primarily on the film surface and grain boundaries.  $  ^{[8,9]}  $   These defects are 1) vulnerable sites for oxygen and moisture permeation and 2) recombination centers that trap photogenerated charge carriers, threatening the performance and stability of the devices.

Solution-processed perovskite films are rich in surface defects and grain boundaries, which limits their performance and stability in photovoltaic application. Surface passivation using bulky organic cations can effectively reduce the surface defects of a perovskite film without affecting its fundamental properties. Herein, the use of hydrophobic bulky aromatic molecules, namely 4-trifluoromethyl-benzylammonium iodide/bromide (CF₃BZA-I/Br), as defect-passivators to heal the surface defects and grain boundaries of perovskite films is introduced. Owing to the presence of the trifluoromethyl (-CF₃) moieties, CF₃BZA-I/Br-passivated perovskite films exhibit a hydrophobic surface with significantly fewer grain boundaries. By suppressing the surface and interfacial imperfections, CF₃BZA-Br-treated perovskite solar cells achieve an outstanding power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 20.75%. The PCE improvement originates mainly from the reduction of trap states and nonradiative carrier recombination. The ultrathin hydrophobic barrier layer formed after passivation also shields the perovskite film surface from moisture ingress and environmental degradation, leading to improved stability of the devices. By optimizing the passivation conditions, the bulky CF₃BZA-I/Br molecules could be the ideal defect passivators, with versatile applications in a wide variety of perovskite optoelectronics.

# 1. Introduction

Solution-processed, archetypal 3D organic-inorganic perovskites have been widely used as a light harvester in perovskite solar cells (PSCs) due to their exceptional optoelectronic properties, including low exciton binding energy, high carrier mobility, long

ation,  $  ^{[15,16]}  $   and heat treatment,  $  ^{[17,18]}  $   have been proposed to tailor the defects of the perovskite films. The surface passivation approach is particularly effective because the perovskite surface typically accommodates the most defects and grain boundaries. Passivating the detrimental surface defects and grain boundaries can efficiently reduce the carrier recombination loss and ion migrations, and promote the radiative recombination of charge carriers. Moreover, surface passivation can also modify the surface properties of perovskite film, such as wettability and surface energy.  $  ^{[19,20]}  $   Previous studies have demonstrated that surface passivation with small organic molecules such as formamidinium bromide (FABr), methylammonium iodide (MAI), and methylammonium bromide (MABr) can considerably reduce the charge carrier recombination losses occurring at the perovskite/HTL interface and throughout the bulk perovskite.  $  ^{[20,21]}  $   These small molecules can penetrate through the surface and react with the bulk perovskite, which leads to complexities such as altering the optoelectronic properties and energy band structure of the entire perovskite.

The fundamental properties of the perovskite film can therefore remain intact after passivation. In certain cases, surface passivation with BOCs can lead to the formation of a 2D perovskite capping layer atop the 3D bulk perovskite, especially when the concentration of BOC is high or when post-annealing treatment is conducted. Although the 3D/2D perovskite heterolayer often shows enhanced moisture resistance, the quantum confinement in the 2D perovskite layer may induce a charge transport barrier that deteriorates device performance. Therefore, selecting the appropriate BOC as surface passivator is the key to producing high-quality perovskite films. Numerous BOCs have been explored for direct surface passivation purposes, including butylammonium,  $  ^{[23]}  $   benzylammonium (BZA),  $  ^{[24]}  $   phenethylammonium (PEA),  $  ^{[15]}  $   ethylammonium,  $  ^{[25]}  $   and octylammonium.  $  ^{[26]}  $   For surface passivation purpose, aromatic BOCs particularly BZA and PEA are more commonly used because their molecular structure contains a benzene ring with high delocalized electron cloud density that can contribute positively to the electronic properties of the perovskite film.  $  ^{[27-29]}  $   The properties of the passivated perovskite films are immensely influenced by the molecular structure of the BOC used. In particular, perfluorinated BOC with fluorine (-F) moiety is found to be a better surface passivator, because they are more hydrophobic and scarcely wetted by water, due to the retarded dispersion forces induced by the high electronegativity of the F moiety.  $  ^{[30-32]}  $   Theoretical calculation anticipates that the perfluorinated BOC may alter the crystal structure of perovskite and improve the energy level of molecules to a greater extent.  $  ^{[33]}  $

In this work, we introduce the use of 4-trifluoromethyl-benzylammonium iodide (CF₃BZA-I) and bromide (CF₃BZA-Br) molecules as novel defect passivators for perovskite film. The molecular structure of CF₃BZA-I/Br consists of a polar ammonium head group, a unique —CF₃ moiety and a halogen (I or Br), each of which plays a different role during the passivation process. The facile passivation treatment was executed by means of dynamic spin coating. We systematically explored the influence of CF₃BZA-I/Br passivation on the defect states, surface properties, and charge transport of the perovskite films. Our results confirm that the passivation effect of CF₃BZA-I/Br is limited on the perovskite surface, due to the steric effect of the bulky CF₃BZA cations which hinders further reaction inside the perovskite crystal. The surface of the perovskite films become hydrophobic after passivation, which can be associated with the steric hindrance in the CF₃BZA cations. More importantly, both CF₃BZA-I and CF₃BZA-Br passivation could significantly reduce the surface defects and grain boundaries in perovskite films. Theoretical simulation shows that upon passivation, the effective filling of the formaminidium and halide (I/Br) vacancies existing on the surface leads to tremendous reduction of trap states in the perovskite film, enabling more efficient charge transport and suppressing nonradiative recombination. As a result, the device performance of PSCs based on CF₃BZA-I/Br passivated perovskite films shows remarkable improvement, in which the champion PSC device achieved a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of

$ \approx 20.8\% $ . In addition, the  $ \mathrm{CF_3BZA - I / Br} $ -treated PSCs also demonstrated much higher ambient stability and photostability due to the enhanced film hydrophobicity that helped to resist oxygen and moisture permeation. Through a series of characterizations, our study demonstrates that  $ \mathrm{CF_3BZA - I / Br} $  passivation can fine-tune the optoelectronic properties and surface defects of the perovskite films, enabling significant enhancement in the performance and stability in perovskite solar cells.

# 2. Results and Discussion

Figure 1a schematically illustrates the complete fabrication process of n–i–p-structured planar PSC with a device configuration of glass/ITO/SnO  $  _{2}  $  /perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD/Au. As is commonly known, the surface of FA  $  _{0.85}  $  MA  $  _{0.15}  $  PbBr  $  _{0.45}  $  I  $  _{2.55}  $  (FAMA)-based 3D perovskite film, in its pristine form, is rich in lattice defects, grain boundaries, and halide vacancies that can deteriorate the photovoltaic performance of PSCs.  $  ^{[34]}  $  To overcome this shortcoming, we introduced trifluoromethyl (—CF  $  _{3}  $ ) tail group bearing benzylammonium halides, namely CF  $  _{3}  $  BZA-I and CF  $  _{3}  $  BZA-Br as interfacial defect passivators to alleviate the surface defects of the bulk perovskite film. Primarily, the CF  $  _{3}  $  BZA-I/Br molecules anchor to the perovskite surface via incorporation of ammonium groups in the A cation sites. The X-ray diffractometer (XRD) patterns of the perovskite films treated with varying concentrations (0, 1, 2, 4, and 8 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $ ) of CF  $  _{3}  $  BZA-I and CF  $  _{3}  $  BZA-Br are displayed in Figure S1, Supporting Information. Generally, all perovskite films are highly crystalline and exhibit a dominant diffraction peak at a 2θ of ≈14.2° that corresponds to the (110) plane of α-perovskite phase. Other diffraction peaks corresponding to the (002), (003), (220), (310), (114), (224), and (314) planes of the α-perovskite phase have also been identified.  $  ^{[34]}  $  Owing to the use of Pb-excess recipe in perovskite precursor, the diffraction peak at 2θ of ≈12.7° which signifies PbI  $  _{2}  $  phase are observed in all of the samples. There are no significant changes in the XRD patterns of the perovskite films following CF  $  _{3}  $  BZA-I/Br passivation, suggesting that the bulky spacer cations do not cause severe distortion in the crystal lattice.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that the extrinsic passivation of aromatic BOCs such as phenethylammonium iodide (PEAI) and butylammonium iodide (BZAI) could lead to the on-site formation of ultrathin 2D perovskite capping layer atop the bulk 3D perovskite film, due to the lower surface energy of the 2D perovskite.  $  ^{[35]}  $   To confirm this, we compared the XRD patterns of the   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $   (2 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) passivated perovskite films with that of the pristine analogue and pure   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $   salt (Figure 1b). There are no traces of the signature peak of   $  (\mathrm{CF}_{3}-\mathrm{BZA})_{2}\mathrm{PbI}_{4}  $   2D perovskite at   $  2\theta  $   of   $  \approx14.2^{\circ}  $  , confirming that there is no formation of 2D perovskite crystal phase in the   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $   (2 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) passivated perovskite films. The representative diffraction peaks of the   $  CF_{3}BZA-I  $   salt (  $  2\theta=5.2^{\circ}, 10.4^{\circ}  $  ) and   $  CF_{3}BZA-Br  $   salt (  $  2\theta=6.7^{\circ}, 13.3^{\circ}  $  ) also were not detected in the XRD patterns of the   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $  -treated perovskite films, suggesting that either the   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $   salts may have completely reacted with the remnant   $  PbI_{2}  $   on perovskite film surface or the passivation concentration (2 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) of   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $   used is too low to produce interpretable signals. In addition, high-resolution F 1s spectra of the perovskite films were recorded at a sputtering time of 0 s (surface region) and 100 s (bulk region). No peak can be observed in the F 1s spectra of the pristine perovskite film (Figure S8c, Supporting Information). In contrast, the F 1s spectra of the CF  $  _{3}  $  BZA-Br-passivated film in Figure S8d, Supporting Information, shows a broad peak corresponding to the C—F bond at a sputtering time of 0 s (surface region). The disappearance of the C—F bond at a sputtering time of 100 s further confirmed that absence of CF  $  _{3}  $  BZA-Br molecules in the bulk region of the perovskite film. Apparently, the bulky size and steric effect of the CF  $  _{3}  $  BZA-Br passivators has confined them on the perovskite surface and hinders further reaction inside the perovskite crystal.

In relation to this, UPS measurements were also conducted to determine the changes in the work function and energy band structure of the   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $  -treated samples. The UPS spectra in Figure 3e reveal a corresponding shift in the secondary cut-off and valence band of the   $  CF_{3}BZA-I  $  - and   $  CF_{3}BZA-Br  $  -treated films. Nevertheless, the secondary cut-off and valence band of   $  CF_{3}BZA-Br  $  -treated films remains somewhere between the pristine and   $  CF_{3}BZA-I  $  -treated perovskite film. We used the   $  E_{g}  $   values obtained from the Tauc plots to calculate the HOMO and LUMO energy levels of the modified perovskite films, and the detailed parameters are summarized in Table S1, Supporting Information. The UPS analysis results reveal that there is a minor downward shift in the energy band of the   $  CF_{3}BZA-I  $  -passivated film. In contrast, the energy band (HOMO/LUMO) of the   $  CF_{3}BZA-Br  $  -passivated film shifted upward. In both cases, the energy band shift of the perovskite films is insignificant, due to the low concentration of   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $   passivators used.

Then, we conducted density functional theory (DFT) simulations to understand further the defect-passivation mechanism of   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $   on   $  FA^{+}  $  vacancies (  $  V_{FA}  $  ) and I/Br vacancies (  $  V_{I/Br}  $  ), which are among the most commonly found ionic defects on

perovskite surfaces. Figure 4a,b depicts the differential charge density distributions for the adsorption of  $ \mathrm{CF}_3\mathrm{BZA - I / Br} $  passivators on the perovskite surface and subsequent occupation of the  $ V_{\mathrm{FA}} $  and  $ V_{\mathrm{I / Br}} $  sites, respectively. The simulation outcome reflects that the  $ \mathrm{CF}_3\mathrm{BZA - I / Br} $  passivators can effectively interact with the surrounding I ions upon filling into the  $ V_{\mathrm{FA}} $ , mimicking the role of the pre-existing FA atoms. Delocalized electrons are transferred from the  $ \mathrm{CF}_3\mathrm{BZA - I / Br} $  molecules to the  $ V_{\mathrm{FA}} $ , leading to the accumulation of electrons in one of the peripheries I/Br halide atoms.[38] A similar finding could also be inferred from the electron localization function (ELF) results. The ELF profile of the  $ \mathrm{CF}_3\mathrm{BZA - I / Br} $ -passivated perovskite system (Figure S9a, Supporting Information) highly resembles that of the perfect (defect-free) cubic-phase perovskite system (Figure S9b, Supporting Information), suggesting that the  $ \mathrm{CF}_3\mathrm{BZA - I / Br} $  passivators could suppress the delocalization of the wave functions around I ions caused by  $ V_{\mathrm{FA}} $  (Figure S9c, Supporting Information). The passivation effect of the  $ \mathrm{CF}_3\mathrm{BZA - I / Br} $  molecules can also be perceived from the difference in the density of states (DOS) of the perfect, passivated, and  $ V_{\mathrm{FA}} $ -containing (standard) perovskite systems. As illustrated in Figure 4c, the Fermi level of the  $ V_{\mathrm{FA}} $ -containing perovskite system lies closer to the valence band, indicating a robust p-type conduction behavior that is undesirable for separation of charge carriers.[39] In the scenario where  $ \mathrm{CF}_3\mathrm{BZA - I / Br} $  molecules fill  $ V_{\mathrm{FA}} $ , the DOS for the passivated perovskite system shows a recovery similar to perfect (defect-free) perovskite system with Fermi level shifting back closer to the bandgap.[40] This finding signifies that  $ \mathrm{CF}_3\mathrm{BZA - I / Br} $  passivation could effectively modulate the defect states associated with  $ V_{\mathrm{FA}} $  in the perovskite.

In contrast, the differential charge density plot in Figure 4b reveals that the adsorbed   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $   molecules donate electrons to the uncoordinated Pb atoms upon filling in the   $  V_{I/Br}  $   on the perovskite surface. The adsorption of the   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $   molecules also helps to passivate the dangling states surrounding the exposed Pb atoms and reduce the electron localization around Pb atoms that leads to nonradiative events.  $  ^{[41]}  $   This explains the high resemblance between the ELF profiles of the   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $  -passivated perovskite system and the perfect perovskite system (Figure S9d–f, Supporting Information). The passivation effect of   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $   molecules can be alternately confirmed by comparing the projected density of states (PDOS) of the relative Pb atom at the surface of the perovskite systems (Figure 4d). The density of the trap states associated   $  V_{I/Br}  $ , appearing at the conduction band, reduced marginally after   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $   passivation. There is no apparent peak shift in the defective states upon   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $   passivation, consistent with the XPS and PL findings. Overall, the DFT simulation herein confirms that   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $   passivation could enhance the electronic structure of the defect-rich perovskite crystal by passivating the defects states associated with   $  V_{FA}  $   and   $  V_{I/Br}  $   with amine and trifluoromethyl moieties.

# 3. Conclusions

In summary, this work demonstrates the beneficial use of hydrophobic trifluoromethylated BOCs with different halide functional groups, namely   $  CF_{3}BZA-I  $   and   $  CF_{3}BZA-Br  $   as surface defect passivators for perovskite film. The surface passivation process was executed by means of dynamic spin coating at various concentrations. DFT simulation reveals that the   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $   passivation could significantly decrease the organic/halide vacancies and defects associated with grain boundaries, on the surface of perovskite film, enabling low trap densities and enhanced charge transfer in perovskite films. The additional   $  -CF_{3}  $   moieties in the BOCs increase the hydrophobicity of perovskite film surface and shield the perovskite structure from moisture ingress and environmental degradation. In terms of PSC device performance, the champion   $  CF_{3}BZA-I  $  - and   $  CF_{3}BZA-I  $  -passivated device achieved a maximum PCE of   $  \approx20.3\%  $   and   $  \approx20.8\%  $  , respectively, which are much higher than the PCE (  $  \approx18.2\%  $  ) of the pristine device. Moreover, the   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $  -treated devices exhibit greatly improved shelf lifetime and photostability, retaining 88% of its initial PCE after 300 h of exposure under continuous one-sun illumination. Therefore, the experimental outcome shows that the   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $   surface passivation strategy can be applied in PSC applications to improve their device performance.

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials: Tin (II) chloride dihydrate   $  \left(\mathrm{SnCl}_{2}\cdot2\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O},98\%\right)  $  , bis (trifluoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt (Li-TSFI, 99.95%), dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.8%), ethyl acetate (anhydrous 99.5%), hexane (anhydrous 99.5%), chlorobenzene (CB, anhydrous 99.9%), 4-tert-butylpyridine (tBP, 96%), acetonitrile (99.8%), ethanol (anhydrous 99%), and 2-propanol (IPA, anhydrous 99.9%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Lead (II) iodide   $  \left(\mathrm{PbI}_{2},99.99\%\right)  $   and lead bromide   $  \left(\mathrm{PbBr}_{2},>98\%\right)  $  , 4-trifluoromethyl-benzylammonium iodide   $  \left(\mathrm{CF}_{3}\mathrm{BZA}-1,\mathrm{MW}:303.06\right)  $   and 4-trifluoromethyl-benzylammonium bromide   $  \left(\mathrm{CF}_{3}\mathrm{BZA}-\mathrm{Br},\mathrm{MW}:256.06\right)  $   were purchased from TCI chemical. Formamidinium iodide (FAI, >98%), methylammonium bromide   $  \left(\mathrm{MABr},>98\%\right)  $  , and methylammonium chloride (MACl, >98%) were purchased from GreatCell Solar. Spiro-OMeTAD (>99%) was purchased from Lumtec. All materials were used as received, without further purification.

Fabrication of PSC Devices:   $  2.5 \times 2.5 \, cm^{2}  $  -patterned ITO-coated glass substrates with a sheet resistance of   $  \approx 10 \Omega/\square  $   and an optical transmittance of 89% (550 nm) were sequentially cleaned via sonication in acetone, ethanol, and IPA. For the preparation of the electron transport layer (ETL),   $  SnO_{2}  $   precursor solution was prepared by dissolving   $  SnCl_{2} \cdot 2H_{2}O  $   salt in ethanol at a concentration of   $  23 \, mg \, mL^{-1}  $  . The solution was spin coated onto the substrates at 3000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at   $  190^{\circ}C  $   for 1 h in air. For the fabrication of the PSC device, FAMA perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving the   $  PbI_{2}  $   (560 mg), FAI (209 mg),   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (80 mg), MABr (20 mg), MACl (5 mg) in 1 mL of a DMF:DMSO (4:1 v v  $  ^{-1}  $  ) solvent mixture. Green antisolvent was prepared by mixing ethyl acetate (EA) and hexane (Hex) in 7:3 v v  $  ^{-1}  $   similar to our previous works.  $  ^{[34,47,48]}  $   Fifty microliters of perovskite precursor solution was spin coated onto the ITO/  $  SnO_{2}  $   substrate via two-step spin coating, i.e., 1500 rpm for 10 s followed by 5000 rpm for 30 s. At the 10 s interval during the second-step spin coating, 100   $  \mu L  $   of the EA–Hex mixed antisolvent was rapidly dripped onto the spinning substrate. The perovskite films were then annealed at   $  150^{\circ}C  $   for 20 min in air. For BOC passivation treatment,   $  CF_{3}BZA-I/Br  $   solution (1, 2, 4, and 8 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   in IPA) was spin coated onto the perovskite film at 5000 rpm for 40 s without further annealing. The passivation treatment was performed inside an   $  N_{2}  $  -filled glovebox. The hole transport layer (HTL) precursor solution was prepared by dissolving spiro-OMeTAD salt in chlorobenzene at   $  72.3 \, mg \, mL^{-1}  $  , doped with 30   $  \mu L  $   of 4-tBp, 35   $  \mu L  $   of Li-TSFI solution (  $  260 \, mg \, mL^{-1}  $   in acetonitrile) and 23   $  \mu L  $   of FK-209 solution (  $  300 \, mg \, mL^{-1}  $   in acetonitrile). The spiro-OMeTAD solution was spin coated onto the perovskite film at 3000 rpm for 40 s. Finally,   $  \approx 100 \, nm  $  -thick Au top electrodes were thermally evaporated inside a high-vacuum chamber (  $  \approx 5 \times 10^{-6} \, torr  $  ) using a shadow mask with an aperture area of   $  0.096 \, cm^{2}  $  .

Device Characterization of PSCs: Structural phase and crystallographic changes of the perovskite films were characterized with an XRD (Bruker D8 Advance) using Cu Kα excitation.
