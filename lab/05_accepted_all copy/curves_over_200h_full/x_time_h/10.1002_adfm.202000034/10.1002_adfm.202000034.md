# Self-Additive Low-Dimensional Ruddlesden–Popper Perovskite by the Incorporation of Glycine Hydrochloride for High-Performance and Stable Solar Cells

Haiying Zheng,  $  ^{*}  $  Weiwei Wu, Huifen Xu, Fangcai Zheng, Guozhen Liu, Xu Pan,  $  ^{*}  $  and Qianwang Chen

The recent rise of low-dimensional Ruddlesden–Popper (RP) perovskites is notable for superior humidity stability, however they suffer from low power conversion efficiency (PCE). Suitable organic spacer cations with special properties display a critical effect on the performance and stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Herein, a new strategy of designing self-additive low-dimensional RP perovskites is first proposed by employing a glycine salt (Gly  $  ^{+}  $  ) with outstanding additive effect to improve the photovoltaic performance. Due to the strong interaction between C=O and Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  , the Gly  $  ^{+}  $   can become a nucleation center and be beneficial to uniform and fast growth of the Gly-based RP perovskites with larger grain sizes, leading to reduced grain boundary and increased carrier transport. As a result, the Gly-based self-additive low-dimensional RP perovskites exhibit remarkable photoelectric properties, yielding the highest PCE of 18.06% for Gly (n=8) devices and 15.61% for Gly (n=4) devices with negligible hysteresis. Furthermore, the Gly-based devices without encapsulation show excellent long-term stability against humidity, heat, and UV light in comparison to BA-based low-dimensional PSCs. This approach provides a feasible design strategy of new-type low-dimensional RP perovskites to obtain highly efficient and stable devices for next-generation photovoltaic applications.

Combined with the above two methods of designing new low-dimensional RP perovskite materials and incorporating additive commonly used to improve the PCE of low-dimensional RP perovskite, we first adopted a method of self-additive by introducing an ammonium salt (glycine hydrochloride, Gly  $  ^{+}  $  ) with additive effect to prepare self-additive low-dimensional RP perovskites. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns showed that Gly-based perovskite films have excellent crystal orientation with low-dimensional RP perovskite structure and proved the self-additive effect of Gly  $  ^{+}  $   by quantitative calculation. XPS spectra confirmed the strong interaction between C=O and Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  , which may create the nucleation sites for the formation of low-dimensional perovskite. Compared with the paradigm BA (n=4) low-dimensional RP perovskite, the Gly-based low-dimensional RP perovskites display stronger absorption intensities, lower bandgaps, longer charge carrier lifetimes, and lower trap density, resulting in the faster charge transfer, reduced carrier recombination and improved charge extraction. Therefore, the Gly-based low-dimensional RP perovskite devices exhibit a significantly increased PCE up to 18.06% with improved   $  J_{sc}  $  ,   $  V_{oc}  $  , FF, and negligible hysteresis. In addition, the Gly-based PSCs present excellent long-term stability against humidity, thermal, and UV light.

# 2. Results and Discussion

Herein, a novel space cation   $  HOOCCCH_{2}NH_{3}^{+}  $   (glycine cation, simplified as   $  Gly^{+}  $  ) was employed into   $  (\mathrm{Cs}_{0.05}\mathrm{FA}_{0.95}-\mathrm{PbI}_{3})_{0.9}(\mathrm{FAPbBr}_{3})_{0.1}  $   (3D) perovskite to prepare low-dimensional

RP perovskites for improving photovoltaic performance and device stability. Based on the general formula of   $  [\mathrm{Gly}_{2}(\mathrm{Cs}_{0.05}\mathrm{FA}_{0.95})_{n-1}\mathrm{Pb}_{n}\mathrm{I}_{3n-1}\mathrm{Cl}_{2}]_{0.9}(\mathrm{FAPbBr}_{3})_{0.1}  $  , we respectively prepared the Gly-based (n=4, 6, and 8) low-dimensional perovskites by regulating the n values and the   $  [\mathrm{BA}_{2}(\mathrm{Cs}_{0.05}\mathrm{FA}_{0.95})_{3}\mathrm{Pb}_{4}\mathrm{I}_{13}]_{0.9}(\mathrm{FA}-\mathrm{PbBr}_{3})_{0.1}  $   (BA, n=4) perovskite for comparison.

XRD patterns of BA (n = 4) and Gly (n = 4, 6, and 8) perovskite films are showed in Figure 1a. It can be found that the Gly-based low-dimensional RP perovskites present prominently stronger 3D perovskite characteristic peaks of (110) and (220) crystal planes, in contrast with BA (n = 4). More importantly, a new peak shows at   $  8.6^{\circ}  $   is the 2D perovskite characteristic peak of BA (n = 4) and a set of equally spaced peaks at around   $  8.1^{\circ}  $  ,   $  15.4^{\circ}  $  , and   $  22.7^{\circ}  $   corresponded to (002), (004), and (006) crystal planes represent the 2D characteristic peaks of Gly-based low-dimensional RP perovskites. We also tested the XRD pattern of   $  (\mathrm{Gly})_{2}\mathrm{PbI}_{2}\mathrm{Cl}_{2}  $   (n = 1) 2D perovskite film in Figure S1b (Supporting Information), which displays a set of equally spaced peaks, further validating the low-dimensional perovskite structures of Gly (n = 4, 6, and 8). In the magnified XRD patterns (Figure 1b), the 2D and 3D perovskite characteristic peaks of Gly-based low-dimensional RP perovskites have some deviation with the increase of the n values, which means that their perovskite crystal lattice constants exhibit slightly changes due to the incorporation of Gly  $  ^{+}  $  . To further compare the crystallinity of BA (n = 4) and Gly (n = 4, 6, and 8) perovskite films, we gathered statistic their peak intensities and full width at half maximum (FWHM) for the (110) crystal plane. It can be observed that, compared to BA (n = 4) perovskite, Gly-based low-dimensional RP perovskites have markedly stronger peak intensities and smaller FWHM values. To compare the performance changes of BA (n = 4) and Gly-based perovskite films and verify the above mechanism process, we measured their X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements. XPS spectra signals of Pb 4f and O 1s of BA (n = 4) and Gly (n = 4) perovskite films are shown in Figure 1d,e. For Pb 4f signals, both BA (n = 4) and Gly (n = 4) perovskite films show two main peaks of Pb   $  4f_{7/2}  $   and Pb   $  4f_{5/2}  $ , whereas, Gly-based perovskite film represent the evident movement toward the high-energy direction compared to BA (n = 4), indicating that   $  Pb^{2+}  $   in Gly-based perovskites has the different chemical environment and verifying the interaction between C=O in   $  Gly^{+}  $  and   $  Pb^{2+}  $ . In Figure 1e, it can be found that the O 1s signal of Gly (n = 4) perovskite film is obviously higher than that of BA (n = 4) film (For BA (n = 4) film, the slight ssO 1s signal is inevitably detected due to the presence of oxygen in the air), which is caused by the existence of COOH in   $  Gly^{+}  $ . In addition, as can be seen from Figure 1f, the Cl 2p signal presents in Gly-based perovskite film due to the use of glycine hydrochloride. The enhancement of O 1s signal and the presence of Cl 2p signal provide strong evidence for the existence of Gly-Cl in Gly (n = 4) low-dimensional RP perovskite film.

The top-view and cross-sectional scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of BA  $ (n = 4) $  and Gly  $ (n = 4,6, $  and 8) perovskite films are presented in Figure 2. From the top-view SEM images, it can be found that the four low-dimensional RP perovskite films have high surface coverage and BA  $ (n = 4) $  perovskite film displays rough surface and many holes on the surface. Notably, compared with 3D (Figure S4, Supporting Information) and BA  $ (n = 4) $  perovskite films, Gly  $ (n = 4,6, $  and 8) perovskite films have significantly enlarged grain sizes. It can also be seen from the cross section in Figure 2a,d that the quality of Gly  $ (n = 4) $  perovskite films is higher and the thickness also observably increases to  $ 694.2\mathrm{nm} $ , which is far greater than that of the BA  $ (n = 4) $  perovskite film  $ (417.7\mathrm{nm}) $

Moreover, Gly   $  (n = 4)  $   perovskite is vertical growth to the substrate, which will be beneficial to the charge transfer and thus improve the   $  J_{sc}  $  . It is resulted from the electron pair on oxygen atom of the C=O bond in   $  Gly^{+}  $  has a strong coordination with   $  Pb^{2+}  $  , which can form the nucleation center around   $  Gly^{+}  $  and promote perovskite layer faster and better growth to acquire more uniform and larger grain sizes.

To further explore the optical properties of the BA ( $ n = 4 $ ) and Gly ( $ n = 4, 6 $ , and 8) perovskite films, UV–vis absorption spectra and steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra are executed and displayed in Figure 3a,c. Figure S5 (Supporting Information) also showed the UV–vis absorption spectra and PL spectra of 3D, BA ( $ n = 4 $ ) and Gly ( $ n = 4 $ ) perovskite films. Compared with 3D perovskite, the absorptions of BA ( $ n = 4 $ ) and Gly ( $ n = 4, 6 $ , and 8) perovskite films have obvious blueshifted, which is consistent with the optical properties of most low-dimensional perovskites. $ ^{[26,40]} $  It is caused by the increased length of Pb-I bond due to the incorporation of BA $ ^{+} $  and Gly $ ^{+} $  into 3D perovskite, leading to the increase of bandgaps ( $ E_{\mathrm{g}} $ ) of low-dimensional perovskites and the blueshifted of absorption edges. The Gly ( $ n = 4, 6 $ , and 8) perovskite films display smaller blueshifted absorption edges than BA ( $ n = 4 $ ) due to the smaller size of Gly $ ^{+} $ . By comparing their absorption intensity, it was found that Gly ( $ n = 4, 6 $ , and 8) perovskite films show significantly enhanced absorption intensities, in comparison with BA ( $ n = 4 $ ), which indicates that the incorporation of Gly $ ^{+} $  is conducive to the formation of low-dimensional perovskite films. Figure 3b exhibited the Tauc plots from the UV–vis spectra to gain the optical  $ E_{\mathrm{g}} $ , the  $ E_{\mathrm{g}} $  of Gly ( $ n = 4, 6 $ , and 8) perovskite films are 1.575 eV ( $ \approx $ 787 nm), 1.572 eV ( $ \approx $ 789 nm), and 1.570 eV ( $ \approx $ 790 nm), respectively. Compared to BA ( $ n = 4 $ ) (1.579 eV,  $ \approx $ 785 nm), there are evident redshifted, which was suitable for the light absorption layer.

In Figure 3c, the steady-state PL peaks of BA ( $ n = 4 $ ) and Gly ( $ n = 4 $ , 6, and 8) perovskite films show consistent position changes with their absorption edges. The lower quenching of steady-state PL was observed on the sample of Gly ( $ n = 4 $ , 6, and 8) perovskite films (The samples were prepared on TiO $ _2 $ ), which demonstrates that the more light-excited electrons are rapidly transferred to the TiO $ _2 $  layer and there are fewer light-excited electrons and holes are used to generate PL, and indicating that the perovskite film has fewer defects with higher quality. Therefore, the defects of Gly ( $ n = 4 $ ) perovskite film with higher quality are notably lower than that of BA ( $ n = 4 $ ) film. We applied transient absorption (TA) spectra to probe the effect of the enhanced film quality on the interfacial charge recombination and normalized TA responses of BA ( $ n = 4 $ ) and Gly ( $ n = 4 $ , 6, and 8) perovskite films displayed in Figure 3d. According to the normalized TA responses, a single exponential decay function ( $ \gamma = A^* \exp(-x/t) + \gamma_0 $ ) was used for fitting to gain the detailed fitted carrier lifetime. The carrier lifetimes are estimated to be 66.1, 85.9, 91.5, and 96.3 ns for BA ( $ n = 4 $ ) and Gly ( $ n = 4 $ , 6, and 8) perovskite films, respectively. The slower decay lifetime indicates that it has a faster carrier transport rate and reduced interfacial charge recombination, and the carrier lifetimes of Gly ( $ n = 4 $ , 6, and 8) perovskite films are significantly enhanced in contrast to BA ( $ n = 4 $ ), suggesting their higher film quality.

At present, the main obstacle to the practical application of PSCs is the instability of perovskite materials and low-dimensional RP perovskites generally have satisfactory stability. Therefore, the humidity, thermal and UV light stability of Gly-based perovskite devices were studied comprehensively. First, we compared the humidity stability of the 3D, BA (n = 4), and Gly (n = 4) perovskite devices. Figure 6a exhibits the normalized PCE variation curves of unsealed 3D, BA (n = 4), and Gly (n = 4) perovskite devices under about 50% RH. Notably, during about 2000 h of humidity aging process, the 3D perovskite device displays poorest stability with rapidly decreased PCE, which finally only retains 30% of the initial value. The PCE of BA (n = 4) PSC slowly declines to about 75% and the normalized PCE of Gly (n = 4) perovskite device is stable of over 94%, which suggests much stronger resistance to humidity.

Moreover, we also verified the humidity resistance of the Gly-based low-dimensional perovskite materials by water contact angle test and XRD patterns before and after aging under about 50% RH. In Figure S12 (Supporting Information) and Figure 6b, we gave the water contact angles of the 3D, BA (n = 4), and Gly (n = 4, 6, and 8) perovskite films. Compared with that of 3D perovskite (61.5°) and BA (n = 4) (74.8°), the water contact angles of the Gly-based perovskite films augment greatly and exceed 88°, revealing their strongly hydrophobic performance. In Figure 6d, the 3D and BA (n = 4) perovskite all exhibits obvious PbI₂ after being placed in 50% RH for 1800 h. Surprisingly, even after humidity aging for 1800 h, only a very weak PbI₂ peak could be observed for the Gly (n = 4) perovskite film. It indicates that the Gly (n = 4) low-dimensional RP perovskite has admirable hydrophobicity and the incorporation of Gly⁺ is helpful to improve the humidity stability of PSCs.

Besides the humidity stability, the self-addition effect of Gly  $  ^{+}  $  also has a positive influence on thermal stability. The thermal stability of the low-dimensional RP perovskite device was measured by tracking their PCE variations and the absorption spectra (Figure 6f,g). After 240 h of thermal aging test at 85 °C, the 3D device shows a rapid degradation with a maximum loss up to 75% of the initial PCE. In contrast, over 80% retention of PCE for the Gly (n = 4) perovskite device can be gained after thermal aging. The change of absorption characteristic of three films further confirmed that the Gly (n = 4) perovskite owns better thermal stability than the 3D and BA (n = 4) perovskite. Compared with the Gly (n = 4) perovskite, the absorption intensities of the 3D and BA (n = 4) perovskite films decline significantly, indicating that the perovskite films have been degraded after enduring the high temperature. Whereas, the Gly (n = 4) perovskite film retains 85% of the original absorption.

In addition, we have also tested the UV light stability of three PSCs. Normalized PCE variation curves of unsealed 3D, BA (n = 4), and Gly (n = 4) perovskite devices are executed under constant UV light. In Figure 6h, it can be seen that the PCEs of the three PSCs significantly decrease. After aging under UV light for 20 h, the Gly (n = 4) perovskite device can maintain 66% of the initial PCE, whereas, the PCE of the 3D and BA (n = 4) PSCs decrease to 20% and 37% of the starting value. In Figure 6i, the images of 3D, BA (n = 4), and Gly (n = 4) perovskite films before and after aging under continuous UV light are recorded. During the aging process, the 3D perovskite film decomposes seriously, the BA (n = 4) film is partially degradation, but the Gly (n = 4) perovskite film is well-maintained morphology, revealing its high illumination stability. Undoubtedly, the overall results indicate that it is beneficial to improve the long-term stability of PSCs simultaneously by preparing self-additive low-dimensional RP perovskites.

According to the above test results, we proposed the possible formation process and mechanism of Gly-based low-dimensional RP perovskite mechanism process for the improved stability. First, after the Gly-based perovskite precursor solution is spin-coated onto the substrate, most of the  $ \mathrm{Gly^{+}} $  will combine with 3D perovskite to form a 2D perovskite structure, whereas, -COOH in a small amount of  $ \mathrm{Gly^{+}} $  will interact with  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  and become a nucleation center, which makes the Gly-based low dimensional RP perovskite around the  $ \mathrm{Gly^{+}} $  slow and uniform grow to obtain the superior crystal structure (Figure 1a) and large grain sizes (Figure 2), as shown in Figure 7c. In addition, a small amount of  $ \mathrm{Gly^{+}} $  not inserted into the perovskite lattice will stay at the crystal boundary to fill the trap sites and reduce the trap density (Figures 3c and 5b), as shown in Figure 7d. Therefore, the high-quality films are obtained, the photoelectric performances of Gly-based low-dimensional RP perovskite are significantly improved and the PCE of final Gly-based PSCs is much higher than that of BA-based perovskite device.

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, we first presented a new method to prepare self-additive low-dimensional RP perovskites with high PCE and

stability by the incorporation of glycine salt. The self-additive effect of Gly  $  ^{+}  $  was confirmed by the quantitative analysis of XRD patterns, and the strong interaction between C=O and Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  was revealed by XPS spectra, which provides the nucleation sites for the growth of Gly-based low-dimensional perovskite with larger grain sizes. Using the BA (n = 4) perovskite as contrast, the Gly-based perovskites with self-additive effect have better film-quality, leading to the PCE of Gly (n = 8) devices significantly increase from 10.79% for BA (n = 4) to 18.06% with negligible hysteresis. In addition, the Gly-based PSCs exhibit superior moisture, heat, and UV light stability. After aging under 50% RH for 1920 h, the unencapsulated Gly (n = 4) device maintains over 94% of its initial PCE. The Gly (n = 4) device without encapsulation can, respectively, retain 80% and 66% of the initial PCEs of after 240 h at 85 °C and 20 h under continuous UV light. Our work provides a new direction for obtaining more efficient and stable low-dimensional RP PSCs.
