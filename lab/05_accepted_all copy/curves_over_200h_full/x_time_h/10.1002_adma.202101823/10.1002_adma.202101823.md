# 1. Introduction

Hybrid organic-inorganic halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have received widespread attention due to their superb performances in the field of renewable energy resources,  $  ^{[1]}  $   and their power conversion efficiency (PCE) has increased from 3.8% to 25.5%.  $  ^{[2-7]}  $   Such rapid progress is owing to the

In 2014, the first work on 2D perovskites with a PCE of 4.73% was reported, where   $  (\mathrm{PEA})_{2}(\mathrm{MA})_{2}\mathrm{Pb}_{3}\mathrm{I}_{10}  $   (PEA = phenethylammonium) was adopted as an active layer with notably improved stability.  $  ^{[18]}  $   Since then, great efforts have been devoted to film processing, additive engineering, and organic spacer ligand selection to improve the performance of 2D PSCs, with the PCEs increased to around 18%.  $  ^{[19,20]}  $   2D perovskites are generally divided into several different structures, including Ruddlesden–Popper (RPP), Dion–Jacobson (DJ), and alternating cations in the interlayer space.  $  ^{[21,22]}  $   The general formula of RPP and DJ families is   $  A^{\prime}_{2}A_{n-1}M_{n}X_{3n+1}  $   and   $  BA_{n-1}M_{n}X_{3n+1}  $  .  $  ^{[22-25]}  $   respectively, where A is general organic cation:   $  MA^{+}  $  or   $  FA^{+}  $  ,   $  A^{\prime}  $   is an aliphatic or aromatic alkylammonium organic spacer cation like butylammonium,  $  ^{[26]}  $   etc., B is a divalent organic cation like 1,4-diaminobutane, etc.  $  ^{[27]}  $   To date, most of researches on 2D perovskites have focused on the RPP and DJ families.  $  ^{[28,29]}  $   It was reported that the presence of organic cation layers brings about the formation of a quantum well structure with strong quantum confinement and dielectric confinement,  $  ^{[30-32]}  $   resulting in a wider bandgap and greater exciton binding energy.  $  ^{[33]}  $   However, the insertion of organic cations with long alkyl chain not only protect inorganic layers from moisture, but also improve structural stability via strong van del Waals interaction and Coulomb interaction forces between inserted organic cations and the   $  [PbI_{6}]^{4-}  $   units.  $  ^{[34]}  $   Unfortunately, the insulating organic cation layers suppress the charge transport between neighboring conductive inorganic layers.  $  ^{[35]}  $   Thus, 2D PSCs usually present relatively lower efficiency by comparing with 3D PSCs.

In view of the instability of the 3D perovskite and the low efficiency of the 2D perovskite, it is a prospective strategy to develop 2D/3D hybrid perovskite materials by combining the advantage of high efficiency of 3D perovskite and high stability of 2D perovskite.  $  ^{[36]}  $   The 2D perovskite positioned on the surface can coordinate the dangling bonds on the surface of the 3D perovskite, thereby significantly reducing the defect density and improving the interface charge extraction efficiency.  $  ^{[37]}  $   In addition, the 2D/3D framework can extend the lifetime of the devices by preventing the penetration of interfacial moisture. Up to now, three main strategies of phase engineering have been proven to effectively improve device performance, including the construction of 2D/3D bilayer structures, the formation of 2D/3D mixtures, and the generation of quasi-2D phases with increased n value. For instance, Liu and coworkers constructed 2D/3D   $  ((FEA)_{2}PbI_{4}/FAPbI_{3})  $   bilayer perovskites with enhanced interfacial hole extraction and suppressed nonradiative carrier recombination, enabling a PCE >22%.  $  ^{[38]}  $   Nazeeruddin's group fabricated 2D/3D mixtures with one-year stable devices and achieved good efficiency.  $  ^{[39]}  $   Besides, the Chen team introduced ThMA spacer cation into 3D perovskite, which formed a 2D/3D hybrid structure and obtained PSCs with excellent photovoltaic properties and intrinsic stabilities.  $  ^{[40]}  $   However, all the methods deliberately introduced 2D or quasi-2D phases into the 3D perovskite, and thereupon required multiple steps of sequential deposition and film post-processing, which not only increases the complexity of device preparation, but also reduces compatibility between 2D and 3D phases. Furthermore, the two-step method may be harmful to the underlying 3D perovskite surface, resulting in unnecessary interfacial doping and undesired interfacial band alignment, and may also introduce extra defects.  $  ^{[41]}  $   In contrast, the way to adjust the value of n is more suitable to obtain efficient and stable perovskite devices. For example, the Etgar team adjusted the n values to obtain quasi-2D perovskites (n = 40, 50, and 60), resulting in high open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) and PCE.  $  ^{[42]}  $   Quan et al. fabricated reduced dimensionality in the perovskite structure using the phenylethylamine hydroiodide as well, where low n values and high n values were studied.  $  ^{[34]}  $   It was shown that the quasi-2D perovskites with high n values could provide better stability than the 3D perovskite.

In this work, a hydrophobic ammonium salt 4-(trifluoromethyl) benzylamine (4TFBZA) was introduced into precursor solution of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  with high  $ n $  values ( $ n = 20 $ , 40, 60, 80) to form quasi-2D hybrid perovskite by one-step spin coating method. Since fluorinated molecules generally have relatively low surface energy, there is an upward molecular driving force produced by the different surface energy during the crystallization process and, as a result, upper gradient low-dimensional structure is formed spontaneously from top to bottom, which will be demonstrated. Due to the formation of upper gradient low-dimensional structure by incorporation of hydrophobic 4TFBZA, higher PCE, and better stability of devices are expected and will be verified.

# 2. Results and Discussion

In order to solve the issue of instability of 3D perovskite devices, hydrophobic 4TFBZA was introduced (Figure 1a) into

the perovskite (MAPbI $ _3 $ ) precursor solution to form a 2D structure by one-step spin coating method. Then it was found that the upper interface layer cannot be spin-coated (Figure S1, Supporting Information) when the value of  $ n $  is relatively low due to the hydrophobicity and strong polarity of 4TFBZA (Figure S2, Supporting Information). Hence the value of  $ n $  was increased from 2, 3, to 20, 40, 60, 80, thereupon the hole transport layer (spiro-OMeTAD dissolved in chlorobenzene) was successfully spin-coated. Owing to the low surface energy property of fluorinated molecules, an upper gradient low-dimensional structure could be formed spontaneously in high  $ n $ -value perovskite films, which will be demonstrated. More importantly, this structure was completed by one-step spin coating method (Figure 1b), which can eliminate the negative impacts of two-step method. The specific renderings of spontaneous upper gradient 2D structure are illustrated in Figure 1c,d. “Spontaneous upper gradient 2D structure” is abbreviated to “SUG-2D” herein.

To investigate the 2D structure, the UV–vis absorption spectra of the controlled 3D and SUG-2D films with different n values were tested. As shown in Figure 2a, the films with n = 2 and n = 3 exhibit multiple strong absorption peaks at   $  \approx  $  571 and   $  \approx  $  606 nm, corresponding to n = 2 and n = 3 phases, respectively, demonstrating that the introduction of 4TFBZA can indeed form a low-dimensional RPP perovskite. It is obvious that 2D RPP films with n = 2 and n = 3 have more than one characteristic absorption peak, which means that those quasi-2D RPP films are not single-phase, but are composed of a group of crystal grains with various n values. When the n value increased to 20, 60, and 80, the absorption peak is basically consistent with that of the controlled 3D perovskite layer because the content of the 2D structure of the perovskite is very little at this moment. Compared with the film with   $  n = \infty  $  , the absorption edge of low n values (n = 2, 3) is slightly blueshifted, but high n values (n = 20, 60, 80) did not show any offset of absorption edge, suggesting that for large n conditions, the quantum and dielectric confinement effects are not significant. Unsurprisingly, reducing the dimension of the perovskite structure leads to an increase in the electronic bandgap. The changed absorption edge with the dimensional adjustment can be observed from the absorption spectrum, which shows a higher-energy bandgap for lower n values.  $  ^{[43]}  $   This is consistent with the results in other reports in the literature.

Previous literature has reported that the introduction of fluorine atoms into the organic cations of perovskites may deform the crystal structure, thereby changing the photoelectric properties of perovskite film.  $  ^{[27]}  $   Thus, the crystal structure of controlled 3D and SUG-2D perovskite films was examined by using X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurement. As shown in Figure 2b, a series of low-angle diffraction peaks were observed in the XRD pattern of the perovskite films with low n value (n = 2, 3), which are ascribed to the typical (0k0) reflections from the layered structure with the aforementioned d-spacing enhancement. Basically, crystal plane spacing of the layered quasi-2D perovskites would decrease with increasing n values due to the existence of more PbI  $  _{6}  $   sheets. As a result, several low diffraction angle peaks in 2D perovskite films with low n value disappear gradually when the n value is higher. In order to preliminarily prove the existence of SUG-2D structure in the RPP perovskite, photoluminescence (PL) measurement was made by exciting the films from front side and back side. In Figure 2c, a dominant emission peak for the bulk phase was observed in the PL spectra of all films and no difference was found in the PL spectra of controlled 3D film no matter from front side or back side. However, in contrast to that spectra from back side, additional shoulder peaks for low-dimensional structure were found in the spectra of SUG-2D perovskite films when the film was excited from front side, implying the SUG-2D layer exist on the top side of the film.  $  ^{[27]}  $   Due to the presence of SUG-2D layer, the intensity of peak for the bulk phase is weaker (front side) than that obtained from back side. Moreover, a blueshift phenomenon was observed by comparing the emission peak position for the bulk phase of films (759, 765, 767, 768, 770 nm for n = 20, 40, 60, 80, and ∞, respectively), which is attributed to a reduced trap density within the SUG-2D film and the wider bandgap.

To manifest the formation of the upper gradient low-dimensional structure, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) testing at different depths of SUG-2D perovskite film ( $ n = 60 $ ) was conducted. Since only the 4TFBZA molecule contains fluorine, fluorine was selected as the characteristic element of the XPS test. As shown in Figure 2d, the F element content on the surface of the perovskite film is relatively higher, which proved that the content of 2D structure on the surface is relatively high. The surface of SUG-2D perovskite films was rinsed with isopropyl alcohol (4TFBZA is completely soluble in isopropyl alcohol) and then energy-dispersive spectrometry (EDS) testing was conducted. It could be found that the weight percentage of F was reduced from  $ 1.85\% $  to  $ 1.79\% $  (Table S1, Supporting Information), indicating the F content was mainly derived from 4TFBZA, which was involved in lattice construction, rather than other forms (Figure S4, Supporting Information). Furthermore, the content of F element was getting less and less with the progress of deeper etching, which indicated the formation of a gradient 2D structure. Meanwhile, the buried compound information was further detected by XPS to check the distribution of  $ 4\mathrm{TFBZA}^+ $  in the formed film. As expected, obvious F1s peak was detected on the surface of SUG-2D perovskite films (Figure 2e). F1s peaks at SUG-2D perovskite surface located at  $ 687~\mathrm{eV} $ , which is mainly originated from the  $ 4\mathrm{TFBZA}^+ $ . It is noted that F1s signal could be detected inside the film with the form of decreasing gradient, which implies a gradient distribution of  $ 4\mathrm{TFBZA}^+ $ . Concurrently, the in-depth XPS of SUG-2D perovskite films deposited by  $ 2000~\mathrm{rpm} $  was measured. It was found that the same SUG-2D structure was formed at different thickness (Figure S5, Supporting Information). In order to accurately show the phenomenon of the upper 2D structure, X-ray diffraction measurements were carried out at different incident angles ( $ 0.01^{\circ}, 0.05^{\circ}, 0.1^{\circ}, 0.5^{\circ}, 1^{\circ} $ , and  $ 2^{\circ} $ ). The small incident angle represents the crystal structure information of the surface, while the large incident angle represents the internal crystal structure information. To show more intuitively,

perovskite film ( $ n = 3 $ ) was applied to the related tests. As shown in Figure S6, Supporting Information, the results show that the upper 2D structure was observed when the 4TFBZA was introduced. Meanwhile, with the increase of X-ray incident angles, the signal intensity of upper 2D characteristic peak gradually decreases, indicating the trend of SUG-2D structure. Combined with the previous data, it is sufficient to demonstrate the existence of the upper 2D structure with the effect of fluorinated molecule. The surface energy of fluorinated molecules with strong polarity is relatively small because of the fluorine atom. When the fluorine atom is combined with other elements, fluorine atom almost grabs its electrons and becomes a stable structure of the outermost 8 electrons. Therefore, it is difficult for other groups to interact with fluorinated molecules by the van der Waals force, leading to its low surface energy. $ ^{[44]} $  Hence there is an upward molecular driving force produced by the different surface energy during the crystallization process and, as a result, upper low-dimensional structure is formed. Furthermore, we measured the solubility of methylammonium iodide (MAI) (2.5 M) and 4TFBZA (2.5 M) in 1 mL anhydrous  $ N,N $ -dimethylformamide/dimethyl sulfoxide mixture solvent (4/1, v/v). MAI dissolved completely, while 4TFBZA only partially dissolved after shaking of 24 h (Figure S7, Supporting Information). Such a difference in solubility results in inhomogeneous crystallization of compositions, which would promote the formation of compositional gradients. $ ^{[45]} $  It has been reported that nucleation of RPP 2D perovskite occurs at the liquid–air interface, and surface tension makes nucleation and growth at the liquid–air interface of perovskite precursor solution more favorable. $ ^{[46]} $  Consequently, due to the different solubility, 4TFBZA $ ^{+} $  precipitates prior to MA $ ^{+} $  in the top-down crystallization process of perovskite to form the upper 2D perovskite, and the structure of gradient distribution is formed with the continuous decrease of 4TFBZA $ ^{+} $ .

To illustrate the topographic change of the SUG-2D perovskite films, top-view scanning electron microscopy (SEM) measurement was performed. Compared with the controlled 3D film, the SUG-2D perovskite films ( $ n = 20 $ ,  $ 40 $ ,  $ 60 $ ,  $ 80 $ ) has a significantly different surface and displays a typical polycrystalline perovskite morphology (Figure 3a). Smaller grains appear on the surface of the SUG-2D perovskite films with the stacked crystal grains and the grain boundaries become less obvious. According to previous literature reports, due to the annealing progressing and the solvent volatilizing, the colloidal particles containing 4TFBZA will crystallize first to form a 2D structure, and this process occurs from top to bottom, thus forming the upper gradient low-dimensional structure. SEM images with high magnification (Figure S8, Supporting Information) also show this point. When the content of 4TFBZA gets less (higher  $ n $  value), the content of 2D structure gets less and typical 2D perovskite morphology becomes less obvious. At the same time, the element distribution on the surface of the film was characterized through the EDS test (Figure 3b and Figure S9, Supporting Information). It can be seen that with the increased value of  $ n $ , the F element content on the surface of the film reduced from  $ 1.6\% $  to  $ 1.1\% $  (Table S2, Supporting Information), which also indicates the existence of 4TFBZA on the surface of the SUG-2D films to a certain extent. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted under dark conditions to investigate the interface charge transport and charge recombination in devices. For the fresh perovskite film, SUG-2D perovskite film presents the most hydrophobic surface among three samples due to the hydrophobic 4TFBZA (Figure 6a; and Figure S13, Supporting Information). In order to compare the moisture stability of three types of devices, all the samples were stored in air with a relative humidity of 55–65% at 25 °C. As shown in Figure 6b, strong (110) diffraction peaks (at   $  2\theta = 13.98^{\circ}  $  ) characteristic of the perovskite structure are observed for all fresh films. Correspondingly, the color of the aged 3D and BZA perovskite films faded from dark brown to yellow, while the color of the aged 4TFBZA-based 2D perovskite

films shows no difference after 45 days (Figure 6d). Since the 4TFBZA-based 2D perovskite film exhibit better moisture resistance, a better moisture stability of the corresponding device is expected. So, corresponding devices were fabricated with the architecture of ITO/SnO  $  _{2}  $  /perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD/ultrathin Ag (10 nm) for moisture stability test by being stored in air with a relative humidity of 55–65% at 25 °C. As shown in Figure S14 (Supporting Information), after 10 days, many spots were observed on the surface of 3D-based and BZA-based devices and the photos of corresponding aged devices show obvious degradation. By comparison, the aged 4TFBZA-based devices did not show any spots on surface even under higher magnification. It is proved that the hydrophobic 4TFBZA-based perovskite has superior moisture resistance, which is due to fluorine compounds with excellent hydrophobic properties.  $  ^{[50]}  $   It can be seen from Figure S15 (Supporting Information) that surface morphology of BZA-based perovskite film did not show 2D structure characteristic due to no molecular force caused by the fluorinated part, which is consistent with the results of the above stability test. Since stability is an important issue in the commercial application of PSCs, we measured the long-term stability of the controlled 3D-, BZA-, and 4TFBZA-based 2D perovskite devices. As shown in Figure 6e, 4TFBZA-based 2D devices exhibit better long-term stability than that of 3D and BZA-based 2D devices. Especially, unencapsulated SUG-2D devices remained nearly 84% of its original efficiency after being stored in the nitrogen atmosphere for 1000 h, while the PCE of the 3D reference BZA-based 2D counterpart devices dropped down to 5% and 10%, respectively.

# 3. Conclusions

An efficient and stable device based on quasi-2D perovskites in MAPbI $ _3 $  with a spontaneously formed upper gradient 2D structure via introducing hydrophobic 4TFBZA through a one-step spin-coating method was demonstrated successfully. Compared with 3D perovskite film, the SUG-2D perovskite film possessed lower trap density, leading to the reduction of nonradiative recombination loss. Consequently, the SUG-2D-based device exhibits a PCE of 17.07% with a high  $ V_{OC} $  of 1.10 V and a notable FF of 71%. Due to the stable SUG-2D structure with hydrophobic 4TFBZA, the SUG-2D perovskite film and corresponding device exhibit good thermal stability at 85 °C in N $ _2 $  and good moisture stability in air with a humidity of 60 ± 5%. Eventually, the unencapsulated devices show no obvious decomposition and ion migration. These results reveal an excellent photovoltaic property and inherent stability of the quasi-2D hybrid perovskites with 4TFBZA as the spacer cation.
