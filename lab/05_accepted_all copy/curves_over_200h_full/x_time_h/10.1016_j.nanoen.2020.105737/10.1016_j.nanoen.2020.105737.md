# 1. Introduction

Flexible perovskite solar cells (F-PSCs) are of great interest because of their flexibility, light weight, portability, and compatibility with various form factors including curved surface, and low fabrication cost via roll-to-roll processes   $  [1-7]  $  . The major difference of F-PSCs with rigid PSCs is the flexibility of substrate itself. Among the various candidates of flexible substrates, polyethylene terephthalate (PET) or polyethylene naphthalate (PEN) have been widely used because of their good chemical resistance, high transmittance in visible range, low thermal expansion coefficient, light weight and low cost   $  [8-11]  $  . However, the processing temperature of F-PSCs based on PET is limited under 150 °C, due to a low glass transition temperature of PET even though heat-stabilization techniques enable slight increase of processing temperature.

Despite of enormous potential in niche applications, flexible perovskite solar cells (F-PSCs) still suffer from low power conversion efficiency (PCE) compared to rigid PSCs largely because of the reduced photocurrent driven by low transmittance of their flexible substrate and transparent electrode. This paper presents a plasma-polymerized-fluorocarbon (PPFC) thin film as an antireflection (AR) coating material for enhancing the efficiency of F-PSCs. The PPFC, which are deposited on flexible polyethylene terephthalate (PET) substrates at low temperature by a mid-range frequency sputtering process, are highly transparent because of their amorphous structure. Due to the very low refractive index (  $  \sim  $   1.38) of PPFC over a wide wavelength range, these thin films decrease the average reflectance by 2.22% and increase the average transmittance in the visible light region by 1.40%. As a result, the AR films increase the PCE of F-PSCs from 18.6% to 20.4%. The thin films have a self-cleaning effect due to their hydrophobic surface, excellent mechanical flexibility (bending radius: 4 mm), and high chemical stability. Accordingly, the thin films improve the long-term stability of F-PSCs in humid environments. Finally, these AR PPFC thin films can be manufactured by a roll-to-roll process, making them suitable for future use in highly efficient F-PSCs.

An antireflection (AR) layer might be able to overcome this issue, by reducing reflection loss of incident light at the interface between substrate and air to induce more light transmission through the substrate   $  [24–26]  $  . Although AR coatings have been previously employed for rigid organic and perovskite photovoltaics (PV), only few studies have examined AR techniques in F-PSCs.

Single-layer AR coating is the simplest and least expensive way to improve the efficiency of F-PSCs. A multilayer thin film structure would further enable broadband AR over a wide wavelength range owing to the internal reflection effect, but it requires much processing time, cost, and a very precise design for the thin film substrate to solve the interlayer stress   $  [27–30]  $  . Nanostructures with a gradient refractive index can effectively minimize reflection on the surface   $  [31–35]  $  . However, they are not easy to fabricate directly on the PET substrate, and there is only little study on the efficacy when the nanostructured film is attached to F-PSCs. In addition, the complexity of the nanostructure manufacturing process and the durability of the fabricated film are challenges that remain to be addressed   $  [36,37]  $  . Porous   $  SiO_{2}  $   and   $  TiO_{2}  $   have very low refractive indices, but their application to flexible substrates is difficult because of the high processing temperatures   $  [38,39]  $  .

In comparison, the deposition of a single-layer AR coating on one side of the PET substrate is quite simple and suitable for F-PSCs, because continuous large-area manufacturing is possible with roll-to-roll processing. To maximize the AR effect of a single thin film layer, the coating thickness must be equal to  $ \lambda /4 $  ( $ \lambda $  is the wavelength of the incident light), and the refractive index should satisfy the equation  $ \mathfrak{n}_{film} = \sqrt{\mathfrak{n}_{air}\cdot\mathfrak{n}_{substrate}} $ , where  $ \mathfrak{n}_{film},\mathfrak{n}_{air} $ , and  $ \mathfrak{n}_{substrate} $  are the refractive indices of the AR coating film, air, and substrate, respectively [40]. For a PET substrate, the ideal refractive index and thickness of the AR coating in the visible wavelength range should be approximately 1.30 and  $ 138~\mathrm{nm} $ , respectively. Although a material with exactly  $ \mathfrak{n}_{film} = 1.30 $  does not exist in nature,  $ \mathrm{MgF_2} $  ( $ \mathfrak{n}_{film} = 1.38 $ ) is widely used in low-refractive index coatings and AR films. However, the  $ \mathrm{MgF_2} $  thin film requires a high-temperature process reaching  $ 300^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ ; else, its optical and mechanical properties will deteriorate. Although  $ \mathrm{MgF_2} $  had been tested as an AR layer for F-PSCs, the device efficiency only displayed limited improvement [8].

Herein, we propose a plasma-polymerized-fluorocarbon (PPFC) thin film as a single-layer AR coating material to compensate for the low light transmittance and enhance the PCE of F-PSCs. The PPFC thin film is produced by decomposing a fluorine-based polymer such as polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) in vacuum and recombination on a substrate   $  [41, 42]  $  . It is composed of the same units of CF,   $  CF_{2}  $  , and   $  CF_{3}  $   as fluorine-based polymers, but has an amorphous structure and exhibits very high transparency and flexible properties   $  [43–45]  $  . In particular, PPFC thin films deposited using a mixed target of carbon nanotubes (CNTs)-PTFE in a large-area roll-to-roll process display a very low

refractive index of 1.38 (similar to that of  $ \mathrm{MgF_2} $ ), as well as a hydrophobic surface that imparts the self-cleaning effect. These PPFC thin films are very suitable surface coating materials for F-PSCs. This work demonstrates that such a single-layer PPFC AR film enhances the PCE of F-PSCs efficiently, from  $ 18.6\% $  to  $ 20.4\% $ . In addition, the mechanical reliability, stability in acid/base solutions, and long-term stability of the F-PSCs in humid environments are evaluated.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1. Preparation of PPFC thin film

2.1.1. Fabrication of sputtering target

Multiwall carbon nanotube (CNT) powder (K-nanos 200 P, particle size 5–15   $  \mu  $  m, KKPC) and polytetrafluorethylene (PTFE) powder (TF-1750, particle size 25   $  \mu  $  m, Dyneon) were mixed in three weight ratios (1:99, 5:95, and 10: 90). The mixed powders were placed in a mold, pressed at 200 kgf cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and heat-treated at 375 °C to prepare the CNT-PTFE bulk compound. The prepared composite target had the form of a disk with a diameter of 4 in and a thickness of 6 mm.

2.1.2. Fabrication of PPFC thin film

Plasma-polymerized-fluorocarbon (PPFC) thin films on polyethylene terephthalate (PET) and PET/ indium tin oxide (ITO) films were fabricated by sputtering with dual cathode mid-range frequency (MF) power (PE II, Advanced Energy) using the CNT–PTFE composite target. The target-to-substrate distance was maintained at 240 mm, and the frequency and power of the MF power supply were 40 kHz and 120 W, respectively. The sputtering chamber was evacuated to a base pressure of   $  5.0 \times 10^{-6}  $   Torr by a mechanical pump and a cryogenic pump. The working pressure during sputtering was   $  1.5 \times 10^{-3}  $   Torr. Pure Ar gas was used as the sputtering gas, and its flow rate was controlled by a mass flow controller. The thickness of the deposited PPFC thin films was tuned to 80, 100, and 120 nm by adjusting the coating time.

2.1.3. Characterization of PPFC thin film

Optical modeling was performed using the Essential Macleod program. The wavelength-dependent refractive index and extinction coefficients of PPFC and   $  MgF_{2}  $   in optical modeling were obtained by analyzing each thin film deposited on a slide glass using ellipsometry (Ellipsometer, Elli-Se-Uam12, Ellipsotech). The structure and elemental composition of the layers were investigated by field-emission transmission electron microscopy (FE-TEM, Tecnai G2 F30 S-TWIN, FEI Co.). The specimens were prepared using microtoming. Chemical analysis of the PPFC thin film on the PET substrate was conducted using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, AXIS NOVA, Kratos) with Al-Kα radiation and an acceleration voltage of 15 keV. The optical transmittance and reflectance were measured in the wavelength range of 400–1200 nm using an optical spectrometer (U-4100, Hitachi).

The surface energy of the PPFC thin film was calculated from the water contact angle (WCA), which was measured with a contact angle analyzer (PHOENIX 300 touch, Surface Electro Optics). The deionized (DI) water droplets were  $ 5\mu \mathrm{L} $  in volume, and their photographs were systematically analyzed three times. The durability of PPFC/PET films was determined by accelerated life tests and mechanical bending tests. The mechanical flexibility in outer bending was examined using a bending test system (JIRBT-610, JUNIL TECH) and characterized by the bending radius. The surface energy was measured after another bending test, in which the bending radius was reduced from 20 to  $ 2\mathrm{mm} $ . The accelerated life of the films was evaluated by observing the change in surface energy over 7 days, after placing several samples in a thermohygrostat (TH-TG-408, Jeio Tech) at  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  and  $ 85\% $  relative humidity and analyzing one each day. In the acid and base tests, the PPFC/PET film was immersed in  $ 1\mathrm{M} $  HCl or NaOH solution in a beaker for  $ 1\mathrm{min} $ . # 2.2. Perovskite solar cells

2.2.1.Materials

$ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  nanoparticles (NPs,  $ 15\mathrm{wt}\% $  aqueous dispersion) were purchased from Alfa Aesar. Formamidinium iodide (FAI,  $ \geq 99\% $ ) and methylammonium bromide (MABr,  $ \geq 99\% $ ) were purchased from GreatcellSolar Ltd. Lead (II) iodide (99.999%) and lead (II) bromide (99.999%) were purchased from TCI. N2',N2,N2,N2,N7',N7',N7',N7'-octakis(4-methoxyphenyl)-9,9-spirobi[9 H-fluorene]-2,2,7,7-tetramine (Spiro-OMeTAD,  $ 99\% $ ) and Tris(2-(1 H -pyrazol-1-yl)-4-tert-butylpyridine) - cobalt(III) tris (bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide)) ( $ >99\% $ , FK209 Co(III) TFSI) were purchased from LumTec. All other chemicals and organic solvents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

# 2.2.2. F-PSCs fabrication

The ITO-coated PET substrate had an ITO thickness of 50 nm and a sheet resistance of   $  75 \Omega sq^{-1}  $  . The PET/ITO film substrate was subjected to sequential ultrasonic cleaning for 15 min in each of the following: a detergent, DI water, ethanol, and 2-propanol. The cleaned substrate was exposed to a UV/O  $  _{3}  $   atmosphere (UVC-150, Omniscience) for 30 min to improve the wettability. As an electron-transporting layer, SnO  $  _{2}  $   NPs were diluted by DI water (1:4 v/v) and spin-coated on the substrate at 3000 rpm for 30 s. The films were then annealed at 100 °C for 30 min. For the perovskite layer, 1.4 M of FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  , 0.07 M of MAPbBr  $  _{3}  $  , and MACl solution dissolved in 0.8 mL of dimethylformamide (DMF) and 0.1 mL of DMSO was used. After the UV/O  $  _{3}  $   treatment, the perovskite precursor solution was spin-coated in sequential steps of 500 and 1000 rpm for 3 s each and 5000 rpm for 20 s. Before the end of the procedure, diethyl ether was poured on the substrate. The resulting films were then annealed at 100 °C for 1 h. Spiro-OMeTAD solution in CB (0.889 g mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) with 23 μL of Li-TFSI (540 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   in acetonitrile), 39 μL of tBP, and 10 μL of FK209 (0.376 g mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was spin-coated as a hole-transporting layer at 2000 rpm for 30 s. The Au electrode was formed by thermal evaporation with a shadow mask to define a cell area of 0.096 cm  $  ^{2}  $  .

2.2.3. F-PSCs characterization

The current density - voltage  $ (J - V) $  curves were obtained using a solar simulator (Newport, Oriel Class AAA) equipped with a voltage source meter (Keithley 2400) under  $ 100\mathrm{mWcm}^{-2} $  light illumination with AM  $ 1.5\mathrm{G} $  standard condition. The light intensity was calibrated before each measurement using a Si-reference cell certified by NREL

(USA). The EQE was measured using a QUANTX-300 QE measurement system (Oriel Instruments). The stability test was conducted by storing samples in a chamber controlled at 25 °C and 65% RH and measuring the J-V curves at regular time intervals. Next, to verify the mechanical durability of the PSCs, bending tests was performed for multiple cycles with a bending radius of 15 mm.

# 3. Results and discussion

The composite CNT–PTFE sputtering targets were prepared by a high-temperature compression molding method using mixed CNT/PTFE powder, as shown in Fig. 1a. With their good conductivity and high aspect ratio, the CNTs can easily transport electrons to the polymer sputtering target. The PPFC thin film can be deposited by a mid-range frequency (MF) sputtering process. Compared to RF sputtering, MF sputtering is able to manufacture large-area films in a roll-to-roll process (Fig. 1b). The thickness of the PPFC film coating on PET was confirmed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and elemental mapping confirmed that fluorine was uniformly distributed in the PPFC thin film (Fig. 1c and d).

PTFE is a well-known fluoropolymer composed of   $  -CF_{2}-  $   units, and has a very low refractive index of 1.38. Although the fluorine atom has a high electronegativity and very strong reactivity, fluorocarbons are very stable and their stability increases with the content of fluorine [46]. The C–F bonds have a short length owing to the small radius of the fluorine atom, and therefore, they have high binding energy due to the high electronegativity of fluorine [47]. These fluoropolymer materials are non-polar, and their bonding electrons are strongly attracted to the nuclei and do not easily react with the external electric field or light. This accounts for the low refractive index of fluorocarbons. Unfortunately, PTFE is opaque in the visible region due to its crystalline structure and is unsuitable for optical applications. When PTFE is fabricated into a thin film using plasma, however, it becomes very transparent. The effect of composition ratio of the CNT–PTFE targets on the refractive index of the PPFC thin films was investigated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Fig. 1e shows the XPS data of samples fabricated using targets containing 1, 5, and 10 wt% CNTs. The C 1 s spectra show broad peaks; four prominent Gaussian deconvolution peaks could be conventionally assigned as follows for the fluorocarbon thin films:   $  \underline{C-F_{3}}  $   (294.0 eV),   $  \underline{C-F_{2}}  $   (292.0 eV),   $  \underline{C-F}  $   (290.0 eV), and   $  \underline{C-CF_{n}}  $   (287.4 eV). An additional peak at 285.2 eV represents   $  \underline{C-(C,H)}  $   (285.2 eV) surface contamination. The intensities of the   $  C-CF_{n}  $   peaks increased as the CNT content increased from 1 to 10 wt%, whereas that of the   $  C-F_{2}  $   and   $  C-F_{3}  $   peaks decreased. Fig. 1f shows the calculated refractive index of the PPFC thin films from the ellipsometry measurement in the visible-near infrared (NIR) wavelength range. As the carbon content in the thin film increases, the refractive index tends to increase. The sample produced using the 1 wt% CNT composite target (and therefore a larger amount of fluorine) shows a very low refractive index of 1.387 at 550 nm (similar to that of PTFE) and exhibits excellent transparency. More importantly, because its refractive index is very consistent from the visible to NIR light region, broadband AR can be achieved with only a single layer. Fig. 1f also displays the refractive index of the   $  MgF_{2}  $   thin film deposited by RF sputtering at low temperature. While its refractive index near 500 nm (1.38) is similar to that of PPFC, it increases as the wavelength increases. Thus, PPFC is very suitable for solar cells that require high transmittance over a wide wavelength range, such as for tandem structures composed of heterogeneous solar cells.

Fig. 1g shows the transmittance and reflection of a  $ 100\mathrm{nm} $  PPFC thin film deposited on a PET substrate using a target containing  $ 1\mathrm{wt}\% $  CNTs. Prior to this measurement, the desired thickness range of the PPFC thin film was determined through optical simulation, by inputting the measured optical constants from ellipsometry analysis into the Macleod simulation program (Fig. S1a, Table S1). Fig. 2a schematically shows the optical structure of PPFC coated on PET/ITO. The ITO side is denoted as the front side and the PET side as the backside, considering the light direction and the fabrication order of the F-PSCs. The structure of PPFC/PET/ITO has a gradient refractive index that increases from the incident medium (air, n = 1) to the ITO. When PPFC with a very low refractive index is coated on PET/ITO, it reduces external reflection, and light at the interfaces of PPFC/PET and PET/ITO is internally re-reflected through the designed multilayer structure to enhance the transmission. This is consistent with the method of implementing the AR effect by creating a gradient refractive index with nanostructures   $  [49,50]  $  .

Fig. 2b, c, and d display the wavelength-dependent transmittance and reflectance of the PET substrate, PET/ITO, and PPFC/PET/ITO, respectively. The transmittance of the PET substrate at  $ 550~\mathrm{nm} $  is  $ 88.5\% $  and the reflectance is  $ 11.0\% $ . Due to the symmetrical film structure, these values are not affected by the direction of incident light (Fig. 2b). PET/ITO exhibits different optical properties depending on the lighting direction. The reflectance from the front and back sides were  $ 13.2\% $  and  $ 12.7\% $  at  $ 550~\mathrm{nm} $ , respectively, with a  $ 0.5\% $  difference, and the transmittance was similar in the two directions (Fig. 2c). This phenomenon occurs because the first layer encountered by the incident light differs according to the light direction. Taking this into account, the optical design proceeded from the back side to the front side using the optical simulation program. Simulation of the PET/ITO (ITO thickness:  $ 50~\mathrm{nm} $ ) yielded maximum transmittance when the PPFC thin film had a thickness of  $ 100~\mathrm{nm} $  (Fig. S1b, Table S1). Considering possible disagreement between the experiment and simulation and in order to understand the effect of film thickness, the transmittance and reflectance were measured for PPFC/PET/ITO with different PPFC thicknesses of 80, 100, and  $ 120~\mathrm{nm} $ . The average transmittances in the visible region were  $ 85.0\% $ ,  $ 85.4\% $ , and  $ 85.5\% $ , and the average reflectances were  $ 10.3\% $ ,  $ 9.3\% $ , and  $ 9.7\% $ , respectively. Therefore, it was confirmed that the optical effect was maximized at a coating thickness of  $ 100~\mathrm{nm} $ . After coating the PET/ITO with  $ 100~\mathrm{nm} $  PPFC film, the average difference in the reflectance decreased by  $ 2.22\% $ , and the average difference in transmittance increased by  $ 1.40\% $  in the visible region, resulting in maximum light transmittance due to the AR effect. Fig. 2e shows the photographs of a PET substrate with/without the PPFC thin film, demonstrating the antireflection effect. Table 2 lists the average transmittance and reflectance values and their difference with and without the PPFC film in the visible range. For comparison, the  $ \mathrm{MgF_2} $  thin film was simulated in the same manner and experimentally deposited on a PET substrate with a thickness of  $ 120~\mathrm{nm} $  (Fig. S2a, Table S2). This, however, reduced both the transmittance and the reflectance (Fig. S2c, Table S3). Thus, the  $ \mathrm{MgF_2} $  thin film sputtered at low temperatures was not effective for the optical enhancement of PET/ITO (Fig. S2b and S2d, Table S2 and S3).

The PPFC thin film was employed to improve the efficiency of F-PSCs, which have the following structure: PPFC/PET/ITO/SnO  $  _{2}  $  NPs/(CH(NH  $  _{2}  $  )  $  _{2}  $  PbI  $  _{3}  $  )  $  _{0.95}  $  (CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}  $  PbBr  $  _{3}  $  )  $  _{0.05}  $  (FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  )  $  _{0.95}  $  (MAPbBr  $  _{3}  $  )  $  _{0.05}  $  /N2,

N2,N2,N2,N7',N7',N7',N7-octakis(4-methoxyphenyl)-9,9-spirobi [9 H-fluorene]-2,2,7,7-tetramine (Spiro-OMeTAD)/Au (Fig. 3a). The PPFC thin film was first formed on the backside of PET/ITO film, then F-PSCs were fabricated on the opposite side of the film. All the layers in the F-PSCs were successfully formed at low temperatures (not exceeding  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ ).

Introduction of the PPFC thin film improved the PCE of the F-PSCs, as presented in Fig. 3b and Table 3. In particular, the short-circuit current density (  $  J_{SC}  $  ) was consistently improved by using the PPFC thin film coating regardless of its thickness (Fig. 3c). The PPFC thin film effectively reduced the reflectance and improved the transmittance of the PET/ITO, thereby increasing light absorption in the perovskite layer, and finally enhanced the photocurrent in the F-PSCs. For example, when using the PPFC thin film (thickness: 80, 100, and 120 nm) as an AR coating, the average   $  J_{SC}  $   of the F-PSCs was improved from   $  22.9 \pm 0.2  $   to   $  23.3 \pm 0.1  $  ,   $  23.4 \pm 0.1  $  , and   $  23.4 \pm 0.1  $   mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , respectively.

The enhanced photocurrent in the F-PSCs with the PPFC thin film was also verified by measuring the external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra of F-PSCs without and with the PPFC thin film (Fig. 3d). All the F-PSCs with the PPFC thin film showed increased EQE in the entire visible range. The  $  J_{\mathrm{SC}}  $  values calculated by convolution of the EQE spectra with the AM 1.5 G solar spectrum were 23.2, 23.8, 23.8, and 23.8 mA cm $ ^{-2} $  for without and with PPFC films having thicknesses of 80, 100, and 120 nm, respectively. These  $  J_{\mathrm{SC}}  $  values from the EQE spectra are well matched to the average  $  J_{\mathrm{SC}}  $  values obtained from the current density – voltage ( $  J-V  $ ) curves. The optimized F-PSCs with the 100 nm PPFC thin film showed a PCE of 20.4% with negligible hysteresis in the  $  J-V  $  scan (Fig. 3e). This is the comparable to the best PCE value reported for F-PSCs [51–53].

PPFC thin films, which are composed of carbon and fluorine atoms, contain C-F, C-  $  F_{2}  $  , C-  $  F_{3}  $  , and C-  $  C_{n}  $   functional groups, as shown in Fig. 1e. The   $  CF_{3}  $   and   $  CF_{2}  $   units have low surface energies of   $  15 \times 10^{-3}  $   and   $  23 \times 10^{-3}  $   N m  $  ^{-1}  $  , respectively [54]. As a result, the PPFC thin film has a low surface energy similar to that of PTFE, and its surface is hydrophobic. The water contact angle (WCA) was measured to be approximately   $  105^{\circ}  $   or more. This hydrophobic property allows the surface of solar panels to self-clean when installed in an inclined manner. In fact, most solar panels already incorporate a low-surface-energy film for self-cleaning. Considering both, the excellent optical property and self-cleaning function, the PPFC thin film has optimal characteristics as the outermost coating film of a solar cell. Fig. 4a is a photograph of the F-PSCs with the hydrophobic PPFC thin film coating, and Fig. 4b demonstrates that surface contaminants or dust on the PPFC/PET can be spontaneously cleaned by adhering to water droplets.

Fig. 4c shows the bending results for evaluating the mechanical reliability of the sample coated with a 100 nm PPFC thin film on a PET substrate. The surface energy was monitored during the outer bending test as the bending radius was reduced from 20 to 2 mm in 2 mm intervals. Even at a bending radius of 4 mm, the surface energy of the substrate was at its initial value (  $  14.7 \times 10^{-3}  $  N m  $  ^{-1}  $  ), but slightly increased to  $  19.4 \times 10^{-3}  $  N m  $  ^{-1}  $  at a bending radius of 2 mm. Note that the surface energy of the PET film substrate itself gradually decreased from 8 mm, and such deterioration of the PET substrate may have affected the PPFC film and therefore increased the surface energy (Fig. S3a). However, the PPFC thin film coated on PET did not show distinct cracking up to 4 mm, whereas the ITO layer currently applied in F-PSCs showed a crack point of 10–15 mm depending on the thickness [55,56]. Consequently, the PPFC thin film is not expected to cause additional mechanical stress. Furthermore, a flexible transparent electrode is required to implement the F-PSCs. Even when using a flexible transparent electrode such as graphene, Ag-mesh, and single-wall CNTs, the PPFC thin film can be applied as the outermost AR coating layer without mechanical failure. Fig. 4d shows that the surface energy of the PPFC/PET surface did not change over 7 days in a hot and humid environment (85 °C, 85% relative humidity). Such strong acid or base solutions failed to dissolve or corrode the film surface, since the transmittance remained unchanged. The long-term stability of F-PSCs with and without the PPFC thin film was compared in a 25 °C, 65% RH environment (Fig. 4g). Transparent polymer substrates used for F-PSCs (including PET) typically show poor gas barrier property, especially against water vapor [58]. A coating of an additional gas blocking layer on the polymer substrate helps block the passage of water molecules through the substrate. In this case, the hydrophobic PPFC thin film (as shown in Fig. 4a) can impede the passage of water molecules through the substrate, thereby enhancing the long-term stability of F-PSCs in humid environments.

To examine the barrier effect of the PPFC thin film, we encapsulated the F-PSCs on the front side so that water vapor could only pass from the backside of the film, which is subject to a coating of PPFC film. Without the PPFC film, the average PCE of the F-PSCs decreased to  $ 57.8 \pm 9.7\% $  of the initial value. In contrast, that of the F-PSCs with the  $ 100\mathrm{nm} $  PPFC thin film retained  $ 80.6 \pm 6.0\% $  of the original PCE up to  $ 957\mathrm{h} $ . The PCE decay rate of F-PSCs with an AR coating under the humid environment is thus  $ 2.3\% / 100\mathrm{h} $ , which is significantly improved from that without the PPFC thin film ( $ 4.7\% / 100\mathrm{h} $ ). This improved long-term stability under humid environment is likely due to the PPFC thin film acting as a barrier against the penetration of water molecules through the substrate.

Finally, a bending test was conducted to investigate the mechanical reliability of F-PSCs with the PPFC thin film, which is important in various applications including portable/wearable devices (Fig. 4h). F-PSCs with the PPFC thin film showed almost identical PCE after 1000 cycles of bending with a radius of  $ 15\mathrm{mm} $ . Overall, the PPFC thin film developed in this study not only reliably improved the efficiency of the F-PSCs even after the bending test, but also enhanced the device stability against high humidity, which is a crucial factor in real operating conditions.

# 4. Conclusions

A transparent PPFC thin film with a very low refractive index of 1.38 was deposited on a PET substrate by scalable MF sputtering, and was found to significantly improve the efficiency and stability of F-PSCs. When using a  $ 100\mathrm{nm} $  PPFC thin film, the average transmittance in the visible region increased by  $ 3.23\% $ . When this film was deposited on PET/ITO, the average reflection decreased by  $ 2.22\% $  and the average transmittance increased by  $ 1.40\% $ , resulting in increased light transmittance due to the AR effect. Such an AR PPFC thin film greatly improved the PCE of F-PSCs from  $ 18.6\% $  to  $ 20.4\% $ . The latter is comparable to the highest reported PCE value of F-PSCs to date.

In addition to its excellent ability to enhance light transmission, the PPFC thin film had a hydrophobic surface and was suitable for removing surface contaminants. Moreover, the thin film was mechanically very flexible, its surface properties were maintained under harsh environmental conditions, and corrosion or dissolution did not occur even when treated with acid or base solutions. Therefore, this PPFC thin film is a

suitable outermost optical layer as well as a protective layer, enabling the F-PSCs to exhibit long-term stability in a humid environment and stability under mechanical bending. Considering their excellent broadband light enhancement characteristics, self-cleaning surface, environmental stability, flexibility, and applicability in a large-area roll-to-roll sputtering process, these PPFC thin films should be useful in not only F-PSCs but also flexible electronic devices in general.
