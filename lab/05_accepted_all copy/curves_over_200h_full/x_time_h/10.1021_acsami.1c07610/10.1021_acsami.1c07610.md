# Strong Electron Acceptor of a Fluorine-Containing Group Leads to High Performance of Perovskite Solar Cells

Xiaoli Gong, Haimin Li,  $  ^{*}  $   Ruonan Zhou, Xian Peng, Yukun Ouyang, Huxin Luo, Xingchong Liu, Jia Zhuang, Hanyu Wang, Yafei Ni, and Yue Lei

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Organic–inorganic hybrid perovskites have become one of the most promising thin-film solar cell materials owing to their remarkable photovoltaic properties. However, nonradiative recombination of carriers usually leads to inferior performance of perovskite (PVK) devices. Interface modification is one of the effective ways to improve separation of charges for perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Here, a small organic molecule of tetrafluorophthalonitrile (TFPN) is used to enhance the extraction and transportation of carriers at the PVK/hole transport layer (HTL) interface. The electron-rich C–F group effectively reduces the trap state density in the perovskite through chemical combination with the empty orbital of   $  Pb^{2+}  $   or other electron traps on the PVK surface, resulting in enhanced interface contact between the PVK and HTL. Meanwhile, the C≡N group in TFPN also inactivates the defects caused by   $  Pb^{2+}  $  . The Fermi level of the perovskite shifts by 0.15 eV to its valence band due to the strong electron acceptor nature of the F atom, indicating that positive dipoles and p-type doping emerge, which validly suppress the recombination of carriers for the PVK film. Therefore, the optimized PSC shows the highest power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 22.82% compared to 19.40% for the control one. The champion FF reaches up to 81.2% (PCE 21.44%) due to the effectively enhanced carrier separation. In addition, the unencapsulated device shows enhanced stability under air conditions.

KEYWORDS: perovskite, electron acceptor, interfacial modification, p-type doping, passivation

# INTRODUCTION

Nonradiative recombination is the main reason for charge loss, which largely determines the performance of PSCs.  $  ^{10,11}  $  To improve their performance and promote their development, great efforts have been made in optimizing the band gap, perovskite crystallization, defect passivation,

Meanwhile, the existence of defects increases the possibility of nonradiative recombination.  $  ^{23,24}  $   Therefore, optimization of the perovskite absorption layer and HTL to validly increase the separation of charges has become one of the research hotspots.  $  ^{17,25-27}  $

Generally, doping is considered to be one of the effective methods for improving the properties of perovskite solar cells.  $  ^{[28,29]}  $   However, as the doping concentration increases, bulk doping will cause performance degradation, which is attributed to the enhancement of carrier recombination. In the meantime, interface doping has a slight influence on the properties of PSCs. Selective doping of the interface layer is usually applied in silicon or other thin-film photovoltaic technologies to enhance charge extraction and reduce recombination at the interface.  $  ^{[30,31]}  $   There are few reports about p-type doping at the interface between PVK and HTL in organic–inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells mainly owing to its limited influence on carrier separation,  $  ^{[32-34]}  $   although interface modification is an effective strategy for improving the performance of PSCs. In 2019, a dilute solution of   $  \mathrm{Pb(SCN)_2}  $   and chlorinated graphene oxide were coated on the perovskite interface, forming strong Pb–Cl and Pb–O bonds, finally realizing stable and efficient perovskite solar cells.  $  ^{[34]}  $   Wang et al. studied a composite material as the interface layer, which not only improved the crystallization of the perovskite film but also inhibited carrier recombination.  $  ^{[35]}  $   In this study, a small organic molecule material tetrafluorophthalonitrile (TFPN) is used to modify the interface of the perovskite/hole transport layer. Being a strong electron acceptor, TFPN induces p-type doping at the interface, thus validly increasing the separation of carriers. It has been reported by some researchers that fluorine atoms would form chemical bonds with perovskites since halide

anions (X $  ^{-}  $ ) can interact with perovskites. $ ^{32,36-40} $  Here, the electron-rich group C–F in TFPN forms a chemical combination with the empty orbital of Pb $  ^{2+}  $ , which can effectively reduce the defect state density on the surface of the perovskite, and the C≡N group in TFPN can also passivate the defects caused by Pb $  ^{2+}  $ . At the same time, upon addition of TFPN as an interface layer, the Fermi level of the perovskite shifts by about 0.15 eV to its valence band, realizing p-type doping, thus inducing the formation of a positive dipole pointing toward the perovskite surface, enhancing the electric field effect at the interface, and making the extraction and transportation of holes more effective. $ ^{31} $  Under optimized concentration modification, the highest PCE reaches up to 22.82%,  $  V_{oc}  $  is 1.161 V,  $  J_{sc}  $  is 24.64 mA cm $  ^{-2}  $ , and FF is 79.8%, compared to the control one of 19.40% with  $  V_{oc}  $  of 1.107 V,  $  J_{sc}  $  of 23.5 mA cm $  ^{-2}  $ , and FF of 74.6%. Apart from this, the champion FF of 81.2% is obtained after TFPN modification due to the enhanced charge separation since FF is closely related to hole transportation. $ ^{42} $  In addition, the unencapsulated device shows enhanced stability in air conditions. When TFPN is dissolved in Spiro-OMeTAD, the C≡N group might interact with Li $  ^{+}  $ , thus leading to the deterioration of conductivity of the hole transport layer, and the devices exhibit poor photovoltaic performance, indicating that although TFPN would induce p-type doping, it is more suited to be an interface modification layer between PVK and HTL.

The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) intensity and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) of the perovskite film deposited on glass are measured to evaluate its carrier dynamics. Being the PVK/HTL interface layer, TFPN can penetrate the shallow surface of the perovskite and passivate the defects at the perovskite grain boundaries. As shown in Figure 1b, the perovskite film modified with  $ 4\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1} $  TFPN shows a much stronger fluorescence intensity. Correspondingly, Figure 1c demonstrates the TRPL spectra of PVK for original and  $ 4\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1} $  TFPN-modified films. By fitting the data with a double exponential attenuation model, the average photoluminescence decay time was calculated (data are shown in Table S1, and the calculation formula is shown in the Supporting Information). Compared with the control PVK film (275.58 ns), the film modified with the ultrathin TFPN layer shows a significantly increased carrier lifetime (609.30 ns), which indicates that because the recombination of charge carriers is effectively reduced, trap states and recombination centers are validly passivated.

The recombination of carriers is closely related to various defects in the perovskite film, and they have a profound influence on the extraction and transportation of carriers.  $  ^{45,46}  $   In this regard, Nyquist plot impedance spectroscopy is used to

study the kinetics of charge transportation and recombination in perovskite devices. The Nyquist plot and the corresponding equivalent circuit diagram (inset) are tested under the dark condition of   $  V_{mpp}  $  , as shown in Figure 1d. Generally speaking, there should be two arc distributions at higher and lower frequencies, representing the charge transport resistance (  $  R_{s}  $  ) and the recombination resistance (  $  R_{rec}  $  ) of charge carriers, respectively.  $  ^{15,45}  $   In this study, it can be seen that two semicircles appear in the low- and high-frequency regions. The detailed data of EIS are listed in Table S2. The 4 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   TFPN-modified sample shows a smaller transport resistance   $  R_{s}  $   (27.21 Ω) and a larger recombination resistance   $  R_{rec}  $   (107 490 Ω) compared with the control sample (  $  R_{s} = 93.88 \Omega  $  ,   $  R_{rec} = 76674 \Omega  $  ). From Figure 1d, it can be concluded that after modification, the charge carrier recombination at the PVK/HTL interface is effectively suppressed. In the meantime, it has been previously proposed that electron-rich C–F groups chemically complex with the empty orbitals of Pb  $  ^{2+}  $   or other electron defects at the perovskite interface, which can efficiently reduce the defect state density of the perovskite, thus increasing   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF values.  $  ^{47}  $   Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy for TFPN and a mixture of TFPN and PbI  $  _{2}  $  , dissolved in deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide-  $  d_{6}  $   (DMSO-  $  d_{6}  $  , 4 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was carried out to gain a deep insight into the chemical interaction between TFPN and perovskites, as shown in Figure 1e. The   $  {}^{19}  $  F NMR peaks of the C–F groups in the pure TFPN sample shift to a lower chemical shift in the TFPN + PbI  $  _{2}  $   mixed sample, indicating that C–F in TFPN interacts with PbI  $  _{2}  $  . In addition, the   $  {}^{13}  $  C NMR peak in TFPN shifted in TFPN + PbI  $  _{2}  $  , which further proves the role of TFPN.  $  ^{48}  $   To further quantitatively verify the change in the defect state density after TFPN modification, dark J–V curves of pure hole devices with a glass/ITO/NiO  $  _{x}  $  /perovskite/SpiroOMeTAD/Ag structure are characterized, as shown in Figure 1f. In this work, the electron-rich group C–F in TFPN (electrophile) chemically complexes with   $  Pb^{2+}  $   through its empty orbital, which effectively reduces the trap state density of the perovskite. In addition, the extremely high electronegativity of fluoride may be strengthened by the surrounding lead chemical bonds with organic cations to inhibit the formation of halide anions and organic cation vacancies, thus effectively passivating the defects caused by halogen vacancies.  $  ^{37}  $   Meanwhile, due to the strong electron acceptor nature of the C–F group in TFPN, it forms a positive dipole layer directed to the perovskite and induces p-type doping at the interface and promotes carrier transportation, which effectively reduces the possibility of charge trapping at the electron-rich defect surface and increases the carriers' lifetime. Therefore, the FFs of the devices are validly enhanced due to the enhanced hole transmission as FF is closely related to hole transportation. The champion FF reached up to 81.2% (PCE 21.44%, J–V curve is shown in Figure S10). Thus, TFPN is used as the interface layer of PVK/HTL, which realizes the formation of a positive dipole layer induced by p-type doping at the interface and promotes the transportation of carriers.

# CONCLUSIONS

In summary, the organic small molecule TFPN is used as a PVK/HTL interface modification layer to enhance the surface and subsurface properties of the perovskite film. KPFM results demonstrate that the Fermi level of the TFPN-modified perovskite film is closer to its valence band compared to that of the control one, indicating the p-type doping of TFPN at the PVK/HTL interface, which mainly results from the strong electron acceptor with the F atom on the aromatic chain in TFPN, inducing interface dipoles and accelerating the transportation of holes. At the same time, XPS, FTIR, SCLC, NMR, and PL results reveal that the C–F and C≡N groups effectively passivate the surface defects of the perovskite film, validly inhibiting the recombination of interfacial carriers, making HTL more valid in transporting holes. Thus, the highest PCE of 22.82% and the highest FF of 81.2% (PCE 21.44%) are obtained. In addition, the optimized modified device shows better long-term stability both in a N₂ atmosphere and in high relative humidity ambient conditions. The unencapsulated TFPN-modified device retains 77.3% of its initial efficiency in a constant humidity and temperature box under ambient conditions with a relative humidity of 45% at RT after 1 week. This work highlights the important role of the modification layer with a strong electron acceptor at the PVK/HTL interface for the realization of high-performance PSCs.

# EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

Materials. ITO glass substrates were purchased from Advanced Electronic Technology Co., Ltd. Tin(IV) oxide, 15% in   $  H_{2}O  $   colloidal

dispersion liquid, was purchased from Alfa Aesar. Formamidinium iodide (FAI), lead iodide (PbI $ _2 $ ), cesium iodide (CsI), lead bromide (PbBr $ _2 $ ), methylamine bromide (MABr), methylamine hydrogen chloride (MACl), lead chloride (PbCl $ _2 $ ), 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis (N,N-di-p-methoxyphenylamine)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD, 99.5%), 4-tert-butylpyridine (tBP, AR), and bis(trifluoromethane) sulfonimide lithium salt (Li-TFSI, AR) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.9%), chlorobenzene (CB, 99.9%), and tetrafluorophthalonitrile (TFPN, 95%) were obtained from Aldrich. Isopropanol (IPA, 99.99%) was purchased from Aladdin. All solvents and raw materials were obtained from commercial sources and used without further purification.

Perovskite Solar Cell Fabrication. ITO glass substrates were cleaned by ultrasonication using ethanol, acetone, deionized water, and ethanol successively for 15 min and then dried with clean nitrogen stream. Ultraviolet-ozone was used to treat the precleaned ITO substrates for 20 min to remove the residual organic matter from the surface. SnO₂ electron transport layer (ETL) was prepared by spinning SnO₂ solution (tin oxide, 15% in H₂O colloidal dispersion liquid, diluted by deionized water with a volume ratio of 1:5) at 3000 rpm for 30 s and then annealed on a hot plate at 150 °C for 30 min. After cooling down to room temperature, the substrates were removed and placed in a glovebox filled with nitrogen for subsequent processing. To fabricate the CsFAMA-based perovskite film, PbI₂ (600 mg), CsI (16.9 mg), and PbCl₂ (9 mg) were dissolved in 1 mL of a mixed solvent of DMF–DMSO (v/v = 9:1) and then stirred overnight on a hot plate at 70 °C. Besides, FAI (60 mg), MABr (6 mg), and MACl (6 mg) were dissolved in isopropanol (IPA, 1 mL) to prepare the organic cation precursor. Especially, before use, the organic cation precursor should be stirred at room temperature until the white powders totally disappear. At first, the PbI₂ precursor was spin-coated on the SnO₂ substrate at 1500 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 70 °C for 10 s. Then, the organic cation precursor was spin-coated on the PbI₂ film at 1500 rpm for 30 s. The film was transferred out of the N₂ glovebox quickly and annealed at 150 °C for 15 min in ambient air (30–40% humidity) to form the perovskite phase. After annealing, after placing the TFPN solution in the middle of the substrate for 10 s, the modified TFPN layer (dissolved in CB, slightly soluble, 2, 4, 6 mg mL⁻¹) was deposited on the perovskite layer by spin-coating at 6000 rpm for 40 s and then annealed on a hot plate at 70 °C for 10 min. Next, the HTL was spin-coated at 3000 rpm for 30 s. The HTL was prepared by dissolving 72.3 mg of Spiro-OMeTAD in CB (1 mL) doped with a bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl) (17.5 μL) imide lithium salt solution (520 mg of Li-TFSI in 1 mL of acetonitrile) and 4-tert-butylpyridine (TBP, 28.8 μL). Finally, about 80 nm Ag electrode was deposited on the Spiro-OMeTAD film by magnetron sputtering. Through the magnetron sputtering system (JPG-450A), the active area of the device was determined to be 0.08 cm² with a shadow mask.

Characterization and Measurements. Steady and time-resolved photoluminescence spectroscopies (PL and TRPL) were carried out by a fluorescence spectrometer (FLS 1000, Edinburgh Instruments). The morphologies of the surface and cross-sectional images of the perovskite films were obtained by a field emission scanning electron microscope (FEI Quanta 650 FEG). X-ray diffraction (XRD) was carried out using a DX-2700B X-ray diffractometer. Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) was measured by a KEYSIGHT Technologies 7500. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrum was obtained by a Nicolet 6700. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed using an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (AXIS Ultra DLD) with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source. The current density–voltage (J-V) curves of the PSCs were obtained using an electrochemical workstation (CHI660D) under illumination of an AM 1.5G solar simulator (Zolix SS150) with a reverse scan from 1.2 to -0.2 V. The electrochemical workstation was also used to carry out impedance spectroscopy under   $  V_{mpp}  $   in the dark. EQE was recorded using an incident photo-to-electron conversion efficiency (IPCE) system (PVE300, Bentham, Inc.), which was calibrated by a reference silicon cell before testing.
