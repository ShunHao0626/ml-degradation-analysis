# Multifunctional ytterbium oxide buffer for perovskite solar cells

The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of single-junction p-type/intrinsic/n-type (p-i-n) perovskite solar cells PSCs (also referred to as inverted PSCs) has surpassed 25% through passivation methodologies  $  ^{3-5,13-15}  $  . However, the published operational stability of p-i-n PSCs under stringent test conditions still lags behind those of commercially viable solar panels  $  ^{16-18}  $  . Although multiple factors are responsible for device degradation under operating conditions  $  ^{19-21}  $  , ion migration under realistic operational conditions represents an important challenge as it is almost ubiquitous within a device. Electrical bias and illumination-induced ion migration within a perovskite absorber layer can vary the chemical composition at the nano-scale and micro-scale levels  $  ^{19,21,22}  $  , inducing higher chemical heterogeneity and increased non-radiative recombination centres and subsequent initial sites at which device degradation occurs  $  ^{2,23}  $  . Furthermore, these mobile ions from the perovskite absorber can diffuse into the charge-selective layers and may accumulate at the metal electrode interface. Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) comprise a solid perovskite absorber sandwiched between several layers of different charge-selective materials, ensuring unidirectional current flow and high voltage output of the devices  $  ^{1,2}  $  . A 'buffer material' between the electron-selective layer and the metal electrode in p-type/intrinsic/n-type (p-i-n) PSCs (also known as inverted PSCs) enables electrons to flow from the electron-selective layer to the electrode  $  ^{3-5}  $  . Furthermore, it acts as a barrier inhibiting the inter-diffusion of harmful species into or degradation products out of the perovskite absorber  $  ^{6-8}  $  . Thus far, evaporable organic molecules  $  ^{9,10}  $   and atomic-layer-deposited metal oxides  $  ^{11,12}  $   have been successful, but each has specific imperfections. Here we report a chemically stable and multifunctional buffer material, ytterbium oxide (YbO  $  _{x}  $  ), for p-i-n PSCs by scalable thermal evaporation deposition. We used this YbO  $  _{x}  $   buffer in the p-i-n PSCs with a narrow-bandgap perovskite absorber, yielding a certified power conversion efficiency of more than 25%. We also demonstrate the broad applicability of YbO  $  _{x}  $   in enabling highly efficient PSCs from various types of perovskite absorber layer, delivering state-of-the-art efficiencies of 20.1% for the wide-bandgap perovskite absorber and 22.1% for the mid-bandgap perovskite absorber, respectively. Moreover, when subjected to ISOS-L-3 accelerated ageing, encapsulated devices with YbO  $  _{x}  $   exhibit markedly enhanced device stability.

Attempting to introduce an effective buffer layer between the electron-selective layer (ESL) and the metal electrode to block atom and ion movement between the perovskite and metal layers (in both directions; Extended Data Fig. 1a) is of technological interest in improving overall device performance  $  ^{6-8}  $  . The method for depositing the buffer layer must be compatible with underlying materials such as   $  (6,6)  $  -phenyl   $  C_{61}  $   butyric acid methyl ester and buckminsterfullerene (  $  C_{60}  $  ). # Properties of ytterbium oxide

Ytterbium (Yb) with a low Gibbs free energy has a strong tendency to form   $  YbO_{x}  $   when exposed to   $  O_{2}  $   (Extended Data Fig. 1b and Supplementary Note 1). The   $  YbO_{x}  $   that can be processed with thermal evaporation (Extended Data Fig. 1c) shows great promise in commercial applications (Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). To understand the oxidation of Yb, we first thermally evaporated a few nanometres of Yb films on a large-scale indium tin oxide (ITO) substrate (100 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ) in the device fabrication vacuum chamber with a base pressure of   $  10^{-4}  $   Pa. The sampling data from different areas of the sample shows identical   $  YbO_{x}  $   states (Extended Data Fig. 2). This rapid oxidation associated with metal Yb is further demonstrated by the colour change of the polished Yb metal surfaces before and after oxidation (Extended Data Fig. 3). We then studied the detailed oxidation dynamics of the clean   $  Yb^{0}  $   surface by tracking the oxidation process over time using in situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Extended Data Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 3). These results indicate that the Yb film (with a thickness of a few nanometres) deposited on the large-area ITO substrate can be fully oxidized in a few minutes when upscaling in a device fabrication vacuum chamber with a base pressure of   $  10^{-4}  $   Pa (see details in Supplementary Note 3). These benefits eliminate the additionally dedicated oxidation step,

which is usually required for other oxides processed with thermal evaporation  $  ^{30}  $  .

Figure 1a–c shows the high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images and the corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns of the Yb film. The HRTEM images and FFT patterns feature the crystalline structure corresponding to a face-centred cubic system with space group 225 (Fm3m) visualized from the [0 –11] direction in the selected red squared region. By clarifying the Yb natural oxidation through XPS spectra  $  ^{31,32}  $  (Fig. 1d,e), the resulting  $  YbO_{x}  $  exhibits a broad and continuous halo in the typical FFT pattern, indicative of the amorphous atomic arrangement  $  ^{33}  $  (Extended Data Fig. 5). The amorphous characteristics of the  $  YbO_{x}  $  films are also confirmed through grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (Extended Data Fig. 6). The energy-dispersive X-ray chemical composition map for the  $  YbO_{x}  $  film suggests that a distribution of both Yb and O elements in the films is spatially homogeneous (Supplementary Fig. 1). The reflection electron energy loss spectroscopy (REELS) spectrum shows that the surface bandgap of the  $  YbO_{x}  $  is as high as 5.5 eV (ref. 34) (Fig. 1f), which is transparent to visible light wavelengths. Important information associated with the electronic structure, including work function, valence band maximum (VBM) and conduction band minimum, is quantified by combining ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) analyses (Fig. 1g) with the REELS data. These results indicate that the  $  YbO_{x}  $  film may be n-type, with a Fermi level of 3.08 eV from the vacuum and a conduction band minimum of 1.26 eV from the vacuum (Fig. 1h).

# Thermal and operational stability

We tested the thermal stability of the PSCs with BCP or   $  YbO_{x}  $   (encapsulated using glass covers sealed by epoxy resin). The encapsulated PSCs are stored at 85 °C in the dark in an   $  N_{2}  $  -filled glovebox. Figure 4a compares the results of the thermal stability of the NBG-based PSCs with BCP or   $  YbO_{x}  $   buffer layers. Compared with the PSCs with BCP, the   $  YbO_{x}  $  -based device exhibits a slight increase in the PCEs initially (<100 h) and retains 98% of its initial PCE after 500 h. By contrast, the BCP-based device shows a sharp PCE drop on exerting thermal stress, and only 82% of its initial PCE can be retained after 500 h. Similarly, in the WBG-based PSCs, the   $  YbO_{x}  $  -based device exhibits enhanced thermal stability (Fig. 4b). However, the WBG-PSCs with either BCP or   $  YbO_{x}  $   buffer layers degrade at faster rates compared with the NBG-based PSCs. This difference correlates with the inherently poor stability of the WBG perovskite with a high bromide ratio  $  ^{39,40}  $  . Nonetheless, the WBG-based PSCs with   $  YbO_{x}  $   can still retain 80% of their initial PCE after 500 h, whereas the BCP-based device drops quickly to 75% of its initial PCE in the initial 24 h and approximately 57% of device performance after 500 h.

We also examined the long-term operational stability tracking at the maximum power output under 1-sun illumination with and without thermal stress. The tested PSCs were also encapsulated and continuously illuminated by a white light-emitting diode (LED) array with an intensity equivalent to   $  100 \, mW cm^{-2}  $   of AM 1.5 G irradiance without active cooling. The most stable NBG-based device with   $  YbO_{x}  $   maintained its initial performance for 800 h and retained 97% of the initial PCE (23.5%) after 1,000 h (Fig. 4c). In comparison, the NBG-based device with BCP dropped quickly to 96% of the initial PCE after 213 h (Supplementary Fig. 15). Notably, when subjected to multiple external stress (complied with ISOS-L-3 protocols), compared with the BCP-based device, the encapsulated   $  YbO_{x}  $  -based device still exhibits improved long-term operational stability tracking at maximum power output point at   $  85^{\circ}C  $   in ambient air (relative humidity, 50%) (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17). Specifically, the encapsulated devices with   $  YbO_{x}  $   retain about 85% of its initial PCE (23.6%) after 500 h, which is comparable to p-i-n PSCs with   $  SnO_{2}  $   (Supplementary Table 8). By contrast, the BCP-based device retains only 60% of the initial PCE after 213 h under ISOS-L-3 test qualifications. These results indicate that the PSCs with the   $  YbO_{x}  $   buffer show more substantial resistance to external stress than the regular BCP-containing ones, consistent with our observation above. Note that although enhanced device stability of p-i-n PSCs using BCP buffer layers in conjunction with Cr/Au electrodes has been reported in the past  $  ^{41}  $  , such a multilayer buffer makes the device much more complex, and Au is not a scalable metal owing to its high cost (Supplementary Table 2).

To gain more insights into the device stability improvements, we then conducted time-of-flight secondary ions mass spectrometry measurements on full PSC devices with BCP or  $ \mathrm{YbO}_x $  before and after ageing. Combining the time-of-flight secondary ions mass spectrometry with the depth-profile XPS results (Fig. 2, Extended Data Fig. 9 and Supplementary Fig. 18), we demonstrate that replacing BCP with  $ \mathrm{YbO}_x $  can suppress undesirable bi-directional harmful species movements

(such as  $ \mathrm{I}^{-} $ ,  $ \mathrm{Cs^{+}} $ ,  $ \mathrm{Rb^{+}} $  and  $ \mathrm{Cu} $ ), and we find that the  $ \mathrm{YbO_x} $  is chemically unreactive with the perovskites (Fig. 4e-g, see details in Supplementary Note 6). Hence, we conclude that multifunctional  $ \mathrm{YbO_x} $  is advantageous for use as the buffer material from various evaluation angles (Supplementary Fig. 19).

# Materials

Lead diiodide (PbI $ _2 $ , 99%), lead dibromide (PbBr $ _2 $ , 99%), (2-(3,6-dimethoxy-9H-carbazol-9-yl)ethyl)phosphonic acid (MeO-2PACz) and (2-(9H-carbazol-9-yl)ethyl)phosphonic acid (2PACz) were purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry. Formamidinium iodide (FAI), methylammonium iodide and methylammonium bromide (MABr) were purchased from Greatcell Solar. Rubidium iodide (RbI, >99.99%) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. Caesium iodide (CsI, >99.99%) and 1-butyl-1-methylpiperidinium tetrafluoroborate ([BMP] $ ^+ $ [BF $ _4 $ ] $ ^- $ , 99%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. (6,6)-phenyl-C $ _{61} $ -butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) was purchased from Nano-C Tech. Poly(bis(4-phenyl)(2,4,6-trimethylphenyl)amine) (PTAA; M $ _W $  ≈ 13,000) and phenylethylammonium iodide (PEAI) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology. Nickle oxide (NiO $ _x $ ) nanoparticles, C $ _{60} $  and BCP were purchased from Advanced Election Technology. Ytterbium (99.9%) was purchased from Kurt Lesker (Shanghai) Trading. Ultra-dry dimethylformamide (DMF), ultra-dry dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), ultra-dry isopropanol (IPA), ultra-dry ethanol, ultra-dry anisole and ultra-dry chlorobenzene were purchased from commercial sources (Acros). Toluene was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent. Cu was purchased from a commercial source with high purity. All reagents were used as received without further purification.

# Perovskite precursor solution

The perovskite composition in the NBG perovskite is   $  Rb_{0.05}Cs_{0.05}MA_{0.05}FA_{0.85}Pb(I_{0.95}Br_{0.05})_{3}  $  . The perovskite precursor solution was prepared by mixing CsI (19.5 mg), RbI (15.9 mg), MABr (8.4 mg), FAI (219.5 mg),   $  PbI_{2}  $   (656.9 mg) and   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (27.5 mg) in 1 ml mixed solvent of DMF:DMSO (4:1, v/v) and the resulting precursor solution was stirred at 50 °C for 2 h before use  $  ^{3}  $  . For the MBG perovskite, the perovskite composition is   $  (Cs_{0.05}FA_{0.85}MA_{0.10})_{0.95}Pb(I_{0.85}Br_{0.15})_{3}  $  . The perovskite precursor solution was prepared by mixing   $  PbI_{2}  $   (1.28 M), FAI (1.21 M), MABr (0.16 M),   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (0.22 M) and CsI (0.07 M) in a mixed solvent of DMF:DMSO (4:1, v/v), and the resulting precursor solution was stirred at 70 °C for 2 h before use. For the WBG perovskite, the perovskite composition is   $  FA_{0.83}Cs_{0.17}Pb(I_{0.6}Br_{0.4})_{3}  $  . The perovskite precursor solution was prepared by mixing   $  PbI_{2}  $   (0.56 M), FAI (1.16 M),   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (0.84 M) and CsI (0.24 M) in a mixed solvent of DMF:DMSO (4:1, v/v), and the resulting precursor solution was stirred at 50 °C for 5 h before use.

# Device fabrication

Before device fabrication, the pre-patterned ITO or FTO substrates were ultrasonically cleaned using an aqueous detergent solution, deionized water, acetone and IPA for 20 min. Subsequently, the as-cleaned ITO and FTO substrates were treated with UV–ozone for 10 min.

For the WBG perovskite, the 15 mg ml  $  ^{-1}  $  NiO  $  _{x}  $  nanoparticle in a mixed solvent of IPA:deionized water (1:3) solution was spin-coated onto ITO substrates at 3,000 rpm for 30 s (with a ramping rate of 1,000 rpm s  $  ^{-1}  $  ), and the as-formed films were heated at 150 °C for 30 min in ambient air. The subsequent overlayers were deposited after the substrates cooled down to room temperature. The following operations were all carried out in an N  $  _{2}  $  -filled glovebox (with H  $  _{2}  $  O and O  $  _{2}  $  concentrations of less than 0.1 ppm). Before depositing the perovskite layer, the NiO  $  _{x}  $  film was modified by the self-assembling monolayer of 2PACz and MeO-2PACz. The solutions of 2PACz and MeO-2PACz with the same concentration (0.5 mg ml  $  ^{-1}  $  in ethanol) were mixed with the volume ratio of 1:1, and then the mixed solution of 2PACz and MeO-2PACz was spin-coated onto the NiO  $  _{x}  $  film at 3,000 rpm (with a ramping rate of 1,000 rpm s  $  ^{-1}  $  ) for 30 s followed by annealing at 100 °C for 5 min. When the films cooled down to room temperature, the perovskite precursor solution was deposited onto the substrates by a consecutive two-step program at 1,000 rpm for 10 s with a ramping rate of 1,000 rpm s  $  ^{-1}  $  and 5,000 rpm for 35 s.

with a ramping rate of 1,000 rpm s  $  ^{-1}  $  . During the second step, 300   $  \mu  $  l of anisole doped with the ionic liquid (  $  [BMP]^{+}[BF_{4}]^{-}  $  , 0.01 mg ml  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was poured on the centre of the spinning substrates at 5 s before the end of the whole program. Then the samples were immediately transferred to the hotplate, annealing at 100 °C for 30 min. For the post-treatment of the WBG perovskite films, 200   $  \mu  $  l of PEAI (5 mg ml  $  ^{-1}  $   in IPA) and 100   $  \mu  $  l of MAI (5 mg ml  $  ^{-1}  $   in IPA) were mixed in 705   $  \mu  $  l of a mixed solvent of DMF:IPA (5:700, v/v) to obtain 1,005   $  \mu  $  l of post-treatment solution. Then 50   $  \mu  $  l of the post-treatment solution was dynamically spin-coated on top of the perovskite film, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 5 min.

For the MBG perovskite, 2 mg ml  $  ^{-1}  $  PTAA (in toluene) solution was spin-coated onto ITO substrates at 5,000 rpm for 30 s (with a ramping rate of 2,500 rpm s  $  ^{-1}  $ ), and as-formed films were heated at 120 °C for 10 min. The subsequent overlayers were deposited after the substrates cooled down to room temperature. Then, the PEAI solution (10 mg ml  $  ^{-1}  $  in IPA) was spin-coated onto the PTAA-coated substrates at 5,000 rpm for 30 s (with a ramping rate of 2,500 rpm s  $  ^{-1}  $ ), and then the samples were heated at 100 °C for 5 min. Subsequently, the perovskite films were spin-coated onto the substrates by a two-step program at 2,000 rpm for 10 s (with a ramping rate of 200 rpm s  $  ^{-1}  $ ) and 6,000 rpm for 30 s (with a ramping rate of 1,000 rpm s  $  ^{-1}  $ ), respectively. During the second step, 100  $  \mu  $  l of chlorobenzene was poured on the centre of the spinning substrates at 16 s before the end of the whole program. Then the samples were immediately transferred to the hotplate, annealing at 105 °C for 30 min.

For the NBG perovskite, the MeO-2PACz (0.5 mg ml  $  ^{-1}  $  , in ethanol) solution was spin-coated onto the pre-cleaned FTO substrates at 3,000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 100 °C for 10 min in an N  $  _{2}  $  -filled glove box. The perovskite films were prepared according to the procedure in a previous work  $  ^{3}  $  .

The samples were then transferred to a vacuum chamber to make the charge-selective layer without air exposure.  $ C_{60} $  (35 nm) was thermally evaporated in a vacuum chamber with a base pressure of less than  $ 4 \times 10^{-4} $  Pa. For the NBG perovskites, the PCBM solution  $ (20\mathrm{mgml}^{-1} $  in chlorobenzene) was spin-coated on top of the perovskite layer at a speed of  $ 1,000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 30~\mathrm{s} $  (with a ramping rate of  $ 200~\mathrm{rpm~s}^{-1} $ ).

For the buffer layer fabrication, the samples were transferred to a separate vacuum chamber with a base pressure of less than  $ 4 \times 10^{-4} $  Pa. The  $ \mathrm{YbO}_x $  buffer layer with a thickness of  $ 1.5\mathrm{nm} $  was obtained by the thermally evaporated metal Yb in the vacuum chamber. The thermal evaporation rate for the metal Yb is  $ 1\AA s^{-1} $ . Note that Yb will be naturally oxidized into the  $ \mathrm{YbO}_x $  in a few minutes without intentional additional oxidation processes. For the control devices with the BCP interlayer, BCP  $ (1\mathrm{mgml}^{-1} $  in ethanol) was spin-coated at  $ 5,000~\mathrm{rpm} $  with a ramping rate of  $ 2,500~\mathrm{rpm~s^{-1}} $  for  $ 30~s $ .

Finally, a 100-nm thick Cu electrode was thermally evaporated on the samples in the vacuum chamber (less than  $ 4 \times 10^{-4} $  Pa) through a metal shadow mask with an aperture area of  $ 10.3\mathrm{mm}^2 $ . The aperture mask area for the photovoltaic performance tests is  $ 7.2\mathrm{mm}^2 $ . Moreover, for the WBG and MBG devices,  $ \mathrm{MgF}_2 $  (100 nm) was deposited on the glass side of the ITO substrate as the anti-reflection coating, whereas for the NBG devices,  $ \mathrm{MgF}_2 $  (125 nm) was deposited on the glass side of the FTO substrate.

# Stability assessment

For the stabilized power output test, the encapsulated devices were measured at the maximum power point (MPP) under full-spectrum white LED illumination (continuous 1-sun illumination) in ambient air or in the  $ \mathrm{N}_2 $  atmosphere. Thermal stability assessments for encapsulated devices were conducted by storing the encapsulated PSCs on a hot plate at  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  in an  $ \mathrm{N}_2 $  atmosphere. Photovoltaic performances were regularly measured when cooled down to room temperature. For the NBG-containing PSCs, the PCBM electron transport layer was replaced by  $ \mathrm{C}_{60} $  (35 nm, prepared by thermal evaporation) in the thermal stability and long-term MPP tracking assessments. The encapsulation

Article

for the device was done by capping the device with a glass slide, using UV adhesive as the sealant. The encapsulation process was finished in an  $ \mathrm{N}_2 $ -filled glovebox (with  $ \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} $  and  $ \mathrm{O}_2 $  concentrations of less than  $ 0.1\mathrm{ppm} $ ). For the stability test under the ISOS-L-3 protocol, the temperature was controlled and monitored by the polyimide heating film with a digital display in temperature control. The ageing devices adhered to the polyimide heating film.

# Photovoltaic characterization

The current density–voltage   $  (J-V)  $   characteristics of the PSCs were performed using a Keithley 2400 Source Meter under AM1.5 G illumination with a Xenon-lamp solar simulator (XES-40S1, SAN-EI). Before the measurement, the light intensity of 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $   was calibrated using a standard monocrystalline silicon solar cell with a KG-5 filter. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) was measured by a QE-R3011 measurement system (Enli Technology). Electroluminescence quantum efficiency (EQE  $  _{EL}  $  ) was carried out at room temperature (297 K) in an N  $  _{2}  $  -filled glovebox. A Keithley 2400 Source Meter and an integration sphere (CME-LP38) coupled with a spectrometer (Flame, Ocean Optics) were used for the measurements. The device was placed on top of the integration sphere, and only forward light emission can be collected. The devices were swept from zero bias to forward bias.

# Film characterization

The field-emission scanning electron microscope was used to investigate the top-view and cross-sectional morphology with an accelerating voltage of 10 kV (Hitachi U8010). Spin-coating perovskite films on the ITO-coated glass substrates prepared samples for ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption spectra characterizations. Then their UV-vis absorption spectra (350–850 nm) were recorded by a spectrophotometer (UH4150) with the background noise subtracted before testing. During the measuring process, the perovskite films were exposed to air for less than 5 min. The time-resolved photoluminescence spectroscopy measurements were performed by an Edinburgh Instruments FLS1000 photoluminescence spectrometer, and a pulsed laser measured all time-resolved photoluminescence-related tests with the excitation wavelength of 450 nm after 120-s illumination. Temperature-dependent J-V characteristics of ITO/[YbO  $  _{x}  $  (5 nm)/Cu (1 nm)]  $  _{20}  $  /Cu and ITO/[YbO  $  _{x}  $  (2 nm)]  $  _{20}  $  /Cu were measured using HP4140B picoammeter dominated by a computer within a cryostat. Both devices were processed by evaporating 20 alternative layers. For [YbO  $  _{x}  $  (5 nm)/Cu (1 nm)]  $  _{20}  $ , we mean we have deposited 20 alternating layers of 5 nm YbO  $  _{x}  $  followed by 1 nm Cu. The starting low temperature is 80 K cooled with liquid N  $  _{2}  $ , and data were acquired by every 30 K increase controlled by the MercuryiTC low-temperature environment controller. Grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering measurements for amorphous Yb films deposited on various substrates were conducted at the BL17B1 beamline of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). The X-ray wavelength was 1.240 Å with a grazing incident angle of 1.0°, and the scattering intensity was detected by a PILATUS 2 M detector.

Thermo Scientific ESCALAB  $ \mathrm{Xi^{+}} $  system was used to acquire the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy and the reflection electron energy loss spectroscopy spectra. All samples were transferred into the analysis chamber using a vacuum transfer module avoiding exposure to the air. Unless otherwise stated, the gas cluster ion beam sputtering technology carried out the depth profiles with the parameters of 2,000 cluster sizes at  $ 4\mathrm{keV} $  ion energy.  $ \mathrm{Ar^{+}} $  ion sputtering was used as a supplement to etch the hard materials, such as metal electrodes. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy and annular dark-field scanning transmission electron

microscopy images were obtained by using a JEM-2100F field-emission gun microscope with a 0.19 nm point-to-point resolution at 200 kV equipped with energy disperse X-ray detectors. The images were analysed using Gatan Digital Micrograph software. To reduce the electron dose during the high-resolution transmission electron microscopy characterization, we limited the total electron beam exposure time to within 5 s. All the time-of-flight secondary-ion mass spectrometry profiles were acquired by a PHI TRIFT V nanoTOF (ULVAC-PHI, Japan) system using a slice-and-view analysis scheme. A 20-kV C $  _{60}  $  $  ^{+}  $ beam with a pulse frequency of 8,200 Hz and a pulse length of 15 ns was used as the primary-ion beam. All the secondary-ion signals were acquired from a 50 μm × 50 μm area. A 1-kV Ar $  ^{+}  $ beam was used as the sputter source to remove the surface material during time-of-flight secondary ions mass spectrometry profiling, and the sputtering crater size was 1,000 μm × 1,000 μm. All the secondary-ion data presented in the profiles were normalized by total ion intensities.
