# 1. Introduction

Owing to its remarkable electronic and optical properties, hybrid organic-inorganic perovskites have become one of the most prospective next-generation photovoltaic materials, such as strong band-edge absorption, long charge-carrier diffusion length, low exciton binding energy, and high carrier mobility, moreover, ease of low-temperature solution processability [1-3]. Evaluated as the most competitive and promising low-cost and high-efficiency photovoltaic technology,

Organic and inorganic metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with an outstanding performance of low-cost manufacturing and low-temperature solution processing, have been regarded as the next-generation photovoltaic technology. However, the solution processed perovskite films usually have abundant defects, especially at the grain boundaries and surfaces, causing carrier recombination, hysteresis, and degradation. Herein, 4-(trifluoromethyl)benzylamine (TFMBA) is deposited on the surface of perovskites to form an ultrathin hydrophobic organic molecular layer, passivating the interfacial imperfections. By optimizing the post-treatment conditions, a remarkable power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 20.56% is realized based on   $  Cs_{0.05}(FA_{0.85}MA_{0.15})_{0.95}Pb(I_{0.85}Br_{0.15})_{3}  $   perovskite. Impressively, the passivated ultrathin organic molecular layer effectively prohibits the penetration of moisture, resulting in unsealed PSCs retaining 84% of the original PCE after aged 1300 h at 65%–75% relative humidity (RH). SEM and XRD confirm that TFMBA can effectively passivate defects on the surface and grain boundaries, thereby prevent the perovskites from the irreversible decomposition with the immersion of moisture. This suggests that the TFMBA post-treatment has great potential in obtaining high-quality and stable perovskite films.

growth and processing condition [12,13]. The defects not only impair the PCE but also the stability, as they are the most vulnerable sites for degradation. Adjusting perovskite components and additives, as well as appropriate annealing methods can effectively passivate these defects of perovskite absorber and improve its crystallinity [14–17]. Nowadays, surface post-treatments are a simple and effective strategy, by passivating defects to reduce the trap-assisted non-radiative recombination and protect from the moisture or oxygen ingress [18,19]. The current surface treatment approaches to form passivation include forming coordinate bonding or ionic bonding with imperfect perovskites, or converting into 2D perovskites  $  ^{13}  $  . Abate et al. utilized iodopentafluorobenzene to passivate the undercoordinated halide ions via halogen bonding, reducing the possibility of charge trapping at the surface defects, thereby increasing carrier lifetime and enhancing charge transportation [20]. Similarly, the N in amines could supply a pair of nonbonding electrons to coordinate with the undercoordinated Pb  $  ^{2+}  $   or Pb clusters, showing effective passivation of perovskite materials [21,22]. Thus, some organic cations with ammonium salt functional groups, such as butylamine (BA  $  ^{+}  $  ), octylamine (OA  $  ^{+}  $  ), phenylethylamine (PEA  $  ^{+}  $  ), and diammonium derivatives, have been proverbially used to enhance the device performance and stability [9,23–26]. Jiang et al. employed an organic halide salt phenethylammonium iodide (PEAI) to fill the iodine vacancies on the perovskite surface, reaching a certified PCE of 23.32% [9]. Zhou et al. introduced a fluorinated aromatic cation, 2-(4-fluorophenyl)ethyl ammonium iodide (FPEAI), as a 2D protective capping layer to protect the 3D perovskite from moisture, which possessed superior devices performance and moisture stability [27]. Wang et al. compared three phenylalkylamines with different chain length, aniline, benzylamine, and phenethylamine, as an passivation layer on the FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite films, and found the benzylamine achieved the optimal results with improved PCE [28]. Zhou et al. also exhibited that the highly defects regions (such as surface and grain boundaries) could be well passivated through post-treatment of benzylamine on CsO  $  _{0.15}  $  FAO  $  _{0.85}  $  Pb(I  $  _{0.73}  $  BrO  $  _{0.27}  $  )  $  _{3}  $   perovskite films [29]. Recently, Grätzel et al. proposed and synthesized a new molecular passivator based on benzylammonium iodide, 4-tert-butyl benzyl ammonium iodide (tBBAI), to effectively passivate perovskite films to reduce non-radiative charge carrier recombination, resulting in a high PCE of 23.5% and an enhancement of the moisture resistivity under ambient air [30].

Here we introduce 4-(Trifluoromethyl)benzylamine (TFMBA) that can form an ultrathin organic molecular layer on the top of perovskite films to passivate the surface defects, as well as protect perovskites from moisture ingress. The amine of the TFMBA plays an important role in passivating uncoordinated Pb defects, increasing the PCE of the treated devices up to 20.56% compared to the pristine one (19.25%). Since the benzene ring and the fluorinated alkyl group are highly hydrophobic, the treated devices exhibit an amazing humidity stability. The unencapsulated devices still maintain 84% of the initial PCE after aged under a high humidity of 65%–75% relative humidity (RH) for 1300 h.

# 2. Results and discussion

The   $  Cs_{0.05}(FA_{0.85}MA_{0.15})_{0.95}\mathrm{Pb(I_{0.85}Br_{0.15})_{3}}  $   (CsFAMA) perovskite thin films were prepared by a one-step spin-coating process using ethyl acetate (EA) as anti-solvent as previously reported, and the corresponding bandgap is about 1.58 eV (as shown in Fig. S1) [31]. After annealing, the TFMBA/IPA solution was dynamically dropped onto the perovskite films during spinning, followed by another annealing to completely remove the residual solvents. The schematic diagram is shown in Fig. 1a. The control sample is referred to as F0. The other samples treated by the TFMBA/IPA solution with different volume

ratios of 0.5%, 1%, 3%, and 5%, are referred to as F0.5, F1, F3, and F5, respectively. The TFMBA containing an amine functional group can be acted as a Lewis base, providing a lone pair of electrons to effectively coordinate and passivate the perovskite films. To investigate the microstructure of different concentrations of TFMBA treated perovskite films, X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurement was conducted. As shown in Fig. 1b, the intensity of the diffraction peaks of perovskite films with TFMBA-treated is slightly increased, and the lead iodide (PbI₂) diffraction peak at 12.6° is slightly weakened with the increase of concentration to F1. The appearance of lead can be attributed to the escape of organic cations and halides during the annealing process, leading to the decomposition of perovskite [13]. TFMBA molecules can occupy these vacancies, effectively passivating uncoordinated Pb²⁺ or Pb cluster on the surface of perovskite films. However, when the TFMBA concentration further increases to F3, the PbI₂ peak starts to increase again. Excessive amines have high reactivity with perovskite thin films, decomposing them to lead [32]. In addition, the optical properties of the perovskite films modified with different amounts of TFMBA were investigated, as shown in Fig. 1c. No significant change in the position of the absorption edge is found. The UV–Vis absorption intensity of perovskite films is increased by the passivation at moderate concentration. In order to explore the elemental distribution on the surface of the perovskite by the TFMBA treatment, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed as shown in Fig. 1d and Fig. S2. In the C1s spectrum, the C≡N and C–N peaks at 288.1 eV and 286.4 eV correspond to FA and MA in CsFAMA perovskite. The C–C peak at 284.5 eV is attributed to common sample surface contaminations [33]. For the CsFAMA perovskite films passivated with F1, a new peak emerges at the binding energy of 291.9 eV in C1s spectra, corresponding to the π-π bond of the phenyl functional group in TFMBA [27]. At the same time, perfluoroalkyl substitution at the para position of the benzene ring is also detected with the F1s signal at the binding energy (BE) of 687.4 eV. It is worth noting that the C≡N peak shifts significantly in the C1s spectrum of the F1-treated perovskite films, indicating that there is a strong interaction between TFMBA and FA. In the Pb4f spectra, not only the BE of 4f₅/₂ and 4f₂/₇ of Pb²⁺ changes obviously, but also the two shoulder peaks at low BE associated with metallic Pb⁰ are weakened with the F1 modified. Furthermore, the corresponding two signal peaks of I3d also shift accordingly (Fig. S3). The TFMBA tends to react with uncoordinated Pb, effectively preventing its redox reaction. The schematic model of the TFMBA capping on the surface of the CsFAMA perovskite is shown in Fig. 1e.

To determine the effect of TFMBA passivation on the surface morphology of perovskite, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) observations of CsFAMA perovskites treated with different concentrations of TFMBA are shown in Fig. 2a and b. The pristine CsFAMA perovskite film has a small grain size, with a maximum grain size of about 450 nm, and there are large lead iodide particles on the surface. When the low concentration of TFMBA is employed, the grain size of perovskites increases to 600 nm. At the same time, the large lead iodide excess becomes small grains, evenly distributing in the perovskite grain boundaries, as evidenced by the weak peaks of   $  PbI_{2}  $   at 12.6° in XRD patterns. Meanwhile, the CsFAMA perovskite surface becomes smoother after the modification, and the average roughness (  $  R_{q}  $  ) decreases from 20.6 nm to 16.5 nm (F1) as shown in Fig. 2b. With the increase in the concentration of TFMBA (F3 and F5), the surface of the perovskite is obviously covered by TFMBA. The morphology turns rugged, accompanying with the appearance of pinholes and flake   $  PbI_{2}  $  . This indicates that the structure of perovskite starts to be destroyed, which is consistent with XRD results. In the static water contact angle test, it is found that the TFMBA treated perovskite can significantly improve the hydrophobicity from 76.05° to 106.38°.

To clarify the effect of TFMBA passivation on the charge transmission and recombination kinetics of CsFAMA perovskite films, we performed steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved PL (TRPL) measurement. As shown in Fig. 4a, the PL intensity of the F1 treated sample is higher than that of the pristine one, while the PL quenching of CsFAMA/F1/Spiro-OMeTAD is much more efficient than that of CsFAMA/Spiro-OMeTAD. This suggests there is less recombination and more effective hole extraction at the interface for the TFMBA treatment. The Time-Resolved Photoluminescence (TRPL) measurement further confirms the positive passivation effect of the

TFMBA. The TRPL spectra of the glass/perovskite and glass/perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD devices with/without F1-treated are presented in Fig. 4b, fitted with a biexponential formula. The extracted parameters are listed in Table S3. The  $ \tau_{\mathrm{ave}} $  of TRPL of the F1-treated perovskite film significantly increases from 1118.60 ns to 1225.83 ns due to the effective passivation of the defects on the surface of perovskite films. With the addition of the Spiro-OMeTAD layer, the F1-treated devices accelerate the hole extraction into the Spiro-OMeTAD layer, thus the PL lifetime decays more quickly, compared with the untreated device. This post-treatment could be proven as a method to effectively suppress the surface and boundary defects, thereby enhancing the carrier lifetime and hole transportation capability at the interface [34]. To further quantitatively evaluate the defect density, hole-only devices of glass/FTO/PEDOT:PSS/CsFAMA/Spiro-OMeTAD/Au were prepared and tested the I-V dark curves (Fig. 4c). The space-charge-limited-current (SCLC) technique was then used to estimate the trap densities of these devices [35,36]. Dark I-V curves generally have three regions, including the ohmic region at low bias, the trap filling region at intermediate bias, and the SCLC region at high bias. In the trap filling region, the density of the trap level to be filled continuously until the trap filling limit voltage ( $ V_{\mathrm{TFL}} $ ). The trap density calculated as  $ N_{\mathrm{t}} = (2\varepsilon \varepsilon_0 V_{\mathrm{TFL}}) / (eL^2) $ , where  $ \varepsilon $  is the dielectric constant of perovskite (32, using the value of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  in the literature),  $ \varepsilon_0 $  is dielectric constant of vacuum,  $ L $  is the thickness of the perovskite film, and  $ e $  is the elementary charge [37,38]. The calculated hole trap density in the pristine perovskite is  $ 2.35 \times 10^{15} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3} $ , which was much higher than that of the passivated device ( $ 1.46 \times 10^{15} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3} $ ). The decrease in trap density with TFMB A post-treatment is mainly originated from the effective coordination and passivation of the defects at the surface of perovskite films, which is beneficial for the extraction and transmission of holes. However, with excessive TFMB A treatment, the defect state density of hole-only devices measured by SCLC begins to increase, as shown in Fig. S6. This change is consistent with SEM images, excessive TFMB A treatment leads to small pinhole and flake  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  defects, increasing the charge carrier recombination. As TFMBA has the benzene ring and the fluorinated alkyl group that are hydrophobic, the capability of TFMBA passivating the surface of perovskite films toward moisture is also investigated. As shown in Fig. S8, soaked in water for 5 s, the pristine CsFAMA perovskite film turns yellow and decomposes immediately. In contrast, the TFMBA-treated perovskite film remains black. Even after 60 s soaking, only the edge of the film show obviously yellow. The device humidity-stabilities are compared, by measuring the PCE under the ambient condition without encapsulation. The long-term stability of the F1-treated PSCs is notably enhanced compared to that of the pristine devices in dry air with 25%–35% relative humidity (RH), maintaining its initial PCE after aged 3500 h, while the PCE of the control device decreases gradually to 80% (Fig. 5a). In addition, an accelerated aging measurement was further carried out with the unencapsulated devices under high humidity of 65%–75% RH in Fig. 5b. The F1-modified PSC demonstrates a prominent superior high-humidity stability throughout the whole test period and maintains 84% of the primary efficiency after aged 1300 h, which is in forceful contrast to the pristine device that drops sharply to only 20% of the initial PCE. To see more clearly, the photovoltaic characteristics of the devices are plotted as a function of the aging time (Fig. S9). It is

found that the FF and   $  J_{sc}  $   of the pristine device decrease very fast under high humidity. To find the cause, XRD patterns (Fig. 5c) of the perovskite films are examined before and after the high-humidity aged tests. After a 35 days aging test, the control CsFAMA perovskite film shows a strong XRD diffraction signal of   $  PbI_{2}  $   at 12.6°, suggesting a severe decomposition induced by the moisture. This is confirmed by the morphology observation (Fig. 5d) of the aged perovskite film, showing a large amount of sheet   $  PbI_{2}  $  . However, for the F1-treated perovskite, there is no significant change before and after aging, indicating a robust moisture-resistance by the TFMBA protection. The surface morphology (Fig. 5e) of the aged perovskite film shows no obvious change. Regarding the thermal stability (Figs. S10 and S11a), the TFMBA device also show advantages when stored in air without encapsulation at 85 °C. Through XRD (Fig. S11b) and SEM (Fig. S11c and d) analysis, it can be concluded that the F1 post-treatment could slow down the thermal decomposition of perovskite. To evaluate the stability under continuous illumination, F0 and F1 devices were measured for 5 cycles including reverse and forward sweeping under the continuous illumination. It can be seen that the light stability of F1 is slightly better than that of the pristine device (Fig. S12). These results indicate that the effective passivation of the perovskite surface is of crucial importance in order to maintain the stability of PSCs devices [43].

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, we apply an ultrathin hydrophobic organic molecular layer on the surface of perovskite film via a dynamic spin-coating, which enhances both the competitive performance and better environmental stability. It is revealed that the formation of a TFMBA capping layer on the perovskite surface could not only passivate surface and grain boundary defects, reduce the charge recombination and promote interfacial hole transportation, but also protect the perovskite structure from environment moisture and thermal degradation. A high PCE of  $ 20.56\% $  is achieved by introducing TFMBA onto the CsFAMA perovskite films with greatly enhanced performance than the pristine devices  $ (19.25\%) $ . Furthermore, the surface post-treated perovskite

films and devices possess profound high humidity stability, sustaining 84% of the initial PCE without encapsulation after aged 1300 h at 65% – 75% relative humidity (RH). Thus, the passivated an ultrathin hydrophobic organic molecular layer strategy on the perovskite surface exhibits a great application potential.
