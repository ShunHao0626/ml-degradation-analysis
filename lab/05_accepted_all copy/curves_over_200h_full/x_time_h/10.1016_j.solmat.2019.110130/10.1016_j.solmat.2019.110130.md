# ARTICLEINFO

Perovskite-based solar cells (PSCs) have emerged as a very promising solar photovoltaic technology with fivefold increase observed in the device efficiency within less than 10 years of its inception. The efficiency of these solar cells is strongly affected by the quality of perovskite layer surface coverage and its grain size. Although use of antisolvents such as chlorobenzene has been used to treat methylammonium lead iodide  $ \mathrm{(CH_3NH_3PbI_3)} $  resulting in grain size of ca.  $ 200 - 300\mathrm{nm} $ , one still needs to minimize the grain boundaries to reduce their impact on recombination. In this study, we have successfully incorporated [6, 6]-phenyl-C70-butyric acid methyl ester  $ \mathrm{(PC_{70}BM)} $  acceptor as an additive in the perovskite layer of the mesoscopic solar cell devices, which not only leads to the formation of larger grains with fewer grain boundaries resulting in reduced charge carriers recombination, the band alignment between  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  and  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  was also found to improve. The device having the optimum  $ \mathrm{PC}_{70}\mathrm{BM} $  concentration in perovskite showed enhanced power conversion efficiency (PCE) due to improvement in current density  $ (\mathrm{J}_{\mathrm{sc}}) $  and fill-factor (FF), correlated to the decrease in charge transfer resistance  $ (\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{CT}}) $ . The devices using  $ \mathrm{PC}_{70}\mathrm{BM} $  additive exhibit over  $ 15\% $  improvement in the power conversion efficiency or PCE ( $ 15.5\% $  for  $ \mathrm{PC}_{70}\mathrm{BM} $  containing device and  $ 13.3\% $  PCE for the reference device with no acceptor additive). More importantly, the device containing  $ \mathrm{PC}_{70}\mathrm{BM} $  acceptor additive, retained ca.  $ 75\% $  of the original PCE for  $ 250\mathrm{h} $  when kept at  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  without encapsulation in an ambient at  $ 20\% $  RH.

# 1. Introduction

Solution-processed Organic-Inorganic hybrid perovskites (MAPbX $ _3 $ ) have emerged very strongly as next generation photovoltaic materials, owing to fast ambipolar charge transport, lower exciton binding energy, tunable band gap, strong light absorption in visible region (400–800 nm), long diffusion length (up to 100 nm for halides and >1000 nm for mixed halides perovskites), and long carrier lifetime [1–6]. These excellent properties of perovskites along with the interfacial engineering, additive incorporation, and improved film deposition methods have led to tremendous improvement in PCEs increasing from ~3% in 2009 to over 22% recently. These numbers are comparable to silicon-based solar cells, thus making perovskite photovoltaic

Charge recombination and charge carrier-trapping are often associated with the defective grain boundaries and pinholes induced by uncontrolled fast crystallization of perovskite materials during spin coating, leading to a reduction in the current density (  $  J_{sc}  $  ) and open circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ) in both planar and mesoscopic PSCs [9]. To minimize these losses, one can incorporate various additives such as chloride, tetrafloroborate, thiocyanate, etc, in the perovskite precursor solution which lead to improved perovskite morphology with reduced density of pinholes as well as less defective grain boundaries by passivating the defects in PSCs [16]. Controlled nucleation and crystal growth of the perovskite films using various polymer additives such as PCBM, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) with perovskite solution have resulted in highly stable crystalline perovskite films [17–19].

Fullerene and its derivatives being electron acceptors were used by Shao et al. in the form of a separate layer deposited on the perovskite layer for reducing trap density and eliminating photocurrent hysteresis [20]. Subsequently researchers also tried to develop Perovskite-PCBM blends, which results in reduced charge recombination losses and hysteresis behavior and, improvement in fill-factor in bulk heterojunction solar cells. Liu et al. mixed  $ \mathrm{PC}_{61}\mathrm{BM} $  in  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  for the first time and showed two times enhancement in the PCE (14.98%) compared to pristine device in planar PSC (6.9%). The improvement in the PCE was attributed to higher electrical conductivity of  $ \mathrm{PC}_{61}\mathrm{BM} $  over  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  [21]. Similarly, mixing PCBM in perovskite is also shown to result in improved fill-factor and low hysteresis in inverted device structures [22,23]. Recently, PCBM doping in perovskite precursor has also been shown to result in interfacial improvement in planar PSC devices [17]. It was shown that the carrier recombination gets effectively suppressed in PCBM doped perovskite films due to grain boundary passivation and elimination of pin holes in perovskite films. Improvement of the interfacial contact between  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ /Perovskite layers by sandwiched PCBM layer also results in efficient electron extraction from Perovskite to  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  layer. Wu et al. used fullerene derivative: PCBM and indene-C60 biadduct (ICBA) in the perovskite solution [24]. ICBA has better solubility in perovskite solution in comparison to PCBM and has higher LUMO level as compared PCBM, it enabled efficient electron extraction and enhanced the efficiency. Torrientes et al. used different fullerene derivative such as pyrazolino and methano [60] fullerene derivatives and showed improved PCE with increased  $ \mathrm{J_{sc}} $ . The new derivatives they synthesized were more soluble in perovskite precursor compared to pristine fullerene [25]. The study suggested that since thermal degradation occurs at perovskite grain boundaries because ions leave the perovskite from there, addition of  $ \mathrm{PC}_{70}\mathrm{BM} $  binds the halogen atoms and restrict their migration due to thermal stress [26].

To the best of our knowledge, so far most of the work on fullerene additives in the perovskite precursor has been employed for planar PSCs. There has been no such report on the  $ \mathrm{PC}_{70}\mathrm{BM} $  blended in  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  for mesoporous scaffold-based PSCs followed by an antisolvent treatment using chlorobenzene. In this work, we have investigated the effect of different concentrations of  $ \mathrm{PC}_{70}\mathrm{BM} $  in perovskite precursor for the first time. Our results show the improved film quality with lesser grain boundaries, which ultimately contribute to higher FF and improved  $ \mathrm{J}_{\mathrm{sc}} $  in comparison to the pristine PSCs without  $ \mathrm{PC}_{70}\mathrm{BM} $ .

# 2. Experimental methods

# 2.1.Materials

Methylammonium iodide (MAI) and 18NRT Titania paste were purchased from Dyesol.  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  from TCI, Fluorine-doped-tin oxide (FTO) coated glass sheet from Global Nanotech, Spiro-OMeTAD, anhydrous Dimethylformamide (DMF), Titanium Isopropoxide (TTIP) from Sigma-Aldrich, [6,6]-phenyl-C70-butyric acid methyl ester  $ (\mathrm{PC}_{70}\mathrm{BM}) $  from Nano-C were purchased and used as received.

# 2.2. Device fabrication and measurements

Zn dust and 2 M HCl solution were used for patterning of FTO coated glass substrate [9]. The FTO has resistance of 7 Ω/square. The patterned substrates were then cleaned using ultrasonication first in a soap solution and then DI water, acetone and isopropanol (10 min for each step) [9]. A ~50 nm thick compact TiO₂ film was deposited on patterned and cleaned substrate by spin coating 70 μL of TiO₂ precursor solution at 4000 rpm for 1 min TiO₂ precursor was prepared by mixing 350 μL of TTIP in 5 mL of ethanol and 35 μL of 2 M HCl. Deposited films were annealed at 130 °C for 10 min and then at 500 °C for 30 min. After cooling down, the mesoporous Titania paste, diluted in ethanol (1:3.5 wt ratios) was coated on compact TiO₂ at 4000 rpm for 40 s and again heated on hot plate for 30 min at 500 °C. TiO₂ films were then subjected to the TiCl₄ treatment at 70 °C for 30 min. The TiCl₄ solution was prepared in DI having concentration of 40 mM. Then the films were rinsed with DI water and were heated on hot plate for 30 min. The film was allowed to cool then it was taken inside the glove box and the perovskite solution (1:1 M ratio of MAI and PbI₂ in 1:4 vol ratio of DMSO: DMF) was spin coated in 2 steps; 800 rpm for 10 s and 5000 rpm for 20 s. After 15 s, 110 μL of chlorobenzene as anti-solvent was drop-casted on perovskite while spinning at 5000 rpm. For PC₇₀BM added solution; 1 mg and 3 mg of PC₇₀BM were mixed in 1 mL of perovskite solution and the solutions were stirred at 55 °C for 3 h. Process was followed as above for perovskite coating. All the deposited perovskite films were annealed for 1 h at 100 °C. Perovskite films prepared from each solution containing 0 mg, 1 mg and 3 mg are hereon denoted as PSO, PS1, and PS3, respectively. The hole-transport material (HTM) was then deposited at 3000 rpm, for 40 s through spin-coating of (20 μL) 72.3 mg/mL solution of Spiro-OMeTAD doped with 28.8 μL of tert-butylpyridine and 17.7 μL of lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (Li-TFSI) solution (520 mg in 1 mL of acetonitrile). The partially prepared device was taken out from glove box and kept in the ambient overnight for the oxidation Spito-OMeTAD layer. Finally, the devices were transferred into a high vacuum chamber to thermally evaporate 80 nm thick Au back contact, resulting the device area of 0.06 cm². For large area devices, the area was measured using the mask of 1 cm² to define the active area.

# 2.3. Characterization

X-ray diffraction spectra (  $  \theta  $  -2θ scan) were done on samples deposited on substrates using X-ray diffractometer of X'Pert Pro PANanalytical in thin film mode with monochromatic CuKα (  $  \lambda = 1.5405 \AA  $  ) radiation source. Scans were performed with a 0.2°/min rate. To understand the optical response of film, absorbance and photoluminescence (PL) spectra were collected employing UV–Vis (Cary 7000) spectrophotometer and photoluminescence spectroscopy of Agilent (excitation at 470 nm). For the Field Emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM), Advance Scientific Tescan Mira3, was employed to take the images of films.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM, Asylum MFP-3D) was used to collect surface morphology and was also employed for Kelvin-Probe force microscopy (KPFM) measurement of layers. Profilometer (Bruker's DektakXT stylus) was used to measure the thickness of different films. Impedance spectroscopy measurements were carried out using Potentiostat/Galvanostat (Autolab 302 N, Metrohm, Netherlands). J-V characteristics of the photovoltaic devices were performed using a Keithley 2420 source unit under a simulated AM 1.5G spectrum. With an Oriel 9600 solar simulator, the light intensity was calibrated by a KG-5 Si diode. I-V measurements were carried out in a nitrogen glove box.

The electrical properties of the devices containing different   $  PC_{70}BM  $   concentrations were characterized by means of Impedance Spectroscopy (IS). IS measurements were carried out under dark conditions under biased voltage of 0.8 V over the frequency range

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Perovskite film characterization

Fig. 1 shows the FESEM images of perovskite layer coated on mesoporous  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  having different amounts of  $ \mathrm{PC}_{70}\mathrm{BM} $  added in the perovskite layer (PS0 (0 mg/mL), PS1 (1 mg/mL) and PS3 (3 mg/mL)). When no  $ \mathrm{PC}_{70}\mathrm{BM} $  was added, very good surface coverage of the perovskite film was achieved, and the grain sizes were in the range of  $ 200 - 300\mathrm{nm} $ . Furthermore, distinct grain boundaries can be seen in Fig. 1(a). Upon increasing the  $ \mathrm{PC}_{70}\mathrm{BM} $  content in sample PS1, the grains become larger. Although few pinholes are visible, good surface coverage is observed for this concentration (Fig. 1(b)). As the  $ \mathrm{PC}_{70}\mathrm{BM} $  concentration was further increased (PS3), grain size further increased but with larger pinholes (Fig. 1(c)) (Pinholes: with red circle). The magnified FESEM micrographs of PS0, PS1 and PS3 are shown in Fig. S1.

To understand the effect of  $ \mathrm{PC}_{70}\mathrm{BM} $  addition on the morphology of perovskite layer and surface roughness, AFM characterization of perovskite films coated on of  $ \mathrm{FTO / c - TiO_2 / meso - TiO_2} $  were carried out and the results are shown in Fig. 2. As the images show, the sample PS1 does not have very different roughness (26.9 nm) than PS0 (26.3 nm). However, the roughness increased with further increase in the  $ \mathrm{PC}_{70}\mathrm{BM} $  concentrations to  $ 30.9\mathrm{nm} $  for PS3. Increase in roughness was attributed due to increase in the pinhole concentration in the samples with

increase in   $  PC_{70}BM  $   concentration, also observed in FESEM images in Fig. 1.

Fig. 3 shows XRD patterns of FTO/c-TiO $ _2 $ /meso-TiO $ _2 $ /Perovskite with different concentrations of PC $ _{70} $ BM in perovskite which shows characteristic peak of tetragonal structured perovskite phase at 2θ of 14.1, 28.4, 31.7, 40.7 and 43.0, confirming the formation of perovskite layer [27]. PS1 film shows highest intensity of 110 plane suggesting improved crystallinity and texture of the perovskite layer. Further, in PS1 sample, PbI $ _2 $  peak at 12.6 is smaller than in the pristine film or other samples, suggesting that the film is more stable for this optimum PC $ _{70} $ BM addition. As the amount of PC $ _{70} $ BM in perovskite film further increases; crystallinity and stability of the film decreases.

# 5. Conclusions

Perovskite films were fabricated by adding varying concentrations of   $  PC_{70}BM  $   into the perovskite precursor solutions. With the addition of   $  PC_{70}BM  $   in mesoscopic PSCs, the grain size becomes larger with diffused grain boundaries. Antisolvent treatment with chlorobenzene, was effectively used to wash off the   $  PC_{70}BM  $   from the grain boundaries. This was supported by the fact that there is no blueshift in PL and larger hysteresis in I-V data. Thus,   $  PC_{70}BM  $   is only acting as additive rather than passivating layer in perovskite. For the optimum concentration of PS1 in the perovskite solution, there are larger grains with negligible pinholes thus resulting in improved   $  J_{sc}  $  . Furthermore, KPFM shows better band alignment for these films (PS1). Thus, best device showing 15.5% PCE compared to pristine device. Also, the addition of   $  PC_{70}BM  $   reduces the grain boundaries, thus prevented perovskite film from degrading at 85 °C and hence making devices thermally stable.
