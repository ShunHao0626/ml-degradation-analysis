# ABSTRACT

Precise control over the charge carrier dynamics throughout the device can result in outstanding performance of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrene sulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS) is the most actively studied hole transport material in p-i-n structured PSCs. However, charge transport in the PEDOT:PSS is limited and inefficient because of its low conductivity with the presence of the weak ionic conductor PSS. In addition, morphology of the underlying PEDOT:PSS layer in PSCs plays a crucial role in determining the optoelectronic quality of the active perovskite absorber layer. This work is focused on realization of a non-wetting conductive surface of hole transport layer suitable for the growth of larger perovskite crystalline domains. This is accomplished by employing a facile solvent-engineered (ethylene glycol and methanol) approach resulting in removal of the predominant PSS in PEDOT:PSS. The consequence of acquiring larger perovskite crystalline domains was observed in the charge carrier dynamics studies, with the achievement of higher charge carrier lifetime, lower charge transport time and lower transfer impedance in the solvent-engineered PEDOT:PSS-based PSCs. Use of this solvent-engineered treatment for the fabrication of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  PSCs greatly increased the device stability witnessing a power conversion efficiency of  $ 18.18\% $ , which corresponds to  $ \sim 37\% $  improvement compared to the untreated PEDOT:PSS based devices.

Efficient charge extraction from the perovskite layer and charge transport in charge-extraction layers play significant role in achieving high device efficiency. It is suggested that the best power conversion efficiency of solar cells can be achieved when the electron flux and the hole flux are balanced   $  [14,17]  $  . Poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):polystyrene sulfonate (PEDOT:PSS) is the most widely used hole transport material in p-i-n structured PSCs. However, charge transport in PEDOT:PSS is limited and inefficient. This is because of its inherent low conductivity due to the presence of the weak ionic conductor PSS   $  [18]  $  . The widely used ETL   $  [6,6]  $   – phenyl-  $  C_{61}  $  -butyric acid methyl ester (  $  PC_{61}BM  $  ) is more conductive than PEDOT:PSS   $  [19]  $  . Inefficient hole transport in PEDOT:PSS results in the imbalance in charge carrier transport that can promote charge accumulation either at the interface or at the traps, which can reduce the shunt resistance and results in hysteresis, respectively   $  [17,20]  $  .

Approaches modifying the PEDOT:PSS layer to achieve higher efficiency in PSCs mostly focused on doping   $  [21-24]  $   or adding interfacial layer   $  [25-32]  $  . Solvent-only treatments have also been considered as an effective method to modify PEDOT:PSS, which till now has been focused only on single step treatment. Few studies have indicated that the PSCs performance is even worsened by use of this single-step treatment   $  [33-36]  $  . Unsuitable surface morphology of PEDOT:PSS resulting into a non-optimized perovskite film morphology led to the inferior cell efficiency. In contrary, there are also some reports that employed solvents with high boiling point to treat PEDOT:PSS, which helped to achieve higher efficiency   $  [35-39]  $  . However, the device efficiencies in these solvent-treatment based reports are modest. Therefore, new strategies involving solvent-engineering of PEDOT:PSS are highly warranted to obtain an even higher efficiency of PSCs comparable to their n-i-p counterparts.

This work is the first approach that employs two solvents ethylene glycol (EG) and methanol (MeOH) at a two-step sequential treatment, which functioned synergistically to achieve the desired non-wetting conductive surface of PEDOT:PSS, suitable for the growth of larger perovskite crystalline domains to achieve higher efficiency and stability of p-i-n structured planar PSCs. This was accomplished by employing post-treatment of the PEDOT:PSS layer by dipping in polar solvents, EG and MeOH, sequentially (for 15 s each). This causes removal of the predominant PSS from PEDOT:PSS resulting in high conductivity and high-quality perovskite films consisting of larger grain size. The consequence of acquiring larger crystalline domain of the deposited perovskite was observed in charge carrier dynamics studies with the achievement of longer charge carrier lifetime, shorter charge transport time and lower charge transfer impedance in the solvent-only engineered PEDOT:PSS-based PSCs. Significant improvement in short-circuit current ( $ J_{sc} $ ) from 20.46 to 22.21 mA cm $ ^{-2} $ , open circuit voltage ( $ V_{oc} $ ) from 0.95 to 1.04 V, fill factor (FF) from 68% to 79%, and correspondingly efficiency from 13.23% to 18.18% was observed for PSCs fabricated using the treated PEDOT:PSS in comparison to the pristine PEDOT:PSS. In addition, device performance of treated PEDOT:PSS based PSCs witnessed improved stability and lower hysteresis. All these results reflected on the scope of implementing a simple and scalable device processing practice to further advance the research in perovskite photovoltaics.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1.Materials

Methylammonium iodide (CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ I) was purchased from Dyesol, PbI $ _2 $  (99%) and chlorobenzene (>99%) were obtained

from Acros Organics, and PC $ _{60} $ BM was acquired from Nano-C. Anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (>99.9%),  $ \gamma $ -butyrolactone (>99%), ethylene glycol (>99%), and rhodamine were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Methanol (>99.9%) and anhydrous isopropanol alcohol (IPA) were acquired from Fisher Scientific. Clevios™ P VP AI 4083 PEDOT:PSS was ordered from Heraeus. All the materials were used as received.

# 2.2. Deposition of PEDOT:PSS films

ITO-coated glass substrates of area   $  1.5 \times 1.5 \, cm^2  $   were cleaned with detergent water, de-ionized water, acetone and isopropanol, sequentially for 20 min each and then dried using nitrogen (  $  N_2  $  ) gun. The washed ITO substrates were then cleaned by UV-  $  O_3  $   for 25 min. PEDOT:PSS filtered through a 0.45   $  \mu  $  m syringe filter was spin coated at 4500 rpm for 60 s on the pre-treated ITO substrates. After spin coating, the films were annealed in ambient atmosphere at 145 °C for 10 min for the untreated PEDOT:PSS film. The post solvent treatment of PEDOT:PSS film was performed by air drying the film for 15 min after the spin coating and then dipping the films in EG and MeOH, sequentially for 15 s each. Then the films were annealed immediately at 145 °C for 10 min to remove the residual solvents.

# 2.3. Device fabrication of PSCs

PSCs were fabricated using both the pristine and treated PEDOT:PSS films. After cooling down the PEDOT:PSS films, the substrates were immediately transferred to a   $  N_{2}  $   filled glove box. The perovskite precursor was prepared by blending 581 mg of lead iodide (  $  PbI_{2}  $  ) and 209 mg of methylammonium iodide (  $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $  ) in 1 mL co-solvent mixture of   $  \gamma  $  -butyrolactone: DMSO (7:3 v/v), which was stirred for 2 h at 70°C inside the glovebox before spin coating. The perovskite precursor was then filtered and spin-coated at 750 rpm for the first 20 s, followed by 3500 rpm for an additional 60 s. The anti-solvent, toluene (160   $  \mu  $  L) was dripped onto the perovskite film after 40 s of spinning to remove excess DMSO solvent. Spin-coated perovskite films were then annealed at 50°C for 1 h. A   $  PC_{61}BM  $   solution in chlorobenzene (20 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was spin-coated at 2000 rpm for 40 s and annealed at 80°C for 5 min. Rhodamine (0.5 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   in IPA) was then spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 40 s to serve as an interfacial layer between the   $  PC_{61}BM  $   and top silver electrode. The devices were completed by thermal deposition of 80 nm silver, at a base pressure below 10  $  ^{-6}  $   Torr through a shadow mask with an active area of 0.16 cm  $  ^{2}  $  .

# 2.4. Characterization

Film thickness was measured using a Veeco Dektak 150 alpha step surface profiler and Agilent 5500 scanning probe microscopy. The transmittance and absorbance of films were measured using an Agilent 8453 UV-visible Spectroscopy System. To measure the absorbance in the ultraviolet region, PEDOT:PSS films were spin coated onto fused silica substrates. Contact angles were measured using an Advanced Surface Technology, Inc. Agilent 5500 scanning probe microscopy was used to take the atomic force microscopy (AFM) images. A Budget Sensors Multi 75-EG tip having platinum/iridium conductive coating was used for KPFM measurements. X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were executed using a Rigaku SmartLab system (2.2 kW and Cu Kα (1.54 Å) radiation). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed on a Thermo Fischer k-Alpha+ (Al Kα radiation). Samples were transferred from a glovebox into the system using a vacuum transfer module. Raman spectra were obtained using a Horiba LabRam HR 800 Raman spectrometer with a green laser beam of 532 nm wavelength.

# 3. Results and discussion

Electrical conductivity of the PEDOT:PSS films strongly depends on the fabrication process and resulting morphology [18,41]. Thus, optimization of the film morphology is crucial to achieve high conductivity. From our experiment, it was observed that consecutive post treatments of the PEDOT:PSS film by EG and MeOH resulted in uniform and smooth films with high conductivity. Fig. 1 presents a schematic diagram of the treatment procedure of PEDOT:PSS.

Negatively charged PSS serves as a charge-compensating counter poly-anion for the positively charged PEDOT backbone   $  [42]  $  . The EG treatment screens the ionic interaction between PEDOT and PSS by forming hydrogen bonding with PSS that leads to phase separation between PEDOT and PSS (Fig. S1 in Supplementary Information shows the PEDOT:PSS structure). Finally, dipping the film in MeOH reduces PSS proportion from the surface of the film along with EG resulting in a conductive PEDOT-rich film.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was employed to investigate the surface morphology of PEDOT:PSS films before and after the treatment. As depicted by the AFM topography images in Fig. 2(a) and (b), both the films are reasonably smooth with a higher RMS (root mean square) roughness of the treated film  $ (1.3\mathrm{nm}) $  compared to the pristine one  $ (0.95\mathrm{nm}) $ . Grain-like morphology appeared after post treatment of the PEDOT:PSS film. The crystallinity of PEDOT is inhibited by strong electrostatic coupling to the entangled PSS chains. However, EG can deplete the proportion of PSS from PEDOT:PSS films, which leads to phase separation between PEDOT and PSS [43]. Methanol can easily dissolve the phase-separated hydrophilic PSS and facilitate its removal from the surface [44]. These steps help PEDOT chains to self-orient or re-assemble themselves,

In order to study the conductivity variation in PEDOT:PSS induced by the post-solvent treatment, current-sensing atomic force microscopy (CS-AFM) measurements of samples was carried out, where the gray scale corresponds to vertical currents. In the case of the pristine PEDOT:PSS film (Fig. 2(c)), it can be seen that bright spots were randomly scattered throughout the film surrounded by insulating PSS area represented by the dominating dark regions. In case of pristine PEDOT:PSS film, conducting PEDOT grains are covered with a very thin layer of insulating PSS material [45,46]. This presence of the PSS-rich top layer led to very low concentration of the bright conducting spots. This indicates that the PEDOT grains are not interconnected, and PSS regions block the current from flowing. The post treatments resulted in a dramatic change in the PEDOT:PSS film (Fig. 2(d)), having uniformly distributed and interconnected conducting spots with currents remarkably higher than that of pristine film. The increase in the current can be attributed to the successful removal of the top PSS layer by the post treatments, which help to form a highly conductive three-dimensional network near to the surface after removal of the solvents by annealing.

Fig. S2(a) shows the in-plane current-voltage (I-V) characteristics of the pristine and treated PEDOT:PSS films obtained using the method described in the supporting information (Fig. S2(b)). The calculated conductivity of treated PEDOT:PSS was   $  19.15 \, S cm^{-1}  $  , which is notably higher than   $  1.6 \times 10^{-3} \, S cm^{-1}  $   of pristine PEDOT:PSS. These conductivity results very well corroborate with the current distribution acquired from CS-AFM measurement. The high conductivity of the post-treated PEDOT:PSS film is attributed to the

formation of a continuous network of the conducting polymer PE-DOT as a result of phase separation and removal of PSS.

Sequential treatment with EG and MeOH was chosen as the optimized approach of post treatment to achieve highly conductive PEDOT:PSS and a suitable surface to grow the perovskite layer. It is observed that, post treatment of PEDOT:PSS with methanol resulted in small change in conductivity of the PEDOT:PSS. On the other hand, EG alone could improve the conductivity significantly but results in an uneven distribution of disconnected conductive spots, as shown by the CS-AFM image in Fig. S3. Moreover, due to high viscosity and boiling point of EG, it resulted in a non-uniform surface (Fig. S4), which is not suitable for deposition of the rest of the layers. Combined treatment of dipping for 15 s each in EG and MeOH sequentially resulted in a both highly conductive and uniform surface ideal for growth of compact, smooth and larger grain perovskite.

Depletion of PSS from the PEDOT:PSS layer after the post-treatment is confirmed by XPS measurement (Fig. 3(a)). The higher binding energy peak at around  $ 168\mathrm{eV} $  represents the sulfur atoms in PSS, whereas, S  $ (2p) $  doublet around  $ 163 - 166\mathrm{eV} $  is assigned to the sulfur atoms in PEDOT [47,48]. The calculated surface ratios of PSS to PEDOT from the integrated S  $ (2p) $  peak area were 6.1:1 and 2.5:1 for the pristine and treated PEDOT:PSS films, respectively, showing the reduction of  $ 59\% $  of PSS content as a result of the post treatment. Furthermore, the reduction of PSS is also confirmed by the UV-visible absorption studies of PEDOT:PSS films.

The decrease in the UV absorption and the film thickness are attributed to the significant reduction of PSS from the film, which results in better-connected PEDOT chains. Similar observations of reduction in film thickness due to decrease in PSS concentration have been reported [44,47]. It is also evident from the transmittance spectra (inset of Fig. 3(b)) that treated PEDOT:PSS appears to be more transparent compared to the pristine film. Reduced thickness due to removal of PSS and the structural changes lead to the increased transmittance of the treated PEDOT:PSS.

In addition, Raman spectra confirm the conformational change from benzoid (coiled conformation) to quinoid structure (linear or expanded-coil conformation) of PEDOT chains due to the treatment of PEDOT:PSS by EG and MeOH (Fig. 3(c)). The intense peak centered at  $ 1439\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $  of pristine PEDOT:PSS is assigned to the  $ \mathrm{C}\alpha = C\beta $  symmetric stretching vibration of PEDOT, which is redshifted after the treatment. This shift indicates that the linear or expanded-coil conformations dominate in the treated PEDOT:PSS film [50]. In the coil conformation, the bond between thiophene rings in the PEDOT chain is similar to a  $ \sigma $  bond. Thus, the density of conjugated  $ \pi $ -electrons is low, and they are not completely delocalized in the benzoid structure. But delocalization of conjugated  $ \pi $ -electrons occurs throughout the entire chain in the linear or coil-expanded conformation as the thiophene rings orient in nearly the same plane. Hence, the restructuring to expanded coil or linear conformation results in increased conjugation length of the PEDOT chain, and charge carriers are expected to be transported more rapidly, which can result in the enhanced conductivity [51-53].

Contact angle can be a good indication of the surface wettability of the pristine and treated PEDOT:PSS films. As shown in Fig. 4(a) and (b), higher contact angle is observed in the treated PEDOT:PSS, indicating that the post treatment increased the hydrophobicity of the PEDOT:PSS. Removal of PSS after the treatment allows PEDOT crystallites to orient and pack together to form a well-ordered crystalline structure, enabling the formed PEDOT:PSS film less hydrophilic [54].

AFM topography images of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite films (Fig. 4(c) and (d)) clearly indicate variation of grain size depending on the wetting capability of the PEDOT:PSS film. The average grain size was much larger for  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  deposited on more hydrophobic films of treated PEDOT:PSS. It is also evidenced from the SEM images in Fig. S5. Recent studies on different hole transport layers used in p-i-n structured PSCs also showed that relatively hydrophobic substrates facilitate larger grain growth of the perovskite layers [55]. AFM 3D topography images (Fig. 4(e), (f)) also show more compact and smooth perovskite film (RMS=14.0 nm) formed on treated PEDOT:PSS film compared to a pristine one (RMS=14.4 nm).

Larger crystalline domain growth of the perovskite layers deposited on relatively non-wetting substrates was also observed in XRD patterns (Fig. 4(g)). After treatment, an increased XRD peak intensity is observed for the perovskite film on top of the treated PEDOT:PSS compared to that on the pristine PEDOT:PSS film. # 4. Conclusions

In summary, growth of larger perovskite crystalline domains on a non-wetting conductive PEDOT:PSS hole transport layer was demonstrated using a facile two-step sequential solvent treatment method for the first time. Sequential post solvent treatment of PEDOT:PSS with EG and MeOH dramatically increased the conductivity and hydrophobicity of the film, which is attributed to the partial depletion or reduction of PSS component from the PEDOT:PSS layer. This facilitated the formation of smooth and highly crystalline perovskite films with larger grains. This achievement of highly conductive HTL and efficient charge extraction with high-quality perovskite films led to a remarkable increase in device efficiency to 18.18%±0.40%, demonstrating a ~37% improvement compared to pristine PEDOT:PSS-based cells. In addition, better stability was also attained using the two-step sequential solvent treatment method. This simple processing practice can easily be extended to any organic optoelectronic based devices to reduce traps/defect sites and facilitate efficient charge transport to realize better performance.
