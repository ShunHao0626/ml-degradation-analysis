# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted intensive attention and emerged as promising candidates for next generation photovoltaics, owing to the excellent optoelectronic properties of perovskite active layer, including high absorption coefficient, long carrier diffusion length, long carrier lifetime and high mobility [1-3]. Recently, a certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 25.2\% $  was recorded after tremendous effort from worldwide research groups [4], such as compositional engineering [5-7], solvent engineering [8], interfacial engineering [9-11] and deposition process [12]. Commonly,

Interfacial engineering is an effective method to improve the performance of perovskite solar cells. Nevertheless, it is still challenging to find a material as interfacial layer with the dual function of defect-passivation and efficient charge-transfer. In this work, organophosphorus ligands, trioctylphosphine oxide (TOPO) and triphenylphosphine oxide (TPPO), were used as passivators through antisolvent process for the interfacial modification. Significantly, the organophosphorus ligands can effectively passivate the defects of perovskite crystals through the formation of Pb–O bond between organophosphorus ligand and undercoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ion in perovskite. More interestingly, benzene rings with   $  \pi  $   electron in TPPO facilitate the charge transfer between perovskite and hole-transporting layer, achieving the best power conversion efficiency of 18.29% with negligible hysteresis, as well as excellent long-term stability with remaining nearly 85% of the initial efficiency after 2000 h storage under 50% humidity ambient air. In contrast, uncharged octyl alkanes in TOPO hinder the charge transfer and lead to the accumulation of charges at interface although it is a good passivator in light emitting devices for charge confinement. Our findings shed light on the importance of efficient transfer when defect-passivation is employed in solar cell.

low-temperature solution-process is widely used to grow perovskite thin films with polycrystalline nature, which have higher density of defect or trap states at the surface and grain boundaries (GBs) than their single-crystal counterparts. Significantly, the trap states can act as charge carrier recombination centers through attracting electron/hole and inducing nonradiative recombination [1,13], further limiting the open-circuit voltage  $ (\mathrm{V_{oc}}) $  and PCE, which is the major reason of current record being far from the theoretical Shockley-Queisser efficiency limit [14]. Moreover, the defect states facilitate moisture and oxygen diffusion and lead to the degradation of perovskite active layer, seriously decreasing the stability of PSCs. Generally, ions migration, oxidization of halide ions and desorption of methylammonium iodide (MAI) are found during the formation of perovskite crystal with thermal annealing process, leaving with vacancies or defects (undercoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ions and/or Pb clusters) at surface and GBs [15,16]. Based on the theoretical and experimental research, the undercoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and/or Pb clusters are the dominant trap states and act as electron-hole nonradiative recombination centers [17,18]. Significantly, positively charged   $  Pb^{2+}  $   coordination with electron donor materials (O-donor, N-donor and S-donor) or Lewis bases is an effective defect-passivation method to reduce nonradiative recombination trap states [19–24]. For instance, phenylalkylamine [22], benzenethiols [9], methimazole [21], 1.1-dicyanomethylene-3-indanone, (3,9-bis(2-methylene-(3-(1,1-dicyanomethylene)-indanone))-5,5,11,11-tetrakis(4-hexylphenyl)-dithieno[2,3-d:2',3'-d']-s-indaceno[1,2-b:5,6-b']dithiophene (ITIC) [20], and dithienobenzodithiophene-based π-conjugated polymer PDTBDT-FBT [25] were used as electron donor- or Lewis bases-based passivators for the coordination of   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ions via interfacial engineering, successfully improving the overall device performance. Additionally, large perovskite grain size with reduced GBs and high-quality perovskite film with enhanced crystallinity are also desired for the effective photon capture, fast charge transfer, suppressed moisture/oxygen penetration and reduced defect density in polycrystalline films [26–29]. Additives, such as dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) [8,30], 1,8-diiodooctane (DIO) [31], thiourea [28] and lead thiocyanate [32], were added into the perovskite precursor to retard crystal growth by forming immediate complexes with   $  PbI_{2}  $ , finally converting into perovskite crystal by an intramolecular exchanging and obtaining large grain size. However, the additives with inherent insulating moieties, which incorporate on the perovskite surfaces, may hamper charge transfer, inducing charge accumulation at the interface between perovskite and charge-transporting layers, as well as being responsible for the hysteresis of PSC performance [33,34]. It is still challenging to find a suitable passivator for the perovskite defect-passivation without sacrificing the charge-transfer, as well as growing high-quality perovskite film for further enhancement of PCE and stability of PSCs.

Herein, simple, air-stable and cost-effective electron donors, organophosphorus ligands, were used as passivators through antisolvent process. Trioctylphosphine oxide (TOPO) and triphenylphosphine oxide (TPPO) were chosen for the comparison to clarify the mechanism of defect-passivation and charge-transfer at the interfaces. Basically, TOPO and TPPO with tetrahedral structure (Fig. S1), which are three organic functional groups (octyl alkanes or benzene rings) at the three vertices in a plane and the oxygen atom at the fourth vertex, were commonly used as stabilizing or crystallization-inducing reagents [35,36]. The rationale for the selection of TOPO and TPPO are: (i) the dipolar phosphorus-oxygen bond and basic oxygen center with rich electrons allow the binding between oxygen atom and undercoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  ion, as well as induce the crystallizing of perovskite crystals, which may reduce the defect density and increase perovskite grain size; (ii) octyl alkanes or benzene rings are hydrophobic, which may prevent the perovskite from moisture. Moreover, benzene rings in TPPO with  $ \pi $ -conjugation structure favor charge transport, which may reduce hysteresis and improve PCE [22,37]. Recently, hybrid perovskite film passivated by TOPO exhibits an external photoluminescence quantum efficiency (PLQE) over  $ 30\% $  [38] and internal PLQE over  $ 90\% $  [39], approaching the radiative limit of ideal solar cell, yet the result has not been verified experimentally at the device level. This further motivates us to investigate the influence of phosphine oxide-based passivation on the device performance.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, organophosphorus ligands are introduced for the interfacial modification with antisolvent process, which can attach at the surface and GBs of perovskite films and passivate the defects with coordination bonds between electron-rich O element in organophosphorus ligand and undercoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  ion in perovskite. After the formation of perovskite crystals, defect density is remarkably reduced

Additionally, benzene rings in TPPO facilitate the charge transfer between perovskite and hole-transporting layer, significantly enhancing the PCE from 17.24% to 18.29% with negligible J-V hysteresis in a mesoporous structure. Moreover, the unencapsulated PSCs with interfacial TPPO modification show a remarkable improvement in air stability, retaining 85% of the initial PCE after 2000 h storing in ambient air with 50% humidity. On the contrary, uncharged octyl alkanes in TOPO hinder the charge transfer and lead to the accumulation of charges at interface, reducing the PCE to 16.85%. These results reveal the effective passivation of organophosphorus ligands and clearly illustrate that defect passivation must be accompanied with efficient charge transfer at the interface to improve the PCE. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that reports the improved performance of perovskite solar cells by TPPO passivation.

# 2. Experimental methods

Device fabrication: FTO-coated glass substrates were cleaned with sonication in detergent, deionized water, acetone and isopropyl alcohol for 15 min, respectively. After being dried by   $  N_{2}  $   flow, the substrates were treated with UV-ozone for 20 min to remove the organic residues. Then, the   $  TiO_{2}  $   precursor solution for compact layer was prepared by dissolving 1 ml titanium (IV) isopropoxide and 12   $  \mu  $  l HCL (12 M) in 10 ml ethanol. The   $  TiO_{2}  $   compact layer was coated on FTO substrates by spinning the precursor solution at 6000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 450 °C for 30 min. After cooling down to room temperature, a mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   was deposited onto compact   $  TiO_{2}/FTO  $   substrates by spin coating at 4000 rpm for 20 s, using a 30 nm particle paste (Dysesol 30 NR-D) diluted in ethanol (150 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ), then sintered at 450 °C for 30 min. The obtained substrates were transferred into argon filled glovebox after cooling down to 120 °C for further deposition. The perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving FAI (1 M),   $  PbI_{2}  $   (1.1 M), MABr (0.2 M) and   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (0.2 M) in anhydrous dimethylformamide (DMF):dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) = 4:1 (v:v). Then 42   $  \mu  $  l CsI stock solution (1.5 M in DMSO) was added into the 1 ml FA/MA mixed perovskite solution to achieve Cs/FA/MA triple cation perovskite precursor solution. The perovskite film was deposited in a two-step spin coating process at 1000 rpm for 10 s and 6000 rpm for 20 s, and 5 s before the end of second step, 110   $  \mu  $  l chlorobenzene or organophosphorus ligand containing chlorobenzene (2 mg ml  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was dripped on the perovskite, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 1 h. To reveal the passivation effect on photoelectric properties and charge carrier recombination dynamics of the three perovskite films, steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved PL (TRPL) decay measurements were performed based on the structure of glass/perovskite. Fig. 4a shows a stronger emission at  $ 765~\mathrm{nm} $  in TOPO and TPPO modified perovskite films, indicating less charge quenching and nonradiative recombination compared with control film. A slight red-shift can be observed in the narrower bandgap modified perovskites, which is in a good agreement with UV-vis result. TRPL decay was further used to analyze the passivation effect (Fig. 4b). The curves are fitted with biexponential decay function  $ (\mathrm{y} = \mathrm{A}_1\exp (-t / \tau_1) + \mathrm{A}_2\exp (-t / \tau_2) + y_0) $  and fitted data are summarized in Table 1. Basically, the fast decay component  $ \tau_{1} $  is attributed to bimolecular recombination of photogenerated free carriers, while the slow decay component  $ \tau_{2} $  is assigned to trap-assisted recombination induced by defects [9,50]. From the fitted results,  $ \tau_{1} $  is not obviously changed in the three perovskite films, on the contrary,  $ \tau_{2} $  is significantly enhanced from 66.8 ns (Control) to 88.2 ns (TOPO) and 129.8 ns (TPPO). Additionally, the average PL lifetime is also greatly increased from 52.5 ns (Control) to 72.1 ns (TOPO) and 121.1 ns (TPPO). The longer PL lifetime in organophosphorus ligands-modified perovskite films indicates less nonradiative recombination and lower defect density, resulting in the longer carrier lifetime and suggesting the effective defect-passivation with TOPO and TPPO at surface and GBs of pervoskite film.

To further elucidate the interfacial charge transfer between perovskite and hole-transporting layer in TOPO and TPPO modified devices, steady-state PL and TRPL measurements were carried out based on the structure of glass/perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD, as shown in Fig. 7a and b. Obviously, a stronger quenching in TPPO-modified device and weaker quenching in TOPO-modified device are observed when compare with control device. Additionally, from the fitting data of TRPL in Table S1, TPPO-modified device exhibits a relatively shorter average PL lifetime of  $ 13.7\mathrm{ns} $ , however, TOPO-modified device shows a relatively longer average PL lifetime of  $ 39.3\mathrm{ns} $ , when compare with control device of 14.7 ns. The PL and TRPL results indicate a faster interfacial charge-transfer between perovskite film and hole-transporting layer in TPPO-modified device, resulting from the benzene rings functional group with  $ \pi $ -conjugation structure in TPPO, which favors the charge transport. Nevertheless, the slower interfacial charge-transfer in TOPO-modified device may be due to the uncharged and insulating octyl alkanes in

TOPO, resulting in the charge accumulation or transport suppression at the interface [54]. Furthermore, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was applied to further investigate the charge-transfer process and resistance information, as shown in Fig. 7c. The device based on FTO/compact  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ /mesoporous  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $ /perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD/Au structure was used for evaluation. Basically, the high frequency and low frequency in Nyquist plots are related to charge-transfer resistance  $ (\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{tr}}) $  and recombination resistance  $ (\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{rec}}) $ , respectively [34]. The fitted parameters of the equivalent circuit model are listed in Table S2. The TPPO- and TOPO-modified devices have higher recombination resistance than control device, indicating a reduction of defect density and carrier recombination in the bulk devices. Moreover, the series resistance  $ (\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{s}}) $  and charge-transfer resistance in TPPO modified device are much lower, while the  $ \mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{s}} $  and  $ \mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{tr}} $  in TOPO modification are much higher compared to that of control device, proving the improved charge transport between perovskite film and Spiro-OMeTAD after interfacial TPPO modification, which is in agreement with the photoluminescence results. In addition, surface conductivity of the three perovskite films was measured in the dark, as shown in Fig. 7d. Obviously, the TPPO-modified perovskite film presents a better conductivity than control and TOPO-modified perovskite film do, further demonstrating the fast charge-transfer in TPPO-modified PSCs. Also, the reduced hysteresis in TPPO-modified PSC is ascribed to not only the decreased defect density in perovskite film but also the fast charge-transfer between perovskite film and hole-transporting layer. Accordingly, the TPPO molecule with phosphorus oxygen and benzene ring functional groups can effectively passivate defects through forming Pb-O bond between oxygen atom in TPPO and undercoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  ion in perovskite film at surface and GBs, resulting in the reduction of defect density, improved crystallinity and facilitation of charge-transfer, as illustrated in Fig. S5.
