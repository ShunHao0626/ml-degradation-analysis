# Abstract:

We report on the improved operational stability of unencapsulated perovskite solar cells (PSCs) aged in ambient atmosphere at elevated temperatures (70 °C) for >1000 hours under constant illumination and bias at 30-50% relative humidity. We identify a previously unseen interfacial degradation mechanism concerning the use of an interlayer,   $  MoO_{x}  $  , which was originally added to increase operational stability. Specifically, the hole-transport layer/  $  MoO_{x}  $   interface buckles under illumination at 70 °C, which leads to delamination and rapid losses in short-circuit current density corresponding to an average   $  t_{80}  $   of ~55 hours. By judicious evaluation of various hole-transport layers, interlayers, and contacts, we find that replacing the   $  MoO_{x}  $   with a   $  VO_{x}  $   interlayer, regardless of the other components in the solar cell, alleviates this buckling issue due to its higher activation barrier towards crystallization, leading to significant gains in PSC operational stability. Unencapsulated devices aged in ambient atmosphere with a   $  VO_{x}  $   interlayer retain 71% of initial PCE on average after 1100 hours of constant illumination and bias at 70 °C (  $  t_{80} \sim 645  $   hours). Currently, this is the highest temperature reported for the operational stability of unencapsulated n-i-p PSCs aged in air. Identification of a new facet of the complex degradation mechanisms in PSCs will allow for targeted acceleration testing to speed the deployment of low-cost, long-lasting electricity generation under realistic operating temperatures.

Developing robust perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with high efficiency at low cost could spur new industrial growth to generate a much larger portion of our energy demand  $  ^{1-3}  $  . While initial efficiencies are often high, long term operation causes PSC performance losses that typically originate from material instabilities associated with individual device components/interfaces and from external stressors encountered during cell operation  $  ^{4}  $  .

Over the last decade, tremendous advances have been realized in enhancing the stability of various cell components  $  ^{5-13}  $  and careful consideration of the mechanical stressors  $  ^{14-18}  $ . In this report, we focus specifically on operational stability, which we define as aging under constant illumination and characterization under degradation conditions (e.g., maximum power point tracking or constant bias with periodic current-voltage sweeps). Many operational PSC stability studies are performed at room temperature, yet operation at higher temperature is more relevant to temperatures seen during field testing  $  ^{19}  $ , which can regularly reach  $  >70\;^{\circ}C  $ . With elevated temperature and increased reaction kinetics, degradation processes accelerate and this performance data can be used to speed up the engineering and optimization of device stability. Accelerated testing to quickly identify points of failure is critical for commercialization efforts.

In Table S1, operational stability reports of both p-i-n and n-i-p PSCs at temperatures   $  >50\ ^{\circ}C  $   are summarized  $  ^{7,8,20-26}  $  . Among these previous studies, initial device efficiencies vary widely (from   $  \sim12\%  $   to   $  \sim21\%  $  ), as do the exact testing conditions, including the atmosphere (  $  N_{2}  $   vs humid air), use of encapsulant, and whether or not the solar cell is held under load during aging. Nevertheless, there have been critical breakthroughs in all components of the solar cell. Arora et al. achieved excellent operational stability with an inorganic hole-transport layer (HTL), CuSCN, and a reduced graphene oxide interlayer to prevent gold contact corrosion  $  ^{21}  $  , and recently eliminated the gold contact to achieve similar stability outcomes  $  ^{27}  $  . Saliba et al.  $  ^{26}  $   and Wang et al.  $  ^{7}  $   focused on improvements to the perovskite active layer (PAL), Ren et al.  $  ^{25}  $   focused on improvements to the HTL, and Bi et al.  $  ^{20}  $   focused on PAL surface treatments. While it is challenging to directly compare these reports because of differences in their stability testing conditions  $  ^{28,29}  $  , it is clear that the bulk properties of each layer in the device stack are extremely important for device longevity even without consideration of mechanical stressors. Overall, a high temperature understanding of the degradation of contacts/interfaces during operation has been relatively unexplored for PSCs.

We recently reported an average power conversion efficiency (PCE) retention of 88% for unencapsulated devices after 1000 hours of continuous operation at 28 °C in ambient atmosphere and 10-20% relative humidity (RH) through careful control of the interfaces and judicious choice of stable PAL, HTL, and electron-transport layer (ETL).  $  ^{9}  $   For instance, the molybdenum oxide (  $  MoO_{x}  $  ) interlayer between the HTL and top metal contact prevented ion migration between the PAL and the aluminum contact  $  ^{9,13,30}  $  . In addition, the HTL was improved by a metal-free radical triarylamine dopant, EH44-ox, ((9-(2-ethylhexyl)-N  $  ^{2}  $  , N  $  ^{2}  $  , N  $  ^{7}  $  , N  $  ^{7}  $  -tetrakis(4-methoxyphenyl)-9H-carbazole-2,7-diamine bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide), which replaces traditional lithium- and cobalt-based HTL dopants that have been shown to be detrimental to device durability  $  ^{31,32}  $  . We then identified an alternate HTL, 3EtCz (3EtCz,  $  N^{2},N^{7}  $  -bis(9-ethyl-9H-carbazol-3-yl)-9-(2-ethylhexyl)-  $  N^{2},N^{7}  $  -bis(4-methoxyphenyl)-9H-carbazole-2,7-diamine), with a  $  T_{g}\sim100\ ^{\circ}C  $  that showed improved operational stability, maintaining  $  \sim70\%  $  initial PCE after 500 hours at 50 °C  $  ^{33}  $ . However, when the operating temperature is held above 50 °C (vide infra), these stability gains were lost despite the intrinsic stability of each component of the device stack. This example demonstrates that even though each component in the device architecture is notionally stable on its own, the device in its entirety can still be unstable due to processes exacerbated at elevated temperatures.

Here, we focus on measuring operational stability of unencapsulated devices under constant illumination and bias at   $  70\ °C  $   in an ambient atmosphere with 30-50% RH. Even though commercialized modules will be encapsulated, no encapsulation completely prevents water and oxygen diffusion. Moreover, the cost of each component, including encapsulation, must be kept low to penetrate the solar market, especially if modules are replaced frequently  $  ^{3}  $  . We also note that encapsulation of solar cells can extend the lifetimes of PSCs by at least double, thus any values reported here are likely underreporting ‘true’ stabilities once encapsulated  $  ^{8}  $  . Our aggressive testing protocol is important as it identifies and expeditiously tests multiple degradation factors for PSCs at once, which is critical to develop realistic degradation rates.

We first identify the HTL/MoO $ _{x} $  interface as morphologically unstable and then report a dramatic improvement in high temperature PSC operational stability by replacing the MoO $ _{x} $  with VO $ _{x} $  to bypass this degradation pathway. For MoO $ _{x} $ -based devices, we observe a large increase in the overall surface roughness of MoO $ _{x} $  at elevated temperatures under ambient illumination which leads to delamination and rapid decay of photovoltaic parameters, primarily short-circuit current (J $ _{sc} $ ). We identify that the increased surface roughness originates at the MoO $ _{x} $ /HTL interface, and determine that MoO $ _{x} $  morphology changes over time on top of the organic HTL. Here, we replaced MoO $ _{x} $  with VO $ _{x} $  $ ^{34} $ , a metal oxide with a relatively higher activation barrier for morphological deformation yet similar workfunction to MoO $ _{x} $ , and successfully remedy J $ _{sc} $  losses. While VO $ _{x} $  has been integrated into PSCs as an interlayer $ ^{34} $ , HTL dopant $ ^{35} $ , or post-treatment $ ^{36} $ , no reports focus upon the operational durability of PSCs. With a VO $ _{x} $ /IZO contact, unencapsulated devices aged at 70 °C for 1100 hours in ambient atmosphere and 30-50% RH maintained on average 71% of their initial PCE (as measured at 70 °C in ambient atmosphere) and 80% of their initial PCE (as measured at room temperature under nitrogen). To the best of the authors' knowledge, this is the first report of operational stability of unencapsulated n-i-p PSCs at 70 °C in ambient atmosphere. A systematic understanding of the degradation mechanisms in addition to instabilities that originate from material selection and mechanical stress in PSCs at 70 °C will allow for the deployment of low-cost, long-lasting electricity generation under realistic operating temperatures.

We first characterize in detail the potential degradation mechanisms commonly associated with the HTL, as it is often thought that the doped organic HTL is the weak link in n-i-p PSC stability. As we will show, however, we see no signs of degradation in the HTL. First, poor operational stability in PSCs can originate from island formation and delamination from the top contact can occur after prolonged device operation above the HTL's   $  T_{g}  $  , resulting in new pathways for moisture ingress and subsequent PAL degradation, and has been linked to   $  J_{sc}  $   loss  $  ^{37}  $  . We re-affirm that the doped 3EtCz-based HTL, as opposed to doped EH44 or doped spiro-OMeTAD, is more robust and maintains its morphological integrity under the aging conditions presented in this study. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) confirms no phase transitions below 100 °C for either the pristine 3EtCz  $  ^{33}  $   or the 3EtCz-ox dopant,   $  ((N^{2}, N^{7}-\text{bis}(9-\text{ethyl}-9H-\text{carbazol}-3-\text{yl})-9-(2-\text{ethylhexyl})-N^{2}, N^{7}-\text{bis}(4-\text{methoxyphenyl})-9H-\text{carbazole}-2,7-\text{diamine bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide}))  $   (Figure S5). Phase transitions of the HTM/dopant are often lower than that of the mixture's constituents (3EtCz, 3EtCz-ox, and 4-tert-butylpyridine (tBP))  $  ^{39}  $  , so we hypothesized island formation in the 3EtCz-based HTL was plausible. However, SEM cross-sections show that the 3EtCz-based HTL remains a continuous film with no evidence of island formation (Figures S6-S8) in devices aged in the dark under nitrogen at 28 °C, 50 °C, and 70 °C for ~4 days.

To probe for potential HTL dopant reduction only at the PAL/HTL interface, we fabricated fresh devices to simulate the impacts of significant dopant quenching over time. Performance metrics of devices with  $ \sim 70\% $ ,  $ \sim 20\% $ , and  $ 0\% $  of the typical dopant concentration were collected (Table S6). To see significant performance losses ( $ >10\% $  PCE loss), more than  $ 30\% $  of the dopant must be quenched during device aging (Table S6), which is not consistent with the amount of quenching seen in aged films (Figure S10). Further, the majority of the performance losses that occur from removing dopant from the HTL result from FF losses, and not in  $ \mathrm{J}_{\mathrm{sc}} $  as observed in the high temperature durability testing (Figure 1). This evidence suggests that dopant quenching does not lead to significant performance losses under aging conditions presented in this work. Thus, we find that the various HTL-related processes (low  $ \mathrm{T_g} $ , dopant diffusion, dopant quenching) do not appreciably occur under aging conditions presented in this work and are not the underlying cause of observed device-level performance losses.

As the HTL alone is not the apparent cause of the observed degradation, we compared devices with different contacts (  $  MoO_{x}/Al  $  ,   $  MoO_{x}/IZO  $  , or Au (no   $  MoO_{x}  $  )) (Figure 1g,h, Figures S3-S4, Table S3). Metal ingress into the device is another commonly identified degradation mechanism where halide ion migration from the PAL results in corrosion and increased roughness of the metal contact after aging  $  ^{43-45}  $  . However, time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) found the distribution of iodide is nearly indistinguishable in aged and fresh devices (Figure S11), demonstrating that no iodine reacted with the top metal contact and there is minimal ion migration during device aging. Additionally, when we exchange the aluminum contact for the inert IZO (Figure 1c, Figure S4), we still observe similar roughening of contacts after aging. Devices with an IZO contact degrade relatively slower than devices with Al, yet we still observe similar losses in   $  J_{sc}  $   after the initial burn-in of   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF (Figure 1, Figure S3). While we do not observe an apparent increase in the surface roughness of the aged gold contact (Figure 1d, Figure S4), these devices ceased to function after 50 hours (Figure 1, Figures S3-S4), demonstrating that high temperature operational stabilities tests exacerbate kinetic reactions (ion movement) not seen at lower temperatures. The entire PAL film, in addition to the aged pixels, appears yellow (Figure 1d, Figure S4), which is consistent with previous reports indicating the need for a metal oxide interlayer between the HTL and metal contact in an n-i-p device architecture  $  ^{9,13}  $  . From this data, we can conclude that the morphological deformation is not due to aluminum corrosion or significant ion migration during device operation.

AFM confirms that the surface roughness of the 3EtCz/  $  MoO_{x}  $   films aged at 70 °C increases by nearly two orders of magnitude as compared to both fresh films and films aged at 28 °C (Figure 2, Table S5), and a characteristic wrinkling pattern is observed  $  ^{16}  $  . This experiment was repeated in a nitrogen atmosphere and we obtained similar results, signifying that this process is intrinsic to material properties and not dependent upon water and oxygen exposure, and thus will occur even in encapsulated devices. We expanded the scope of organic surfaces to determine whether this increased surface roughening was dependent upon the radical triarylamine-based dopant, 3EtCz-ox, or the HTL matrix, 3EtCz. We find increased surface roughening in undoped 3EtCz films (Table S8, Figure S14-S15) and LiTFSI/FK209-doped spiro-OMeTAD films as well, demonstrating the ubiquity of this   $  MoO_{x}  $   morphology change (Table S9, Figure S16-S17). Surprisingly, X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements show that both the HTL and   $  MoO_{x}  $   in 3EtCz-based doped HTL/  $  MoO_{x}  $   films (3EtCz, 3EtCz-ox, tBP) remain amorphous even with this morphological deformation after ~4 days of aging at either temperature (Figure 2). Yet, over the course of months of storage in the dark after ~168 hours of aging in light and heat, we observe the crystallization onset  $  ^{48,49}  $   of   $  MoO_{x}  $   in aged films (Figure S18). While the buckling and crystallization rates are beyond the scope of this work, it is clear that the morphology of   $  MoO_{x}  $   deposited on an organic substrate changes significantly over time after exposure to elevated temperatures and ambient illumination and demonstrates that the interactions between these materials (i.e. organic/metal oxide interface) within the device stack are also critically important to consider when investigating device stability.

To test whether metal oxides are generally incompatible with organic interfaces at elevated temperatures, we also assessed   $  3EtCz/VO_{x}  $   films, as   $  VO_{x}  $   and   $  MoO_{x}  $   have both been used as hole-injecting layers in similar applications  $  ^{34,52}  $  . Amorphous   $  VO_{x}  $   films (15 nm) were deposited by atomic layer deposition (ALD) as outlined in the Supporting Information. Low-temperature (80 °C) ALD   $  VO_{x}  $   was used in place of thermally evaporated   $  VO_{x}  $   to ensure the deposition of an amorphous metal oxide  $  ^{53}  $  .   $  VO_{x}  $   demonstrates greater morphological stability as   $  3EtCz/VO_{x}  $  , spiro-OMeTAD/  $  VO_{x}  $  , and undoped   $  3EtCz/VO_{x}  $   films all remained as smooth as fresh samples after aging at 28 °C and 70 °C (Figure 2, Tables S5, S8-S9, Figures S12, S15, S17). XRD shows the   $  VO_{x}  $   remains amorphous as well when deposited on 3EtCz-based HTL (Figure 2). Taken together,   $  VO_{x}  $   demonstrates greater morphologically stability at elevated temperatures as compared to   $  MoO_{x}  $   when deposited on organic surfaces.

It is evident that the soft organic substrate is the origin of the   $  MoO_{x}  $   morphological instability. Buckling and wrinkling generally arise from isotropic compressive stress  $  ^{54}  $  , and the origins of the nucleation sites for morphological deformation can be highly varied. It is possible that the compressive stress originates from redox chemistry at the HTL/  $  MoO_{x}  $   interface, but after probing the HTL/metal oxide interface by X-ray photoemission spectroscopy (XPS) we find little evidence of a significant change in oxidation state of either molybdenum or vanadium (Figure S19). Thus, oxidation of the HTL is likely not the primary origin of the observed wrinkling. Even without redox chemistry at the HTL/metal oxide interface, thin film crystallization activation barriers have been shown to be substrate dependent. Nanoscopic pores on polymer substrates have been shown to lead to order of magnitude changes in crystallization nucleation rates of the film deposited on top of the polymer substrate  $  ^{55-59}  $  . This suggests nanoscopic pores on the organic surface upon which the metal oxide is deposited likely serve as nucleation sites for the observed morphological deformation, and that the activation barrier for   $  MoO_{x}  $   buckling is greatly reduced on the amorphous organic HTL surface. While the surfaces of the organic HTLs used in this work remain smooth over aging, there is evidence of nanoscopic surface pores that are inevitable in solution-processed organic materials (AFM, see Figures S9, S14, S16). Thus,   $  MoO_{x}  $  , with a reported thin-film crystallization temperature of 200 °C, could begin to wrinkle at much lower temperatures in the presence of the nanoscopic surface pores acting as nucleation sites on the HTL (Figure 3h). We attempted to relieve the compressive stress at the HTL/  $  MoO_{x}  $   interface during the PSC device fabrication process by annealing devices  $  ^{60}  $   at 85 °C for 15-20 minutes after (a) HTL deposition but before   $  MoO_{x}  $   deposition and (b) after both HTL and   $  MoO_{x}  $   deposition. Even so, we observe no gains in operational stability (Figure 1g,h, Figures S3-4), suggesting that the compressive stress may not be easily relieved in timescales relevant to rapid device manufacture, in alignment with another recent report  $  ^{61}  $  . We anticipate similar wrinkling of   $  VO_{x}  $   films deposited on organic substrates is possible, but at temperatures well above any crystallization or decomposition temperatures of the organic HTL or PAL, as ALD   $  VO_{x}  $   is reported to crystallize at 600 °C  $  ^{62}  $  . Thus,   $  VO_{x}  $   is morphologically stable on the organic HTL substrate at temperatures relevant to PSC operational stability.

Since the   $  VO_{x}  $   does not morphologically deform on top of the organic HTL under the aggressive device aging conditions studied here, we replaced the   $  MoO_{x}  $   interlayer with a   $  VO_{x}  $   interlayer within the device stack previously described. While multiple HTLs are compatible with a VOx interlayer  $  ^{34}  $  , we continued to use the 3EtCz-based HTL as the formulation is mobile ion-free and IZO instead of a metal top contact to preempt the well-known interactions that dominate device degradation  $  ^{63}  $  . In conclusion, we present a new interface engineering consideration for PSCs to enable stable operation at high temperatures. Instead of instabilities that originate from material properties or mechanical stress, we find the unlikely HTL/MoO  $  _{x}  $   wrinkling as the major source of this degradation, and, importantly, demonstrate that this can be easily remedied. At elevated temperatures in ambient lighting, the HTL/MoO  $  _{x}  $   interface is morphologically unstable and buckles, which leads to poor operational stability at 70 °C. When MoO  $  _{x}  $   is replaced with VO  $  _{x}  $ , a metal oxide that is more morphologically robust on top of the organic HTL, operational stability is dramatically improved. Unencapsulated devices with a VO  $  _{x}  $   interlayer aged for 1100 hours under constant illumination and load in ambient atmosphere (30-50% RH) maintain 71% initial PCE on average when measured at 70 °C and 80% initial PCE on average when measured under nitrogen at room temperature. This study represents one of the most complete reports of the operational stability of unencapsulated n-i-p PSCs in ambient atmosphere conducted at an elevated temperature. Reducing vanadium ion diffusion with a barrier layer and/or new HTL will allow for improved initial performance - specifically, functionalizing the HTL to provide a favorable growth surface or exploring other vanadium ALD precursors besides VTIP. Given the tremendous efforts towards barrier layer/HTL development, we are confident initial PCEs can approach record efficiencies. With this robust device stack, this motif will be important for integration into tandem architectures or perovskite-based solar windows, which will allow for the commercialization of inexpensive, clean electricity generation.

The films were immediately annealed at 100 °C for 1 hr. The HTL solution (85  $  \mu  $  L) was spin coated on top of the perovskite active layer (PAL) at 5000 rpm for 30 s. The spiro-OMeTAD solution was prepared by adding 72 mg of spiro-OMeTAD, 28.8  $  \mu  $  L tBP, 17.5  $  \mu  $  L LiTFSI stock solution (520 mg/mL in ACN), 29  $  \mu  $  L FK209 stock solution (300 mg/mL in ACN), with 1 mL of cb. The HTL solutions of EH44 and 3EtCz were prepared by mixing an HTM (e.g. 3EtCz) with its analogous HTM-ox salt (e.g. 3EtCz-ox) as well as with tBP (25  $  \mu  $  L per mL of solution) in cb. The total concentration (HTM + HTM-ox) used for EH44 (30 mg/mL) and 3EtCz (30 mg/mL). The wt% HTM-ox (wt% of HTM+HTM-ox total weight) used was 14 wt%. One of the following methods were used to deposit the metal oxide interlayer/electrode:

a.  $ 15\mathrm{nm} $  of  $ \mathrm{MoO_x} $  and  $ 100\mathrm{nm} $  of Al or Au were thermally evaporated with an active device area of  $ 0.1~\mathrm{cm}^2 $

b.  $ 15\mathrm{nm} $  of  $ \mathrm{MoO_x} $  was thermally evaporated and  $ 250~\mathrm{nm} $  of IZO was sputtered with an active device area of  $ 0.1~\mathrm{cm}^2 $ ,

c.  $ 15\mathrm{nmVO_x} $  was deposited (outlined below) and  $ 250~\mathrm{nm} $  of IZO was sputtered with an active device area of  $ 0.1~\mathrm{cm}^2 $ .

# 1.4. Device Characterization

Devices were tested under a Newport Oriel Sol3A solar simulator with a xenon lamp. The intensity of the solar simulator was calibrated to   $  100 \, mW cm^{-2}  $   AM1.5G using a KG2 filtered NREL-certified mono-Si reference solar cell. JV scans were taken from forward bias to reverse bias with the following scan parameters: step size, 10 mV; delay time, 10 ms; number of power-line cycles, 1; with a scan speed of   $  \sim 200 \, mV/s  $  . The devices were approximately   $  0.1 \, cm^{2}  $   and were masked with a metal aperture to define an active area of   $  0.059 \, cm^{2}  $  . Stable power output (SPO) was measured by recording the current output when the illuminated device was held at a constant voltage near the maximum power point of the JV scan. External Quantum Efficiency (EQE) measurements were taken using a Newport Oriel IQE2.

# 1.5. Device Stability Testing

Ambient atmosphere: Devices were tested with a ‘triple A class’ sulfur plasma lamp, Plasma-I AS 1300 Light Engine. Each device had an active area of   $  0.1 \, cm^2  $   and was masked with a metal aperture with a defined area of   $  0.059 \, cm^2  $  . The devices were under constant illumination during the stability test. Custom built electronics were used to measure the current-voltage scans of each device at 30 min intervals. The devices were subjected to a static load of   $  510 \, \Omega  $   in between scans. The substrates were unencapsulated so they were exposed to laboratory atmosphere and humidity (recorded in house, 30-50% relative humidity, RH). The samples were heated to approximately   $  70 \, ^{\circ}C  $   at the device surface by underlying copper tubing filled with water circulating at a temperature of   $  70 \, ^{\circ}C  $  . The actual temperature of the device films were not monitored, and are likely at a slightly lower temperature due to imperfect contact with aluminum heating blocks. Devices were randomly distributed within the testing platform to control for light intensity. Indium solder was used to improve connectivity to electronics, and up to four devices were tested per substrate as summarized in Figure S1.

Nitrogen atmosphere: Solar cells without any encapsulation were loaded into a home-built degradation testing setup, dubbed the Stability Parameter Analyzer (SPA). The setup consists of a flow chamber to control the environment of the cells, electrical housing, and electronics that switch between devices, measure J-V curves, and hold the devices under resistive load, and a light source to provide constant illumination. In this study, devices were kept in a nitrogen environment underneath white LEDs at 1 sun and held under a resistive load of 510 Ohms (placing the cells near maximum power point). The samples were heated to approximately 70 °C at the device surface by underlying copper tubing filled with water circulating at a temperature of 70 °C. Every 30 minutes, the system removes the resistive load and takes a J-V scan using a Keithley 2450 source-measure unit. J-V curves are then analyzed to extract relevant parameters. Indium solder was used to improve connectivity to electronics, and up to two devices were tested per substrate as summarized in Figure S1.
