# Stable and High-Efficiency Methylammonium-Free Perovskite Solar Cells

Xiao-Xin Gao, Wen Luo, Yi Zhang,* Ruiyuan Hu, Bao Zhang, Andreas Züttel, Yaqing Feng,* and Mohammad Khaja Nazeeruddin*

Organic-inorganic metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have achieved certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 25.2% with complex compositional and bandgap engineering. However, the thermal instability of methylammonium (MA) cation can cause the degradation of the perovskite film, remaining a risk for the long-term stability of the devices. Herein, a unique method is demonstrated to fabricate highly phase-stable perovskite film without MA by introducing cesium chloride (CsCl) in the double cation (Cs, formamidinium) perovskite precursor. Moreover, due to the suboptimal bandgap of bromide (Br  $  ^{-}  $  ), the amount of Br  $  ^{-}  $  is regulated, leading to high power conversion efficiency. As a result, MA-free perovskite solar cells achieve remarkable long-term stability and a PCE of 20.50%, which is one of the best results for MA-free PSCs. Moreover, the unencapsulated device retains about 80% of the original efficiencies after a 1000 h aging study. These results provide a feasible approach to enhance solar cell stability and performance simultaneously, paving the way for commercializing PSCs.

Hybrid organic–inorganic metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted much attention owing to the rapid development of their PCE from 3.8% to above 25% in the past few years.  $  ^{[1-19]}  $   Due to favorable optical bandgap and thermal stability, formamidinium (FA) was used as the cation in most of high efficiency PSCs.  $  ^{[20-22]}  $   However, the   $  \alpha  $  -phase FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   (with photoelectric activity) was thermodynamically stable only above

cation   $  Cs_{x}FA_{1-x}PbI_{3}  $   composition.  $  ^{[33]}  $   The advantage of this method is to substantially enhance FA   $  \alpha  $  -phase stability, eliminating thermal instability due to the volatile nature of the MA cation.  $  ^{[24,34]}  $   To further increase the stability of the FA   $  \alpha  $  -phase and improve the performance of the device, a large amount of research was carried out using double cation (Cs, FA), Rb or 2D cation doped.  $  ^{[16,35]}  $   Although the non-MA PSCs rarely have PCEs over 20%, we believe, with the development of fabrication methods and device engineering, the status will be improved increasingly. Therefore, in order to achieve high PCE with the long-term stability, choosing MA-free system with thermal stability and moisture resistance is highly desired for perfect PSCs.

In this work, we demonstrate using cesium chloride (CsCl) and lead bromide  $ (\mathrm{PbBr}_2) $  in the double cation (Cs, FA) perovskite that can significantly stabilize the black phase ( $ \alpha $ -phase) of  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $ , and enhance the stability and performance of MA-free perovskite solar cells. Consideration of suboptimal bandgap of bromide  $ (\mathrm{Br}^-) $ , we regulate the amount of bromide  $ (\mathrm{Br}^-) $ , leading to highly crystalline and large grain size perovskites with optimal cesium chloride (CsCl) and lead bromide  $ (\mathrm{PbBr}_2) $  at the same time. As a result, MA-free PSCs with optimal condition exhibited the best PCE of  $ 20.50\% $ . In addition to the thermal and long-term stability of devices, the performance also has been improved. Our results indicate that the cesium chloride (CsCl) and lead bromide  $ (\mathrm{PbBr}_2) $  can effectively restrict the undesirable phase transition thus significantly enhance the stability of double cation (Cs, FA) devices.

Several approaches have been developed to stabilize the black phase (  $  \alpha  $  -phase) of FA-based perovskite. The most common method is to introduce MA cation to FA-based PSCs, such as   $  MA_{x}FA_{1-x}PbI_{3}  $    $  ^{[28-30]}  $   and   $  (FAPbI_{3})_{1-x}(MAPbBr_{3})_{x}  $  .  $  ^{[10]}  $   Unfortunately, the low thermal instability of MA molecule can cause the degradation of the perovskite film, remaining a risk for the long-term stability for the devices.  $  ^{[31,32]}  $   Therefore, the MA-free perovskite film will be the best way to develop long-term stable PSC devices. The morphology of perovskite films was investigated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and the grain sizes were analyzed using nanomeasurer as summarized in Figure S1 and Table S1 (Supporting Information). Figure 1a shows the typical top-view SEM image of the   $  (\mathrm{Cs}_{0.17}\mathrm{FA}_{0.83})\mathrm{Pb}(\mathrm{I}_{0.97}\mathrm{Cl}_{0.03})_{3}  $   film without lead bromide   $  (\mathrm{PbBr}_{2})  $  . The surface morphology of the perovskite layer without   $  PbBr_{2}  $   was composed of crystals in an average size of 620 nm with large quantity small crystals (  $  \approx  $  100 nm) which were evenly distributed between grain boundaries. Moreover, clear pinholes were found on this layer. Interestingly, with the introduction of   $  PbBr_{2}  $   in the perovskite precursor (Figure 1b–e), the grain size of perovskite can be increased to micro meter scale while the pinholes are sharply decreased. After adding an increasing amount of lead bromide   $  (\mathrm{PbBr}_{2})  $   in perovskite precursor, the grains grew up and the pinholes were disappeared, indicating that the   $  PbBr_{2}  $   combined with CsCl apparently improved perovskite films quality, leading to densely packed perovskite films. As shown in Figure 1b–d, when a small amount of   $  PbBr_{2}  $   was added to   $  (\mathrm{Cs}_{0.17}\mathrm{FA}_{0.83})\mathrm{Pb}(\mathrm{I}_{0.97}\mathrm{Cl}_{0.03})_{3}  $   system, the average grain size increased from 680 to 860 nm, importantly, the pinholes were remarkably reduced. However, as shown in Figure 1e, while the content of   $  PbBr_{2}  $   further increased, the average grain size slightly decreased to 830 nm. The pinhole-free perovskite films with the largest crystalline grains can be achieved by adding   $  PbBr_{2}  $   amount to sample 0.15. Worth to highlight, as to reference (R), the average grain size of triple cation perovskite (Figure 1f) was 270 nm and there was no small crystal in the perovskite grain boundary.

Figure 2a–d shows the device architectures and cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images for double cation compositions   $  (\mathrm{Cs}_{0.17}\mathrm{FA}_{0.83})\mathrm{Pb}(\mathrm{I}_{0.97-x}\mathrm{Br}_{x}\mathrm{Cl}_{0.03})_{3}  $   and triple cation compositions   $  (\mathrm{Cs}_{0.08}\mathrm{FA}_{0.8}\mathrm{MA}_{0.12})\mathrm{Pb}(\mathrm{I}_{0.88}\mathrm{Br}_{0.12})_{3}  $  . For all PSC devices, perovskite absorber layer was prepared via a one-step spin-coating method as shown in the experimental section. As can be seen, the   $  (\mathrm{Cs}_{0.17}\mathrm{FA}_{0.83})\mathrm{Pb}(\mathrm{I}_{0.89}\mathrm{Br}_{0.08}\mathrm{Cl}_{0.03})_{3}  $   (0.15) perovskites consisted of continuous single-layer crystals

without any pinholes or grain boundaries in the layer, which was ordered and vertically aligned capping layer. Compared with the   $  (\mathrm{Cs}_{0.17}\mathrm{FA}_{0.83})\mathrm{Pb}(\mathrm{I}_{0.89}\mathrm{Br}_{0.08}\mathrm{Cl}_{0.03})_{3}  $   (0.15) perovskite, the PSC device with   $  (\mathrm{Cs}_{0.08}\mathrm{FA}_{0.8}\mathrm{MA}_{0.12})\mathrm{Pb}(\mathrm{I}_{0.88}\mathrm{Br}_{0.12})_{3}  $   (R) clearly showed that perovskite films consisted of 2–3 layers of several small crystals, which was harmful to the transport of carrier.  $  ^{[36]}  $

To get the information of small crystals shown in Figure 1, the elemental analysis of surface was carried out by confocal laser scanning fluorescence microscopy (CLSM) for without MA and with MA samples. The CLSM can map the emissive chemical constituents spatially by exploiting the differences in their emission spectra.  $  ^{[37]}  $   The emission peak between 500 and 550 nm is identified as due to   $  PbI_{2}  $   and assigned as the green color scale, by contrast, the emission between 700 and 800 nm is attributed to perovskite and assigned as the red color scale.  $  ^{[38]}  $   As shown in the CLSM images of the without MA perovskite films (Figure 2e–i), we detected small amount of   $  PbI_{2}  $   (shown in green), which were presented in the boundaries of perovskite (shown in red). Then, we focused the CLSM image of triple cation perovskite film with MA (Figure 2j), obviously, it was only shown the crystals of perovskite, resulting in all red not green in the full range of CLSM image. In accordance with SEM results, the surface morphology of the perovskites was presented completely different for without MA and with MA perovskite samples. It is worth mentioning that the small grains (  $  PbI_{2}  $  ) were distributed around the boundaries of perovskite for without MA samples, which was preferred for high performance and stability of PSCs, due to moderately excess   $  PbI_{2}  $   can passivate the defects and reduce recombination in the perovskite.  $  ^{[39,40]}  $   As shown in Figure 2k, X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of all perovskite films showed similar features which can be well-indexed to same crystal system, indicating that introduction of CsCl and   $  PbBr_{2}  $   did not change the perovskite structure. Biexponential fits were further performed on TRPL curves to quantify the carrier dynamics, as shown in Figure 3b. The fitting results are summarized in Table S2 (Supporting Information). The without MA (0.15) perovskite demonstrated a long PL lifetime of 270.7 ns, while the PL lifetime for with MA (R) was only 165.8 ns. The large reduction of PL lifetime could also signify the crystalline quality of without MA film was much better than with MA film, reducing the density of defect states. Further, we had also prepared these two kinds of perovskite layers with the structure of FTO/ETM/perovskite and glass/perovskite/HTM, respectively. Compared to the perovskite films coated on the glass, the lifetime of perovskite coated with ETM and HTM were significantly reduced. The PL lifetime of FTO/ETM/without MA (0.15) perovskite was 35.7 ns; however, FTO/ETM/with MA (R) perovskite had long PL lifetime reaching to 57.2 ns. Similarly, The PL lifetime of glass/without MA (0.15) perovskite/HTM was 33.9 ns, in contrast, glass/with MA (R) perovskite/HTM can get a little longer lifetime (36.4 ns). Therefore, without MA (0.15) perovskite coated with ETM and HTM had more efficient PL quenching than with MA (R) coated with ETM and HTM, respectively, demonstrating that without MA (0.15) perovskite had the higher interfacial charge and hole transfer efficiency. As shown in Figure S6 (Supporting Information), ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption spectra were shown for without MA perovskite films, including the samples 0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2 and with MA perovskite films (sample R). For the without MA samples, it is noticeable that a blueshift occurred for all samples with an increased the amount of PbBr $ _{2} $  in agreement with the analysis results of the steady-state PL. For the with MA samples, we found that the UV data were very close to sample 0.15 and showed a slight blueshift, indicating that both of samples had similar bandgap for perovskite films.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was implemented to probe the surface composition and chemical state of all films. Figure 3c shows the Br 3d spectra of all films, where no Br could be detected for the sample 0.05 due to the low concentration of   $  PbBr_{2}  $  . As the amount of   $  PbBr_{2}  $   was increased from 0.1 to 0.2, the intensity of Br 3d peak and the Br/Pb atomic ratio were also increased (Figure 3c; Table S3, Supporting Information). In addition, the Br 3d peak was also observed for the reference sample (R) due to the presence of   $  PbBr_{2}  $   and MABr. Furthermore, we compared the thermal stability between the without and with MA perovskite films using XPS analysis to verify the degradation products. We chose the without (sample 0.15) and with MA (sample R), then putting these two samples on the hotplate at 150 °C for 30 min in the air. As shown in Figure 3d, the main peaks at 138.6 and 143.5 eV of Pb 4f spectra are ascribed to the binding energy of Pb–X (X: I, Br, and Cl) in perovskite.  $  ^{[45-47]}  $   Compared with the without MA sample (0.15), with MA sample (R) showed additional peaks at 136.8 and 141.7 eV, which can be ascribed to the metallic Pb at low binding energy.  $  ^{[46,48]}  $   The presence of metallic Pb indicated the existence of iodide deficiencies in the perovskite lattice of with MA sample, which was confirmed by the decreased I/Pb atomic ratio (Table S4, Supporting Information). In addition,

the metallic Pb species in the film were likely to act as recombination centers. For the without MA films with CsCl and   $  PbBr_{2}  $  , the peaks for metallic Pb were not observed after heating in the air atmosphere, indicative of the markedly improved stability.

The photovoltaic performances of PSCs were measured under standard AM 1.5G solar illumination and the current-density-voltage (J-V) curves for the champion devices of six different conditions were recorded in Figure 4a. Meanwhile, the corresponding photovoltaic parameters for champion cell are summarized in Table 1. Notably, with the content of   $  PbBr_{2}  $   was increased regularly, it can be seen that open circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) of without MA PSCs were enhanced from 1.06 V (sample 0) to 1.09 V (sample 0.05), 1.11 V (sample 0.1), 1.12 V (sample 0.15), and 1.13 V (0.2). Compared with the   $  V_{OC}  $   of with MA PSC (sample R), without MA devices exhibited significantly higher   $  V_{OC}  $   due to introduce the CsCl and   $  PbBr_{2}  $   as additives simultaneously. However, the short circuit current density (  $  J_{SC}  $  ) of without MA PSCs were decreased from 23.84 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   (sample 0) to 23.60 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   (sample 0.05), 23.39 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   (sample 0.1), 23.28 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   (sample 0.15), and 22.77 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   (sample 0.2). In contrast with   $  J_{SC}  $   of with MA PSC (sample R), the   $  J_{SC}  $   of without MA devices reduced slowly with adding increasing amount of   $  PbBr_{2}  $   from sample 0.05 to 0.15, the   $  J_{SC}  $   value of sample 0.15 (23.28 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) is similar with MA sample R (23.32 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ). Nevertheless, after that, a little content of   $  PbBr_{2}  $   had led dramatically decreased for   $  J_{SC}  $   to 22.77 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , indicating the double-edged sword effect of additives for   $  PbBr_{2}  $ . Generally speaking, the fill factor (FF) of the PSCs are sensitive to the quality of the perovskite layer as well as those of the electron transport layers and the hole transport layers.  $  ^{[49]}  $   As for FF in without MA PSCs, it exhibited better change from 72.96% (sample 0) to 75.10% (sample 0.05), 76.82% (sample 0.1), and 78.33% (sample 0.15). With the same reason for   $  PbBr_{2}  $ , the FF of sample 0.2 was decreased to 77.21%. In comparison with the FF of with MA device (76.80%), most of without MA devices showed better FF than sample R. From the above, the sample 0.15 showed the best performance with a PCE of 20.50% in all without MA samples, comparing to sample 0 (18.41%), sample 0.05 (19.26%), sample 0.1 (19.98%), and sample 0.2 (19.88%); meanwhile, this PCE was higher than with MA sample R (19.51%). Thus, it can be seen that the without MA PSCs with appropriate content of CsCl and   $  PbBr_{2}  $   simultaneously (sample 0.15) can reach best integrated performance. The enhancement should be ascribed to the reduced photo-generated charge recombination at the perovskite interface as evidenced by PL measurements. In general, the hysteresis between forward and reverse scanning is a key aspect to evaluate the integrated performance of PSCs.  $  ^{[50]}  $   It can be seen that we compared the hysteresis effect between the best without MA (sample 0.15) and with MA (R) PSCs in the inset of figure 4a. The hystereses of PCEs are 4% for sample 0.15 and 6% for sample R respectively. Apparently, the introduction of optimized CsCl and   $  PbBr_{2}  $   not only improved the efficiency but also alleviated the hysteresis. The statistics analysis confirmed that the most of without MA devices with CsCl and PbBr  $  _{2}  $  demonstrated a higher PCE than with MA PSCs. In consideration of the same ETL and HTL were used for all devices, the improved average efficiency and reproducibility of the PSCs should be attributed to the perovskite layer.

For the devices' stability test, first, the steady-state photocurrent density of the PSCs was measured at a fixed bias potential, where the maximum output power was detected in the  $ J-V $  curve. As shown in Figure S7 (Supporting Information), the photocurrent output was preserved after 250 s consecutive operation for both the best without MA device (0.15) and the MA device (R). The without MA device (0.15) showed better stability than with MA device (R) due to slightly decreased for sample R after 175 s, whereas, there was no change for sample 0.15. In order to get more reliable data, furthermore, we evaluated the best without MA device (0.15) and the with MA device (R) at the maximum power output (MPO) decay. Both of PSC devices were maintained in an argon atmosphere under a constant illumination of 100 mW cm $ ^{-2} $ . The current-voltage curves ( $ J-V $ ) were recorded every 2 h, the device without MA double cation (0.15) showed better stability than with MA triple cation (R) device, which can be seen in Figure 4h. The performance of both PSCs decreased rapidly at beginning of the aging study, possibly owing to the migration of gold through the HTM to the perovskite layer. After 1000 h of continuous light soaking, the MPO values of the best without MA device (0.15) and with MA device (R) maintained 80% and 40% of their initial values, respectively. In stark contrast, the loss for with MA device (R) is relatively higher than best without MA device (0.15). These stability data highlighted the without MA devices, which were obviously advantageous for the long-term stability of PSCs. Due to thermally unstable MA molecule can cause the degradation of the perovskite composition; thus, it is key to assess thermal stability of the without MA device and the with MA device. Herein, Figure S8 (Supporting Information) presents the thermal stability of the best without MA device (0.15) and the with MA device (R). We put both the PSCs on a hotplate at 80 °C directly. After every 15 min, we measured the photovoltaic parameters for these PSCs after cooling down. The without MA device exhibited superior thermal stability. On the one hand, the reason for the better stability was attributed to the absence of MA cation for the perovskite composition, due to the volatile nature of MA. $ ^{[24]} $  On the other hand, the introduction of CsCl and PbBr $ _2 $  played significant roles to stabilize the double cation (Cs, FA), resulting in excellent stability and performance.

In summary, we used cesium chloride (CsCl) and lead bromide  $ (\mathrm{PbBr}_2) $  to stabilize the black phase ( $ \alpha $ -phase)  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  in the double cation (Cs, FA) perovskite system, achieving the better stability and performance for the methylammonium-free PSC device. The optimized amount of CsCl and  $ \mathrm{PbBr}_2 $  can lead to high crystal and large grain size films, which prolonged carrier lifetime and showed excellent long-term stability. Moreover, the small crystals  $ (\mathrm{PbI}_2) $  were found around the grain boundaries of perovskite in this double cation system, which would stabilize the structure and passivate the defects of perovskite. As a result, the MA-free device demonstrated a high PCE  $ 20.50\% $  at the illumination of  $ 100\mathrm{mWcm}^{-2} $ , which was appreciably higher than the PCE of the device with triple cation perovskite. It is worthy highlighting that the thermal and the long-term stability of devices were enhanced. These results indicated that the cesium chloride (CsCl) and lead bromide  $ (\mathrm{PbBr}_2) $  can effectively replace the MA thus significantly reducing the degradation of double cation (Cs, FA) devices performance compared with the triple cation (Cs, FA, and MA) PSCs.

# Experimental Section

Fabrication of Perovskite Solar Cell: All starting materials were used directly without further purification. Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)-coated glass substrates (Nippon Sheet Glass, TEC8) were cleaned with detergent, deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol in the ultrasonic bath for 15 min respectively. The FTO substrates were then treated with a UV/ozone cleaner for 15 min before being used. TiO₂ compact layers (c-TiO₂) were deposited onto the clean FTO substrates by spray pyrolysis using a titanium diisopropoxide bis-(acetylacetonate) solution (75% in 2-propanol, Sigma-Aldrich), which was diluted in 2-propanol (99.8%, Acros Organics) at 1:15 volume ratio on 450 °C and followed by in situ annealing for 30 min. As for the mesoporous TiO₂ (meso-TiO₂) solution, which was composed of 1 g TiO₂ paste (30NR-D, Greatcell Solar) diluted in 10 mL anhydrous ethanol solution, then the meso-TiO₂ layer was deposited onto the FTO/c-TiO₂ substrate by one-step spin-coating process at 4500 rpm for 20 s. After annealing at 125 °C for 30 min, the meso-TiO₂ films were gradually heated to 525 °C in air, and then they were baked at the same temperature for 20 min to remove organic components. SnO₂ layers were prepared by spin-coating 0.1 M SnCl₄ aqueous (99%, Acros Organics) solution by one-step process at 3000 rpm for 20 s, then the substrates were transferred onto a hotplate and heated between 150 and 190 °C for 1 h. Before using, the FTO/c-TiO₂/meso-TiO₂/SnO₂ substrates were treated with UV/Ozone for 30 min.

The with MA precursor solution (sample R) was prepared by dissolving   $  PbI_{2}  $   (1.190 M, TCI),   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (0.155 M, TCI), CsI (0.105 M, TCI), FAI (1.040 M, Dyesol) and MABr (0.155 M, Dyesol) in a mixed solvent of DMF:DMSO = 4:1 (volume ratio). The without MA precursor solution was prepared by mixing CsCl (0.105 M, TCI), CsI (0.105 M, TCI), and FAI (1.040 M, Dyesol) by adding increasing amount of   $  PbBr_{2}  $   from 0 M (sample 0), 0.05 M (sample 0.05), 0.1 M (sample 0.1), 0.15 M (sample 0.15) to 0.2 M (sample 0.2); meanwhile reducing same molar   $  PbI_{2}  $   from 1.345 M, then, were mixed in solvent of DMF:DMSO = 4:1 (volume ratio). After that, all kinds of perovskite precursors were spin-coated on the top of FTO/c-  $  TiO_{2}  $  /meso-  $  TiO_{2}  $  /  $  SnO_{2}  $   substrate at 1000 rpm for 10 s and continuously at 5000 rpm for 30 s. During the second step, 100 mL of chlorobenzene was dropped on the films at 15 s. Afterward, the films were annealed on the hotplate at 100 °C for 1 h. After cooling to room temperature, 40 μL spiro-OMeTAD solution was deposited by spin-coating at 4000 rpm for 20 s on the perovskite layer as the hole transport layer. The spiro-OMeTAD solution was prepared by dissolving in chlorobenzene with 4-tert-butylpyridine, Li-TFSI in acetonitrile, and Co [t-BuPyPz]  $  _{3}  $   [TFSI]  $  _{3}  $   (FK209, Dyesol) in acetonitrile at a molar ratio of spiro-OMeTAD:FK209:Li-TFSI:TBP of 1:0.03:0.5:3.3. Film Characterization: The morphology and particle size of obtained samples were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, FEI Sirion-200) operating at 3 kV.
