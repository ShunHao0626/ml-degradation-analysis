# Releasing Nanocapsules for High-Throughput Printing of Stable Perovskite Solar Cells

Zengqi Huang, Xiaotian Hu, Zhipeng Zhao, Xiangchuan Meng, Meng Su,  $  ^{*}  $   Tangyue Xue, Jimei Chi, Hongfei Xie, Zheren Cai, Yiwang Chen,  $  ^{*}  $   Lin Li, and Yanlin Song  $  ^{*}  $

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) are promising photovoltaic technologies due to their impressive power conversion efficiency (PCE) and low-temperature fabrication process, while it is still challenging to print uniform perovskite film with high crystalline quality over a module size. Here, a printable and stable perovskite nanocapsules ink to realize the high-throughput printing of large-area, highly uniform perovskite films with micron grain size is reported. It is discovered that the releasing effect of these perovskite nanocapsules promotes homogeneous nucleation by diffusion-controlled growth due to the steady-state diffusion of the solute in solution. Remarkably, the printed PSCs and 25 cm $  ^{2}  $  modules achieve power conversion efficiencies of 22.10% and 16.12%, respectively. They exhibit negligible efficiency loss after continuous operation for over 1000 h under AM1.5 illumination, and excellent thermal (85 °C) stability with over 87% of the initial efficiency after aging for 500 h. This perovskite nanocapsules ink is expected to facilitate the high-yield fabrication of perovskite photovoltaics.

And the encapsulated devices show nearly no PCE loss after over 1000 h of operation at the max power point (MPP) under simulated AM1.5 illumination.

A uniform, 2D lamellar structure is observed from both the surface morphology and the fracture area. This phenomenon is due to that the   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   can incorporate with some fraction of the organic amines in APTES. However, PSCs devices resulted from excess lamellar structure exhibit poor photovoltaic performances.  $  ^{[28]}  $   Thus, an enhancement on the growth of 3D   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   is critical for this nanocapsules ink. Among the commonly used solvents, the strong coordination of B sites in DMSO or NMP systems can lead to the formation of intermediate Lewis base-acid adducts.  $  ^{[29-31]}  $   These intermediate phases may affect the kinetic control of crystal growth, thus influence the release of perovskite nanocapsules. Different from DMSO and NMP, DMF has the highest vapor pressure among these solvents, which leads to faster evaporation during or after coating.  $  ^{[22]}  $   As a result, DMF with a relative density of 0.944 g mL  $  ^{-1}  $   has been introduced as a coordination and diffusion solution to replace part of the APTES (with a similar relative density of 0.946 g mL  $  ^{-1}  $ ). Hence, the   $  MAPbI_{3}  $ -NCs ink finally consists of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   nanocrystals, APTES, ACN, and DMF. As shown in Figure S5 (Supporting Information), the   $  MAPbI_{3}  $ -NCs ink exhibits a transparent yellow solution with a significant Tyndall effect. The colloidal size distribution of the   $  MAPbI_{3}  $ -NCs ink has been carried out by the dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurement (Figure S6, Supporting Information). Compared with the size distribution of 1.9–2.5 nm in traditional perovskite ink, the   $  MAPbI_{3}  $ -NCs ink exhibits an enhanced distribution value of 22.9–30.7 nm, which is mainly resulted from the nanocapsules in the precursor solution. And this value gradually increases as enhancing the concentration of the siloxane shell.

To confirm the formation of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -NCs, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometry (EDS) mapping analysis have been carried out. The TEM image in Figure 2a shows a nanocapsule structure which is collected by dropping the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -NCs ink onto the copper grid. This nanocapsule structure is further confirmed by the low magnification TEM micrograph with a colloidal size distribution of  $ \approx 20\mathrm{nm} $  (Figure S7, Supporting Information), which is consistent with the DLS results. A cubic phase with a corresponding  $ d $  spacing value of  $ 3.1\AA $  for the typical (001) reflection of cubic perovskite plane is observed in the inset high-resolution

TEM (HRTEM) image. This result demonstrates that the inner core is corresponding to MAPbI $ _3 $  nanocrystals. As shown in Figure S8 (Supporting Information), the EDS mapping of the Pb, I, C components from the MAPbI $ _3 $ -NCs ink further confirms the MAPbI $ _3 $  nanocluster structure. And a network distribution of the APTES is observed from the EDS mapping of Si, C components. The results indicate that this siloxane network can not only surround the MAPbI $ _3 $  nanocluster to form the perovskite nanocapsules, but also lead to a uniform distribution in the bulk liquid phase. And this distribution is critically important for the solution nucleation and crystal growth. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurement is performed to further confirm the formation mechanism of perovskite nanocapsules and the interaction between perovskite and APTES. All the samples are prepared by dropping different solutions on poly(ethylene terephthalate) (PET) substrates to avoid the interference of Si signals in the glass. The Si 2p signals of the APTES/ACN solution, traditional perovskite ink, and nanocapsules ink are shown in Figure 2b. A significant Si signal has been observed from the nanocapsules ink with a shift by 0.1 eV toward higher energy in comparison with the APTES/ACN solution, while no Si signal is detected from the traditional ink. As shown in Figure S9 (Supporting Information), the fractional peak of the XPS spectrum demonstrates that the Si 2p core levels can be well fitted by two different features (Si–O–Si at 102.6 eV and Si–O–C at 101.9 eV) in the form of a Gaussian function. $ ^{[32,33]} $  These two features are observed in both the APTES/ACN solution and nanocapsules ink. However, it is important to mention that a higher percentage of Si–O–Si electron binding energy has been obtained in the nanocapsules ink. More siloxanes are condensed to form a three-dimensional network structure (denoted as the capsule shell), thus leading to a slight shift of Si 2p core level toward higher energy. Moreover, N 1s spectra are also collected in Figure 2c. The N 1s peak of the traditional ink is located at 401.9 eV, corresponding to the protonated amines (NH $ _3^+ $ ) from the perovskite component of CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3^+ $ . And a shift by 0.2 eV toward lower binding energy for the NH $ _3^+ $  peak indicates that these protonated amines (R-NH $ _3^+ $ ) are easy to incorporate with the PbI $ _3^- $ . They can partially act as A site role for controlling the solution nucleation and crystal growth. To gain deeper insight into the evolution mechanism from precursor ink to solid film, an in situ laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM) measurement is performed (Figure 3). The samples are prepared by placing the perovskite ink-coated substrate on a customized hot plate, and followed by annealing at 80 °C for continuous record. As shown in Figure 3a, the LSCM images of the traditional MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ink show uniform dotted fluorescence signals with no distinct features, which corresponds to the intermediate solvent phase in the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ink. Beginning with annealing at 80 °C, the concentration of the solute at the particle-solution interface quickly reaches the value near the bulk concentration of monomers in solution, thus leading to heterogeneous nucleation and dendritic crystallization (Figure 3b). After that, the fluorescence signals of both the intermediate phase and MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  phase increase. The increase corresponds to the crystal growth stage (Figure 3c). In contrast, the LSCM images of the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $ -NCs ink exhibit distinguish fluorescence signals with some clusters corresponded to the perovskite nanocrystals core and siloxane shell (Figure 3d). It should be noted that the monomers inside the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $ -NCs

spread out through the solvent diffusion at the beginning of the annealing process (Figure S11, Supporting Information), which is attributed to the releasing and diffusion-controlled growth process. As shown in Figure 3e, only MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite signals can be observed by continuously heating at 80 °C for 20 s, and MAPbI $ _3 $  nanocrystals are surrounded by some amorphous objects. These amorphous objects are gradually filled with MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite crystals in the crystal growth process (Figure 3f), which confirms that the amorphous objects are the siloxane shell at the GBs. Moreover, the perovskite crystallization of MAPbI $ _3 $ -NCs films can be completed in only 2 min, without obvious change in the crystal structure with further increasing of annealing time (Figure S12, Supporting Information). Comparing these images of MAPbI $ _3 $ -NCs ink with those obtained from traditional MAPbI $ _3 $  ink, we notice major differences: (I) The clusters constitution inside the perovskite precursor is unique and uniformly distributed. (II) The perovskite crystals are directly formed in few seconds, indicating that the crystallization rate is much faster in the MAPbI $ _3 $ -NCs ink. (III) Uniform crystals with larger grain sizes are formed without assisted treatment instead of dendritic crystallization. We attribute the unique film formation process to the fast diffusion of the monomers from the bulk solution to the particle surface, rather than to the slower phase transition.

We further fabricate perovskite solar cells and modules using this nanocapsules ink. The p-i-n planar heterojunction devices are structured as ITO/NiO $ _{x} $ /perovskite/PCBM/bathocuproine (BCP)/silver (Ag) and fabricated by continuous meniscus-coating processes. The current density–voltage ( $ J-V $ ) curves of the MAPbI $ _{3} $  devices are shown in Figure 5a, which are performed under simulated AM 1.5G solar irradiation (100 mW cm $ ^{-2} $ ). The control devices show a PCE of 17.45% with a short-circuit current density ( $ J_{sc} $ ) of 21.24 mA cm $ ^{-2} $ , an open-circuit voltage ( $ V_{oc} $ ) of 1.08 V, and a fill factor (FF) of 76.0%. We also fabricate PSCs with APTES as an additive. The concentration of the best performing devices is determined to be 0.04 wt% (Figure S18, Supporting Information). The device delivers a PCE of 19.63% with a  $ J_{sc} $  of 22.61 mA cm $ ^{-2} $ , a  $ V_{oc} $  of 1.11 V, and an FF of 78.2%. The above results are similar to the previous report which used other siloxanes as additives. $ ^{[42]} $  However, it still exists some pinholes in the printed perovskite films with APTES as additive. These pinholes may cause carrier recombination and hinder carrier transportation, which leads to the deterioration of photovoltaic performance. As a result, the design of nanocapsules ink is beneficial for reducing the   $  V_{oc}  $   loss, thus resulting in an enhancement in device performance.

Suppressing defects and performing molecular modification on perovskite grains and GBs may have the additional effect of reducing ion migration, which is an unwanted effect in PSCs that contributes to hysteresis and poor stability. We conduct the electric poling experiment with the structure of ITO/Au/perovskite/Au (Figure S35, Supporting Information). The unpolarized devices exhibit ohmic resistance under one sun illuminated (Figure S36, Supporting Information). With a constant bias applied on the two electrodes, the electrical field will drift the ions/defects toward two electrodes and result in a built-in potential   $  V_{\mathrm{bi}}  $   of poling film.  $  ^{[53,54]}  $   A   $  V_{oc}  $   can be detected by illuminating the poled devices, and the magnitude of   $  V_{oc}  $   is essentially determined by the number of polarized ions toward

two sides. The device with larger   $  V_{oc}  $   reveals the film with easier ions migration. After poled under 0.98 V for 30 s, the I-V curves of the devices are immediately recorded on one sun illuminated. As shown in Figure 5f, the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  -NCs based device exhibits a smaller   $  V_{oc}  $   value of 0.13 V in comparison to the control device (0.34 V), indicating that ion migration is more hindered in the perovskite film fabricated from MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  -NCs ink. To study the long-term stability of different perovskite layers, we conducted MPP tracking on the encapsulated devices under constant simulated AM1.5 illumination (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ). As shown in Figure 5g; and Table S3 (Supporting Information), the control device rapidly loses 51% of its initial PCE after 270 h of testing, which exhibits a significant degradation after light-soaking. By contrast, the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  -NCs based device exhibits negligible PCE loss after continuous operation for over 1000 h under AM1.5 illumination (Table S4, Supporting Information). Furthermore, the thermal stability is also investigated by collecting J-V curves at various times for devices maintained at a fixed temperature of 85 °C (Figure 5h). The control device loses ≈54% of its initial PCE value after 72 h of testing, while the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  -NCs based device can maintain over 87% of its initial PCE value after a thermal stability test of 500 h. As a result, our design of nanocapsules ink can effectively inhibit the solution aging and ion migration, thus improving the reproducibility and stability of the PSCs devices.

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, we have demonstrated the formation and release of perovskite nanocapsules, in facilitating the large-area processing of uniform and oriented crystallized perovskite films. This strategy based on MAPbI $ _3 $ -NCs ink enables the high-throughput printing of invert planar structured PSCs and PSMs with a remarkable PCE of  $ 22.10\% $  and  $ 16.12\% $ , respectively. The releasing of nanocapsules is dominated by a diffusion-controlled nucleation growth, which exhibits a record manufacturing efficiency of 140 s for PSMs installation of 1 kW. Importantly, the siloxane network at the GBs can serve as a protective layer and ion migration blocker, thus improving the thermal and operational stability of PSCs. The best PSCs exhibit negligible PCE loss after continuous operation for over 1000 h under AM1.5 illumination, and remain over  $ 87\% $  of the initial PCE after a thermal stability test of 500 h.
