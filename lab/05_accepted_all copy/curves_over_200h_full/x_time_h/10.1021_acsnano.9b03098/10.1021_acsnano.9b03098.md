ABSTRACT: While research on building-integrated photovoltaics (BIPVs) has mainly focused on power-generating window applications, the utilization of other underutilized surface areas in buildings, including exteriors, facades, and rooftops, has still not been fully explored. The most important requirements for BIPVs are color, power conversion efficiency (PCE), and long-term stability. In this work, we achieved colorful (red, green, blue, RGB) perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with minimized PCE loss

(<10%) and enhanced photostability by exploiting the optical properties of nonperiodic multi-nanolayer, narrow-bandwidth reflective filters (NBRFs). The NBRFs were fabricated by multilayering high-index   $  TiO_{2}  $  /low-index   $  SiO_{2}  $   in a nonperiodic manner, which allowed devices to demonstrate various colors with effectively suppressed unwanted baseline ripple-shape reflectance. The PCEs of PSCs with nonperiodic RGB-NBRFs were 18.0%, 18.6%, and 18.9%, which represent reductions of only 10%, 7%, and 6% of PCE values, respectively, compared to a black control PSC (20.1%). Moreover, the photostability of the PSCs was substantially improved by using the NBRFs because of ultraviolet blocking in the   $  TiO_{2}  $   layers. The G-PSC retained 65% of the initial PCE after 60 h of continuous illumination (AM 1.5G one sun) at the maximum power point, whereas the black PSC retained only 30%. Aesthetic color value, low PCE loss, and enhanced photostability of PSCs were simultaneously achieved by employing our NBRFs, making this a promising strategy with potential applicability in power-generating building exteriors.

KEYWORDS: colorful perovskite solar cell, optical interference effects, narrow-bandwidth reflective filters, nonperiodic multi-nanolayer, long-term stability

Since the pioneering discovery by Miyasaka et al. in 2009,  $  ^{1}  $  organic–inorganic metal halide-based perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have received much attention as promising next-generation solar cells owing to their high efficiencies and low-cost solution processability.  $  ^{2-5}  $  Large absorption coefficients (  $  >10^{5}  $  cm  $  ^{-1}  $  ), high charge carrier mobilities (  $  \sim10  $  cm  $  ^{2}  $  /V·s), tunability of optical properties, and optimization of device structure enable the PSCs to reach a certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 24.2%.  $  ^{6}  $  Recently, the potential of PSCs for use in building-integrated photovoltaics (BIPVs) has been suggested owing to their excellent combination of high PCE, low-cost solution

processability, and color tunability compared to other thin-film solar cells such as amorphous silicon (a-Si),  $  ^{7}  $  cadmium telluride (CdTe),  $  ^{8}  $  organic solar cells (OSC),  $  ^{9}  $  and copper indium gallium diselenide (CIGSe).  $  ^{10}  $  Studies of PSCs for BIPVs have mainly focused on power-generating window applications; however, the full potential of PSCs has still not been fully explored for BIPV applications.  $  ^{11-13}  $

Applications of solar cells in BIPVs are not limited solely to power-generating windows but also include exteriors,  $  ^{14}  $   facades,  $  ^{15}  $   and rooftops.  $  ^{16}  $   While current commercial Si-based solar modules are typically mounted on rooftops, other underutilized surface areas account for more than twice the area of rooftops. These areas can provide a tremendous opportunity for additional installation of solar cells to achieve the ultimate goal of buildings with net-zero energy consumption.  $  ^{17}  $   One of the most important requirements for BIPVs is the color of the solar cells because the colors of building exteriors demonstrate the traditional, cultural, and religious features of cities. However, despite their high PCEs, it is intrinsically difficult to tune the colors of commercial Si-based solar cells. In response to these challenges associated with Si-based solar cells, color-tunable PSCs have begun to emerge as cost-effective candidates that can be printed in high-throughput manufacturing processes.  $  ^{18-20}  $

Thus far, strategies reported for the color tuning of PSCs, such as composition tuning,  $  ^{21}  $   plasmonics,  $  ^{22}  $   and photonic crystal  $  ^{23}  $   and microcavity  $  ^{24}  $   strategies, have focused on the development of semitransparent PSC devices for power-generating windows. However, colored PSCs exhibit significantly lower PCEs compared to their conventional uncolored forms (black color). The use of a multi-nanolayered reflective color-generated filter is another strategy to tune the color of solar cells without modification of their photoactive layers.  $  ^{9,25,26}  $   In our earlier works, multi-nanolayered cutoff

dichroic filters or narrow-bandwidth reflective filters (NBRFs), realized by periodic alternating deposition of low-refractive-index   $  SiO_{2}  $   and high-refractive-index   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $  , were used to tune the color of copper indium gallium sulfide selenide (CIGSSe) solar cells.  $  ^{[27,28]}  $   Using a periodic   $  SiO_{2}/TiO_{2}  $  -based cutoff filter, red and yellow CIGSSe solar cells were achieved, although their PCEs were reduced by a factor of   $  >40\%  $   due to the large overlap between the reflective spectra of the filters and the absorption of the CIGSSe solar cells.  $  ^{[27]}  $   Recently, a series of full-color NBRFs (notch-type filters) was also developed to produce red (R), green (G), and blue (B) CIGSSe solar cells while retaining small PCE drop with the help of an antireflection effect.  $  ^{[28]}  $

To apply PSCs for long-term use BIPVs, sufficient device stability should be secured under various environmental conditions such as chemical, heat, moisture, and ultraviolet (UV) irradiation. While the chemical, heat, and moisture stabilities of PSCs can possibly be improved by employing encapsulation techniques, the UV stability of PSCs cannot be greatly improved by encapsulation. In general, the absorption of UV light is inevitable for solar cells. # RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Various NBRFs were designed to generate assorted pure colors over the visible range, while reduction in PCE of colored PSCs was minimized. Figure 1a and b show schematic diagrams of NBRF color films with two different structures (periodic and nonperiodic stacking) of multiple nanolayers. These NBRF films are composed of multiple layers with alternating low-refractive-index  $ \mathrm{SiO}_2 $  (1.45) layers and high-refractive-index  $ \mathrm{TiO}_2 $  (2.31) layers. Various peak wavelengths and full-width at half-maximum (fwhm) values of NBRFs have been demonstrated by manipulating optical interference effects between the high- and low-index layers. By controlling the periodic and nonperiodic stacking sequence and the thickness of each high- and low-index layer in the multiple nanolayers, we also simulated seven colors of the two types of NBRFs (Figure 2a,b). Violet (V), blue (B), cyan (C), green (G), yellow (Y), orange (O), and red (R) NBRFs were obtained by tuning the peak wavelength. Moreover, we simulated G-NBRFs with various bandwidths by varying the number of stacks and the thicknesses of the high- and low-index layers (Figure 2d,e). The simulated results successfully demonstrated the color-generating capabilities of NBRFs, such as reflected brightness and color purity, with respect to wavelength and bandwidth.

In general, desired peak wavelength and bandwidth of pure-colored NBRFs are sensitively affected by changes in the structural parameters. Particularly, owing to the large index-contrast between   $  SiO_{2}  $   (1.46) and   $  TiO_{2}  $   (2.31), narrow-band and pure colors can be achieved by combinatorial optimization of the thickness, stacking order, number of stacking pairs, and periodicity of high- and low-index layers. Among the various structural parameters, the thickness of the high-index   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer in NBRF plays the most important role in determining the bandwidth of both types of NBRFs. Moreover, as the thickness of each multi-nanolayer increases, the peak wavelength of the NBRF shifts to the longer wavelength region. As the number of layers stacked by depositing   $  SiO_{2}  $   and   $  TiO_{2}  $   increases, the shapes of the reflectance spectra of both NBRF types approach a rectangular form. Figure 2a shows simulated reflectance spectra of VBCGYOR NBRFs with similar fwhm values (  $  \sim50  $   nm). Similarly, Figure 2d shows simulated reflectance spectra of G-NBRFs with different fwhm values (30, 50, 70, and 90 nm). These were obtained by using the “OpenFilters” software to optimize the thickness and numbers of periodically stacked high- and low-index layers (see Table S1). Based on simulation studies of G-NBRF, it is clearly revealed that purer color was obtained from reflective spectra with narrower bandwidth, whereas brighter color was achieved using reflective spectra with wider bandwidth.

As shown in Figure 2a and d, baseline ripple-shape patterns in the reflectance spectra were observed in all studied NBRFs due to the secondary interference effect of the one-dimensional photonic crystal (1D PC) multiple high/low layers. This effect decreases the purity of the color generation and creates unwanted reflection, which might reduce the PCEs of the PSCs. According to the basic concept of a bandstop filter, the characteristics of baseline ripple-shape patterns are induced by high secondary constructive interference and equivalent

admittance of the system.  $  ^{34}  $   Many researchers have attempted to eliminate baseline ripple-shape patterns,  $  ^{35}  $   and one popular strategy is to utilize computer simulation. In our simulations, we first used the "OpenFilters" software to design a periodic array of multi-nanolayer NBRFs; the layer structure was further refined using "The Essential Macleod", along with a conjugated gradient method. As a result, the elimination of baseline ripple-shape patterns was achieved by nonperiodic stacking. Figure 2b and e show the complete elimination of baseline ripple-shape patterns of VBCGYOR NBRFs and G-NBRFs with various bandwidths. Figure 2c compares the color gamut on the CIE color coordinate diagram of the two VBCGYOR NBRFs fabricated by periodic and nonperiodic stacking of high-/low-index layers. It can be seen that the color gamut of the ripple-free, nonperiodic NBRF is broader, with enhanced color purity, than that of control periodic NBRFs.

On the basis of the simulated results, we fabricated nonperiodic NBRFs composed of high-index   $  TiO_{2}  $   and low-index   $  SiO_{2}  $  . Figure 3a and b show the basic optical properties of the fabricated RGB-NBRFs, as well as their images. The baseline ripple-shape patterns of the fabricated RGB-NBRFs were nearly eliminated, though not completely, due to the practical limitations in the fabrication process. The peak wavelengths of the RGB-NBRFs are 642, 542, and 468 nm, respectively. All RGB-NBRFs showed high reflectance (  $  >90\%  $  ) at the stopband wavelength, while they maintained high transmittance (  $  >90\%  $  ) in other wavelengths. Figure 3c and d show color coordinate diagrams of the reflectance and transmittance spectra of the simulated and fabricated RGB-NBRFs. As can be seen in Figure 3c, the color gamut of the simulated RGB-NBRFs is broader than that of the fabricated RGB-NBRFs because the reflectance baseline of the fabricated RGB-NBRFs is increased due to the inherent reflectance of the glass surface. In the transmittance spectrum, though it is difficult to expect transmitted color due to the complementary color of the light, the location of the color coordinates, i.e., the simulated and fabricated transmittance, nearly match, with only a negligible difference (Figure 3d). Figure 3e–g show FE-TEM cross-sectional images of FIB-sliced RGB-NBRF specimens. The structures of all RGB NBRFs were well-matched with the simulated structures (thickness and order of stacks), as indicated in Table S2. Particularly, as indicated in Figure S1, the nonperiodic array deposition of   $  SiO_{2}  $   and   $  TiO_{2}  $  , used to eliminate the ripple-shape patterns, was also successfully achieved, just as in the simulation. Moreover, the increases in the thickness of the high- and low-index layers red-shifted the wavelengths of the NBRF films (Table 1), identical to the simulation results.

It is well known that 1D multi-nanolayers show optical property variation with respect to viewing angle. Therefore, it is expected that the RGB-NBRF films will show different reflected colors depending on the viewing site. Figure 4 shows the gradual change of the reflected color of the RGB-NBRFs with angular variation. According to the reflectance spectra of the RGB-NBRFs shown in Figure 4a–c, all spectra are blue-shifted when the viewing angle increases. In addition, the actual color of the RGB-NBRFs changes gradually with the viewing angle, ending in a pearl-like color (Figure 4d–f), which reflects the trend in the spectra of the RGB-NBRFs (Figure 4a–c). This pearl-like color formation with respect to viewing angle was beneficial from an aesthetic standpoint when our NBRFs were used in BIPV applications. # CONCLUSIONS

High-efficiency colorful PSCs with improved long-term photostability were achieved by exploiting color-controlled nonperiodic multi-nanolayer NBRFs. To optimize the multicolor NBRFs, their optical properties were first investigated via simulation, and a nonperiodic stacking concept with multi-nanolayer deposition was employed for fabrication by e-beam evaporation. While the NBRF-based PSCs demonstrated an aesthetic value, with pearl-like color, there was a decline in the PCE. Through the development of nonperiodic high-index/low-index stacking architecture for NBRFs, the unwanted baseline ripple-like reflectance was suppressed while minimizing PCE decrease. The PCE values of PSCs with nonperiodic RGB-NBRFs were 18.0%, 18.6%, and 18.9%, which are reductions of only 10%, 7%, and 6%, respectively, compared

The PSC with G-NBRF retained 65% of the initial PCE after 60 h under continuous illumination (AM 1.5G one sun) at MPP, whereas the black PSC retained only 30%. Aesthetic color value, pearl-like color appearance, minimum PCE loss, and enhanced photostability were simultaneously achieved by our developed, nonperiodic   $  SiO_{2}/TiO_{2}  $   NBRFs. # METHODS AND EXPERIMENTAL

Simulation and Fabrication of RGB Color and G-NBRFs with Various fwhm's. RGB NBRF and G-NBRFs with various fwhm's were simulated by using the "Openfilter" software to generate high reflection zones with interference effect; "Essential Macleod" software was used to eliminate ripple patterns of the reflectance while controlling the thickness and number of stacked high- and low-index layers. Periodic stacked multi-nanolayers (terminal eighth-wave-thick  $ \mathrm{SiO}_2 $  and quarter-wave-thick  $ \mathrm{TiO}_2 $  multi-nanolayer ((0.5 $ \mathrm{SiO}_2 / \mathrm{TiO}_2/ $   $ 0.5\mathrm{SiO}_2) $ ) were initially simulated and then were modified via a refinement process that employed a conjugated gradient method. Based on simulated data, RGB NBRFs and G-NBRFs with various fwhm's were fabricated on glass and opposite sides of ITO-coated glass substrates by depositing alternatively high- and low-index materials. Table 1 shows the thicknesses of each NBRF of the resulting  $ \mathrm{SiO}_2 $  and  $ \mathrm{TiO}_2 $  multi-nanolayer structures.

Characterization of NBRF Films. The structural properties of the RGB NBRF films were investigated using field-emission transmission electron microscopy (FE-TEM) at 200 kV (JEM-2100F, JEOL Ltd.). A FIB (XL 30, Philips) process was applied to obtain TEM samples of the RGB NBRF film. All transmittance spectra were measured using an S-3100 device (Scinco Co. Ltd.), and the diffusive reflectance spectra were measured using an LS-F100HS source unit with a 100 W halogen lamp (PSI).

Fabrication of PSC. Before use, indium-doped tin oxide coated glass (ITO/glass) substrates were sequentially cleaned using acetone and isopropyl alcohol (IPA). After cleaning,   $  ZnO/TiO_{2}  $   bilayer electron transporting layers (ETL) were deposited. The ZnO layers and   $  TiO_{2}  $   layers were deposited by in situ sol–gel conversion (thickness of 40 nm/30 nm).  $  ^{38,39}  $   The ZnO layers were fabricated using a 0.45 M solution with annealing at 130 °C for 10 min. The   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer was prepared using a precursor solution (a mixture of HCl and titanium(IV) isopropoxide) in anhydrous IPA (0.15 M).  $  ^{40}  $   The   $  TiO_{2}  $   solution was spin-coated onto ITO/ZnO substrates at 2000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 150 °C for 10 min and subsequently at 500 °C for 20 min. The   $  MA_{1-x}FA_{x}Pb(I_{x}Br_{1-x})_{3}  $  -based organic–inorganic hybrid perovskite (OIHP) active layers were prepared via a two-step sequential method.  $  ^{41}  $   First, a   $  PbI_{2}  $   solution was prepared by dissolving 461 mg of   $  PbI_{2}  $   in 1 mL of dimethylformamide. Then, the solution was spin-casted at 1800 rpm for 20 s, followed by thermal annealing at 100 °C for 3 min. After cooling, the   $  PbI_{2}  $   layers were dipped into a mixed solution of formamidium iodide (FAI)/methylammonium bromide (MABr). To prepare the FAI/MABr solution, 600 mg of FAI and 100 mg of MABr were dissolved in 10 mL of IPA and heated at 60 °C for 10 min before use. Then, the films were annealed at 80 °C for 2 min. After cooling, the perovskite films were washed with IPA using a drop-casting method at 3000 rpm for 20 s, followed by thermal annealing at 100 °C in a vacuum (  $  10^{-4}  $   Torr) for 20 min. To prepare the hole transport layer, spiroOMeTAD was coated over the OIHP layers. A 0.6 mM spiroOMeTAD solution was prepared in 1 mL of chlorobenzene additive (31 mM bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide lithium and 190 mM tert-butylpyridine). The entire device fabrication process was performed under an air atmosphere with controlled humidity (  $  \leq35\%  $  ). Finally, a Au electrode (thickness of 100 nm) was thermally evaporated at reduced pressure (  $  \sim10^{-6}  $   Torr).

Characterization of the PSC. The structural properties of the PSC were measured by FE-SEM (JSM-7610F, JEOL Ltd.). The current density–voltage   $  (J-V)  $   characteristics were analyzed using a Keithley 2401 source unit; the devices were illuminated by a xenon lamp solar simulator (Newport) under a light intensity of AM 1.5G (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ). The light intensity was calibrated by a KG-5 filter-covered monosilicon standard cell (Newport). All devices were measured under ambient air with a 100 mV s  $  ^{-1}  $   scanning rate. EQE measurements were performed using QuantX 300 (ORIEL), calibrated with a 603621 silicon and germanium reference detector. A continuous photostability test was performed under AM 1.5G one sun illumination without UV filter. The unencapsulated devices were measured every 12 h for 504 h at stabilized power output conditions. The relative humidity was 20%, and the temperature was 45 ± 5 °C. For long-term storage stability test, unencapsulated devices were stored in a N  $  _{2}  $  -filled box under dark conditions. The devices were measured under AM 1.5G one sun illumination (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ). The relative humidity was 20% at room temperature. The devices were measured every 120 h for more than 1000 h of storage.
