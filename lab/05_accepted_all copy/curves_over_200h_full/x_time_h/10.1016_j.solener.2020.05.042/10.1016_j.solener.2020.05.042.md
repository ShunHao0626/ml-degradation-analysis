# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been widely studied for their advantages of low cost (Jeon et al., 2014), easy manufacturing (Ye et al., 2016), tunable bandgap (Eperon et al., 2014) and excellent photoelectric performance (Lal et al., 2017; Tong et al., 2019; Zheng et al., 2019b). Based on above reasons, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs stupendously increased from  $ 3.8\% $  (Kojima et al., 2009) to  $ 25.2\% $  (Best Research-Cell Efficiencies, 2019) within a short time. Despite PSCs have been very impressed with the soaring PCE, the long-term stability of conventional three-dimensional (3D) perovskite, particularly under a condition rich in moisture and oxygen, remains an obstacle to the road of the mass-produced and application of PSCs.

Therefore, many scientific research teams developed two-dimensional (2D) structured perovskite material with organic spacers and gain lots of interests to achieve more stable PSCs in the research field. Due to the size of R cation in 2D perovskite (aliphatic or aromatic alkylammonium cation) is too large to be well accommodated for 3D perovskite (Roghabadi et al., 2019), its initial tetragonal structure becomes energetically stabilized (Krishna et al., 2019). # ABSTRACT

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have shown a great potential in the field of alternative energy due to their superior performance and processing compatibility. However, prior to commercialization, a major drawback of poor stability needs to be addressed imperatively. Recently, mixing 3D and 2D perovskite as active layer is a promising strategy for blocking water and oxygen to increase stability, as well as mitigating the effect of insulated spacer in bulk to strengthen charge migration. Here, we report a novel method to fabricate high-performance inverted PSCs by involving Phenethylammonium bromide (PEABr) as passivator. During the fabrication, PEABr will diffuse into 3D perovskite and form a mixed 3D/2D structure at the lower interface. This ingenious structure will restrain the charge recombination and reduce the trap state density at the transport layer/perovskite interface and bulk of 3D perovskite. Due to the bottom-up passivation effects, mixed 3D/2D PSCs achieve an improved PCE from  $ 17.59\% $  to  $ 19.46\% $ , in combination with enhanced long-term air stability. This 3D/2D mix structure demonstrates a great potential for developing high-performance and high stability PSCs.

However, since most of Ruddlesden–Popper perovskite has a wide bandgap (Krishna et al., 2019) and the inhibition of out-of-plane charge transport induced by insulated organic cations (Tsai et al., 2016), PCEs of 2D PSCs are usually inferior than 3D PCSs. For example, in the research of Karunadasa et al, the stability was successfully improved but the PCE only reached to 4.73% for 2D PSCs based on   $  (\mathrm{PEA})_{2}(\mathrm{MA})_{2}\mathrm{Pb}_{3}\mathrm{I}_{10}  $  , while the devices containing   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   have exceeded PCEs of 15% (Smith et al., 2014). Sargent's group investigated the link between PCE and long-term stability of quasi-2D perovskite   $  (\mathrm{PEA}_{2}\mathrm{MA}_{n-1}\mathrm{Pb}_{n}\mathrm{I}_{3n+1})  $  , with the decrease of n, quasi-2D perovskite is more similar to 2D perovskite, PSCs will become more stable at the cost of sacrificing part of its PCE (Quan et al., 2016). It is noted that this major conflict between stability and performance limits the application of 2D perovskite in the field of photovoltaic (Lee et al., 2018; Quan et al., 2016), which means that it is urgent to find a better strategy to solve this drawback.

3D/2D mix perovskite which combines the advantages of 2D and 3D perovskite has been used as an efficient method, where the main body of the light absorption layer is 3D perovskite to ensure a high efficiency. Whereas the hydrophobic 2D perovskite thinly covers the surface of the 3D perovskite, the stability is greatly improved and the defects are reduced efficiently at the interface. Park's group used 5-AVA to form the 2D perovskite capping layer which plays a vital role in suppressing charge carriers recombination at the interface, therefore, high PCE of  $ 16.75\% $  was obtained (Chen, J.Z. et al., 2018). Etgar's group synthesized quasi 2D perovskite using various long organic barriers (benzyl ammonium, BA; phenylethyl ammonium, PEA; and propyl phenyl ammonium, PPA), and the quasi 2D perovskite based on PEA shows the best stability, which indicates the potential of PEA (Cohen et al., 2017). To the best of our knowledge, most 3D/2D stacking structures construct 2D perovskite layer on 3D to passivate the top surface. However, Bolink's group fabricated 2D/3D, 2D/3D/2D, 3D/2D perovskite heterostructures by dual-source vacuum deposition to study the interaction between the 3D and 2D phases (La-Placa et al., 2019). Although the performance of the device based on mixing 3D and 2D perovskite is worse than the device based on pure 3D perovskite, it is an illuminating work to construct 3D perovskite on 2D perovskite layer.

In this work, we introduce the 2D perovskite spaced by PEABr as a passivation layer at hole transport layer (HTL)/3D perovskite layer  $ (\mathrm{MAPbI}_3) $  interface. When spin-coating  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  on the PEABr, the PEABr layer will be dissolved in the perovskite solution. Different from the ordinary doping, dissolved PEABr firstly contact the bottom surface of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  and diffuse upward, then react with  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  to form a peculiar bottom-up phase segregated graded architecture. By this strategy, the PEA cation and Br anion can diffuse into the 3D perovskite to passivate the defects, especially defects on the lower surface and the bulk near the bottom surface. This passivation effect leads to the lower nonradiative recombination and longer carrier lifetime, which is supported by steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL). And after treated by PEABr, the trap-state density has apparently decreased. By means of X-ray diffraction (XRD) and scanning electronic microscopy (SEM), we observed PEABr-treated perovskite film has better crystallinity and larger grains. Besides, 2D perovskite acts as a moisture barrier, which brings a more stable structure to against the humidity outside. As results, the PCE was improved from  $ 17.59\% $  to  $ 19.46\% $  when using the PEABr. Furthermore, the device treated by PEABr retained  $ 86.11\% $  of initial PCE after the  $ 1300\mathrm{h} $  stored in  $ \mathbf{N}_2 $ , which exhibits superior stability compared with control devices (retained  $ 55.32\% $  of initial PCE). These results demonstrate that the 3D/2D mix structure formed by PEABr is an effective barrier for moisture, at the same time, this structure can modify the bottom surface of 3D perovskite to improve the PCE of PSCs. Thus, this novel bottom-up passivation mechanism with 3D/2D perovskite provides a new pathway to overcome defects in PSC devices.

# 2. Experimental sections

# 2.1. Materials and solvent

Indium tin oxide glass substrates (ITO, 15  $ \Omega $  sq $ ^{-1} $ ) were purchased from South China Science & Technology Company Limited. Isopropyl alcohol (IPA) was purchased from Tianjin Zhiyuan Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. MAI (99.5%), PbI $ _2 $  (99.99%), TAPC (99%), PC $ _{61} $ BM (99%), Bphen (99%), were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. Phenethylammonium Bromide (PEABr, 98%), N, N-Dimethylformamide (DMF, anhydrous, 99.9%), chlorobenzene (CB, 99.8%), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

# 2.2. Fabrication of PSCs

ITO glass was cleaned by using an ultra-sonication bath successively with soap water, acetone, de-ionized water, isopropanol, and then the ITO substrate was placed in the hot oven to dry 30 min. Before use, the ITO was treated with ultraviolet ozone for 15 min. TAPC (5 mg/ml, diluted by chlorobenzene) was spin-coated (at 3000 r.p.m. for 40 s) on the top of the substrates and annealed in glove box filled with nitrogen

at 120 °C for 15 min. For PEABr passivation layer, different concentrations of PEABr dissolved in isopropanol and spin-coated on the TAPC film at 5000 rpm for 30 s. Next, 1.6 M perovskite precursor solution (MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  in DMF) was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 25 s, and then 200 μL chlorobenzene was poured on the spinning substrate 20 s prior to the end of the program. Then these substrates were annealed at 110 °C for 20 min to form 350 nm perovskite film. After the substrate was cooled to the room temperature, 50 μL PC  $  _{61}  $  BM solution (20 mg/ml, diluted by chlorobenzene) was spin-coated at 2000 rpm for 40 s onto perovskite film, and then annealed at 110 °C for 20 min. Finally, Bphen (0.2 nm) and Ag (80 nm) were deposited on top of the PC  $  _{61}  $  BM film at vacuum degree below 3.0 × 10  $  ^{-4}  $  Pa, the active area of the device was 0.02 cm  $  ^{2}  $ .

# 2.3. Device characterization

All measurements below were carried out in ambient air at room temperature without encapsulation. The current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics of the PSCs were measured by a Keithley 4200 semiconductor characterization systems under AM1.5G solar simulator (xenon lamp,  $ 100\mathrm{mW/cm}^{-2} $ ) which was calibrated by a standard Si cell. The J-V curves of single-carrier devices were also measured by the Keithley 4200 systems. The EQE spectra were measured under EQE system (QTEST HIFINITY 5, Crowntech Inc., USA) with a monochromatic illumination. The morphology was characterized by scanning electron microscope (SEM) equipped with an energy-dispersive spectrometry (ZEISS, Geminis SEM 300) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) (Agilent, AFM-5500). The PL spectrum of the perovskite layer was measured with PL spectroscopy (Hitachi, F-7000). The crystal structure of the film was tested by X-ray diffraction (XRD, TongDa, TD-3500, China) with a Cu Kα radiation ( $ \gamma = 0.154\mathrm{nm} $ ).

# 3. Results and discussion

The molecular structure of PEABr is illustrated in Fig. 1(a), which is introduced to form a 3D/2D mix structure for efficient and stable p-i-n type PSCs. As showed in Fig. 1(b), the device structure employed in this work is ITO (135 nm)/TAPC (20 nm)/PEABr/MAPbI $ _3 $  (350 nm)/PC $ _{61} $ BM (40 nm)/Bphen (0.2 nm)/Ag (80 nm).

# 3.1. Fabrication of 3D/2D mix structure

Noted that after making a complete device, the PEABr layer does not appear as a single layer structure due to the high solubility of PEABr in solvent of perovskite (DMF). As depicted in Fig. 1(c), the main process to fabricate the 3D/2D mix structure contains five steps: (1) spin-coating the PEABr layer on TAPC. (2) Dropping the 3D perovskite precursor solution onto the PEABr during the rotary table spinning at 4,000 rpm. (3) The PEABr film dissolves and diffuses into the perovskite precursor film while the spinning process. (4) Pouring the antisolvent on the spinning substrate to quickly remove excess solvent and form a mesophase. (5) After annealing, PEABr reacts with  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  to form a 3D/2D structure (Fig. 1(c)), which 2D perovskite formed on the bottom surface of 3D perovskite.

# 3.2. Morphology and composition analysis

The surface morphology was investigated by SEM and AFM to figure out the impact of PEABr on 3D perovskite film. As shown in Fig. 2(a-b), perovskite film without PEABr has some pin-holes at the surface. In contrast, the PEABr-treated perovskite film had better surface coverage and larger grains. It indicates that the PEA cation can self-assemble at grain boundaries to improve the topography (Li et al., 2017). In Fig. 2(c-d), the AFM images show that the root-mean-square roughness  $ (\sigma_{\mathrm{RMS}}) $  of perovskite with and without PEABr is  $ 9.80~\mathrm{nm} $  and  $ 9.15~\mathrm{nm} $ , respectively. The control device yields a max PCE of  $ 17.59\% $  with an open-circle voltage  $ (\mathrm{V_{OC}}) $  of  $ 1.05\mathrm{V} $ , a  $ \mathrm{J}_{\mathrm{SC}} $  of  $ 22.98\mathrm{mA/cm}^{-2} $ , an FF of  $ 75.62\% $ . The optimal thickness of the 2D layer and the passivation of PEA cation/Br anion lead to the increase of FF and  $ \mathrm{J}_{\mathrm{SC}} $ . Moreover, the hysteresis index (HI) was tested next, it can be calculated by  $ (\mathrm{PCE}_{\mathrm{reverse}} - \mathrm{PCE}_{\mathrm{forward}}) / \mathrm{PCE}_{\mathrm{reverse}} $  (Kim and Park, 2014). The HI of the PEABr-treated champion device is  $ 2.31\% $ , whereas the best control device only has a HI of  $ 9.15\% $ , which is attributed to the passivation effect of PEABr. # 4. Conclusions

In conclusion, we introduce a novel approach to form a special 3D/2D mix structure by employing 2D perovskite as passivator. Firstly, spin-coating the PEABr before the fabrication of 3D perovskite film. Then, during the spinning process of 3D perovskite, PEABr will be dissolved and diffuse upward into the interphase of 3D perovskite. In consequence, the 3D/2D mix structure is formed. The PEABr-treated device shows a lower trap-state density and the reduced nonradiative recombination, owing to the passivation effect of 2D perovskite (PEA  $  ^{+1}  $  cation and Br  $  ^{-1}  $  anion). The long-term stability of PSCs treated by PEABr also gets substantially improved due to the hydrophobic nature of 2D perovskite. As a result, compared to a PCE of 17.59% yield by the traditional 3D perovskite structure, a dramatically enhanced PCE of 19.46% is achieved for the device with 3D/2D mix structure. Furthermore, the PEABr-treated device exhibits a superior stability which retained 86.11% of its initial PCE after 1300 h stored in N  $  _{2}  $  atmosphere, while the control device only retained 55.32% of its original PCE. These results demonstrate that introducing the 3D/2D mix structure in PSCs is an effective strategy to improve the PCE as well as device
