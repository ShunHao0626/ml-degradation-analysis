# Acid Dissociation Constant: A Criterion for Selecting Passivation Agents in Perovskite Solar Cells

un-Ho Lee, Seonghwa Jeong, Seongrok Seo, Hyunjung Shin, Chunqing Ma,* and Nam-Gyu Pa

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Although post-treatment has been regarded as one of the effective ways to passivate the underlying defects in perovskite solar cells (PSCs), little attention has been paid to how to select suitable passivation agents. Here, we report on the dependence of photovoltaic performance on acid dissociation constant  $ (K_{\mathrm{a}}) $  of passivation agents to guide a criterion for selecting passivation agents in PSCs. Power conversion efficiency (PCE) is increased after post-treatment with high- $ \mathsf{pK}_{\mathrm{a}} $  (10.6) cyclohexylammonium chloride (CYCl), whereas low- $ \mathsf{pK}_{\mathrm{a}} $  (4.6) anilinium chloride (ANCI) decreases PCE because of more traps generated adversely by ANCI. Degree of deprotonation  $ (\mathsf{pK}_{\mathrm{a}} $  value) is responsible for generation of defect-mediated traps, where relatively more deprotonation from lower- $ \mathsf{pK}_{\mathrm{a}} $  ANCI generates free iodide, resulting in iodide defects. The CYCI-treated  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  film deposited on a highly transparent FTO substrate shows a reverse-scanned PCE of  $ 24.98\% $ , and

91% of the initial PCE is maintained after storage in the dark for over 1300 h.

Since the seminal reports of solid-state perovskite solar cells (PSCs) in 2012,  $  ^{1,2}  $  following two important seed technologies applied to sensitization in a liquid-junction structure,  $  ^{3,4}  $  PSCs have been regarded as a preponderant technology in photovoltaics because of superb power conversion efficiency (PCE) of above 25% for a submicromerter-thick perovskite film.  $  ^{5}  $  PCE increment from 9.7%  $  ^{1}  $  to 25.5%  $  ^{5}  $  has been achieved by significant enhancement of open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ) and fill factor (FF) rather than short-circuit photocurrent (  $  J_{sc}  $  ).  $  ^{6}  $  Because the  $  V_{oc}  $  and FF are closely related to nonradiative and interfacial recombination due to bulk and interface defects,  $  ^{7-10}  $  such a remarkable improvement in  $  V_{oc}  $  and FF is probably attributed to defect passivation technologies. This indicates that passivation of defects in PSCs is critical in achieving high efficiency. Moreover, stability and current–voltage hysteresis can be simultaneously improved by defect passivation.  $  ^{11-15}  $

Defect passivation is able to be performed by using certain chemicals, where the passivation agents are included in the perovskite precursor solution for passivating bulk and/or grain boundary defects or post-treated on the perovskite film for passivating grain boundary and/or interface defects. Many molecules or their ionic salts have been studied to passivate defects, where functional group should be carefully considered depending on type of defect, such as cation or anion defect.  $  ^{16}  $

Here, we report on the dependence of photovoltaic performance on the acid dissociation constant of organic ammonium chlorides in PSCs. Organic ammonium chlorides with different acid dissociation constant (  $  pK_{a}  $  ) are post-treated on the perovskite films, from which photovoltaic performance is found to be sensitive to   $  pK_{a}  $  . As compared to the PCE of the control device,   $  pK_{a}  $   of around 10–11 shows higher PCE, whereas lower PCE is observed for the   $  pK_{a}  $   of around 5–6. Strength of the N–H  $  ^{+}  $  bond on the passivation agents is found to be critical in determining the interaction between the passivation agents and perovskite and the formation of mobiles ions on the perovskite surface. In particular, post-treatment using cyclohexylammonium chloride (CYCl) with   $  pK_{a} = 10.6  $   shows the reverse-scanned PCE of 24.98% with a stabilized

PCE of 23.85% and maintenance of PCE over 90% of initial PCE after 1315 h.

The   $  pK_{a}  $  -dependent effect of surface passivation by various ammonium salts on the photovoltaic performance is examined for devices with an FTO/SnO  $  _{2}  $  /FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  /spiro-MeOTAD/Ag configuration (a cross-sectional SEM image for the full device is depicted in Figure S1).   $  K_{a}  $   of the conjugated acid of amine is defined by   $  K_{a} = \{[RNH_{2}] \times [H^{+}]\}/[RNH_{3}^{+}]  $   and   $  pK_{a} = -\log K_{a}  $  . High   $  pK_{a}  $   means weak acid, which leads to strong N-H  $  ^{+}  $  bond in RNH  $  _{3}^{+}  $  . We have used five ammonium salts with different   $  pK_{a}  $   values: anilinium chloride (ANCl), ethylenediammonium dichloride (EDACl), ammonium chloride (ACl), cyclohexylammonium chloride (CYCl), and ethylammonium chloride (EACl) for post-treatment.   $  pK_{a}  $   values were reported to be 4.6, 6.9, 9.2, 10.6, and 10.8 for ANCl, EDACl, ACl, CYCl, and EACl, respectively,  $  ^{24,25}  $   which is confirmed to be   $  pK_{a} = 4.6, 6.9, 9.2, 10.6  $  , and 10.8 by titration of aqueous 0.1 M amine and 0.2 M HCl. For the detailed study on the influence of the   $  pK_{a}  $   values, ANCl (  $  pK_{a} = 4.6  $  ) and CYCl (  $  pK_{a} = 10.6  $  ) sharing similar structure (the 6-membered carbon ring with the primary ammonium group) are selected. Electron density of the N atom is more localized in cyclohexylamine than in aniline, which is attributed to the inductive effect of the 6-membered carbon ring between the electron-withdrawing aromatic ring and the electron-donating aliphatic ring.  $  ^{26}  $   The basicity of cyclohexylamine is thus stronger than aniline, which results in weaker conjugate acid (CYCl) and, in consequence, the higher   $  pK_{a}  $   (lower   $  K_{a}  $  ) value due to stronger N–H  $  ^{+}  $  bond.  $  ^{27}  $   ANCl is a relatively stronger conjugate acid of aniline because of weaker N–H  $  ^{+}  $  bond. It is expected that the deprotonation of ammonium groups on ANCl can induce more mobile ions on the perovskite surface, which will be studied in detail below. Surface scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images in Figure S2 and absorption spectroscopy in Figure S3a show that the post-treatment has little effect on surface morphology and optical properties of bulk film.   $  J_{sc}  $   is slightly higher for CYCl than for ANCl as observed in Figure 1i, which is confirmed by the EQE in Figure S3b.

Because photovoltaic parameters are improved by CYCl but slightly decreased by ANCl, steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved PL (TRPL) are measured to understand change in recombination and carrier lifetime. Steady-state PL in Figure 2a shows that the PL intensity of perovskite post-treated with CYCl is about 2.7 times higher than that of the control sample, which is indicative of a reduced nonradiative recombination. On the other hand, PL intensity is significantly reduced after post-treatment with ANCl, which underlines more traps might be generated even after post-treatment. TRPL data in Figure 2b are fit with a biexponential decay equation (see fitting results in Table S1). The relatively fast decay component   $  (\tau_{1})  $   is related to trap-mediated nonradiative recombination on the surface, and the much slower component   $  (\tau_{2})  $   is associated with radiative recombination of free carriers in the bulk.  $  ^{28-30}  $   The control sample shows   $  \tau_{1}  $   and   $  \tau_{2}  $   values of 10.5 and 592.8 ns, respectively. For the post-treatment with CYCl,   $  \tau_{1}  $   and   $  \tau_{2}  $   are elongated to 28.4 and 1187.5 ns, which indicates that the surface treatment with high pK  $  _{a}  $   value material reduces surface trap density and enhances charge carrier lifetime.  $  ^{31}  $   In contrast to CYCl,   $  \tau_{2}  $   is significantly shortened to 108.3 ns after post-treatment with ANCl in spite of the elongated   $  \tau_{1}  $   to 23.7 ns, which underlines that the bulk property might be damaged by post-treatment with ANCl despite improved surface properties.

The space-charge-limited current (SCLC) method is applied to calculate trap density  $ (n_{\mathrm{t}}) $  of the control and post-treated samples as shown in Figure 2c.  $ n_{\mathrm{t}} $  is determined by the trap

filled limit voltage   $  V_{TFL} = en_{t}d^{2}/2\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon  $  , where e, d,   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $  , and   $  \varepsilon  $   are the electric charge   $  (1.602 \times 10^{-19} \mathrm{C})  $  , the perovskite film thickness (thickness was determined from the cross-sectional SEM image in Figure S1), the vacuum permittivity   $  (8.8542 \times 10^{-14} \mathrm{F/cm})  $  , and dielectric constant, respectively.  $  ^{32}  $   The dielectric constants are calculated from the geometric capacitance (Figure S4).  $  ^{33,34}  $   On the basis of these   $  V_{TFL}  $   values,   $  n_{t}  $   are calculated to be   $  1.5 \times 10^{16}  $  ,   $  1.6 \times 10^{16}  $  , and   $  1.4 \times 10^{16} cm^{-3}  $   for the control, ANCl, and CYCl, respectively. The post-treatment with CYCl reduces   $  n_{t}  $  , while the ANCI-treated increases   $  n_{t}  $  . The SCLC results are consistent with the TRPL results.

To further elucidate the effect of   $  pK_{a}  $   on photovoltaic parameters, capacitance–frequency (C-f) plots at different temperatures ranging from 293 to 353 K are examined to acquire the density of trap state (tDOS). From C-f plots in Figure S5, tDOS is calculated by the following equation: tDOS =   $  -\frac{V_{\omega}dC\omega}{eWd\omega kT}  $  , where   $  V_{bi}  $   and W represent the built-in potential and the depletion width extracted from Mott–Schottky plot (Figure S6); C is the capacitance,   $  \omega  $   the angular frequency, k the Boltzmann's constant, and T the temperature.  $  ^{28,35,36}  $   The x-axis is converted from   $  \omega  $   to the demarcation energy   $  E_{\omega} = E_{T} - E_{V}  $   (  $  E_{T}  $  , trap energy;   $  E_{v}  $  , valence band maximum), where   $  E_{\omega} = kT\ln\frac{\omega_{0}}{\omega} = kT\ln\frac{2\beta N_{r}}{\omega}  $  . Based on the parameters of   $  V_{bi} = 0.9032  $  , 0.9027, and 0.9230 V and W = 40, 20, and 40 nm for control, ANCI, and CYCI, respectively, tDOS profiles at different temperature are obtained in Figure 2d–f. tDOS is increased with   $  E_{\omega}  $   at a given temperature and with temperature at a given energy, which is similarly observed regardless of passivation agent. The increased trap density with temperature is due to higher chance of ion migration at higher temperature. Because it was reported that relatively deep trap states are mainly ascribed to the surface defects and shallow trap states are related to the bulk perovskite,  $  ^{36}  $   the most probable tDOS at around 0.4S eV can be attributed to the deep trap state due to surface defects of perovskite. At near room temperature of 293 K (Figure 2g), the CYCI-treated device shows lower trap density, whereas the ANCI-treated one exhibits trap density higher than that of the control device in the entire   $  E_{\omega}  $   range from 0.35 to 0.5 eV. The relatively higher tDOS for ANCI implies that perovskite generates more defects after post-treatment with ANCI. On the other hand, the reduced tDOS for CYCI is thus indicative of relatively lower defects. Because tDOS depends on   $  pK_{a}  $  , the stronger N–H  $  ^{+}  $  bond in CYCI with high   $  pK_{a}  $   is more beneficial for passivating defects than the weaker N–H  $  ^{+}  $  bond in ANCI with low   $  pK_{a}  $   because of the released H  $  ^{+}  $  ions and mobile iodide ions (Figure S7).  $  ^{37}  $   Thus, a significant difference in   $  V_{oc}  $   between   $  pK_{a} = 4.6  $   and 10.6 observed in Figure 1g can be explained by change in trap density.

In addition, the ammonium functional group in the passivation agents can form ionic and/or hydrogen bonding with iodide, which can lower the binding energy of core-level iodide. $ ^{44} $  Pronounced chemical shift in the I binding energy by CYCl is indicative of stronger ionic and/or hydrogen bonding between CYCl and iodide (including interstitial iodide), which can grab effectively the mobile iodide. $ ^{45} $  The reduced HI by CYCl as observed in Table 1 can be explained by ionic and/or hydrogen bonding effect in relation with pK $  _{a}  $ .

There are essentially two types of mobile ions in organic lead halide perovskite: positive ions (organic cation and proton) and negative halide ion.  $  ^{46}  $   It was reported that the orientation of the organic cation can affect the mobile halide ion, which can provide effective pathways for ion migration.  $  ^{47}  $   Because release of proton depends on   $  pK_{a}  $  , iodide migration is expected to be affected by   $  pK_{a}  $  . In order to investigate the effect of   $  pK_{a}  $   on the extent of interaction with mobile ions, temperature-dependent conductivity was measured with the lateral device

structure of Au/perovskite/Au in the dark under vacuum (<10 $  ^{-2}  $  Torr) at a bias voltage of 2 V. The activation energy (σ) for ion diffusion can be calculated from the Nernst–Einstein equation, σ(T)T = σ₀ exp(−Eₐ/kT), where σ₀ is a constant, k the Boltzmann's constant, and T the temperature.⁴⁸ Eₐ value is evaluated to be 139 meV for the control sample, which is significantly improved to 203 meV after post-treatment with CYCl but slightly decreased to 128 meV after treating ANCl (Figure 3c). This indicates that ion migration is substantially suppressed by CYCl, while ANCl has an adverse effect on ion migration, which can explain again the decreased and increased HI by CYCl and ANCl, respectively.

UV–vis absorption spectroscopy was further studied to understand the extent of mobile ions by detecting triiodide species (  $  I_{3}^{-}  $  , stemmed from   $  I^{-}  $  and   $  I_{2}  $  ) formation on the perovskite surface. The ANCI- and CYCI-treated perovskite films along with the nontreated one are immersed in isopropyl alcohol (IPA), which is followed by measuring absorbance hourly under 1 sun light illumination. As shown in Figure 3d–f, the absorption peaks of   $  I_{3}^{-}  $  around 289 and 357 nm evolve with time.  $  ^{49,50}  $   As compared to the nontreated perovskite film, the peak intensity is markedly higher for ANCI but slightly lower for CYCI after 6 h, which indicates that the CYCI treatment tends to inhibit growth of   $  I_{3}^{-}  $  , whereas the ANCI treatment facilitates growth of   $  I_{3}^{-}  $  . Under illumination, holes of perovskite can oxidize the free   $  I^{-}  $  to   $  I_{2}  $   that is further reacted with   $  I^{-}  $  to form   $  I_{3}^{-}  $  (Figure 3g). Thus, higher concentration of   $  I_{3}^{-}  $  is indicative of more iodide defects in the ANCI-treated perovskite film. This apparent difference in formation of triiodide species between ANCI and CYCI is well consistent with the results of ion diffusion activation energy in Figure 3c and clearly suggests that higher   $  pK_{a}  $   is beneficial to suppressing iodide defects.

We have demonstrated that the   $  pK_{a}  $   value played an important role in passivating the surface defects of the perovskite film. The passivation agents with high   $  pK_{a}  $   values were found to efficiently reduce the trap states and improve the performances, while passivation with materials with low   $  pK_{a}  $   values decreased photovoltaic performance because of more iodide defects resulting from deprotonation. On the basis of the current study, the effective passivation through CYCl with   $  pK_{a}  $   of 10.6 led to a reverse-scanned PCE of 24.98%. # EXPERIMENTAL METHODS

Materials Synthesis. Formamidinium iodide (FAI) was synthesized as follows: 20 g of formamidinium acetate (99%, Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in 10 mL of ethanol (99.5%, SamChun Chemicals) in an ice bath, to which 40 mL of HI (57 wt % diluted in   $  H_{2}O  $  , Sigma-Aldrich) was added dropwise under stirring. After being stirred for 30 min at 0 °C, the solution was further stirred for 30 min at room temperature. The precipitate was collected by using a rotary evaporator at 65 °C, which was washed with diethyl ether (99.0%, SamChun Chemicals) three times and dried in an oven at 65 °C for 30 min. (Caution: A closed oven should not be used for drying a larger quantity.) The dried product was recrystallized using ethanol and diethyl ether, which was repeated three times; the sample was then dried in a vacuum chamber for 24 h. FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   yellow powder was synthesized according to the method reported elsewhere.  $  ^{37}  $   FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   yellow powder was first synthesized, which was dissolved in γ-butyrolactone (GBL, 99.5%, SamChun Chemicals) to make 1.0 M solution. After filtration, the solution was heated in an oil bath at 150 °C for 2 h. The single crystals grown in GBL solution were collected and then dried in the vacuum oven for 30 min after washing with acetonitrile and diethyl ether three times. Various Ammonium chloride salts were synthesized by reacting HCl (37 wt % diluted in   $  H_{2}O  $  , Sigma-Aldrich) and each amine (aniline, ethylenediamine, amine, cyclohexylamine, methylamine, and ethylamine) in a similar way for synthesizing FAI,

where the mixing ratio of amine to HCl was 1:1.3 for monoamine and 1:2.3 for diamine.

Device Fabrication. F-doped tin oxide (FTO) coated conductive glasses were laser-etched and cleaned by detergent, deionized (DI) water, acetone, and ethanol, which was further treated with UV/ozone (UVO) for 60 min prior to use. The   $  SnO_{2}  $   film was spin-coated using a commercial   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloidal solution (  $  SnO_{2}  $  , 15% in   $  H_{2}O  $  , Alfa Aesar) (diluted with DI water to adjust to 4 wt %) at 4000 rpm for 20 s, which was annealed at 185 °C on a hot plate for 30 min. The   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   perovskite precursor solution was prepared by mixing 663.0 mg of the synthesized   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   single crystal, 13.5 mg of the synthesized MACl, and 8.4 mg of CsCl (trace metal basis, ≥99.999%, Sigma-Aldrich) with 74.4 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, anhydrous, ≥99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich) and 557.6 μL of dimethylformamide (DMF, anhydrous, ≥99.8%, Sigma-Aldrich). The precursor solution was spin-coated on the   $  SnO_{2}  $ -coated FTO substrate at 1000 rpm for 5 s and 5000 rpm for 20 s, where 1 mL of diethyl ether was dripped while spinning to form an adduct intermediate. The adduct films were annealed at 150 °C for 10 min. Post-treatment was performed on the annealed perovskite film by dripping 5 mM of ammonium salts at 4000 rpm for 20 s, which was annealed at 100 °C for 3 min. The   $  N^{2}, N^{2}, N^{2'}  $  ,   $  N^{2'}  $  ,   $  N^{7}, N^{7'}, N^{7'}  $  ,   $  N^{7'}  $  -octakis(4-methoxyphenyl)-9,9'-spirobi[9H-fluorene]-2,2',7,7'-tetramine (spiro-MeOTAD, 99%, Merck) layer was formed on the perovskite layer by spin-coating of 20 μL of the solution comprising 60 mg spiro-MeOTAD in 0.7 mL chlorobenzene including 25.5 μL t-BP (4-tert-butylpyridine, 98%, Sigma-Aldrich) and 15.5 μL Li-TFSI solution (540 mg Li-TFSI in 1 mL acetonitrile (99.8%, Sigma-Aldrich)) at 3000 rpm for 20 s. Finally, the Ag electrode with a thickness of about 70–100 nm was deposited on top of the spiro-MeOTAD layer by a thermal evaporator under <2 × 10⁻⁶ Torr at an average rate of 0.2 Å/s. The PL measurements of the perovskite films were based on the bare glass without ETL and HTL. For temperature-dependent conductivity measurement, the lateral devices with a Au/perovskite/Au configuration were prepared by deposition of Au electrodes on the perovskite film (deposited on bare glass) with distance of ~30 μm by a thermal evaporator. The devices for the SCLC measurements were fabricated with a configuration of FTO/perovskite/Au. The FTO/  $  SnO_{2}  $ /perovskite/spiro-MeOTAD/Au structured device was prepared for electrochemical impedance measurement.

Characterizations. J-V measurements were performed using a Keithley 2400 source meter under simulated AM 1.5G 1 sun illumination (100 mW/cm²) using a solar simulator (Oriel Sol 3A class AAA) equipped with a 450 W xenon lamp (Newport 6280NS). The light intensity was adjusted using a Si solar cell with KG-2 filter that was calibrated by National Renewable Energy Lab (NREL). External quantum efficiency (EQE) was measured using a QuantX-300 Quantum Efficiency System (Newport), where a monochromatic beam was generated by chopping the white light using a 100 W xenon lamp. The optical absorbance was measured by a UV-vis spectrometer (Lambda 45, PerkinElmer). Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) was measured using a fluorescence spectrometer (QuantaurusTau C11367-12, Hamamatsu). Samples were photoexcited with a pulsed laser with wavelength of 464 nm and the frequency of 10 MHz.
