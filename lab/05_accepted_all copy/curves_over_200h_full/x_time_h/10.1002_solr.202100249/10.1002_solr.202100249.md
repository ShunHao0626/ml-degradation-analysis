# Revealing the Mechanism of   $  \pi  $   Aromatic Molecule as an Effective Passivator and Stabilizer in Highly Efficient Wide-Bandgap Perovskite Solar Cells

Jiwei Liang, Cong Chen, Xuzhi Hu, Meng Xiao, Chen Wang, Fang Yao, Jing Li, Haibing Wang, Jingwang He, Bo Da, Zejun Ding, Weijun Ke,* Chen Tao,* and Guojia Fang*

Trap-induced nonradiative recombination and decomposition are the major limiting factors that hinder the development of mixed-halide wide-bandgap perovskite solar cells. Specifically, the incorporation of formamidinium (FA  $  ^{+}  $  ) and bromide in wide-bandgap (WBG) perovskite materials leads to shallow-energy-level traps and inferior light stability. Herein, the electron-withdrawing molecule 2,3,5,6 tetrafluoro-7,7,8,8-tetracyanoquinodimethane (F4-TCNQ) is used as an effective passivator and stabilizer, which has molecular interaction with the FA  $  ^{+}  $  cation for reducing trap densities and enhancing the stability of WBG perovskite solar cells. It is found that the extended  $  \pi  $  aromatic system in F4-TCNQ can enhance the binding energy and stabilize the FA  $  ^{+}  $  cation in perovskite films. Furthermore, the 1.67 eV bandgap inverted perovskite solar cells with a small amount of F4-TCNQ have shallower defect levels, reduced trap density, and decreased nonradiative recombination, therefore giving a remarkably improved power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 20.0%. Most importantly, the unencapsulated devices with F4-TCNQ additive have greatly enhanced stability, maintaining 88% of their peak PCEs under continuous illumination after 840 h, whereas the control devices only retain 48% of their PCEs after 500 h.

# 1. Introduction

Organic–inorganic mixed-halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have drawn worldwide attention because of their high absorption coefficients, long charge carrier diffusion lengths, and high power conversion efficiencies (PCEs).  $  ^{[1-7]}  $   To date, single-junction PSCs have already reached a record PCE of 25.5%,  $  ^{[8]}  $

but they are still dominated by the theoretical Shockley–Queisser (S–Q) limits.  $  ^{[9-14]}  $   Tandem solar cells consisting of a top wide-bandgap (WBG) subcell and a bottom narrow-bandgap subcell have an enormous opportunity to overcome the S–Q limits for single-junction solar cells.  $  ^{[15-18]}  $   To achieve excellent output performance of the tandem solar cells, the top subcell is required to simultaneously possess a high open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ), a high fill factor (FF), and a suitable short-circuit current density (  $  J_{SC}  $  ) to match with the bottom subcell.  $  ^{[19-23]}  $

Many strategies have already been devoted to reducing the defect densities of perovskite films, such as incorporating a Lewis base,  $  ^{[34]}  $  loading alkali metal ions,  $  ^{[35]}  $  and constructing a 2/3D heterojunction structure.  $  ^{[36]}  $  Additionally, numerous organic molecules with carboxyl,  $  ^{[37]}  $  amino,  $  ^{[38]}  $  and sulfhydryl  $  ^{[31]}  $  functional groups have been reported to passivate the traps via interaction with  $  Pb^{2+}  $  in perovskite films. Recently, Lin et al. used a  $  \pi  $ -conjugated Lewis base as an interlayer between the perovskite and cathode, which accelerated charge carrier extraction and passivated traps.  $  ^{[34]}  $  Wu et al. introduced an organic donor-  $  \pi  $ -acceptor (D-  $  \pi  $ -A) molecule into perovskite films, which resulted in good binding with undercoordinated  $  Pb^{2+}  $  cations and improved stability of PSCs.  $  ^{[39]}  $  Wang et al. demonstrated an indacenodithieno[3,2-b] thiophene (IDTT) molecule can not only effectively passivate the traps, but also enhance the charge carrier extraction/transport at the device interface.  $  ^{[40]}  $  The interaction between an additive and the  $  FA^{+}  $ cation has been seldom reported for efficiency improvement.  $  ^{[41]}  $  The addition of the extended  $  \pi  $  aromatic molecule into perovskite materials almost always focuses on lead halides; however, the interaction mechanism between the aromatic molecule and  $  FA^{+}  $ has not been reported. Moreover, both the efficiency and stability improvement mechanisms behind the  $  \pi  $  aromatic molecule for PSCs still deserves to be further studied.

In this work, we found that 2,3,5,6 tetrafluoro-7,7,8,8-tetracyanoquinodimethane (F4-TCNQ) with the extended  $ \pi $  aromatic system has intermolecular interactions with perovskites. The mechanism behind the extended  $ \pi $  aromatic network for PSCs as both an effective passivator and good stabilizer has been well understood. The  $ \pi $  aromatic network of the F4-TCNQ molecule has strong molecular interaction with the  $ \mathrm{FA^{+}} $  cation, which significantly increases the binding energy of the  $ \mathrm{FA^{+}} $  cation and thus enhances the stability of the  $ \mathrm{FA_{0.75}Cs_{0.25}Pb(I_{0.8}Br_{0.2})_3} $  WBG perovskite absorbers and devices. The time-dependent PL measurements manifested that the F4-TCNQ molecule enables us to effectively reduce the trap states at the band edges of perovskite absorbers and the nonradiative recombination in devices. Furthermore, the admittance spectroscopy results proved that the perovskite films doped with  $ \pi $  aromatic molecules present shallower trap levels and lower trap densities than the control sample. As a result, we achieved a high PCE of  $ 20\% $  in an inverted  $ 1.67\mathrm{eV} $  bandgap PSC. Additionally, an unencapsulated F4-TCNQ-incorporated WBG PSC retained  $ 85\% $  of its initial PCE under  $ 65\pm 5\% $  relative humidity after  $ 84\mathrm{h} $ . The F4-TCNQ-incorporated WBG PSCs maintained  $ 88\% $  of their peak PCE and kept their initial high  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  under continuous illumination in nitrogen atmosphere after  $ 840\mathrm{h} $ , whereas the control devices without F4-TCNQ showed serious degradation under the same conditions.

# 2. Results and Discussion

During the preparation of the F4-TCNQ-incorporated WBG perovskite precursor solution, we observed that the color of the F4-TCNQ molecule in the perovskite precursor would fade with aging, as shown in Figure S1a, Supporting Information. The aged F4-TCNQ precursor always keeping a black color

indicates that this fading process is irreversible. To exclude the internal influence of the F4-TCNQ solution, we measured the absorption spectra of both fresh and aged (about 3 weeks) F4-TCNQ in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF)/dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) mixed solutions, shown in Figure S2a, Supporting Information. The stable characteristics of the absorption spectra (peaked at 473 nm) indicate that the fading of the perovskite solution with the F4-TCNQ incorporation did not arise from the intrinsic character of the F4-TCNQ molecule. The result shows that the precursor solution can remain stable for several months without significant change (caption in Figure S2a, Supporting Information). To investigate the potential interactions of the F4-TCNQ molecule with the perovskite raw materials in the DMF/DMSO solution, we added F4-TCNQ to different raw precursor solutions, i.e., formamidinium iodide (FAI), CsI, PbI₂, and PbBr₂. The results are shown in Figure 1a and Figure S1b–d, Supporting Information. It is apparent that only FAI and the perovskite precursor solutions presented the fading phenomena. The absorption spectra of different precursor solutions also confirm the fading phenomenon, as shown in Figure S2, Supporting Information. After aging for several days, the absorption spectrum of the faded FAI solution with added F4-TCNQ blueshifted from 473 to 364 nm (Figure 1b). From these results, we conclude that only the FA⁺ cation can interact with the F4-TCNQ molecule in the precursor solution. Considering the fading process, we suppose that the molecular interaction between FA⁺ and F4-TCNQ may have formed. To investigate the components in the faded precursor, a liquid chromatography high-resolution mass spectroscopy (LCMS) study was conducted to analyze the final product of the FAI and F4-TCNQ mixed solution. However, we found that the F4-TCNQ-incorporated FAI solution did not form any new chemicals after aging (Figure S3, Supporting Information), indicating that the interaction of FA·F4-TCNQ should be formed after the fading process. Therefore, we conclude that the interaction between the FA⁺ cation and the π aromatic molecule may influence the vibration of the molecular bond of F4-TCNQ, which changes the color and absorption spectrum of the precursor solution.[42]

It is well known that the   $  \pi  $   aromatic system has a stronger electron-withdrawing capacity compared with fluorine. To further study the interaction mechanism between   $  FA^{+}  $  and F4-TCNQ, we conducted nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) measurements of   $  {}^{13}C  $   spectra for the samples, as shown in Figure 1c. The characteristic spectrum of the pristine   $  FA^{+}  $  cation located at around 157.05 ppm, corresponding to a   $  {}^{13}C  $   signal for   $  FA^{+}  $  under an external magnetic field. However, the spectrum of the F4-TCNQ-processed   $  FA^{+}  $  solution had a 0.02 ppm low-field shift compared with the control sample. The results indicate that there is a strong electric field between the F4-TCNQ and   $  FA^{+}  $  cation and a reduced electronic cloud density around   $  {}^{13}C  $ . The reduced density of the electronic cloud results in attenuation of the shielding effect, which induces a chemical shift increase.  $  ^{[43]}  $   By contrast, two peaks of   $  {}^{1}H  $   spectra (around 7.85 and 8.8 ppm) shift to a high-field region (Figure 1d), which manifests that the   $  \pi  $   aromatic system of F4-TCNQ also has hydrogen-bond interaction with   $  FA^{+}  $ . These results provide a new insight to understand the interaction between the F4-TCNQ molecule and the   $  FA^{+}  $  cation.

To explore the light-induced halide phase segregation in the WBG perovskite films, we conducted time-dependent PL measurements to analyze the behavior of the phase segregation under light illumination.  $  ^{[44]}  $   Under more than 40 min of continuous laser irradiation, the WBG perovskite films (without or with F4-TCNQ incorporation) exhibited very different characteristics of the PL spectra, as shown in Figure 2c,d. The F4-TCNQ passivation effectively suppressed the PL peak redshift compared to the control WBG perovskite film, suggesting that the F4-TCNQ molecular passivation minimizes the phase segregation caused by the ion migration. Furthermore, the PL peak location of the control WBG perovskite significantly shifted to long wavelength, as shown in Figure 2f. However, the F4-TCNQ-passivated WBG perovskite film did not exhibit an obvious shift under light illumination, which further confirms that the F4-TCNQ passivation can reduce the halide ion migration and hence enhance the perovskite stability.

The location of trap states and the density of trap states in the WBG perovskite films were characterized by temperature-dependent admittance spectroscopy (AS). AS has been widely used to analyze both the location of trap states and the density of trap states in perovskites.  $  ^{[45]}  $   For a p-type semiconductor of perovskite, the relationship of the trap activation energy   $  (E_{\mathrm{a}})  $  , the depth of the trap-state energy level   $  (E_{\mathrm{T}})  $  , and valance energy level   $  (E_{\mathrm{V}})  $   can be described as a formula of   $  E_{a}=E_{T}-E_{V}  $  . Temperature-dependent AS measurements without (Figure 3a) and with (Figure 3b) F4TCNQ incorporation were conducted at different temperatures (230–310 K) in the dark from 5 to 10 000 Hz. The corresponding derivatives of the AS are shown in Figure S7, Supporting Information. The relationship can be derived by the following equation  $  ^{[45]}  $

$$
E _ {\varepsilon} = k _ {\mathrm{B}} T \ln \left(\frac {\beta T ^ {2}}{\omega}\right)
$$

$$
N _ {\mathrm{T}} (E _ {\omega}) = - \frac {V _ {\mathrm{bi}}}{q W k _ {\mathrm{B}} T} \frac {\omega d C}{d \omega}
$$

(2)

where   $  \omega_{0}  $   is the characteristic transition angular frequency,   $  \beta  $   is the temperature parameter, and   $  E_{a}  $   is the activation energy of trap state. The built-in potential (  $  V_{bi}  $  ) and the depletion width (W) in the perovskite layer can be estimated by the depletion approximation equation (Mott–Schottky)

We measured the Mott–Schottky curves of WBG PSCs at room temperature under a frequency of 1000 Hz with bias voltages ranging from 0 to 1.4 V, as shown in Figure 3c. The results suggest that the   $  V_{bi}  $   of the control and F4-TCNQ-incorporated perovskites are 0.84 and 0.96 V, respectively, and the corresponding W values are 105 and 116 nm, respectively. Clearly, the improvement on   $  V_{bi}  $   stemmed from the decrease in nonradiation recombination after F4-TCNQ molecule passivation, which resulted in internal and interfacial voltage loss.

The measurements of the trap state density and the trap state distributions were also conducted to analyze the effects of F4-TCNQ on the WBG perovskite absorber. Figure 3d shows that the F4-TCNQ molecule incorporation not only reduced the trap state level from 0.25 to 0.20 eV but also efficiently decreased the trap density from   $  7.0 \times 10^{14}  $   to   $  4.5 \times 10^{14}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  . Correspondingly, the F4-TCNQ-treated sample had reduced nonradiative recombination. The Arrhenius-temperature-dependent plots calculated from the derivative of the AS are shown in Figure 3e. The calculated activation energy   $  E_{a}  $   of the perovskite absorbers for the control and F4-TCNQ-passivated devices are 0.19 and 0.27 eV, respectively. The increased   $  E_{a}  $   indicates that the extended   $  \pi  $   aromatic system of F4-TCNQ can efficiently suppress the trap migration in WBG perovskite film. # 3. Conclusion

In this work, we used a strong electron-withdrawing molecule with the extended   $  \pi  $   aromatic system to efficiently passivate the traps in WBG perovskite absorbers. It is found that the F4-TCNQ additive can interact with the FA cation in the perovskite and stabilize the perovskite absorber. Furthermore, the addition of the F4-TCNQ molecule effectively reduced the trap states at the band edges of perovskite absorbers and the nonradiative recombination in devices. The trap-level position moved from 0.25 to 0.20 eV when using the conduction band minimum as a reference and the trap density decreased from   $  7.0 \times 10^{14}  $   to   $  4.5 \times 10^{14}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $   after the F4-TCNQ treatment. Therefore, the 1.67 eV bandgap PSCs incorporating F4-TCNQ achieved a high PCE of 20.0% in a p-i-n planar structure. The incorporation of F4-TCNQ enhanced the structure stability and increased the trap activation energy. Therefore, the ion migration was greatly suppressed, and the humidity and photostability of the devices were significantly enhanced. Furthermore, we have revealed the interaction mechanism of viable additives with perovskite absorbers and provided a simple and effective method to suppress the traps, paving the way for further efficiency and stability improvements.

# 4. Experimental Section

Precursor Solution Preparation: Lead iodide (PbI $ _2 $ , 99.99%) was purchased from TCI. Lead bromide (PbBr $ _2 $ , 99.999%) and cesium iodide (Csl, 99.999%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. FAI (99.99%) was purchased from GreatCell Solar Materials. F4-TCNQ was purchased from Lumtec. Poly-TPD and C60 were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. PFN-P2 was purchased from 1-Materials. DMF (99.99%), DMSO (99.99%), chlorobenzene (CB, 99.9%), and methanol (99.9%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Diethyl ether (98%) was purchased from Yonghua Chemical Co., Ltd.

The 1.3 M   $  FA_{0.75}Cs_{0.25}Pb(I_{0.8}Br_{0.2})_{3}  $   perovskite precursor solution was prepared by mixing   $  PbI_{2}  $  ,   $  PbBr_{2}  $  , FAI, and CsI in 1 mL mixed solvent of 0.75 mL DMF and 0.25 mL DMSO. The F4-TCNQ power was dissolved in DMF/DMSO mixed solution (volume ratio = 3:1) and aged at 60 °C for 3 h. The F4-TCNQ-treated perovskite precursor solutions were prepared by dissolving previous perovskite precursor solutions in the DMF/DMSO mixed-solvent system with F4-TCNQ in the desired volume ratios. Before deposition, all the perovskite solutions were kept stirring at 60 °C for at least 4 h. For the hole transport layer, poly-TPD was dissolved in CB in a concentration of 2 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   without any dopant. For an ultrathin modified layer, PFN-P2 was dissolved in methanol at a concentration of 0.4 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  .

Perovskite Film and Cell Fabrication: The preetched   $  1.5 \times 1.5 \, cm^2  $   indium-doped tin oxide (ITO,   $  7\Omega sq^{-1}  $  ) coated glass substrates (Huanan technology corporation) were cleaned by ultrasonication in deionized water, acetone, and ethanol (each process for 15 mins). After the ultrasonic process, the ITO glasses were blown dry using a nitrogen gun and then treated with UV–ozone for 15 min at least. After all the rinsing procedures, the substrates were transferred to a nitrogen-filled glove box with the following step. The poly-TPD hole transport layer was spun cast at 4000 rpm for 30 s, followed by thermal annealing at   $  100^{\circ}C  $   for 10 mins. After cooling to room temperature, the ultrathin modified layer of PFN-P2 was spun cast at 5000 rpm for 20 s without further thermal annealing. The perovskite layers were spun cast using an antisolvent method with processing parameters: starting at 500 rpm for 2 s and then 4000 rpm 60 s (with maximum acceleration). Before the end of the spin coating, 500   $  \mu L  $   of diethyl ether was used as an antisolvent onto the spinning substrates at 35 s after the start of the second process. The as-prepared films were then carried out at   $  60^{\circ}C  $   2 min and   $  100^{\circ}C  $

10 min for the formation of the perovskite absorbers under the thermal annealing process. All of the perovskite films were prepared under the same condition. After cooling down, 20 nm of   $  C_{60}  $   was deposited by thermal evaporation at a vacuum of   $  1.5 \times 10^{-4}  $   Pa.

For the ALD process, a buffer layer of 20 nm thick SnO  $  _{x}  $  was deposited at 90 °C, using tetrakis(dimethylamino) tin (TDMA-Sn, Strem chemicals, 99.99%, part number 50-1815) and ultrapure water as the precursors. The 120 cycle ALD of SnO  $  _{x}  $  consisted of the processing sequence: 40 ms TDMA-Sn pulse, 15 s purge, 15 ms H  $  _{2}  $  O pulse, and 15 s purge under 20 sccm N  $  _{2}  $  as the carrier gas.

Then,  $ 80~\mathrm{nm} $  of Cu metal contact was evaporated on the surface of the devices at a vacuum of  $ 2.0\times 10^{-4}\mathrm{Pa} $ .

Material Characterizations: The crystal structure of the perovskite polycrystalline films was analyzed by an X-ray diffractometer (D8 Advance, Bruker). The absorption spectra of perovskite films and precursor solutions were performed using a spectrophotometer (UV–vis, mini UV-1208 model, Shimadzu). The incident photoelectric conversion efficiency (IPCE) measurements were recorded with a QE/IPCE system (Enli Technology Co. Ltd). The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and TRPL spectra were measured using a DeltaFlex fluorescence spectrometer (HORIBA) with a semiconductor intensity to 1 sun equivalent intensity and a wavelength of 481 nm. J–V characterizations and steady-state power outputs (SPO) of the PSCs were performed under air mass 1.5 global (AM 1.5 G) conditions (Enli Technology Co. Ltd) in a nitrogen atmosphere. The J–V measurements were performed with a scan rate of 20 mV s  $  ^{-1}  $  ranging from 1.2 to -0.1 V and then reverse again from 0.1 to 1.2 V. The top and cross-sectional images of the perovskite films and devices were observed with a field emission scanning electron microscope (Zeiss SIGMA). XPS spectra were measured using an XPS/UPS system (Thermo Scientific, Escalab 250Xi).  $  {}^{1}  $  H and  $  {}^{13}  $  C nNMR spectra were recorded on Bruker DRX-400/600 spectrometers. Chemical shifts were recorded in parts per million (  $  \delta  $  ), and the residual solvent peak was used as an internal reference: proton (DMSO  $  \delta  $  2.50), carbon (DMSO  $  \delta  $  39.5).

Electrical Performance Characterization: The SCLC of the single carrier devices was measured using a CHI 660E electrochemical workstation (Shanghai Chenhua Instruments, Inc). The structure of the single-carrier device was ITO/poly-TPD/PFN-P2/perovskite/Spiro/Au and all of the samples were stored in dry air for 12 h before measurements. The Mott–Schottky curve with capacitance–voltage measurements was carried out by a CHI760E electrochemical workstation (Shanghai Chenhua Instruments, Inc) at 1000 Hz with the bias voltages ranging from 1.4 to 0 V and an AC voltage of 0.02 V was used to test the corresponding capacitance at shifty bias voltage. Admittance spectroscopy measurements were performed with a CHI760E electrochemical workstation (Shanghai Chenhua Instruments, Inc.) at different temperatures ranging from 230 to 310 K in the dark at a low vacuum (  $  \approx  $  10 Pa) with a small AC disturbed voltage of 20 mV. The frequency scope from 1 to 100 kHz and a DC bias voltage of 0 V was kept during the measurements.

Perovskite Solar Cell Stability Measurements: All of the unencapsulated perovskite solar cells were aged using an AHD500W xenon lamp light-soaking chamber in a nitrogen-filled box with 80–90 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  irradiance and conducted under open-circuit conditions. The samples were taken out from the box to test J-V curves under air mass 1.5 global (AM 1.5G) conditions (Enli Technology Co. Ltd) in a nitrogen atmosphere. The aging box was kept at room temperature with an air conditioner.
