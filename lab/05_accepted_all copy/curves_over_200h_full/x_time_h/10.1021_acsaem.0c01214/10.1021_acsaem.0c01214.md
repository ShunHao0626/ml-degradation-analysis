# Abstract:

State-of-the-art perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have achieved a record-breaking power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 25.2% with remarkable operational stability, surpassing the PCE of silicon-based solar cells. In the pristine state, bulk perovskite film often exhibits intrinsic interstitial/vacancies defects, and non-coordinated ions especially at the film surface and grain boundaries, leading to undesirable recombination losses and crippled PCE in the PSCs. To address this issue, we rationally designed an extrinsic double-halide passivation (DHP) treatment, composed of methylammonium bromide and chloride ions in different ratios, to tailor the morphology, intrinsic defects and optical band gap of the bulk perovskite films. Owing to the better-aligned energy levels, suppressed grain boundaries recombination losses, and cascade charge transfer, the champion DHP device delivered a PCE of ~20.3%, which is a tremendous improvement from that of the reference device (PCE ~18.0%). More importantly, the air-, photo- and thermal-stabilities of the PSC devices are all enhanced after passivation treatment. The DHP treatment proposed herein represents a facile, yet effective approach to elevate the efficiency and stability of the PSCs.

Keywords: bulk passivation, halides, defects, stability, thermal, band gap.

# 1. Introduction

State-of-the-art perovskite solar cells (PSCs), with a record-breaking efficiency of 25.2%, have surpassed the efficiency threshold of silicon-based photovoltaic technology.  $  ^{1}  $   Such excellent device performance of PSCs can be attributed to the good light-absorbing coefficient (  $  \sim10^{5}  $   cm  $  ^{-1}  $  ) with low Urbach energy, long diffusion lengths (>1   $  \mu  $  m), high carrier mobility, tunable band gap and low exciton binding energy of the organic-inorganic perovskite absorber film.  $  ^{2-4}  $   Similar to other thin film photovoltaic materials such as gallium-arsenide (GaAs), cadmium-telluride (CdTe) copper-indium-gallium-selenide (CIGS) and copper zinc tin sulfide (CZTS), the bulk perovskite films (regardless of the preparation technique) are susceptible to structural disorders, impurities, intrinsic interstitials/vacancies defects and accumulation of the non-coordinated ions especially at the grain boundaries.  $  ^{5}  $   Grain boundaries are the most abundant surface/bulk defects in the perovskite film and they accommodate impurity phase such as PbI  $  _{2}  $   and crystallographic defects such as cation vacancies and antisite substitutional defects which form deep-level trap states within the perovskite crystal.  $  ^{6}  $   These trap states could cause non-radiative recombination of photogenerated charge carriers and detrimentally affect the power output, hysteresis and stability of the PSCs. Therefore, grain boundary defect is the culprit behind the hindered interfacial charge-transfer process in PSC. To overcome these shortcomings, extrinsic defect passivation strategies are commonly applied to the perovskite film to unlock its full potential as an absorber material. Defect passivation approach could help to tailor the surface morphology including grain size and grain boundaries, and effective quenches the formation of bulk and surface defects in the perovskite films.  $  ^{7}  $   On device level, the defect passivation strategy can also be adopted to enhance the intermolecular interaction at the interface between the perovskite absorber and electron transport layer (ETL) or hole transport layer (HTL), thereby reducing the probability of charge

There are numerous literatures detailing the compositional engineering and defect passivation of perovskite films via the incorporation of various organic halide compounds, including formaminidium bromide,  $  ^{12}  $   methylammonium chloride,  $  ^{13}  $   methylammonium iodide  $  ^{14}  $  , trimethylsulfonium iodide,  $  ^{15}  $   guanidinium chloride,  $  ^{16}  $   choline chloride  $  ^{17}  $   and etc.. Additionally, the use of insulating polymer capping layers such as poly(methyl methacrylate)  $  ^{18}  $   and polystyrene  $  ^{19}  $   atop the perovskite film has also yielded considerable improvements. Besides these, the attempt of using inorganic compounds particularly an ultrathin compact aluminum oxide layer  $  ^{20}  $   has also produced positive outcome. In all of the passivation studies, the passivated perovskite films demonstrated significantly improved surface morphology with quenched crystallographic defects, improved device performance and operational stability. In terms of chemical composition, iodide (I) ions are the primary constituents in the formamidinium lead triiodide (FAPbI $ _3 $ ) based perovskite structure. Apart from the I ions, other halide elements such as bromide (Br) and chloride (Cl) ions are also capable of forming perovskites of various compositions and structures. In previous studies, it has been demonstrated that a partial substitution of I ions by other halide elements bromide (Br) and chloride (Cl) ions in the perovskite matrix is capable of boosting the performance and stability of the PSCs. $ ^{25-28} $  For instance, Br-passivation plays an essential role in healing the pre-existing halide vacancies and reducing the remnant PbI $ _2 $  in the perovskite films. This often results in substantial enhancement in the V $ _{\text{oc}} $  and stability of the PSCs. $ ^{25} $  Contrarily, Cl-passivation favors the formation of large grain size and con contribute predominantly to the short-circuit current density (J $ _{\text{sc}} $ ) of the PSCs. $ ^{13} $  Most of the preceding passivation studies demonstrate the use of single-halide passivation agent (I/Br/Cl) and quite often, the performances of the passivated PSCs are only marginally enhanced with little guarantee in reproducibility. There is a scarce in the literature on the use of double-halide passivation agent. In this work, we propose the extrinsic deposition of mixed methylammonium halide compounds, namely methylammonium bromide (MABr) and methylammonium chloride (MACl) to realize synergistic improvements on the performance of the PSCs. The influence of the single halide passivation (SHP; MABr only) and double-halide passivation (DHP; MABr:MACl) treatment on the morphology, optical properties and device performance of perovskite films was investigated in-depth. # 2. Experimental Section

# 2.1 Materials

Formamidinium iodide (FAI, >98%) and methylammonium bromide (MABr, >98%) were purchased from GreatCell Solar. Lead iodide (PbI  $  _{2}  $  , 99.99%), lead bromide (PbBr, 99.99%) and cesium iodide (CsI beads, 99.99%) were purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (TCI, Japan.). Methylammonium chloride (MACl, >98%) was purchased from Merck. Spiro-OMeTAD (>99%) was purchased from Lumtech. Tin (II) chloride dehydrate (SnCl  $  _{2}  $  ·2H  $  _{2}  $  O, 98.0%), bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonamide lithium salt (Li-TSFI, 99.95%), 4-tert-butylpyridine (4-tBP, 96%), acetonitrile (99.8%), dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.8%) and ethanol (99.5%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. All materials were used without further purification.

# 2.2 PSC device fabrication

In this study, n-i-p structured PSCs were fabricated based on a device configuration of glass/ITO/SnO  $  _{2}  $  /perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD/Au. Pre-patterned ITO coated glass substrates (2.5 cm × 2.5 cm) were successively sonicated in acetone, ethanol, and IPA. The glass/ITO substrates were UV-ozone treated for 20 min before further processing. An SnO  $  _{2}  $  ETL precursor solution was prepared by dissolving SnCl  $  _{2}  $  ·2H  $  _{2}  $  O in ethanol (23 mg/mL). The SnO  $  _{2}  $  precursor solution was spin-coated onto the ITO substrate at 3000 rpm for 30 s to yield a ~25 nm thick SnO  $  _{2}  $  film, followed by annealing treatment at 190 °C for 1 h in air. PbI  $  _{2}  $  -rich triple-cation perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 530 mg of PbI  $  _{2}  $ , 189 mg of FAI, 75 mg of PbBr  $  _{2}  $ , 18.5 mg of MABr and 4.5 mg of MACl in 1 mL of DMF:DMSO (4:1 v/v) solvent. The perovskite layer was deposited onto the SnO  $  _{2}  $  coated substrate via a two-step spin-coating method, i.e., 1500 rpm for 10 s followed by 5500 rpm for 30 s in an N  $  _{2}  $  glovebox. # 3. Results and Discussion

Figure 1(a) schematically illustrates the core-idea of tailoring the interfacial morphology and bulk composition of the SnO $ _2 $ -triple cation perovskite film via an extrinsic halide passivation (SHP/DHP) treatment, and the subsequent use of the composition-engineered films for PSC application. To study the influence of the halide passivation on the phase transition and structural crystallinity of the bulk perovskite film, XRD characterization was conducted. The XRD patterns of all the samples in Figure 1(b) exhibit a dominant peak at a 2θ position of 14.2°, confirming the formation of α(110) black-phase perovskite. Noticeably, the spectrum of the reference film displays a clear peak at a 2θ position of 12.6°, which signifies the high PbI $ _2 $  content in the bulk perovskite film. The presence of the remnant PbI $ _2 $  in the perovskite film has been anticipated due to the use of PbI $ _2 $ -rich recipe in the making of perovskite precursor solution. In most of the circumstances, the presence of remnant PbI $ _2 $  in the perovskite film is undesirable. Due to the large band gap of PbI $ _2 $  (~2.3 eV), it can easily generate potential barriers at the extraction layers, subsequently induce carrier accumulation at the interface and hinder the charge transport. Additionally, the remnant PbI $ _2 $  on the perovskite surface disintegrates easily upon exposure to moisture and is responsible for an accelerated degradation of the perovskite layer. $ ^{30} $  Therefore, the amount of remnant PbI $ _2 $  content in the bulk perovskite film must be carefully tailored to prevent detrimental effect on the PSC device performance and stability. After halide passivation, the PbI $ _2 $  peak in the spectra of the SHP/DHP samples quenched, whereas the α(110) peak becomes more pronounced. This finding demonstrates that the SHP/DHP treatment has successfully promoted the transition of the remnant PbI $ _2 $  in the bulk film into the photoactive perovskite phase, thereby enhancing the crystallinity of the perovskite film. Next, we investigated the optical properties of the perovskite films before and after halide passivation. The optical absorption of the perovskite films increased marginally after halide passivation due to the larger grain size and increased thickness of the perovskite film. In relation to this, we conducted UPS measurements on the perovskite films to investigate the effect of the SHP/DHP treatment. The UPS spectra in Figure 4(b) reveal a corresponding shift in the secondary cut-off and valence band of the SHP- and DHP-treated films. # 4. Conclusion

In conclusion, our work has systematically demonstrated the noble influence of double halide passivation on various aspects of triple-cation perovskite films, including on the morphology, energy band alignment, surface chemical composition, and optical properties. The SHP/DHP treatment substantially improved the quality of the perovskite film by inducing larger grain growth, suppressing the surface defects, and facilitating charge transport. Owing to the synergistic use of bromide and chloride ions during the passivation treatment, the DHP PSCs exhibited considerably higher   $  V_{oc}  $   and   $  J_{sc}  $   values when compared to the reference PSCs. As a result, the PCE of the champion DHP-treated cell (  $  \sim20.32\%  $  ) was tremendously higher than the reference cell (  $  \sim18.00\%  $  ). The improved device performances of the SHP- and DHP-treated PSCs can be primarily attributed to the enhanced crystallinity, wider band gap and lesser surface defects of the perovskite absorber film after the passivation treatment. The optimum MABr to MACl ratio was found to be 8:2 to realize a synergistic improvement and balanced trade-off on the device performance and stability of the PSCs. The mixed halide passivation approach introduced herein is facile, easy to execute, promises high reproducibility and extremely low-cost, which are important factors to consider for the future commercialization of PSCs.
