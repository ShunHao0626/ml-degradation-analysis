# In Situ Growth of 2D Perovskite Capping Layer for Stable and Efficient Perovskite Solar Cells

Peng Chen, Yang Bai,* Songcan Wang, Miaoqiang Lyu, Jung-Ho Yun, and Lianzhou Wang*

2D halide perovskites have recently been recognized as a promising avenue in perovskite solar cells (PSCs) in terms of encouraging stability and defect passivation effect. However, the efficiency (less than 15%) of ultrastable 2D Ruddlesden–Popper PSCs still lag far behind their traditional 3D perovskite counterparts. Here, a rationally designed 2D-3D perovskite stacking-layered architecture by in situ growing 2D PEA  $  _{2}  $  PbI  $  _{4}  $  capping layers on top of 3D perovskite film, which drastically improves the stability of PSCs without compromising their high performance, is reported. Such a 2D perovskite capping layer induces larger Fermi-level splitting in the 2D-3D perovskite film under light illumination, resulting in an enhanced open-circuit voltage (V  $  _{oc}  $  ) and thus a higher efficiency of 18.51% in the 2D-3D PSCs. Time-resolved photoluminescence decay measurements indicate the facilitated hole extraction in the 2D-3D stacking-layered perovskite films, which is ascribed to the optimized energy band alignment and reduced nonradiative recombination at the subgap states. Benefiting from the high moisture resistivity as well as suppressed ion migration of the 2D perovskite, the 2D-3D PSCs show significantly improved long-term stability, retaining nearly 90% of the initial power conversion efficiency after 1000 h exposure in the ambient conditions with a high relative humidity level of 60 ± 10%.

# 1. Introduction

The emerging organic-inorganic metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted tremendous attention in the photovoltaic community due to their rapidly improved power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) from 3.8 to  $ 22.7\% $  in only several years.[1-3] One origin of the high PCE in PSCs is the relatively high ratio  $ (\approx 0.69) $  of the attained open-circuit voltage  $ (V_{\mathrm{oc}}) $  to bandgap  $ (E_{\mathrm{g}}) $ .[4,5] Given that this  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} / E_{\mathrm{g}} $  ratio is still lower than that (0.80) of silicon and GaAs thin film solar cells, there is still considerable space to further boost the PCE of PSCs by maximizing the  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  toward its thermodynamic limit, which is calculated to be  $ 1.32\mathrm{V} $  for perovskites with a band gap of  $ \approx 1.6\mathrm{eV} $ .[5-7] Theoretically, the  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  upper limit in PSCs is defined by the splitting of electron and hole quasi-Fermi levels  $ (E_{\mathrm{fn}} - E_{\mathrm{fp}}) $ , which

The pristine 3D perovskite film shown in Figure 1d is compact and pinhole-free with an average grain size of   $  \approx300  $   nm, while the pure 2D perovskite film is ultrasmooth with a larger grain size of   $  \approx1  $     $  \mu  $  m (Figure S3, Supporting Information). After PEAI treatment, the exposed surfaces of 3D perovskite crystals are fully covered by the in situ grown 2D perovskite materials as shown in the top-view SEM image of 2D-3D perovskite films, serving as a self-encapsulating layer to block the moisture invasion (Figure 1e).

To further verify the formation of the 2D perovskite layer, the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were carried out on 3D, 2D, and 2D-3D perovskite films. In the C1s XPS spectrum of 3D perovskite films, the presence of  $ \mathrm{FA^{+} / MA^{+}} $  cations can be confirmed by the detected C-C (284.8 eV) and  $ \mathrm{C = N} $  (286.5 eV) bonds (Figure 2a).[39] For the emerged  $ \mathrm{C = O} $  bond (288.1 eV), we speculate these oxidized carbon species may come from the decomposition of the 3D perovskite film induced by the absorption of water or oxygen when exposing to the ambient condition.[40-43] The featured bonds of  $ \mathrm{C - N} $  (400.3 eV) and  $ \mathrm{C = N} $  (402.0 eV) from the  $ \mathrm{FA^{+}} $  cation can be observed in the N1s spectrum of 3D perovskite films (Figure 2b). In the C1s XPS spectrum of the pristine 2D perovskite films, the emerged peak at a binding energy of  $ \approx 292.0\mathrm{eV} $  can be ascribed to the  $ \pi-\pi $  bonding from the phenyl functional group in the  $ \mathrm{PEA^{+}} $  cation (Figure 2c). Only one peak for  $ \mathrm{C = N} $  (402.0 eV) can be found in the N1s spectrum of the 2D perovskite films, which

is consistent with the functional group in the PEA  $  ^{+}  $  cations (Figure 2d). In the C1s and N1s XPS spectra of the 2D-3D perovskite films, the typical peaks for both 3D perovskite and 2D perovskite films can be observed, verifying the formation of 2D-3D hybrid perovskite films (Figure 2e,f). It is worth noting that the C=O bond shown in pristine 3D perovskite associated with oxygen/moisture-induced degradation is absent in the C1s XPS spectrum of the 2D-3D perovskite films, revealing the synergistic effect of steric hindrance from the in situ grown thin 2D perovskite layers and the partially passivated ionic vacancies in the 3D perovskite films.  $  ^{[42]}  $

To evaluate the effect of 2D perovskite layers in photovoltaic performance, we fabricated solar cells based on 3D perovskite films with and without PEAI treatment. The current density–voltage (J-V) curves of the fabricated 3D and 2D-3D PSCs are shown in Figure 3a. The best cell employing typical 3D perovskite material exhibits a short-circuit current density (  $  J_{sc}  $  ) of 22.83 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , a   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.05 V, and a fill factor (FF) of 0.71, yielding a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 17.02%. After a post-treatment step using PEAI solution with a concentration of 1 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  , the champion device employing 2D-3D perovskite films shows a   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.11 V, a FF of 0.73, and a   $  J_{sc}  $   of 22.89 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , leading to an improved PCE of 18.51%. Further increasing the concentration of PEAI solution to 4 and 8 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   results in thicker 2D perovskite capping layers, which leads to reduced   $  J_{sc}  $   and FF (Figure S6a, Supporting Information). The deteriorated performance can be explained by the poor charge transport property in thicker 2D perovskite films originated from the severe quantum confinement effect, while the preserved high   $  V_{oc}  $   is due to the low nonradiative losses. Only when the thickness of 2D perovskite capping layers is controlled within their effective charge-carrier diffusion length, a decent charge-carrier mobility can be attained in the 2D-3D perovskite stacking layer.  $  ^{[14]}  $   All the photovoltaic parameters of the fabricated PSCs using different concentrations of PEAI solutions are summarized in Table 1. The PCE achieved in the 2D-3D PSCs with the optimized capping layer thickness is among the best in the reported PSCs incorporating 2D perovskite materials (Table S1, Supporting Information). As shown in Figure S4a (Supporting Information), the 3D PSCs displayed obvious photocurrent hysteresis with PCEs of 17.02 and 13.08% under a reverse and forward scan, respectively. The PCE enhancement mainly comes from the improved   $  V_{oc}  $   and we hypothesize that the higher   $  V_{oc}  $   results from larger Fermi-level splitting and suppressed nonradiative recombination by the 2D perovskite capping layer. To reveal the mechanism for the   $  V_{oc}  $   enhancement in the fabricated 2D-3D PSCs, the Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) technique was employed to detect the surface potential difference of perovskite films, which is associated with the work function with respect to the vacuum energy level. As shown in Figure 4a,c, the atomic force microscope (AFM) image of the as-prepared 2D-3D perovskite film indicates a smoother surface morphology with a root-mean-square (RMS) roughness of 17.8 nm, which is lower than that of the pristine 3D perovskite film (RMS = 20.8 nm). The work functions of the perovskite films are determined by the contact potential difference (CPD) between a Pt-coated conductive cantilever probe and the samples. In the statistic CPD of KPFM results, the 2D-3D perovskite film shows a lower average CPD (-108 mV) than that (-41 mV) of 3D perovskite film, indicating a downward shift of Fermi level after PEAI post-treatment (Figure S7, Supporting Information). Moreover, the KPFM image of the 3D perovskite film exhibits notable dark grains related with heterogeneous surface potential distribution (Figure 4b), which is consistent with a larger variation in the measured CPD of the 3D perovskite film (Figure S7, Supporting Information). In sharp contrast, the KPFM result of the 2D-3D perovskite film presents uniformly distributed surface

potentials (Figure 4d), which may be due to the partial elimination of subgap defect states and thus suppressed nonradiative recombination.  $  ^{[52,53]}  $   Benefiting from the deeper Fermi level in the dark condition, the 2D-3D perovskite films tend to exhibit a larger gap between the splitting of electron quasi-Fermi level (  $  E_{fn}  $  ) and hole quasi-Fermi level (  $  E_{fp}  $  ) under light illumination, as illustrated in Figure 4e,f.  $  ^{[54]}  $   The downward-shifted   $  E_{fp}  $   of 2D-3D perovskite matches better with the HOMO level of HTM under illumination, leading to optimized energy band alignment and reduced nonradiative recombination loss.  $  ^{[7,9,10]}  $   The   $  V_{oc}  $   enhancement extracted from the J-V measurements is nearly equal to the downward shift of Fermi level calculated from KPFM results, which verifies our hypothesis on the origin of   $  V_{oc}  $   enhancement.

We also investigated the charge carrier dynamics of the pristine 2D perovskite film deposited on glass substrates and its thickness was determined to be  $ \approx $ 500 nm by a profilometer. As shown in the TRPL result of the pristine 2D perovskite film (Figure S8a, Supporting Information), the average lifetime was fitted to be 1.43 ns, which was much smaller than those of 3D and 2D-3D samples. This was because the charge-carrier diffusion length of PEA $ _{2} $ PbI $ _{4} $  ( $ \approx $ 60 nm) was much smaller than the film thickness (500 nm), leading to severe bulk recombination. $ ^{[14]} $  To investigate the influence of 2D perovskite layer on the charge transport dynamics at the interface of perovskite and HTM layers, we prepared another set of samples by coating HTM layer on the top of various perovskite films on glass substrates and the TRPL spectra were measured on these samples as shown in Figure 4h. When contacting with HTM layer, the HTM quenched the emission of perovskite films and all samples including the 2D perovskite sample (Figure S8b, Supporting Information) exhibited decreased PL lifetime, which confirms the injection of holes from the perovskite layer into the HTM layer. As shown in Table S2 (Supporting Information), the PL lifetime calculated for 3D perovskite/HTM sample was 117.0 ns, while much shorter lifetime of 26.3 ns was obtained for the 2D-3D perovskite/HTM sample, which indicates faster hole extraction. We attributed the facilitated hole extraction at 2D-3D perovskite/HTM interface to the reduced interfacial nonradiative recombination and optimized energy band alignment. The in situ grown thin 2D perovskite capping layer acted as a passivation layer that can effectively eliminate the trap states at the 3D perovskite surface and suppressed the nonradiative recombination. This has already been verified in the steady-state PL spectra as shown in Figure S2b (Supporting Information). Furthermore, the passivation of subgap defect states by the 2D perovskite capping layer

led to an optimized energy band alignment between the 2D-3D perovskite film and the HTM layer.  $  ^{[11,12,22,59]}  $   Thus, more efficient hole transfer from the 3D perovskite layer to HTM layer can be obtained in the 2D-3D PSCs.

In addition to the enhanced photovoltaic performance, the moisture stability of the fabricated PSCs is significantly improved by employing 2D-3D perovskite films. After exposure in the ambient environment with RH   $  60 \pm 10  $   for 2000 h, the pristine 3D perovskite films experienced a fast degradation to   $  \delta  $  -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   (nonperovskite yellow phase) and PbI  $  _{2}  $  , corresponding to the emerged peak at 11.2° and the intensified peak at 12.7° in the XRD pattern (Figure 5a), respectively.  $  ^{[60]}  $   In sharp contrast, no obvious change is observed in the XRD pattern of 2D-3D perovskite film, indicating the superior moisture stability. Thereafter, we have also evaluated the long-term stability of perovskite devices with and without 2D perovskite capping layer in the ambient conditions with RH   $  60 \pm 10  $   as shown in Figure 5b. During the long-term stability monitoring in terms of PCE, the majority of initial efficiency is lost rapidly in typical devices based on pristine 3D perovskite, while the PSCs employing 2D-3D perovskite films (PEAI-1 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) demonstrate much better stability and maintain almost 90% of the original PCE after 1000 h. During the long-term exposure in a humid environment, the   $  J_{sc}  $   and FF of the controlled devices experienced a rapid decline, while those of 2D-3D PSCs keep quite constant (Figure S6b–d, Supporting Information). These well-maintained photovoltaic parameters of the 2D-3D PSCs can be ascribed to the increased moisture resistance as well as suppressed ion migration from 2D perovskite capping layers with large and hydrophobic PEA  $  ^{+}  $   cations.  $  ^{[17,51]}  $   Note that the thicknesses of the 2D perovskite capping layers in this study are very small. By increasing the concentration of PEAI solution to 8 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  , the thickness of the 2D perovskite capping layer can be up to ≈30 nm, as shown in Figure S9 (Supporting Information). The resultant thicker 2D perovskite capping layers enabled the fabrication of even more stable PSCs (Figure 5b), retaining 93% (4 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) and 97% (8 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) of initial PCEs, respectively; though the PCEs were slightly lower (Figure S6a, Supporting Information). To the best of our knowledge, the surpassing 1000 h stability with a high PCE value over 16% for unsealed PSCs exposing to a highly humid environment (>RH 60) has not been witnessed in previously reported 2D-3D PSCs (Table S1, Supporting Information). In addition, the thermal stability of 2D-3D PSCs was investigated by heating the devices on a hotplate at different temperatures in nitrogen atmosphere (Figure S10, Supporting Information). Under continuous thermal treatment at 60 °C for 100 h, the 3D PSCs lost the majority of the initial performance, while the 2D-3D PSCs with 1 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  PEAI post-treatment can retain around 75% of the original PCE under the same conditions. Such significantly improved thermal stability in the 2D-3D PSCs may be due to the suppressed iodide ion diffusion by the 2D perovskite capping layer. Compared to the 3D perovskite material with a small binding energy of ≈2 meV, 2D perovskite PEA  $  _{2}  $  PbI  $  _{4}  $  has a relatively large binding energy of 230 meV, which needs much higher activation energy to induce ion migration upon heating.  $  ^{[61-63]}  $  As a thermal-stable layer, the 2D perovskite capping layer can effectively block the iodide ion diffusion from the 3D perovskite film to the HTM and electrode.  $  ^{[24,51]}  $  With thicker 2D perovskite capping layers (8 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  PEAI), the 2D-3D PSCs demonstrated even better thermal stability, maintaining nearly 90% of the original PCE after 100 h at 60 °C. When aging at an elevated temperature of 85 °C, the 2D-3D PSCs based on the thin and thick 2D perovskite capping layers still can retain around 60 and 70% of the original PCEs, respectively. This relatively deteriorated thermal stability at higher temperature can be ascribed to the low glass transition temperature (≤85 °C) of Spiro-OMeTAD with additives.  $  ^{[64]}  $  We anticipated that the incorporation of robust inorganic HTMs such as CuSCN would further improve the thermal stability of 2D-3D PSCs.

# 3. Conclusions

In summary, we report a facile strategy to simultaneously improve the stability and efficiency of 2D-3D PSCs by introducing a rationally designed 2D  $ \mathrm{PEA}_2\mathrm{PbI}_4 $  capping layer on top of the 3D perovskite film. Such capping layer induces larger Fermi-level splitting and reduces nonradiative recombination loss in 2D-3D PSCs, leading to a higher  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  and thus an enhanced PCE. The downward shift of quasi-Fermi level calculated in KPFM results is consistent with the  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  enhancement observed in the  $ J $ -V measurement. The optimized energy band alignment and suppressed nonradiative recombination with the incorporation of 2D perovskite capping layer are further verified by the TRPL decay measurements. Moreover, these 2D-3D stacking layered PSCs showed significantly improved long-term stability and the majority (nearly  $ 90\% $ ) of their original high efficiency can be retained after exposure in a humid environment for  $ 1000\mathrm{h} $ . Our findings provide an effective strategy to reduce the voltage losses and address the main hindrance to the practical application of perovskite devices, which is also applicable to other perovskite-based optoelectronic devices beyond photovoltaics.

Materials and Precursor Preparation: Lead iodide (99.9985%), lead bromide (99.999%), cesium iodide (99.998%), and hydroiodic

acid (57 wt% in water) were purchased from Alfa-Aesar, while methylammonium bromide (MABr) and Spiro-OMeTAD were bought from Dyesol and Borun Chemicals, respectively. Titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetylacetonate) (75 wt% in isopropanol), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), chlorobenzene (CB), acetonitrile (ACN), bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonamide lithium (LiTFSI), and 4-tert-butylpyridine (TBP) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.  $ \mathrm{HC(NH_2)_2I} $  (formamidinium iodide, FAI) and  $ \mathrm{C_6H_5C_2H_4NH_3I} $  (phenethylammonium iodide, PEAI) were synthesized according to previously reported methods.[1,17] All the purchased chemicals were used as received unless stated otherwise. To prepare  $ \mathrm{Cs_{0.05}(FA_{0.83}MA_{0.17})_{0.95}P} $  b(10.83Br0.17)3 precursor solution with excess lead iodide, FAI (1 M),  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  (1.1 M), MABr (0.2 M), and  $ \mathrm{PbBr_2} $  (0.2 M) were dissolved in a mixed solvent of DMF and DMSO (1:4 v/v), followed by adding CsI stock solution (1.5 M in DMSO) to achieve the desired triple-cation composition. To obtain the HTM solution, 72.3 mg of Spiro-OMeTAD was dissolved in 1 mL of CB with additives of  $ 17.5~\mu \mathrm{L} $  of LiTFSI solution  $ (520\mathrm{mgmL^{-1}} $  in ACN) and  $ 28.8~\mu \mathrm{L} $  of TBP.

Device Fabrication: Patterned FTO glass (FTO22-7, Yingkou OPV) substrates were sequentially cleaned with detergent solution, acetone, and isopropanol. A compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer (50 nm, c-  $  TiO_{2}  $  ) was first coated on the clean FTO glass by spray pyrolysis using titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetylacetonate) in isopropanol (1:20, v/v) at 450 °C, followed by spin-coating a mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer (200 nm) using commercial   $  TiO_{2}  $   nanoparticle paste (Dyesol, 30 NR-D) diluted in ethanol (1:6 in weight) at 2500 rpm for 30 s. After drying on 100 °C hotplate for 10 min, the substrates were calcined at 500 °C in air for 30 min. After cooling down to room temperature, the perovskite precursor was spin-coated on mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   substrates at 2000 rpm for 10 s and 6000 rpm for 30 s, in which 200 μL of CB was dropped on the spinning substrate 15 s prior to the end of second step. The substrates were then annealed on hotplate at 65 °C for 10 min and 100 °C for 60 min to obtain crystalline 3D perovskite films. To grow an additional 2D perovskite layer, 100 μL of PEAI solution dissolved in isopropanol with different concentrations (1, 4, and 8 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) were further spin-coated on the as-prepared 3D perovskite substrates at 5000 rpm for 30 s, and annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, 70 μL of Spiro-OMeTAD solution was spin-coated on the perovskite layers at 4000 rpm for 20 s. Finally, the devices were completed by depositing 80 nm of gold electrodes using thermal evaporation.

Characterizations and Measurements: XRD data were obtained from a Bruker Advanced X-ray diffractometer (40 kV and 30 mA) with Cu Kα radiation. UV–vis absorption spectra of deposited films were measured by a spectrophotometer (V-650, Jasco). The functional groups of the films were characterized by an FTIR spectrometer (Nicolet 5700 FTIR) with an ATR accessory.
