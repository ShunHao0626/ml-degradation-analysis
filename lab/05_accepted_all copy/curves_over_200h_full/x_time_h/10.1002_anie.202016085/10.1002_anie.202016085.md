# Efficient Inverted Perovskite Solar Cells with Low Voltage Loss Achieved by a Pyridine-Based Dopant-Free Polymer Semiconductor

Xianglang Sun  $  ^{+}  $  , Zhen Li  $  ^{+}  $  , Xinyu Yu, Xin Wu, Cheng Zhong, Danjun Liu, Dangyuan Lei, Alex K.-Y. Jen, Zhong'an Li,  $  ^{*}  $   and Zonglong Zhu  $  ^{*}  $

Abstract: Currently, the performance improvement for inverted perovskite solar cells (PVSCs) is mainly limited by the high open circuit voltage   $  V_{OC}  $   loss caused by detrimental nonradiative recombination (NRR) processes. Herein, we report a simple and efficient way to simultaneously reduce the NRR processes inside perovskites and at the interface by rationally designing a new pyridine-based polymer hole-transporting material (HTM), PPY2, which exhibits suitable energy levels with perovskites, high hole mobility, effective passivation of the uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and iodide defects, as well as the capability of promoting the formation of high-quality polycrystalline perovskite films. In absence of any dopants, the inverted PVSCs using PPY2 as the HTM deliver an encouraging PCE up to 22.41 % with a small   $  V_{OC}  $   loss (0.40 V), among the best device performances for inverted PVSCs reported so far. Furthermore, PPY2-based unencapsulated devices show an excellent long-term photostability, and over 97 % of its initial PCE can be maintained after one sun constant illumination for 500 h.

# Introduction

Solution-processable organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells (PVSCs) have achieved significant research progress in the past decade with the power conversion efficiency (PCE) beyond 25% reported, rivaling those for commercialized inorganic solar cells.  $  ^{[1,2]}  $   Among various

device configurations, planar inverted PVSCs exhibit attractive advantages of unobvious photo-current hysteresis, easy of fabrication, excellent compatibility of fabricating tandem and flexible cells, and high interface stability.  $  ^{[3,4]}  $   Since its first report in 2013, the PCE of inverted PVSCs has also made an impressive increase, exceeding 22%.  $  ^{[5,6]}  $   Nonetheless, the top-performance of inverted PVSCs still lags behind those of the mesoporous and planar conventional devices, mainly attributed to the relatively larger open circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) loss of the former,  $  ^{[7]}  $   which is largely caused by the non-radiative recombination (NRR) processes within the perovskite bulk and/or at its interface with charge transporting layers (CTLs).  $  ^{[8,9]}  $

Due to the polycrystalline nature, the perovskite films unavoidably present different type of intrinsic defects such as vacancies and interstitials at their grain boundaries (GBs) and the surfaces, leading to detrimental trap states that induce NRR of photogenerated carriers.  $  ^{[10-12]}  $   To reduce the defect formation, several techniques have been developed to increase the size of GBs, including compositional/additive engineering, processing methods optimization, and so on.  $  ^{[13-17]}  $   Alternatively, GB and surface passivation has demonstrated as an efficient and general approach to heal the detrimental defects to boost the device performance.  $  ^{[18-26]}  $   For example, Huang et al. recently designed a new passivation molecule containing several effective passivation groups, i.e. 4-tert-butylphenylalanine (D4TBP), which can simultaneously reduce multiple defects at the GBs and the surface to obtain a record small   $  V_{OC}  $   loss of 0.34 V in inverted PVSCs.  $  ^{[27]}  $   Moreover, Neher et al.  $  ^{[28]}  $   have shown that the NRR losses at the surface in most cases are substantially larger than that within perovskite bulk, and a substantial reduction on interfacial losses can be achieved by inserting a polymer interlayer between the perovskite layer and hole transporting layer (HTL) as the passivation layer, leading to a significant increase of   $  V_{OC}  $   from 1.12 to 1.17 V. Also, we recently demonstrated that the NRR losses at both top and bottom interfaces of perovskite are effectively reduced by using alkylammonium salts as the passivation interlayer, realizing a high PCE of   $  \approx22\%  $   with a   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.21 V.  $  ^{[29]}  $

In addition to introducing an additional passivation layer, directly endowing the HTLs with passivation functions should be a more efficient way to reduce the NRR in PVSCs,  $  ^{[30-32]}  $  since it can further simplify the device fabrication and avoid hindering charge transfer at the interface. # Results and Discussion

The synthetic route of PPY1-2 is shown in Scheme S1, while the synthetic details and characterization data are provided in Supporting Information (SI). PPY1-2 are cross-conjugated polymers simply synthesized by copolymerizing 2,6-diethynylpyridine (M1) and 3,5-diethynylpyridine (M2) with the diphenylamine-substituted-fluorene unit (M3), respectively. Their molecular weights were estimated by gel permeation chromatography (GPC) using tetrahydrofuran (THF) as the eluent. The weight-average molecular weights (  $  M_{n}s  $  ) of PPY1-2 were measured as 2200 and 7600 with the polydispersity indexes (  $  D_{s}  $  ) of 1.46 and 1.90, respectively. Based on this result, PPY1 needs to be considered as an oligomer. We have carefully optimized the polymerization conditions such as changing the catalysis and reaction solvents, however, the resulting molecular weights were still low, probably attributed to the electron-withdrawing property of nitrogen atom in M1 that will decrease the electron density of two adjacent ethynyl groups to reduce the reactivity.  $  ^{[57,58]}  $

In our molecular design, the passivation ability of resulting polymers can be simply tuned by changing the linkage sites of pyridine unit. Compared to 2,6-linked pyridine, 3,5-linked analog is expected to exhibit stronger coordination ability due to its outward orientation of N atom. To verify this hypothesis,   $  {}^{1}  $  H NMR measurement was carried out to gain a clear comparison of the coordination ability to Pb  $  ^{2+}  $   of two pyridine-based monomers. As shown in Figure 1b, the 2,6-protons of pyridine group in M2 shows a more visible shift to high field after adding excess PbI  $  _{2}  $   while a negligible shift is observed for 3,5-protons in M1, suggesting an enhanced lead coordination ability for M2 due to the reduced steric effect. Based on this, PPY2 based on M2 is expected to have the same interaction ability. We then performed fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) of polymer films since their proton peaks are difficult to be assigned. In comparison with no change for PPY1, a shift of   $  \nu_{C=N}  $   from 1605 to 1602 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   can be found for PPY2 film after mixing with PbI  $  _{2}  $   (Figure 1c), thereby further indicating the enhanced interaction ability between the 3,5-linked pyridine group in PPY2 and PbI  $  _{2}  $  . [52] In addition to the uncoordinated Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  , iodine (I  $  _{2}  $  ), which can be photooxidized from iodide in perovskites, has been proven to be another source of defects in perovskites. [12,59] Here, the absorption experiments (Figure S1) also indicate the possible interactions between I  $  _{2}  $   and pyridine. The absorption band of PPY1 exhibits a long-wavelength tail compared to that of PPY2, suggesting that 2,6-linkage might induce a more efficient intramolecular charge transfer (ICT). Both polymer films have good optical transparency (over 80%, Figure S5) in visible region, which will be beneficial to light harvesting as HTMs in inverted PVSCs. Moreover, their films not only show quite smooth film morphology (Figure S6), but also possess suitable surface wettability (Figure S7) to promote the formation of large grain size crystals with uniform coverage. The charge transporting properties were tested using space charge limited current (SCLC) method (Figure S8), while both polymers exhibit high hole mobilities, beyond   $  10^{-3}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $  V  $  ^{-1}  $  s  $  ^{-1}  $   (Table 1), fulfilling the critical requirement of dopant-free HTMs.  $  ^{[60,61]}  $   The possible reason for higher hole mobility of PPY2 could be its large   $  M_{w}  $   that might result in strong inter-chain interactions.

Modifying linkage sites of pyridine unit further affects the energy levels of resulting polymers. The HOMO levels of PPYs were determined by cyclic voltammetry (CV) and ultraviolet photoelectron spectrometer (UPS), and the data are summarized in Table 1. The HOMO levels of PPY1–2 (Figure S9) were measured as -4.96 and -5.17 eV, respectively. In this context, the subtle modification of pyridine linkage position can effectively regulate the HOMO levels of resulting HTMs, providing a possibility of studying the impact of energy level alignment. Moreover, the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) levels of PPY1–2 were estimated as -2.29 and -2.69 eV, respectively. The UPS results (Figures S10,11) are consistent with CV data, and the HOMO levels of -4.95 and -5.19 eV are obtained for PPY1 and PPY2, respectively.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to understand the difference in passivation effects and energy levels of PPYs. The DFT-optimized structure and HOMO/LUMO energy levels of analogous trimer model are illustrated in Figure S12 and S13, respectively. Both polymers

show interesting Zig-zag structure with a folded conformation due to two different torsional angles between two ethynylpyridine units and the central fluorene unit, being conducive to hole transport. The N atom on pyridine in PPY2 is clearly exposed outward while it is inward in PPY1, well explaining the difference on their coordination ability with   $  Pb^{2+}  $  . Both polymers also show similar energy level distributions; the HOMOs mainly localize on the diphenylamine-capped fluorene units and the LUMOs delocalize over the diacetylene substituted pyridine backbone and extend to the fluorene units. Moreover, the calculated HOMO/LUMO levels for PPY2 are deeper than those of PPY1, consistent with experimental data.

Control devices based on well-known polymer HTM, that is, poly(triarylamine) (PTAA), were also fabricated for comparison. Here,   $  (\mathrm{FA}_{0.92}\mathrm{MA}_{0.08})_{0.9}\mathrm{Cs}_{0.1}\mathrm{Pb}(\mathrm{I}_{0.92}\mathrm{Br}_{0.08})_{3}  $   is used as the light-absorber. The device fabrication details are described in SI. Figure 2a presents the energy-level alignment of devices. Clearly, PPY2 exhibits a more matched HOMO energy level with perovskite compared to PPY1, which will be beneficial to the hole extraction.

# Conclusion
