# ABSTRACT

Fullerene has been demonstrated to be one of the most efficient electron extractors in perovskite solar cells (PSCs). For feasibly preparing photovoltaic device by solution process, fullerene is normally functionalized in multiple for efficient increase of its solubility in polar solvents. However, multifunctionalization typically results in neighboring fullerene cages alienating to each

1

Journal Pre-proof

other and, in principle, reduces electron mobility. Herein, we show a star-like multiadducted fullerene electron extractor, hexakis[di(ethoxycarbonyl)methano]- $ \mathrm{C}_{60} $  (HEMC), which possesses a proper solubility in polar solvents as well as a close packing as revealed by crystallography. Likely due to its  $ T_{h} $ -symmetric structure with twelve ester 'tentacles' that can interact with neighboring fullerene derivatives or unsaturated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  of perovskites, the HEMC displays efficient photovoltaic performances with a power conversion efficiencies of  $ 20\% $ , a storage stability of  $ 3840\mathrm{h} $ , a thermal stability of  $ 300\mathrm{h} $  and a light-soaking stability of  $ 240\mathrm{h} $  in the HEMC-interfaced PSCs. The present work exemplifies an unusual application of fullerene multiadducts, in addition to increasing solubility, for solving charge accumulation and recombination problems in high efficient PSCs.

# 1. Introduction

With rapid development of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) [1, 2], particular attentions have been paid to improving both the power conversion efficiency (PCE) and stability based on optimization of interfaces with perovskite layers [3-5], in respect of strengthening electron extraction,

2

Journal Pre-proof

suppressing charge accumulation, reducing interfacial recombination and trap-assisted non-radiative recombination, the so-called ‘Shockley-Read-Hall’ (SRH) non-radiative recombination [6]. Interfacial and trap-assisted recombination have been effectively reduced via coordination between perovskite and passivators including Lewis bases, such as thiophene groups [7], pyridine groups [7], amine groups [8], carbonyl groups [9], and Lewis acids like fullerenes [8, 10]. Efforts were further directed to improve electron extraction by tailoring energy levels [11-13], or doping fullerenes into perovskite layers for suppressing intrinsic charge accumulation [14, 15]. Specifically, fullerenes have been demonstrated as the most efficient electron extractors [16-18], due to their strong electron accepting ability and excellent stability [19]. Among them, pristine fullerene C $  _{60}  $  possesses the closest packing mode and the highest electron mobility suitable for electron extraction [20]. However, the lack of solubilizing groups bound to C $  _{60}  $  cage precludes its application in solution-processed devices [17]. Upon the solubility is addressed, the fullerene monoadduct, [6, 6]-phenyl-C $  _{61}  $ -butyric acid methyl ester (PC $  _{61}  $ BM) [21], is still the most prevalent fullerene electron transport material. To further increase the solubility in different kinds of polar solvents, fullerenes have typically been multiple-functionalized to create various fullerene multiadducts. Unfortunately, most available fullerene multiadducts with large side chains, such as multiadduct analogs of PC $  _{61}  $ BM or indene-C $  _{60}  $  multiadducts, apparently alienate neighboring fullerene cages, which speciously affects the close packing and thus decreases the electron mobility among fullerene derivatives in the solid state [22-24]. Such a contradiction between solubility and electrical mobility causes uncertainty and disadvantage for fullerene materials applicable in solution-processed PSC devices.

Here we show a multiadducted fullerene electron extractor, hexakis[di(ethoxycarbonyl)-

3

Journal Pre-proof

methano]- $ \mathrm{C}_{60} $  (HEMC), facilitating to improve both the photovoltaic performance and stability of PSCs by enhancing the electron extraction and suppressing both charge accumulation and charge recombination. In the present HEMC molecule, the ester groups are as many as twelve, resembling 'tentacles' bound onto the fullerene cage to form a star-like molecule and ensuring its particularly high solubility in commonly-used polar solvents, such as  $ N $ ,  $ N $ -dimethylformamide (DMF) and isopropyl alcohol (IPA). The flexible 'tentacles' in high symmetry of  $ T_{h} $  contribute to ordered packing of HEMC itself and compact with other fullerene derivatives such as  $ \mathrm{PC}_{61}\mathrm{BM} $  in the solid state. In addition, HEMC can be anchored to the perovskite layer to enhance electron extraction in the interface of PSCs since carbonyl (C=O) groups have Lewis base-acid interactions with unsaturated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  ions in the perovskite layer.

# 2. Results and discussion

# 2.1 Characterization of the structure and packing of HEMC as an electron extractor

The structurally symmetric HEMC with twelve ester groups  $ \mathrm{C_{66}(COOEt)_{12}} $ , which has proper solubility in polar solvents (e.g., DMF or IPA) and possibility for an electron extractor in PSCs with inverted structure of  $ p - i - n $  [25], was synthesized by means of Bingel-Hirsch synthetic strategy (Fig. S1) [26]. The star-like molecular structure of HEMC with  $ T_{h} $  symmetry was confirmed by single-crystal X-ray diffraction (Fig. 1 and Table S1) and mass spectrum (Fig. S2). As shown, the HEMC displays an ordered packing in all the directions of  $ a- $ ,  $ b- $  and  $ c $ -axis, which means the centroid distances of all the neighboring fullerene cages are uniform. Such a highly ordered orientation of fullerene cages ensures the excellent electron extraction ability of HEMC [23, 27], largely due to smaller and softer ester groups that are similar to the tentacles of an octopus and

4

Journal Pre-proof

suitable for keeping the packing mode of fullerene cages more than previously reported large side chains in fullerene multiadducts [27, 29]. Due to the soft ester groups, HEMC exhibited excellent solubility in polar solvents such as DMF and IPA as illustrated in Fig. S3. As for the electronic property, an electron mobility value of  $ 2.0 \times 10^{-4} \mathrm{~cm}^2 \mathrm{~V}^{-1} \mathrm{~s}^{-1} $  (Fig. S4) was measured and calculated by using space charge limited current (SCLC) method, clearly confirming the higher electron mobility of HEMC in comparison to other fullerene multiadducts. Additionally, theoretical calculations suggest that electron distributions of both the lowest unoccupied molecular orbitals (LUMO) and highest occupied molecular orbitals (HOMO) of HEMC mostly locate on the fullerene cage itself, with implication about HEMC acting as an electron extractor excellent for electron hopping from the conduction band minimum (CBM) of the perovskite to the LUMO of the electron transport layers (ETLs) (Fig. 1e and 1f) according to the mechanism responsible for electron hopping between donors and acceptors [30, 31]. We also characterized the thermal stability of HEMC by thermalgravimetric analysis. As shown in Fig. S5, the HEMC possesses a thermal stability over  $ 300^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ , which is stable enough for the annealing process of the devices.

# 2.3 Enhanced electron extraction of the perovskite MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  interfaced by HEMC

To verify the electron extraction of the perovskite strengthened by the introducing of HEMC, the conductive atomic force microscope (c-AFM) measurements were carried out for the samples of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  hybridized and interfaced by HEMC in the perovskite junction and surface [i.e., the sample specified as  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3(\mathrm{HEMC})/\mathrm{HEMC} $ ]. Considering the current measurement in c-AFM is affected by the conditions of working tip, together with applied bias voltage and force loading, we used a standard diamond tip with a radius of  $ 10\mathrm{nm} $  for all samples in the c-AFM measurements under the same constant bias of  $ 1.5\mathrm{V} $ . As observed, the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3(\mathrm{HEMC})/\mathrm{HEMC} $  sample (Fig. 3b) displayed a much higher current than the control sample (Fig. 3a), which obviously implies the enhancement of conductivity attributing to the interlayer of HEMC in the junction and surface of the perovskite [15, 18, 32].

Considering good solubility of fullerene derivatives in chlorobenzene (CB) solvent, however, the HEMC layer in  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3(\mathrm{HEMC})/\mathrm{HEMC} $  sample is susceptible to washing by CB that has routinely been used as a solvent for fabrication of PCBM layer in  $ p $ -i- $ n $  PSCs. To rule out such a

Furthermore, the electron extraction abilities of different devices with the  $ p $ - $ i $ - $ n $  PSCs configuration of FTO/NiO $ _x $ /perovskite(HEMAc)/HEMAc/PCBM/BCP/Ag and its control group were investigated by photocurrent density-effective voltage ( $ J_{\text{ph}} $ - $ V_{\text{eff}} $ ) measurement [33]. # 2.5 Operational Stability of the HEMC-interfaced PSCs

Long-term stability should be envisaged for development of commercial PSCs nowadays [42-44]. As a prototypical absorber for PSCs, though the PCE of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -type PSCs has been developed rapidly, several intrinsic instability issues hinder its practical application in commercialized solar cells, such as charge accumulation and recombination, ion migration, phase decomposition, etc. [45-47]. Herein, we demonstrate a simple, feasible and efficient methodology to enhance stability of the prevalent  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -type PSCs significantly by incorporating the star-like fullerene multiadduct HEMC. We initially measured the water contact angles on the perovskite surfaces. As shown in Fig. S25, the hydrophobicity of HEMC can protect the perovskite layers from moisture, which contributes to the enhanced stability of the PSCs. Subsequently, we have

10

Journal Pre-proof

performed four kinds of stability tests including storage, thermal, light-soaking stability as well as light-humidity-heat aging test for the HEMC-interfaced PSCs [48, 49]. Fig. 5 presents the storage stability performance of unsealed HEMC-interfaced and control PSCs under  $ \mathrm{N}_2 $  atmosphere in the dark. The HEMC-interfaced PSCs exhibited a  $ \mathrm{T}_{80} $  of over  $ 3840\mathrm{h} $ , nearly triple more than the control PSCs. Likewise, we carried out thermal stability test of sealed devices under  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  in dry air as shown in Fig. S26. The HEMC-interfaced PSCs show a  $ \mathrm{T}_{80} $  of  $ 300\mathrm{h} $  that is much better than the control PSCs. Light-soaking stability of sealed devices at  $ 30^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  under continuous AM 1.5G illumination without UV filter in ambient air is shown in Fig. S27. After 'burn in' stage, we obtained a stable stage of at least  $ 200\mathrm{h} $  for the HEMC-interfaced PSCs. Besides, we further performed light-humidity-heat aging test of sealed devices at  $ 65^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  in ambient environment under continuous AM 1.5G illumination without UV filter. As shown in Fig. S28, the HEMC-interfaced PSCs also exhibited significantly enhanced stability compared to the control devices. We ascribed the enhancement of stability to the improvement of molecular contact and electron extraction in the PSCs, thus mitigated charge accumulation and recombination, leading to the retardant of ion migration, which is in principle supported by previous works [37, 47, 50, 51]. Overall, all these results significantly imply that the star-like HEMC can be an efficient electron extractor in the PSCs for enhancing operational stability towards commercial requirements.

# 3. Conclusion

n summary, our work exemplifies the star-like fullerene derivative HEMC as an excellent

11

Journal Pre-proof

electron extractor to reduce charge accumulation and recombination in the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -based,  $ \mathrm{MAPbI_xCl_{3 - x}} $ -based and CsFAMAPbIBr-based PSCs with enhanced photovoltaic performances as well as operational stability. The highly symmetric structure of  $ T_{h} $  and flexible 'tentacles' of ester groups contribute to the compact and ordered packing of fullerenes in the HEMC-interfaced PSCs. In addition, the ester 'tentacles' facilitate to anchor HEMC to the perovskite with unsatisfied  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  ions, in favor of electron extracting from perovskite to PCBM ETL in the perovskite(HEMA)/HEMA/PCBM configuration. PCE of  $ 19.53\% $  in the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -based PSCs,  $ 20.02\% $  in the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI_xCl_{3 - x}} $ -based PSCs and  $ 19.98\% $  in the CsFAMAPbIBr-based PSCs have been achieved for the devices with the HEMC-interfaced structure of  $ \mathrm{FTO / NiO_x / perovskite} $  (HEMA)/HEMA/PCBM/BCP/Ag. As for stability, the HEMC-interfaced PSCs showed  $ \mathrm{T}_{80} $  over  $ 3840\mathrm{h} $  for storage stability,  $ 300\mathrm{h} $  for thermal stability, and  $ 240\mathrm{h} $  for light-soaking stability, which significantly surpasses the control  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -based devices due to the enhancement of electron extraction by the star-like HEMC molecule. We believe that this fullerene multiadducts can be promising electron extractor for improving photovoltaic performance as well as stability, and finally promoting commercialization of the PSCs.

# 4.3. Device fabrication

Patterned FTO substrates were etched by Zn powders and diluted hydrochloride acid and were cleaned by ultrasonic treatments in a detergent, deionized water, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol for 20 min. The FTO substrates were dried by   $  N_{2}  $   and stored in a dry container before using. To prepare the   $  NiO_{x}  $   precursor solution, 0.6 M nickel (II) nitrate hexahydrate   $  (\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{NO}_{3})_{2}\cdot6\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O})  $   was dissolved in ethylene glycol and ethylenediamine solution and then was stirred overnight at 70 °C in a sealed container. Before fabricating the   $  NiO_{x}  $   layers, the FTO substrates were treated in a UV ozone chamber for 10 min. The   $  NiO_{x}  $   precursor solution was spin-coated on the FTO at 3000 rpm for 60 s and then annealed at 300 °C for 60 min in a muffle furnace. After cooling to 80 °C, the

13

Journal Pre-proof

substrate was moved into a glove box for further cooling and then fabricated the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ , the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI_xCl_{3 - x}} $  and the CsFAMAPbIBr layer according to previous works [40, 52, 53]. As for the HEMC-interfaced devices, the HEMC was dissolved in DMF with a concentration of  $ 10\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1} $  and added into different perovskite precursor solutions with different volumes. To fabricate the HEMC interlayers, the HEMC was dissolved in IPA with different concentrations and then spin-coated at  $ 4000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 30~s $  following annealing at  $ 80^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 10\mathrm{min} $ . The  $ \mathrm{PC}_{61}\mathrm{BM} $  film was dynamically spin-coated on the top of the perovskite layer or the HEMC layer with a solution of  $ 20\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1} $  in chlorobenzene at  $ 3000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 20~s $ . To prepare the BCP film, BCP powders were dissolved in the IPA with the concentration of  $ 0.5\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1} $  and treated in an ultrasonic bath for 60 min. The BCP solution was spin-coated on the top of the fullerene layer at  $ 6000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 30~s $  and annealed at  $ 80^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 10\mathrm{min} $ . Finally, an Ag electrode (100 nm) was thermally deposited under a base pressure of  $ 2.0\times 10^{-4}\mathrm{Pa} $  to complete the device.

# 4.4. Device Characterization

The photovoltaic performances of devices were tested under the illumination of AM 1.5G 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  using a 300 W xenon solar simulator (Newport Oriel Solar Simulators) under RH 30 % at 20 °C in the air. Current-voltage curves were characterized by a Keithley 2420 source meter. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) measurement was carried out by Merlin lock-in amplifier equipped with a 300 W xenon lamp which was calibrated with a standard single-crystal Si photovoltaic cell. A mask with an aperture area of 0.06 cm  $  ^{2}  $  was employed during the measuring process to obtain an accurate active area. The scan conditions are reverse scan (1.2 V to -0.2 V) and forward scan (-0.2 V to 1.2 V) at a scan rate 30 mV s  $  ^{-1}  $  with a delay time of 20 ms. The

14

Journal Pre-proof

maximum-power-point (MPP) output was tested under continuous AM 1.5G 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  illumination without encapsulation under ambient environment with RH 30 % at 20 °C without UV cutoff filters. Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) measurement was performed on a Thermo ESCALAB 250XI instrument. UV-Vis absorption spectrum was obtained by using a Cary-5000 UV-Vis-NIR spectrophotometer. The thickness of HEMC film was estimated by using HITACHI S-4800 scanning electron microscopy (SEM) in the cross-sectional view. The conductive atomic force microscope (c-AFM) measurements were carried out under illumination in Ar atmosphere on an Agilent SPM 5500 atomic force microscope using a diamond tip (Adama). The steady-state photoluminescence spectra were recorded by a Hitachi F-7000 fluorescence spectrometer equipped with a 150 W xenon lamp as an excitation source using an excited light of 516 nm. The time-resolved photoluminescence spectra were obtained by an Edinburgh Instruments FLS980 fluorescence spectrometer with the time-correlated single-photon counting method. The Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) measurement was taken on a Nicolet330 infrared spectrometer (Thermo Electron Corporation, U.S.). The measurements of defect density and electron mobility were conducted in the dark by space charge limited current (SCLC) method on a computer-controlled Keithley 2420 source meter for the electron-only devices. The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed on a CHI-650E electrochemical workstation in dark conditions with an applied bias of 0.8 V. The water contact angle measurements were carried out by using the SDC-100 contact angle analyzer.
