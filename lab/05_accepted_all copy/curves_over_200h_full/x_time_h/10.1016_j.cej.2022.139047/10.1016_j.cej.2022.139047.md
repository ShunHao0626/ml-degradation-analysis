# 1. Introduction

During the past few years, the metal halide hybrid perovskite semiconductor with excellent photoelectric performance has aroused great interest and been widely used in the field of solar cells   $  [1-5]  $  . Since 2009, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs with the organic – inorganic perovskite as active-layer material has skyrocketed to 25.7%   $  [6]  $  . However, the unsatisfactory thermal stability of hybrid perovskites derived from the volatile organic ammoniums severely hinders their further development and commercial applications   $  [7,8]  $  . Therefore, all-inorganic perovskites without volatile organic components have drawn burgeoning concerns owing to their superior thermal stability   $  [9-11]  $  .   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   perovskite stands out among all-inorganic perovskites in view of its ideal bandgap (  $  E_{g}  $  ) of   $  \approx1.70  $   eV for photovoltaic applications, especially as the top cell of silicon/perovskite tandem solar cells   $  [12-14]  $  .

# ABSTRACT

Inorganic   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   perovskite has been regarded as a kind of promising light-harvesting material due to its outstanding photoelectrical characteristics especially in perovskite solar cells (PSCs). However, there are still some problems hindering its further development, among which non-radiative recombination induced by traps and voltage loss are very pivotal. Here, the passivation of surface defects and energy level alignment of perovskite films are greatly ameliorated by facile surface modification with alkylamines. It is found that the undercoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   are effectively passivated by the terminal amino group, leading to significantly reduced surface trap density, while the charge lifetime of treated perovskite films is prolonged. Besides, the bilateral amino groups from hexanediamine (HDA) can provide additional hydrogen bond to coherently connect the   $  PbI_{6}^{4-}  $   octahedra. Moreover, gradient energy levels are created after surface treatment, which promote the carriers transfer and reduce the voltage deficit. Consequently, a champion power conversion efficiency (PCE) of inverted   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   PSCs over 18 % with higher fill factor (FF) of 81.21 % and open circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) of 1.10 V is obtained compared to the pristine device with a lower PCE of 12.69 % (  $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.01 V, and FF of 62.49 %). Besides, the unencapsulated device with HDA treatment exhibits good stability with keeping 80 % of its initial efficiency after being aged for 1000 h at 35 % relative humidity. Our work can offer a facile but effective approach for the development of highly efficient and stable inverted   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   PSCs.

However, the unideal tolerance factor of 0.8 could result in a poor phase stability, and easily convert to thermodynamic stable non-perovskite phase (  $  \delta  $  -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $  ) with the distortion of lead iodine octahedra at room temperature [15]. With the utilization of dimethylammonium iodide (DMAI) or DMAPbI  $  _{3}  $  , the intermediate phase and crystallization processes of the CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   can be flexibly regulated, which helps to achieve pure and stable CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   black phase at relatively low annealing temperature [16–19]. Although the CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   PSCs have been rapidly developed since then, there still remains multifarious problems to be solved.

It is well known that the surface and grain boundary of solution-processed perovskites exist a lot of defects, which are considered to be one of the main reasons for enrichments of the Shockley–Read–Hall (SRH) nonradiative recombination centers and attack sites for external erosions, leading to the terrible device performance with low open-circuit voltage   $  V_{\mathrm{OC}}  $   and poor stability [9,20]. have been conducted to reduce the detrimental defect states of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film [21,22]. Meng et al. introduced inorganic additive   $  NH_{4}I  $   into the precursor solution to manipulate the preparation process and obtained the high-quality   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film with low defects [23]. Liu et al. successfully fabricated the compact perovskite grain with fewer pinholes and cracks by the molten-salt-assisted crystallization method, resulting in fewer shunt paths and better interface contact [24]. And they also developed a simple approach to passivate the undercoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   via the post-treatment of histamine to gain the exceptional device PCE with promoted long-term stability of PSCs [25]. Apart from the vexing surface defects, the problem of energy loss associated with   $  V_{OC}  $   is also noteworthy, which has a direct impact on the final photovoltaic performance. Various strategies including the compositional/interface engineering have been introduced to optimize the energy band alignments of functional layers to mitigate the   $  V_{OC}  $   deficit. For example, Meng et al. reported an easily operated urea-ammonium thiocyanate (UAT) additive approach to improve the perovskite film morphology and energy level alignment, which helped decrease interfacial charge recombination loss [26]. Jin et al. employed guanidine hydrobromide as a post-treatment agent on the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   surface, accomplishing the band alignment optimization and recombination reduction simultaneously [20]. Lately, Zhang et al. demonstrated that the derivatives of cinnamic acid could be applied to functionalize the perovskite surface and form the benign interfacial dipole, resulting in a peak PCE of up to 19.03 % with high   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.16 V [27].

Nevertheless, it's worth noting that, up to now, most of the high efficiency   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   PSCs are based on regular (n-i-p) device structure, which usually employ 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis[N,N-di(4-methoxyphenyl)amino]-9,9'-spirobifluorene (SpiroOMeTAD) as the hole-transport layer (HTL) and high-temperature sintered   $  TiO_{2}  $   as the electron-transport layer (ETL). Both the instability of additional dopants from the doped organic HTL and the photocatalytic susceptibility of   $  TiO_{2}  $   seriously affect the PSCs durability [28,29]. Thus, it is extremely desired to exploit the inverted (p-i-n)   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   PSCs, especially considering this device structure possesses huge advantages in flexible/multijunction photovoltaic applications [30–32]. Han et al. adopted an inverted structure with PTAA as HTL and suppressed the lattice distortion of cubic   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   via surface modification with controlling steric hindrance of organic terminal groups, contributing to a stable PCE of 13.2 % for the planar PSCs [30]. Fang et al. synthesized an agent of methylammonium pyridine-2-carboxylic to in situ heal and stabilize the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film, and generated the promoted efficiency of 16.67 % and stability attributed to the effective passivation and blocked moisture erosions [31]. Very recently, they developed a novel additive strategy to fabricated inverted PSCs with widened humidity operating window and achieved the highest recorded PCE of over 19 % [33]. Although some promising results have been acquired from the aforementioned studies, the state-of-art advancements of inverted   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   devices still lag far behind those of regular PSCs. Moreover, one can find that the problems discussed earlier in regular devices, such as defects and mismatched band alignments, also exist in inverted PSCs, which severely restrict the development of high-performance inverted photovoltaic devices. Therefore, it is extremely urgent to explore a facile and efficient approach, and elucidate its relevant mechanism to further promote the performance of inverted   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   PSCs.

In this respect, we employ a feasible method to modify the surface of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   perovskite by alkylamines. The utilization of hexylamine (HA) and hexanediamine (HDA) can effectively passivate the surface trap state of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   films, and simultaneously adjust the surface energy level of perovskite film to form a more matched band alignments, resulting in the significantly suppressed non-radiative charge recombination and voltage loss. Thus, the optimized all-inorganic inverted   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   PSC with HDA-treatment exhibits a best PCE of 18.21 % with much higher fill factor (FF) of 81.21 % and   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.10 V compared to the control devices with lower PCE of 12.69 %,   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.01 V, and FF of 62.49 %, respectively. Moreover, the HDA-based device delivers an excellent humidity

stability with remaining over 80 % original PCE after 1000 h exposed in room temperate with 35 % humidity, while the control device just remains 50 % original PCE after less than 500 h exposed in the identical environment.

# 2. Experimental section

# 2.1. Materials and solution preparation

Poly[3-(4-carboxylbutyl)thiophene (P3CT, Mw: 30–40 k) was purchase from the Rieke Metals. DMAI, CsI, PbI $ _2 $ , [6,6]-phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCB $ _{61} $ M) and 2,9-dimethyl-4,7-diphenyl-1,10-phenanthroline (BCP) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. The anhydrous N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF), isopropanol (IPA) and methanol were purchased from Aladdin. Chlorobenzene (CB) was purchased from J&K Scientific. The hexylamine (HA) was purchased from Macklin and 1,6-hexanediamine (HDA) purchased from Adamas. The P3CT-N solution was prepared by dissolving 10 mg P3CT in 67 μL methylamine and 1 mL methanol mixed solution then stirred 10 min at 50 °C and dilute the solution five times before use. 0.6 M CsPbI $ _3 $  perovskite optical absorption material was prepared as following: 0.6 M CsI, 0.6 M PbI $ _2 $  and 0.6 M DMAI in the DMF solvent. The solution was kept stirring at 70 °C for around 2 h until a clear yellowish solution was formed. The PCBM solution was prepared by dissolving 20 mg PCBM in 1 mL chlorobenzene. For the HA and HDA solution, 3 μL HA and HDA were added in 1 mL IPA, respectively.

# 2.2. Device fabrication

Glass/FTO substrates (1.5 cm*1.5 cm) were cleaned by sonication in deionized water, ethanol and isopropanol and then dried in an oven. Then, the dry substrates were treated with UV-ozone for 20 min before used. The P3CT-N layer was spin-coated (4000 rpm/40 s) on FTO and annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. Then the FTO/P3CT-N templates were transported on the 70 °C hotplate for preheating substrates before   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   films deposition. And the hot   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   precursor (70 °C) were deposited on the preheated FTO/P3CT-N by 3000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 200 °C for 5 min to form photovoltaic phase in a nitrogen-filled glove box. For the control device, electron transporting layer (ETL) was spin-coated on the FTO/P3CT-N/   $  CsPbI_{3}  $ , and 40 μL HA or HDA solution was spin-coated on the perovskite film (3000 rpm/30 s), respectively. Next, PCBM was spin-coated (1000 rpm/5s 3000 rpm/30 s) on film. Finally, isopropanol saturated solution of BCP was applied at a rate of 5000 r/min, with a time of 30 s and the Ag with a thickness of 80 nm to form an electrode through evaporation.

# 2.3. Characterizations

All the structures in this work were optimized using density functional theory (DFT) method implemented in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP). The Generalized Gradient Approximation (GGA) with Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional was applied. All the calculations were on the CsPbI3 (100) with I-related point defect. X-ray diffraction patterns (XRD, Bruker D8 Advance instrument with Cu Kα X-ray radiation about 0.154 nm) were used to test crystal structure and diffraction angle range from 5° to 50°. The morphology characterization of the films was measured by SEM (JSM-7800F). The surface morphology of the perovskite films was tested by atomic force microscopy (AFM, Asylum Research Cypher, UK). PL and TR-PL spectra were recorded via using the FluoTime 300 fluorescence lifetime spectrometer (PicoQuant). The chemical states of ions were tested by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Kratos Axis Ultra DLD) using Al Kα as radiation and the C 1 s line at 284.6 eV was used for correction the all spectra. The UPS measurements were performed with a He I (hν = 21.22 eV) gas discharge lamp for excitation and a total instrumental energy resolution of 100 meV and samples were negatively biased for the

# 3. Result and discussion

The   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   perovskite films were prepared by a one-step deposition and annealing process without any anti-solvent treatment. Then two kinds of alkylamines (HA and HDA) dissolved in isopropyl alcohol were directly spin-coated atop the as-prepared   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   films, followed by a low temperature thermal treatment at 100 °C for 120 s. The molecular structures and simulated electrostatic potential (ESP) maps of HA and HDA molecules are shown in Fig. 1a, from which we can observe that both molecules are short-chain alkylamine with same chain length, and HA possesses methyl   $  (-CH_{3})  $   and amino group   $  (-NH_{2})  $   at the molecular terminals, respectively, whereas HDA presents a symmetrical molecular structure with an amino group at each end. Obviously, the symmetrical negative potential distribution (red region) of HDA indicates the abundance of electrons around the amino groups, which could offer more active site to interact with perovskite than HA molecule. According to previous reports, the amino groups as Lewis bases can effectively interact with the undercoordinated lead ions or iodine vacancy to passivate the defects of perovskite by the typical Lewis base–acid

Thus, we propose a hypothetical mechanism model to interpret the passivation mechanism between HDA and   $  CsPbI_{3}  $ , as illustrated in Fig. 1b. The   $  -NH_{2}  $   group at one end from HDA can passivate the defects on the perovskite surface and grain boundaries via Lewis base–acid reaction, and the other terminal   $  -NH_{2}  $   can connect with the iodide from   $  PbI_{6}^{4-}  $   octahedra via H-bond formation, which would largely reduce the nonradiative recombination losses and favor the construction of efficient and stable   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   PSCs. Besides, the hydrophobic alkyl components exposed on the film surface can effectively prevent the perovskite from external water erosion to enhance stability [38]. In order to confirm our hypothesized mechanism model, density functional theory (DFT) was first carried out to calculate the adsorption energy between different molecules and perovskite. It is acknowledged that among the various defect sources, iodine vacancy (  $  V_{I}  $  ) dominates the perovskite surface defects of the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film [25,39]. Fig. 1d-e clearly depict the perovskite surface passivated with HA and HDA molecules in the optimized adsorption direction, respectively. The calculated results reveal that the Lewis base–acid binding energy between   $  -NH_{2}  $   from HA and   $  V_{I}  $   from the perovskite surface is -0.77 eV, while that between the   $  -NH_{2}  $   from HDA and   $  V_{I}  $   is -1.17 eV. This result suggests that the interaction of the amino groups at both ends from HDA and the   $  V_{I}  $   from the surface of perovskite is much easier compared to the similar reaction resulted by HA, which indicates that HDA molecules could more tightly anchor on the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   surface with a stronger coordination with the undercoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $ .

The surface topography of perovskites has a significant impact on the final film quality and photovoltaic performance, so the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) were performed to examine the effect of HA and HDA treatments on the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   morphology. As depicted in Fig. 3a, the pristine perovskite film shows medium quality with stacked morphology and few pinholes. While the perovskite films modified with HA or HDA present more conformal coverage over the substrates without pinholes (Fig. 3b-c). It is worth noting that the crystal grain of HDA-treated film seemed to be merged together and the grain boundaries become blurred, which may be correlated with the crystal secondary growth, and the underlying causes require further in-depth exploration. Obviously, appropriate annealing temperature and time (100 °C for 2 min) are necessary to obtain favorable crystallization and morphology after depositing alkylamines on perovskite surface (Figure S3 and S4). Besides, the top-view SEM images of perovskite films treated with different concentration of HA and HDA are displayed in Fig. S5a-f. It can be observed that with the increase of concentration, the compact morphology of films would be destroyed along with the appearance of pinholes and nonuniform size. Therefore, it is crucial to precisely control the optimized concentration of alkylamine modifier to obtain high-quality   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film, which is a prerequisite for obtaining desired interfacial contact and suppressed nonradiative recombination. AFM was further conducted to test the microstructure of perovskite surface. Almost identical root mean square (RMS) roughness about 21 nm is obtained for all the perovskite films without or with the alkylamine treatments despite their different appearance as shown in Fig. 3e-f. Overall, the surface treatment with appropriate amount of alkylamine will bring a significant effect on the perovskite morphology especially for HDA. Besides, contact angle of water droplets on various perovskite films were measured as illustrated in Figure S6a-c. It can be observed that the contact angle of water droplets increase from 36.64° to 51.28°, 60.34° for the pristine, HA- and HDA- treated perovskite films, respectively. The increased contact angle may result from the hydrophobic carbon chain exposed at the surface of perovskite films and forms a moisture-repelling barrier, which is beneficial to achieve high stable PSCs.

Apart from the photovoltaic efficiency, the device stability is another critical aspect for perovskite solar cells. From the increase of contact angle of water droplets for HDA-treated   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film in Figure S6, we would expect the humidity stability of the film/ device to be improved. Then, we first exposed the unencapsulated films to a relative humidity (RH) of 35 % at ambient temperature to explore the degradation of those films. As illustrated in Fig. 5g, the pristine   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film without modification began to decompose after 0.5 h exposed to 35 % RH atmosphere, and most of the film has degraded after aging for 4 h. In contrast, the HDA-treated   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film still exhibit the black brownish phase even after 5 h exposure. From the corresponding XRD patterns of the aging of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   films (Figure S12), it can be clearly observed that the pristine film exhibit obvious diffraction peak of   $  PbI_{2}  $   and δ-phase   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   after 4 h, while the XRD patterns of HDA-treated film still present favorable diffraction peak of β-phase   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   under the same conditions. Furthermore, we evaluated the long-term stability of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   PSCs with and without HDA modification. The unencapsulated devices were stored at a RH range about 25 ~ 35 % at ambient temperature, and their normalized efficiencies were tracked over time and recorded, as shown in Fig. 5h. The pristine device exhibited inferior storage stability with just remaining only 36 % of the original efficiency during a testing period about 650 h, while the HDA-treated PSC presents a relatively better stability by maintaining above 80 % of its original efficiency after 1000 h, which is in good agreement with the corresponding elevated film moisture stability. It can be interpreted that the outstanding humidity stability of the HDA-treated   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film and PSC may originate from the exposed hydrophobic alkyl chains of HDA on the perovskite surface, acting as a moisture-blocking barrier, as well as the greatly reduced surface defects to protect the grain boundaries from moisture intrusion. Besides, we have performed the operational stability measurements of the pristine and HDA-treated   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   PSCs via maximum power point (MPP) tracking under continuous 100 mW/cm² illumination in   $  N_{2}  $   atmosphere. As shown in Figure S13, the pristine   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   device exhibits rapid decrease with remaining 44 % of its initial PCE after almost 190 h, while the HDA-treated device still maintains 87 % efficiency under the same condition, which further verifies the efficient surface defects passivation and grain protection effects by a facile alkylamine modification.

# 4. Conclusion

In summary, we have successfully developed an effective surface modification method using alkylamines to treat   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   films, which can not only passivate the trap states of perovskite films, but also adjust the surface energy levels to better match the PCBM layer with forming a gradient band alignment. The defect passivation by the bilateral amino group from HDA can effectively reduce the non-radiative recombination, and the resulting gradient energy levels can promote the charge transportation and mitigate the voltage loss. Consequently, a champion PCE of 18.21 % was obtained for the HDA-treated inverted   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   PSC. Besides, both the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   perovskite films and solar cells exhibited excellent humidity stability after surface treatment with HDA. The unencapsulated HDA-based PSCs stored at a RH of 35 % for 1000 h still remain over 80 % of its original PCE. This work provides a facile surface modification strategy to fabricate high-quality perovskite film and device, which will promote the development of high performance all-inorganic PSCs.
