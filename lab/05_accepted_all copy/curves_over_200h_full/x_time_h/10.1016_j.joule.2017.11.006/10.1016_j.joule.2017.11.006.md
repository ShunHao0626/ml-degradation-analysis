# Highly Efficient and Stable Perovskite Solar Cells Enabled by All-Crosslinked Charge-Transporting Layers

Zonglong Zhu, $ ^{1} $  Dongbing Zhao, $ ^{1} $  Chu-Chen Chueh, $ ^{1} $  Xueliang Shi, $ ^{1} $  Zhongan Li, $ ^{1} $  and Alex K.-Y. Jen $ ^{1,2,3,4,*} $

# SUMMARY

Despite the demonstrated high power conversion efficiency (PCE) of perovskite solar cells (PVSC), long-term stability of the device operated in humid environments under photo- and thermal stresses is still a serious concern prior to any commercialization. To provide possible solutions to overcome this hurdle, we have synthesized an n-type conjugated molecule, c-HATNA, that can be cross-linked as an electron-transporting layer (ETL) on top of the desired perovskites. By proper doping to increase its electron-transporting property, a high PCE of 18.21% can be obtained with respectable moisture and thermal stability without encapsulation. Moreover, this c-HATNA ETL can be used in conjunction with another crosslinkable hole-transporting layer, c-TCTA-BVP, to fabricate all-crosslinked charge-transporting layers (CTLs) for PVSCs and achieve 16.08% and 13.42% PCEs on rigid and flexible substrates, respectively. More importantly, the device with all-crosslinked CTLs showed impressive thermal stability in ambient environment: almost 70% of its initial PCE after being heated at 70°C for 300 hr.

# INTRODUCTION

With the vigorous progress made recently, solution-processable organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite materials have emerged as a very promising candidate for next-generation photovoltaic (PV) technology.  $  ^{1-4}  $   Besides their superior semiconducting properties that are comparable with those of common inorganic semiconductors, they laudably inherit several distinct advantages from the organic semiconductors, such as light weight and facile solution processability. These enable them to be used for realizing high-performance solar cells with low cost, flexibility, and high power-per-weight properties.  $  ^{5-7}  $   However, there are some growing concerns regarding their long-term stability besides the demonstrated high efficiency in perovskite solar cells (PVSCs).  $  ^{8-10}  $   Since the performance of PVSCs has already been demonstrated to rival those of the prevailing inorganic counterparts,  $  ^{11-16}  $   more research efforts are redirected to improve devices' long-term stability under thermal, photo-, and moisture stresses.  $  ^{17-20}  $   To effectively improve the stability of PVSCs requires an integrated engineering effort to simultaneously optimize the perovskite layer, compatibility of the interfacial materials, and device configuration.  $  ^{21-23}  $

Solution-processable organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite materials have emerged as a very promising candidate for next-generation photovoltaic technology due to their superior semiconducting properties. Although the performance of perovskite solar cells (PVSCs) has already been demonstrated to rival those of the prevailing inorganic counterparts, the device's long-term stability under thermal, photo-, and moisture stresses has been raised as one major challenge before the commercialization of this technology. Here, we have designed and synthesized a crosslinkable n-type conjugated molecule, c-HATNA, to serve as the electron-transporting layer on top of the perovskite to improve photo-, moisture, and thermal stability of the fabricated PVSCs. Numerous studies unveiled the conspicuous degradation of perovskite films when they were exposed in air or under light illumination.  $  ^{29-34}  $   To address this issue, several strategies to optimize the thin-film quality, engineer the perovskite compositions, and crosslink the perovskite crystals have been vigorously investigated.  $  ^{35-37}  $

In the meantime, there are also several studies that reveal the degradation of associated interfaces and electrodes in devices caused by redox chemistry/reactions triggered by perovskites.  $  ^{38-40}  $   Hence, strategies related to interfacial and device engineering aspects have also been proposed to address these device stability issues besides improving the intrinsic stability of perovskites. For example, the encapsulation of a device with a hydrophobic polymer layer has been adopted to prevent moisture and oxygen from permeating into the perovskite layer and to also mitigate the interfacial redox reactions.  $  ^{41}  $   However, this method with hydrophobic polymer layer encapsulation could not improve the photo- and thermal stability of the device effectively. In this regard, inorganic interlayers,   $  SnO_{2}  $  , ZnO, and   $  ZnSnO_{3}  $  , with better photochemical and thermal stability, are also proposed to replace the organic materials as the capping layers.  $  ^{42-46}  $   Lately, the carbon counter electrode has been proven as an effective method to significantly enhance the device's lifetime in ambience, which has nowadays become one of the most prevailing scaffolds for constructing stable PVSCs.  $  ^{47-49}  $

While most of these studies were focused on improving the robustness of the device against moisture, little attention has been paid to improving the overall thermo-/photostability of PVSC. In this aspect, thermo-/photorobustness of the employed CTLs will play an even more profound role than that of the perovskite materials.  $  ^{50-52}  $   It has been reported that the improper thermo-/photostability of CTL will engender its morphological degradation, which further hastens the device degradation through the mechanisms disclosed above.  $  ^{9,53}  $   Therefore, it is very important to exploit CTLs with adequate thermo-/photorobustness. To this end, several crosslinkable organic CTLs have recently been exploited,  $  ^{54-57}  $   in which not only respectable robustness against moisture but also decent thermal stability have been demonstrated for the derived devices.  $  ^{58-62}  $   These results highlight the potential of using crosslinkable organic CTLs to realize efficient and stable PVSCs. However, it is still a formidable challenge to develop a CTL that can be crosslinked under conditions that are compatible with perovskite processing and at the same time possesses decent electrical conductivity and thermal stability for improving charge extraction and device stability.  $  ^{47,48}  $

Recently, we have developed a series of hexaazatrinaphthylene (HATNA)-based electron-transporting layers (ETLs) that can be used to fabricate inverted p-i-n PVSCs,  $  ^{63}  $  wherein the chemical structures of HATNAs could be fine-tuned to realize high PCE. Furthermore, similar to the commonly used fullerene-based ETLs, the HATNA ETLs were found to be hydrophobic and could passivate the surface defects of the sandwiched  $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $  layer to improve device stability and reproducibility.  $  ^{63}  $  Encouraged by this result, we have further developed a crosslinkable ETL based on c-HATNA to enhance its thermo-/photorobustness. An aliphatic pentaerythritol tetrakis(3-mercaptopropionate) (PETMP)  $  ^{54}  $  was employed in the thiol-ene reaction to lower the crosslinking temperature of c-HATNA compounds to  $  \sim110^{\circ}C  $ . By combining with another crosslinkable hole-transporting layer (HTL), we demonstrate an all-crosslinked CTL that enables the fabrication of efficient inverted p-i-n PVSC with high efficiency and remarkable stability.

# Synthesis of Crosslinkable HATNA and Film Characteristics

As shown in Figure 1A, a crosslinkable HATNA derivative, (3,9,14-trifluorodiquinoxalino[2,3-a:2',3'-c]phenazine -2,8, 15-trisulfinyl)tris(propane-3,1-diyl) tris(2-methylacrylate) (namely HATNASOACRYLATE or c-HATNA) was designed and

synthesized. This compound comprises an HATNA core with three crosslinkable side chains, in which the acrylate group can be crosslinked easily in solid state. The detailed synthetic route is outlined in Scheme S1. In brief, 4,5-difluoro-2-nitroaniline was first reacted with 3-mercaptopropanol through a nucleophilic substitution to obtain the alkylsulfanyl-substituted derivative. It was further reduced to obtain the corresponding diamine, followed by the reaction in HBr solution to obtain the brominated diamine. Finally, the hexaazatrinaphthylene derivative S1 was synthesized by the condensation reaction between the diamine with hexaketocyclohexane octahydrate in the presence of acetic acid. To incorporate the crosslinkable moiety into the side chain, the HATNA derivative S1 was reacted with methyl acrylic acid to yield the crosslinkable c-HATNA derivative S2 bearing the methacrylate group. To ensure its energy levels would match with those of the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  active layer, the product S2 was further oxidized by  $ m $ -chloroperoxybenzoic acid (m-CPBA) to produce the corresponding sulfoxide S3 as shown in Scheme S1. Since only its side chains were modified, the designed c-HATNA should still keep the superior electron-transporting properties of the parent HATNA compound.

To efficiently crosslink c-HATNA, its thermal transition properties with and without the assistance of PETMP were investigated first by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC).  $  ^{52,53}  $   As shown in Figure 1B, the crosslinking temperature estimated from the broad exothermic peak decreased significantly from   $  \sim179^{\circ}  $  C for the pristine c-HATNA film to   $  \sim87^{\circ}  $  C for the c-HATNA/PETMP blend film. This result clearly indicates that the fast and complete thermal crosslinking of c-HATNA/PETMP film proceeds at a much lower temperature due to rapid thiol-ene reaction. This provides more tolerance for the perovskite layer below compared with most of the traditional thermal crosslinking reactions reported so far.  $  ^{64-66}  $   Presented in Figure 1C is the UV-visible absorption spectra of the c-HATNA/PETMP blend film (on glass substrate) before and after crosslinking at   $  110^{\circ}  $  C, wherein the crosslinked film was further rinsed by various organic solvents (acetone, dichloromethane [DCM], toluene, and tetrahydrofuran [THF]) after crosslinking to remove any uncrosslinked residues. A high-optical-quality film obtained after solvent rinsing with similar absorption intensity (compared with the as-cast film with similar thickness before rinsing) indicated good solvent resistance as a result of crosslinking.

As shown in Figure 1C, the absorption edge of the c-HATNA film is located at  $ \sim 460\mathrm{nm} $ , corresponding to an optical bandgap of  $ 2.70\mathrm{eV} $ . Its commensurate cyclic voltammetry spectrum is depicted in Figure S1, in which the reversible redox process suggests its good electrochemical stability. The onset of the first oxidative potential versus  $ \mathrm{Fc / Fc^{+}} $  was found to be  $ -1.12\mathrm{eV} $ , corresponding to the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) level of  $ -6.70\mathrm{eV} $ , which will provide better hole-blocking capability and charge selectivity (Figure 1D). Meanwhile, its lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) level was estimated to be  $ -4.00\mathrm{eV} $ , which matches reasonably well with the conduction band minimum (CBM) of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $ , affirming the feasibility of achieving efficient electron transfer between them.

The contact angle measurement (by water droplet) of c-HATNA film is displayed in Figures 1E and S2, wherein the c-HATNA and crosslinked film showed a higher contact angle of   $  \sim96^{\circ}  $   and   $  \sim97^{\circ}  $   compared with that (  $  \sim68^{\circ}  $  ) of [6,6]-phenyl-C-61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) (Figure 1F). This enhanced hydrophobicity is in part attributed to the introduced alkyl thiol co-crosslinking group, which will benefit the moisture stability of the derived inverted PVSC.  $  ^{54}  $

# Photovoltaic Performance and Stability of c-HATNA-Based PVSCs

The planar p-i-n PVSCs in this study are fabricated with the configuration of indium tin oxide (ITO)/NiO/CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ PbI $ _3 $ /PCBM or c-HATNA/bis-C $ _{60} $ /Ag, and the detailed information for device fabrication is described in Experimental Procedures. Notably, the thin bis-C $ _{60} $  layer (~5 nm) serves as a work-function modifier for the top Ag electrode. $ ^{67,68} $  To further enhance the electron-transporting property of c-HATNA, it was first doped with triethylamine (Et $ _3 $ N), followed by isothermal heat at 110°C for 30 min, then thoroughly rinsed with toluene. The cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (EM) image of the c-HATNA-derived device is shown in Figure 2A, where the thickness of each constituent layer is estimated to be ~30–40 nm for the NiO compact layer, ~450 nm for the perovskite layer, and ~150 nm for the crosslinked ETL. In addition, the surface morphology of the crosslinked c-HATNA is presented in Figure S3 for direct comparison with the device using PCBM ETL.

The performance of the c-HATNA-based devices is shown in Figure 2B. The optimized thickness of the crosslinked ETL is   $  \sim150  $   nm to provide respectable water resistance and decent electron transport. A proper film thickness is important because if the c-HATNA film is too thin it may not have enough waterproof function and, on the contrary, a too thick c-HATNA film will result in increased series resistance to impede electron transport in the device.  $  ^{63,69}  $   The c-HATNA-derived device showed a quite low PCE of 11.97% with a   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.05 V, a   $  J_{SC}  $   of 16.79 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and an FF of 67.9% (Table 1). This low performance is attributed to the unsatisfactory electron transport of the c-HATNA layer.

To alleviate this problem, a small amount of   $  Et_{3}N  $   was added to dope c-HATNA before crosslinking in order to enhance its electron-transporting properties, especially for the thicker-film case.  $  ^{70-73}  $   As shown in Figure 2C and Table 1, the electron mobility (measured through the space-charge-limit-current [SCLC] technique) of the doped films was significantly increased from   $  6.92 \times 10^{-5} cm^{2} V^{-1} s^{-1}  $   to   $  8.42 \times 10^{-4}  $  ,   $  1.14 \times 10^{-3}  $  , and   $  1.68 \times 10^{-3} cm^{2} V^{-1} s^{-1}  $   with 1 wt.%, 3 wt.%, and 5 wt.% doping ratio, respectively. It has been reported that   $  Et_{3}N  $   can effectively dope some electron-deficient cores such as perylene diimide via a photoinduced electron transfer process.  $  ^{74}  $   Its doping effect on c-HATNA was evident by the color change of the c-HATNA precursor solution from light yellow to dark red after adding   $  Et_{3}N  $   (see the inset image of Figure 2C), wherein the radical anions were generated by the amine.

The efficacy of increasing c-HATNA's electron transport is clearly reflected in the overall performance of the derived devices, for which great performance improvement was achieved after doping. All the photovoltaic parameters were improved after doping, especially the FF (Table 1), affirming the improved charge collection efficiency as a result of the increased electron transport of the used ETL. Among the doped ETLs, the device with 3 wt.% doped c-HATNA showed a best PCE of 18.21% with a   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.09 V, a   $  J_{SC}  $   of 21.25 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and an FF of 78.6%. # Conclusion

A HANTA-based electron-transporting molecule, HATNASOACRYLATE (or c-HATNA), functionalized with crosslinkable acrylate side chains, has been designed and synthesized to serve as an efficient and robust ETL for PVSC. By employing an alkyl thiol crosslinker, c-HATNA can be efficiently crosslinked via the thiol-ene reaction at a relatively low temperature of 110°C, allowing it to be used on top of the perovskite active layer. The obtained c-HATNA film possesses very good water resistance and thermal stability, enabling the realization of stable PVSC (ITO/NiO/CH₃NH₃PbI₃/c-HATNA/bis-C₆₀/Ag) without encapsulation. By proper doping to increase its electron-transporting property, a high PCE of 18.21% can be achieved with outstanding ambient and thermal stability. It shows a slight loss (<10%) of PCE

Encouraged by these promising results, we have also fabricated a PVSC using all-crosslinked CTLs by combining it with another crosslinked HTL developed earlier to realize a stable and efficient solar cell. The derived cell with the configuration of ITO/c-TCTA/CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ PbI $ _3 $ /c-HATNA/bis-C $ _{60} $ /Ag can deliver a PCE of 16.08% and 13.42% on rigid and flexible substrates, respectively. These excellent results validate the concept of incorporating a facile cross-linkable CTL in PVSC to improve both its efficiency and ambient and thermal stability.

# Device Fabrication and Measurements

All chemicals and reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used as received without further purification. All solutions are filtered with a 0.45-  $  \mu  $  m PTFE filter before use.   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $   was synthesized according to the reported procedure. For the typical synthesis, 0.3 mol (38 mL) methylamine (  $  CH_{3}NH_{2}  $  ) solution (33 wt.% in absolute ethanol) was reacted with equimolar (40 mL) hydroiodic acid (HI) (57 wt.% in water) with stirring in ice bath for 2 hr to obtain methylammonium iodide (  $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $  ). Crystallization of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $   was achieved using a rotary evaporator at 60°C for 2–3 hr.

All the ITO-coated substrates were cleaned with isopropanol, acetone, and distilled water sequentially, and treated with UV-ozone for 20 min. To prepare NiO HTL, Nickel(II) acetylacetonate was dissolved in ethanol (0.1 mol/L) with the addition of 10   $  \mu  $  L of HCl (38 wt.%) into the solution. The solution was then stirred in a sealed glass vial overnight. ITO-coated glass substrates were cleaned and then UV-ozone treated for 15 min prior to depositing the NiO layer. The NiO layer was then spin-coated onto the ITO-coated glass at 5,000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 350°C for 1 hr in air. To prepare crosslinked HTL, a precursor solution of TCTA-BVP mixed with pentaerythritol tetrakis(3-mercaptopropionate) (PETMP) with a molar ratio of 2.2:1 in chlorobenzene (8 mg/mL of TCTA-BVP) was prepared first. Then the precursor solution (1 mL) was doped by adding 10   $  \mu  $  L of t-butylpyridine (TBP) solution and 10   $  \mu  $  L of Li-bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (Li-TFSI)/acetonitrile (170 mg/mL) additives. After shaking for 1 min, the doped precursor solution was spin-coated on top of perovskite layer at 2,000 rpm for 30 s and 4,000 rpm for 5 s. The resulting films were heated on a hotplate in the nitrogen glovebox at a temperature 150°C for 30 min. After cooling, the film was rinsed with toluene on the surface and then spun at 2,000 rpm for 30 s.

For preparation of the perovskite layer, the precursor solution was prepared by mixing   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $   with lead(II) iodide (  $  PbI_{2}  $  ) at a molar ratio of 1:1.08 in the DMF and DMSO co-solvent (with a volume ratio of 4:1) and stirred at 60°C overnight. The perovskite precursor solution was then spin-coated onto prepared HTL at 1,000 rpm for 15 s and 4,000 rpm for 45 s. During the last 25 s of the second spin-coating step, the substrate was treated with toluene drop-rinsing (0.7 mL). The as-spun films were annealed at 100°C for   $  \sim  $  20 min.

For preparation of the ETL, the HATNASOACRYLATE precursor solution was first mixed with PETMP at a molar ratio of 5:1 in chlorobenzene (15 mg/mL in 1,4-dichlorobenzene). The precursor solution (1 mL) was then doped by adding triethylamine with different concentration (1 wt.%, 3 wt.%, and 5 wt.%, triethylamine to HATNASOACRYLATE). The solution was deposited onto perovskite layer by spin-coating at 2,000 rpm for 30 s and 4,000 rpm for 5 s.
