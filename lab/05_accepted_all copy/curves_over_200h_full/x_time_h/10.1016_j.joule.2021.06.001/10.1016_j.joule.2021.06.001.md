# SUMMARY

With the rapid progress of perovskite solar cells (PSCs), both high efficiency and sufficient stability are required simultaneously for their real-life application, wherein interfaces play an essential role. In this work, we develop sandwiched electrode buffer (SEB) with respect to the hole-transport layer (HTL), wherein dual back surface fields are implemented at two interfaces in relevance. The SEB bridges the absorber to the back electrode with the desired band alignment and multi-defect passivation effects, which stabilize the perovskite, HTL, and metal electrodes. Accordingly, planar n-i-p PSCs with SEB achieve an efficiency of 23.9% (certified 23.4%). Notably, they exhibit a remarkable operational stability with only a 3% efficiency decline for 2,000 h maximum power point tracking under 1-Sun illumination. Furthermore, the devices also show excellent thermal stability and humidity stability. Therefore, the SEB configuration boosts both efficiency and stability of PSCs, which paves the way for the commercialization of perovskite optoelectronics.

# INTRODUCTION

The organic-inorganic hybrid perovskites show superior intrinsic properties as the ideal photovoltaic absorbers, such as large absorption coefficients, small exciton binding energies, long carrier diffusion lengths, efficient photogeneration of free carriers, and high charge-carrier mobilities.  $  ^{1}  $   Thanks to worldwide research efforts recently, the efficiency and stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been improved sequentially.  $  ^{2,3}  $   It is gradually understood that perovskite thin films often exhibit a significant surface recombination to consume photocarriers and to deteriorate device efficiency.  $  ^{4}  $   Subsequently, various defect passivation techniques have been widely applied  $  ^{5,6}  $   to achieve fruitful results with a large category of materials documented, including native/self-passivators (e.g., PbI  $  _{2}  $  , methylammonium iodide, wide-band-gap perovskites),  $  ^{7-9}  $   alkylammonium halogens (e.g., butylammonium, guanidinium, phenethylammonium iodide),  $  ^{10-12}  $   Lewis acids and bases (e.g., fullerene and its derivatives, derivatives of pyridine and thiophene, trioctylphosphine oxide),  $  ^{13-15}  $   and other wide-band-gap materials (e.g., Al  $  _{2}  $  O  $  _{3}  $  , PbSO  $  _{4}  $  , polymers).  $  ^{16-18}  $   However, materials reported so far are limited mostly in terms of insulators, wherein carriers have to tunnel over to contribute to electricity. # Context & scale

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) require both high efficiency and sufficient stability simultaneously for their real-life applications. The back surface field (BSF) technique has been successfully adopted in crystalline silicon solar cells to dominate the photovoltaic market. We start up with this well-established idea of BSF, which is further replenished with passivation and stabilization that is urgently required for PSCs.

A sandwiched electrode buffer (SEB) is proposed to bridge perovskite absorber to metal contact in PSCs, wherein dual BSFs are implemented. This novel SEB adjusts the band alignment and passivates multiple defects for efficient carrier extraction and transport. Moreover, it effectively impedes mass loss and ion migration of the perovskite, protects the HTL from moisture attack, and prevents the electrode from diffusing. This demonstration provides an effective strategy to address both efficiency and stability concerns simultaneously, which pushes forward the commercialization of PSCs.

When reviewing the outcome of photovoltaic research for over half a century, the back surface field (BSF) technique can never be neglected, which has been successfully adopted in crystalline silicon solar cells with over GW production.  $  ^{[19,20]}  $   BSF is used to reduce the recombination of minority carriers at the back surface of silicon absorbers and improve the collection efficiency of photogenerated carriers by electronic doping induced tilted band alignment.  $  ^{[21]}  $   In halide perovskites, electronic doping has been proposed to tune their energy level at the band edge and has been further verified experimentally.  $  ^{[22-28]}  $   It provides applicable toolboxes to further implement the BSF in a perovskite device, but technical challenges remain, mainly as the contact between perovskite absorbers and electrodes is distinguished from the scenario in c-silicon solar cells.

In most high-efficiency PSCs, a layer of electrode buffer (also known as the carrier transport layer) is often inserted to bridge the absorber layer and the electrode, which suppresses carrier recombination at the interface.  $  ^{29}  $   When realizing a stable BSF at the electrode buffer that is distinguished from c-silicon, it requires compatible materials and relevant low-temperature processing due to the soft crystal lattice of halide perovskite. Moreover, while traditional interface engineering mostly focuses only on one side of the interface (e.g., perovskite/electrode buffer), the other side (e.g., electrode buffer/electrodes) is largely unexplored.  $  ^{30}  $   Indeed, these unknowns suggest plenty of possibilities that could address both efficiency and stability concerns simultaneously for the real-life application of PSCs.

In this work, we demonstrate a sandwiched electrode buffer (SEB) as the back contact in the n-i-p planar PSCs (Figure 1A), wherein dual BSFs (d-BSFs) are implemented for the first time. The d-BSF is introduced by employing 2,3,5,6-tetrafluoro-7,7,8,8-tetracyanoquinodimethane (F4-TCNQ, Figure S1) at the two interfaces that are adjacent to the hole-transport layer (HTL) to form a SEB with two BSFs on both sides of the HTL (e.g., BSF1/HTL/BSF2). The SEB bridges the perovskite absorber to the metal contact with desired band alignment and superior multi-defect passivation for efficient carrier extraction and transport, leading to resultant planar PSCs with an efficiency of up to 23.9% (certified 23.4%) on an aperture area of 0.102 cm². Moreover, device stability is enhanced significantly because of the stabilization effects at both interfaces. At the perovskite interface, it forms multiple bonds to impede mass loss and ion migration. At the electrode interface, it protects the HTL from moisture attack and prevents the electrode from diffusing. The corresponding devices exhibited a remarkable operational stability with only a 3% PCE decline for 2,000 h under 1-Sun equivalent white LED illumination and maximum power point (MPP) tracking at room temperature. Overall, SEB not only adjusts the interfacial energy band structure but also passivates the defects and suppresses ion migration and electrode diffusion simultaneously, which shows great potential to improve device efficiency and stability. It suggests that the ultra-high efficiency and stability of PSCs have been achieved simultaneously, which addresses one major concern for their commercialization.

We first investigated the energy band alignment at the perovskite and HTL surfaces along with BSF introduction and conducted ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) measurement. F4-TCNQ is a strong electron acceptor, which is expected to change the work function of the perovskite and HTL to introduce BSF at the surfaces in touch. It showed that F4-TCNQ doping increased the work function of the perovskite surface from 4.17 to 4.47 eV (Figure 1C), and that of the spiro-OMeTAD from 4.63 to 5.09 eV (Figure 1D). Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) provides a reliable measurement of the local surface potential stemming from contact potential differences (CPD) between the tip and the sample surface, which actually reflects their work functions.  $  ^{31}  $   Compared with the reference, the perovskite thin film with BSF showed a significantly increased surface potential from   $  \sim  $  117 to 324 mV, indicating a deeper work function (Figure S3). The HTL with F4-TCNQ also showed an increased surface potential from   $  \sim  $  375 to 480 mV. In addition, the sample with F4-TCNQ showed the narrowest distribution, indicating that the introduction of d-BSF may be helpful in improving the device yield for mass production. The energy difference between the valence band (VB) and the Fermi level (  $  E_{F}  $  ) for the perovskite surface with F4-TCNQ decreased from 1.20 to 0.90 eV, which confirms the p-type doping. Similarly, the   $  E_{F}  $   for the HTL with F4-TCNQ treatment shifted close to the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) (from 0.60 to 0.14 eV). Consequently, the conductivity of the perovskite films also increased from   $  7.91 \times 10^{-4}  $   to   $  5.01 \times 10^{-3}  $   S/cm after F4-TCNQ treatment (Table S1), and that of the spiro-OMeTAD from   $  3.92 \times 10^{-5}  $   to   $  9.74 \times 10^{-5}  $   eV. The increase in conductivity is also indicative of an increasing carrier density within the films, which confirms the p-type doping effect of F4-TCNQ on the perovskite and the HTL.

Based on this finding, schematic energy band diagrams for the SEB with the introduction of the d-BSF are displayed in Figures 1E and 1F. The introduction of F4-TCNQ creates the BSF by p-type doping effects at both perovskite and HTL surfaces, which results in a greater upward band bending at the corresponding interfaces. Specifically, owing to the deep lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) level of F4-TCNQ, electrons in the VB of perovskite can be thermally activated into the LUMO level of F4-TCNQ, which leaves holes in the perovskite and thus causes its p-type doping.  $  ^{32}  $   Hence, the perovskites are partially doped by F4-TCNQ with positive charges at the interface, possibly providing an electric field in the same direction as that of the built-in potential (  $  V_{Bi}  $  ) in the device during operation. This greater upward band bending promotes the extraction of photogenerated holes to the HTL and pushes electrons away from the perovskite/HTL interface, which should reduce the interface charge recombination and thus increase the open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) in the device. In crystalline silicon solar cells, the BSF functions to rebound the electrons back to the absorber. The same principle is followed in our SEB-based PSCs; that is, the p-doping region at the back constitutes a barrier to the movement of electrons to the back contact. It is widely observed that the hole injection barrier (  $  \Phi_{h}  $  ) often exists at the organic-metal interface due to the "push effect," which can be modulated by appropriate electron acceptor intercalation.  $  ^{33,34}  $   For the HTL/electrode interface here, the role of F4-TCNQ is to provide spacers between the metal electrode and organic HTL to mitigate the "push effect" that affects the Fermi level, which does not essentially change the work function of the metal electrode.  $  ^{33}  $   Instead, it changes the work function of the organic HTL. The nature of the HTL/electrode contact is best demonstrated by J-V characteristics for single-carrier devices.  $  ^{30}  $   For Li-TFSI and tBP-doped spiro-OMeTAD, literature reported that no ohmic contact could be observed in the absence of FK209.  $  ^{36}  $   As shown in Figure S4, the hole-only device of ITO/PE-DOT:PSS/spiro-OMeTAD/Ag presented diode-like J-V characteristics, resembling those of a Schottky-type barrier contact for hole injection from the Ag electrode into the spiro-OMeTAD, and hole extraction from spiro-OMeTAD to Ag. # The BSF passivates the absorber

We found the introduction of the BSF at the perovskite surface can passivate the absorber effectively. The TRPL measurements were conducted, and the results (Figure 1G) show that the perovskite/BSF1 sample exhibits a longer carrier lifetime than that of the reference. It is consistent with the steady-state PL measurement (Figure 1H), wherein the enhanced PL intensity and PL decay lifetime were observed. It indicates that the nonradiative recombination of photogenerated carriers has been largely suppressed by surface passivation due to induced BSF.

To gather more atomistic information regarding the interaction between perovskite and BSF1 and its effect on the passivation, we performed a first-principles density functional theory (DFT) simulation. We considered (001) FAI-terminated FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  surface with three typical point defects, FA vacancies (V  $  _{FA}  $ ), I vacancies (V  $  _{I}  $ ), and I interstitials (l  $  _{i}  $ ) in touch with F4-TCNQ units, as shown in Figures 2A–2C, Note S2, and Table S3. The green cloud denotes the electron loss from the relative bonds or atoms after charge redistribution, while the yellow cloud means the electrons trapped by the relative atoms. There is charge transfer between surface I (in perovskites) and -C≡N (in F4-TCNQ), suggesting a strong interaction between I and N. This bonding restricts the movement/vibration of I to form I vacancies potentially. It also observes strong interaction between FA (in perovskites) and N (in F4-TCNQ), as well as that between N-H (in perovskites) and F (in F4-TCNQ), which restricts FA escape.

We performed X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) in order to clarify the interaction between F4-TCNQ and the perovskite (Note S3 and Figure S8). Unfortunately, this chemical interaction is expected to convolute shifts in XPS peaks in addition to shifts due to band bending (see Note S3 for detailed discussion). To get more experimental evidence of the interaction, we carried out Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) characterization accordingly. Compared with the reference, the FTIR spectrum of the perovskite/F4-TCNQ film (Figure 2D) exhibited a substantial peak shift of the N–H vibration modes toward a lower wavenumber (3,408.1 to 3,405.2 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  ). We attribute this shift to the formation of hydrogen bonds (N–H  $  \cdots  $  F and N–H  $  \cdots  $  N) between the FA/MA (in perovskite) and F/N (in F4-TCNQ) species.  $  ^{38}  $   In addition, as shown in Figure 2E, the shift of the C≡N vibration in F4-TCNQ to lower wavenumbers is indicative of the interaction between F4-TCNQ and perovskite (FA, Pb, and/or I). The shift of the C≡N vibration to lower wavenumbers means longer C≡N bonds, indicating that N atoms in F4-TCNQ are offset toward Pb and I atoms in perovskites, which results in higher binding energies of Pb and I. # The SEB stabilizes the device

The device configuration with SEB shows excellent stability, which is attributed to the following aspects. First, due to these newly formed halogen and hydrogen bonds between F4-TCNQ and the perovskite, the formation energies of typical surface defects significantly increased from negative values to positive values upon adsorbing the F4-TCNQ, as compared with the pristine perovskite (Figure 3A). That is to say, the BSF restricts the escape of organic cations and halogen anions. To prove this effect of SEB on the perovskite, we aged perovskite/F4-TCNQ films at 85°C for 192 h in a nitrogen glove box to observe the negligible change in the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns as well as the UV-vis absorption spectra (Figure S9). In contrast, the perovskite without BSF passivation differed substantially from the fresh perovskite sample (Figure 3B), wherein the relative content of PbI₂ increased obviously, indicating the decomposition of perovskite. The improvement in thermal stability of the perovskite sample with BSF is possibly attributed to the hydrogen bonds between the N–H (in MA/FA ions) and F/N (in F4-TCNQ) that inhibit the diffusion and dissociation of organic cations. DFT simulation results show that the I ions migration activation energy significantly increased from 0.66 to 2.75 eV after F4-TCNQ passivation, as shown in Figure 3C. The mapping signal of I in spiro-OMeTAD is counted and presented by pixel ratio in Figures 3D and 3E. The HTL in the aged reference was already filled with I, whereas only a low signal intensity was observed in the aged perovskite/F4-TCNQ sample. # Improved photovoltaic performance

To investigate the photovoltaic performance of SEB devices, we introduced the BSF on the perovskite surface and the HTL surface, respectively (see Note S4 for details). The detailed performance parameters of cells with different BSF configurations are summarized in Table 1. BSF at the perovskite/HTL interface improved the mean PCE of the cells from  $ 21.06\% $  to  $ 22.56\% $ , and the mean PCE of the cells further increased to  $ 23.53\% $  when BSF2 was further applied. The enhancement of PCE is attributed to the significant improvement in  $ V_{OC} $  and fill factor (FF), while  $ J_{SC} $  increased slightly (the corresponding external quantum efficiency [EQE] spectra together with integrated  $ J_{SC} $  are shown in Figure S11). The SEB helps block electrons from recombination and facilitates transporting holes, which is responsible for high  $ V_{OC} $  and FF.

To further illustrate the effect of BSF, we doped the HTL with F4-TCNQ in bulk and fabricated photovoltaic devices subsequently (see Note S5 for details). However, the PCE of devices with bulk doping HTL was obviously lower than that of the BSF-based devices (Table 1). As shown in Figure 4A, the best device with SEB produces a PCE of  $ 23.92\% $ , with a  $ \mathrm{V_{OC}} $  of  $ 1.16\mathrm{V} $ , a  $ J_{SC} $  of  $ 26.14\mathrm{mA/cm^2} $ , and an FF of  $ 78.76\% $ . The best device achieved a certified efficiency of  $ 23.4\% $  at an accredited photovoltaic laboratory (the PV Metrology Lab of National Institute of Metrology, China, Figure S12). By holding a bias near the maximum power output point  $ (0.98\mathrm{V}) $ , we obtained a stabilized photocurrent of  $ 23.98\mathrm{mA/cm^2} $ , corresponding to a stabilized efficiency of  $ 23.50\% $  (the inset in Figure 4A). The universality of our approach was demonstrated by applying the SEB strategy to different metal back electrodes (Au, Ag, and Cu) and HTL (spiro-OMeTAD, PTAA, and P3HT). We observe enhanced device performance for all materials combinations upon SEB introduction, as listed in Table S4.

# Conclusions

In summary, we have developed a SEB device configuration to bridge the perovskite absorber to the metal contact. It features the desired band alignment due to a dual BSF, and superior multi-defect passivation for efficient carrier extraction and transport. By employing this strategy, we have achieved a PCE of up to 23.9% (certified 23.4%) in the resultant device. More importantly, it forms a stabilized device configuration of perovskite/SEB/electrode at the back contact via multiple chemical bonding (halogen and hydrogen bonds between perovskite and SEB, -CN···Au/Ag/Cu linking between SEB and electrode), which effectively impedes the mass loss and ion migration during device operation. The SEB devices, therefore, exhibited remarkable operational stability with only a 3% PCE decline for 2,000 h under 1-Sun illumination and MPP tracking. Furthermore, these devices also showed excellent thermal and humidity stability. This work suggests a general device layout to fabricate ultra-highly efficient and stable PSCs and other optoelectronic devices.

# Fabrication

The ITO glass substrates were sequentially cleaned using detergent, ultra-pure water, and isopropanol. After 5 min of   $  O_{2}  $  -plasma treatment, the   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloidal solution (2.5 wt %) was spin coated on the ITO substrate at 4,000 rpm for 30 s, and annealed on a hot plate at the displayed temperature of 150°C for 30 min in ambient air. The   $  PbI_{2}  $   precursor solution was prepared in a mixed solvent of DMF and DMSO with a volume ratio of 890:110, containing   $  PbI_{2}  $   (1.5 M) and CsI (0.015 M). The   $  PbI_{2}  $   film was spin coated onto   $  SnO_{2}  $   at 1,500 rpm for 30 s, then annealed at 70°C for 1 min. For perovskite film deposition, a solution of FAI:MAI:MACl (90 mg:2 mg:9 mg in 1 ml IPA) was spin coated onto the   $  PbI_{2}  $   film at 1,900 rpm for 30 s. The substrate was then immediately transferred to a hot plate and was heated at 150°C for 15 min under ambient air conditions (30%–40% relative humidity). After cooling down to room temperature, the F4-TCNQ/IPA solution (0–1.5 mg/mL, Figures S17–S19) was spin coated onto the perovskite surface at 4,000 rpm for 30 s, followed by thermal annealing at 100°C for 1 min. Then the hole-transport layer was subsequently deposited on top of the perovskite film by spin coating at 3,000 rpm for 30 s using a spiro-OMeTAD/CB solution, which consisted of spiro-OMeTAD (72.3 mg), tBP (30 μL), Li-TFSI/ACN (35 μL, 260 mg/mL), and CB (1 mL). For F4-TCNQ treatment, the F4-TCNQ molecule (0–1.5 nm, Figures S20 and S21) was deposited on top of the spiro-OMeTAD film by thermal evaporation (evaporating speed: 0.1 Å/s, vacuum condition: <1 × 10  $  ^{-4}  $   Pa). Finally, 200 nm metal contact (Au, Ag, or Cu) was deposited by thermal evaporation. For perovskite:F4-TCNQ samples, we introduced F4-TCNQ (0–1 mg, Figure S22) into a solution of FAI:MAI:MACl (90 mg:2 mg:9 mg in 1 ml IPA) to fabricate perovskite films. For spiro-OMeTAD:F4-TCNQ samples, we doped spiro-OMeTAD with F4-TCNQ in bulk, which consisted of spiro-OMeTAD (72.3 mg), F4-TCNQ (0–0.15 mg, Figures S23 and S24), tBP (30 μL), Li-TFSI (35 μL, 260 mg/ml in ACN), and CB (1 mL). For the cell using PTAA or P3HT as the hole-transport layer, the PTAA or P3HT was dissolved in CB (20 mg/mL) with additives of 16 μL tBP and 30 μL Li-TFSI/ACN (170 mg/mL). The other process can refer to the fabrication process of the cell using spiro-OMeTAD as the hole-transport material.

# DFT calculation

All calculations were based on density functional theory (DFT)  $  ^{47}  $  as implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP)  $  ^{48,49}  $  code with projector augmented wave (PAW) method.  $  ^{50-52}  $  The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) and van der Waals correction proposed by the Grimme potential (D2) were employed.  $  ^{53}  $  The migration barriers were calculated using the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method,  $  ^{54,55}  $  which is an efficient way to find saddle point configuration as implemented in VASP through the VTST tools. The plane-wave basis cutoff energy was set to be 400 eV. All structures were relaxed until the residual force on each atom less than 0.01 eV/  $  \AA  $ . The self-consistent convergence accuracy was set at  $  10^{-5}  $  eV/atom in the structural calculation, and  $  10^{-7}  $  eV/atom in the CI-NEB calculation.
