# Dual Role of Cu-Chalcogenide as Hole-Transporting Layer and Interface Passivator for p–i–n Architecture Perovskite Solar Cell

Anupam Sadhu, Monika Rai, Teddy Salim, Xin Jin, Joel Ming Rui Tan, Shin Woei Leow, Mahmoud G. Ahmed, Shlomo Magdassi, Subodh G. Mhaisalkar, and Lydia Helena Wong*

Inorganic hole-transport layers (HTLs) are widely investigated in perovskite solar cells (PSCs) due to their superior stability compared to the organic HTLs. However, in p–i–n architecture when these inorganic HTLs are deposited before the perovskite, it forms a suboptimal interface quality for the crystallization of perovskite, which reduces device stability, causes recombination, and limits the power conversion efficiency of the device. The incorporation of an appropriate functional group such as sulfur-terminated surface on the HTL can enhance the interface quality due to its interaction with perovskite during the crystallization process. In this work, a bifunctional Al-doped CuS film is wet-deposited as HTL in p–i–n architecture PSC, which besides acting as an HTL also improves the crystallization of perovskite at the interface. Urbach energy and light intensity versus open-circuit voltage characterization suggest the formation of a better-quality interface in the sulfide HTL–perovskite heterojunction. The degradation behavior of the sulfide-HTL-based perovskite devices is studied, where it can be observed that after 2 weeks of storage in a controlled environment, the devices retain close to 95% of their initial efficiency.

The current global environmental crisis is beseeching for the replacement of the conventional carbon-based energy sources with cleaner and renewable forms of energy. Among the various available clean energy sources, solar energy is the most promising one. Even though silicon solar cell technology has become very cost-effective over the last few years, the energy budget for its production and initial investment remains high.  $  ^{[1]}  $   Perovskite solar cells (PSCs), on the other hand, whose efficiency has reached almost the efficiency of the conventional silicon solar cells, can be fabricated at a much lower cost and energy budget.  $  ^{[2]}  $   However, researchers in the perovskite community are yet to solve many challenges such as poor long-term stability, high interface recombination, and uneconomical charge-transport layers before PSC could be employed for large-scale commercial applications.  $  ^{[2b,3]}  $

The efficiency and stability of a PSC are governed not only by the perovskite layer itself but also by other auxiliary layers like the electron-transport layer (ETL) and hole-transport layer (HTL), both of which have a very strong impact on those properties.  $  ^{[4]}  $   Besides facilitating the charge transport, ETL and HTL can also govern the crystallization of perovskite, if it is used as the bottom transport layer. When employed as the top transport layer, ETL/HTL can passivate the surface defects of the absorber and act as a protective barrier layer for the perovskite layer against humidity and oxygen.  $  ^{[5]}  $   Therefore, finding the appropriate top transport layer and bottom transport layer might determine the fate of the future-generation PSC. Replacing some of the commonly used organic ETLs and HTLs with inorganic ETLs and HTLs has been shown to improve the stability of the device due to the higher stability of those inorganic compounds.  $  ^{[6]}  $   However, several reports have pointed out that many oxide-based inorganic ETLs or HTLs tend to form suboptimal interfaces with perovskite, especially when they are used as the bottom transport layer.  $  ^{[7]}  $   To counter this problem, surface treatment of the bottom transport layer with organic molecules has been widely reported as an effective strategy.  $  ^{[8]}  $   One of the commonalities among those interfaces enhancing organic molecules is that many of them have electron-donating functional

The Al 2p peak has not been used directly as evidence of the presence of Al because of the significant overlap between Cu 3p and Al 2p peaks (Figure S2b, Supporting Information). To find out the impact of Al doping on the valence band maxima position of CuS, ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) and XPS characterizations were used. UPS is used to find out the Fermi energy level position with respect to the vacuum energy state, and the XPS is used to investigate the energetic separation between Fermi and valence levels (Figure 2e). The Fermi level position or the work function of the compound is defined as   $  \varphi = h\nu - E_{s}  $  , where   $  \varphi  $   is the work function, h  $  \nu  $   is the energy of the incident UV light which is 21.22 eV in this case, and   $  E_{s}  $   is the secondary electron cutoff, shown in Figure 2e (left) image. Using the information from Figure 2e, we have calculated the absolute position of the VBM before and after the Al doping, which is shown in Figure 2f. It can be observed that after the Al doping, the VBM position of CuS has come down by 0.13 eV with respect to vacuum and becomes more favorable to the perovskite VBM position. We hypothesize that this downshift of VBM position after Al doping can be due to two reasons: first, the VBM of CuS mainly consists of Cu 3d and S 3p; therefore, reducing the contribution from any of these might reduce the density of state (DOS) at the top of the valance band, which can lower the VBM position. It has been shown in the literature that due to the energetically lower position of Al 3s and 3p as compared to Cu 3d and 4s orbitals, after hybridization with S, the molecular orbitals corresponding to Al form the lower part of the valance band (unlike Cu which contributes the top orbital of valance band).  $  ^{[24]}  $   Therefore, the partial replacement of Cu with Al is expected to increase the DOS toward the lower part of the valance band and reduce the DOS at the top part of the valance band, leading to the deeper VBM position. The second reason might be that the Al atoms reside in the interstitial positions leading to the increase in the Cu–S bond length, which has been reported to generate deeper VBM due to the change in the hybridization behavior.  $  ^{[24]}  $   Even though the change in VBM position after the doping of Al is not very drastic, this slightly improved band alignment should help in more efficient charge transfer in the HTL–perovskite interface.

Figure 3a and Figure S5 (Supporting Information) show the XRD of perovskites deposited on different sulfide and oxide HTLs. The perovskite layers deposited on Al–CuS and CuS showed higher diffraction intensity of (110) and (220) peaks situated at   $  14.3^{\circ}  $   and   $  28.6^{\circ}  $  , respectively. To further understand the crystallinity of perovskite on different HTLs, the full width at half maximum (FWHM) values (fitted by Pseudo-Voigt function) of the XRD peaks for several planes were calculated (Table S3, Supporting Information). It is observed that the perovskite XRD peaks on sulfide HTLs have lower FWHM as compared to that on NiO, indicating better crystallinity of

perovskite on sulfide HTLs. Additionally, the intensity ratio between the (110) and (200) peaks is calculated to be 2.6, 3.8, and 3.6 for NiO, CuS, and, Al–CuS, respectively. This indicates more directional growth of perovskite on sulfur-based substrates in the   $  \langle110\rangle  $   direction, which might be due to a more favorable growth environment due to the presence of sulfur.  $  ^{[25]}  $   Yin et al. have shown that preferential growth of perovskite in the   $  \langle110\rangle  $   direction can improve the hole injection to the HTL, which can be beneficial for charge collection.  $  ^{[26]}  $   Here it can be seen that sulfur-based surface can naturally promote the directional growth of perovskite, therefore, leading to a better interface charge transfer without increasing the cost or complexity of the fabrication process. We also calculate the FWHM of the   $  PbI_{2}  $   001 peak (at   $  12.8^{\circ}  $     $  2\theta  $  ) for the perovskite films on all three HTLs (Table S3, Supporting Information). Interestingly, we notice that the   $  PbI_{2}  $   FWHM is lower in sulfide HTLs as compared to the oxide HTL. This indicates that the interaction of Pb–S not only leads to better perovskite crystallization but also leads to higher crystallinity of   $  PbI_{2}  $  . This might be the reason for the increased intensity of   $  PbI_{2}  $   peaks in the perovskite films on the sulfide HTLs. It should also be emphasized that the   $  PbI_{2}  $   peak merely indicates the presence of crystalline   $  PbI_{2}  $   in the film whereas quantifying the total free   $  PbI_{2}  $   (crystalline + amorphous) is very challenging. Additionally, considering that 10% excess   $  PbI_{2}  $   is added in the precursor solution, a significant amount of unconverted   $  PbI_{2}  $   is bound to stay in the film in some form or another. However, there is still a lack of clear understanding about which form of   $  PbI_{2}  $   (amorphous and crystalline) has a higher impact on the stability or efficiency of the device. Figure 4a and Figure S10b (Supporting Information) show the J-V curve of the optimized champion perovskite devices with CuS, Al-CuS, and NiO as HTL. It can be observed that Al-CuS HTL-based devices outperform CuS-based devices in terms of power conversion efficiency (PCE). The higher PCE of the Al-CuS device compared to the CuS device mainly comes from its higher short-circuit current density, which is   $  16.96 \, mA cm^{-2}  $   for Al-CuS as compared to   $  15.85 \, mA cm^{-2}  $   for CuS. We believe this improvement is due to the better band alignment of perovskite with Al-CuS as discussed earlier. The incident photon to current efficiency (IPCE) graph shown in Figure S10a (Supporting Information) also follows a similar trend to the J-V curve; an overall improvement in the conversion efficiency is observed in the CuS device after the Al doping, indicating improved charge collection with Al doping. Despite the better quality interface, the efficiency of the CuS- and Al-CuS-based devices remained slightly lower as compared to the standard NiO-based devices, which might be due to the better band alignment of NiO with perovskite.  $  ^{[32]}  $   We expect that the efficiency of the devices can further be improved by more rigorous rounds of optimization of the whole device fabrication process and better band alignment at the HTL/perovskite interface. The light-intensity-dependent   $  V_{oc}  $   was measured to calculate the ideality factor of the devices (Figure 4b). It is observed that the ideality factors of the Al-CuS-, CuS-, and NiO HTL-based devices are 1.32, 1.39, and 1.15,

respectively. In common terms, a lower ideality factor is considered to be the reflection of a better quality device. However, this simplification is based on several assumptions such as all the recombinations are taking place from the same carrier reservoir, and the electron concentration is the same as the hole concentration in every part of the absorber. These assumptions do not hold when there are phenomena like band bending or the presence of different kinds of trap states in the absorber. Caprioglio et al. showed that the ideality factor of perovskite devices should be close to 2 considering that the kind of photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) value is reported in perovskite. They pointed out that the presence of heavy interface recombination can reduce the ideality factor value of the device to close to 1. The study also shows that with interface treatment both the ideality factor and device performance increase due to the reduction in the interfacial recombination.[33] Here, we see that the perovskite bulk quality is quite similar for all three HTLs (except for the more directional growth in sulfide HTLs); however, the interface (Urbach energy) qualities of the perovskite in CuS and Al–CuS are better compared to NiO. Hence, the higher ideality factor of CuS and Al–CuS is more likely to re-emphasize the better-quality perovskite–HTL interface. Very interestingly, the ideality factor of the device has not been dramatically affected by the suboptimal band alignment between perovskite and Al–CuS (and CuS), which otherwise would have reduced the ideality factor very close to 1 due to heavy interfacial recombination.[33b] Therefore, it might be possible that the dependency of the ideality factor of the device is significantly more on the quality of the interface as compared to the interfacial band alignment. However, a significant reduction in the ideality factor (<1) can be observed toward the higher intensity part of the graph (>0.6 sun for CuS and Al–CuS). This low value of the ideality factor has been reported previously and has been commonly linked to the energetically misalignment in the interface.[34]

# 3. Conclusions

In conclusion, we have developed a facile low-temperature-processed Al-doped CuS as a novel HTL for inverted (p–i–n) architecture perovskite solar cells. Al doping has been incorporated to improve the band alignment of CuS with perovskite, which ultimately helps in the improvement of the PCE. XPS study shows the presence of interaction between Pb and S ions in the perovskite–sulfide HTL interface. As a result of this interaction, a better-quality interface in sulfide-HTL-based device is observed through Urbach energy and ideality factor measurements. Due to this improved interface quality, sulfide-HTL-based devices outperform oxide-HTL-based devices in terms of stability. This study shows that the Al–CuS is not only a stable and economically viable HTL but can also play the role of an interface passivator, which will eliminate the need for any additional defect passivation layer, thus making sulfide-based materials very appealing for HTL application in perovskite solar cells. We believe that with the use of a better energetically aligned metal sulfide compound, the improved interface property of sulfide HTLs will lead to higher efficiency and improved stability of perovskite solar cells.

# 4. Experimental Section

CuS and Al–CuS Layer Synthesis and Optimization: CuS and Al–CuS layers were deposited by the chemical bath deposition process. In this work,   $  Na_{2}EDTA  $   was used as the complexing agent,   $  CuSO_{4}  $   as the source of copper ion, thioacetamide as the source of sulfur ion, and   $  \mathrm{Al(NO_{3})_{3}}  $   as the Al source. First, the 0.1 M solution of   $  Na_{2}EDTA  $ ,   $  CuSO_{4}  $ , and thioacetamide was prepared by dissolving the respective precursors in the Deionized (DI) water. The mixtures were shaken and kept for 1 h at room temperature for the formation of a clear solution. The CBD solution was prepared by mixing   $  CuSO_{4}  $ ,   $  \mathrm{Al(NO_{3})_{3}}  $ ,   $  Na_{2}EDTA  $ , and thioacetamide successively in different ratios as discussed latter; the total volume of the solution was always made to be 45 mL by diluting with water as per requirement. All the CBD depositions were carried out at room temperature. The substrates were kept in the horizontal position for a certain amount of time in the CBD solution. After taking out the substrates from the CBD solution, they were washed thoroughly with DI water and then dried with compressed air. Finally, to complete the HTL thin-film fabrication, the films were annealed at 90 °C for 30 min.

Optimization for CuS Film Deposition: First, the complexing agent (  $  Na_{2}EDTA  $  ) optimization was done for depositing the CuS layer. The ratio between   $  CuSO_{4}  $   and   $  Na_{2}EDTA  $   in the solution was varied from 20:20 to 20:3, and it was observed that the best device performance was obtained when the ratio between   $  CuSO_{4}  $   and   $  Na_{2}EDTA  $   was 20:12. Hence, this ratio was maintained while carrying out other optimizations. Next, the solution concentration was optimized, and it was observed that the devices performed the best when the concentrations of   $  CuSO_{4}  $ ,   $  Na_{2}EDTA  $ , and thioacetamide were   $  4 \times 10^{-3}  $ ,   $  2.4 \times 10^{-3}  $ , and   $  4 \times 10^{-3}  $   M, respectively. Therefore, for obtaining the CuS film for device fabrication, these precursor concentrations were used, and the optimized deposition duration was 8 min.

Optimization for Al–CuS Film Deposition: For Al doping,   $  \mathrm{Al(NO_{3})_{3}}  $   solution (0.1 M) was added to the   $  CuSO_{4}  $   solution in different ratios, on which   $  Na_{2}EDTA  $   and thioacetamide solutions (0.1 M) were added

subsequently. For Al doping optimization (Figure 1c), the total Cu + Al precursor concentration in the solution was kept constant at   $  44 \times 10^{-3}  $   M; therefore, when the Al concentration in the solution increased, the Cu concentration decreased by the same amount. The concentrations of   $  Na_{2}EDTA  $   and thioacetamide were kept constant at   $  11 \times 10^{-3}  $   and   $  44 \times 10^{-3}  $   M, respectively. After the Al optimization, it was found that   $  22 \times 10^{-3}  $   M   $  \mathrm{Al(NO_{3})_{3}} + 22 \times 10^{-3}  $   M   $  CuSO_{4}  $   in the precursor solution gave the best device efficiency (Figure 1c). Thereafter, for subsequent optimizations of complexing agent concentration and solution concentration, the ratio between Cu, Al, and S precursors was kept constant to keep the Al concentration constant in the deposited film. After all round of optimizations, the optimized concentrations of   $  CuSO_{4}  $  ,   $  \mathrm{Al(NO_{3})_{3}}  $  ,   $  Na_{2}EDTA  $  , and thioacetamide were found to be   $  2 \times 10^{-3}  $  ,   $  2 \times 10^{-3}  $  ,   $  1 \times 10^{-3}  $  , and   $  4 \times 10^{-3}  $   M, respectively, and the optimized deposition duration was 8 min.

NiO Film Fabrication: NiO precursor solution was prepared by mixing   $  0.1 \times 10^{-3}  $   M Ni acetate and   $  0.1 \times 10^{-3}  $   M ethanolamine in 1 mL of ethanol. The solution was kept for stirring at a temperature of 70 °C for a few hours. The change in the solution from bluish to green was observed after some time. The solution was cooled down before using it for spin coating. For the deposition of the NiO layer, the cleaned substrates were placed in the spin coater holder, where the previously prepared solution was dripped in the amount of 50 μL. The spin coating was started immediately after the solution was dripped on the substrate, and it was observed that with the delay between the spinning start and solution dipping even for few seconds rough surfaces might form. Finally, the NiO film was annealed at 325 °C for 30 min in ambient conditions.

Perovskite Device Fabrication: First, FTO substrates were partially etched by the use of HCl and zinc. After etching, the substrates were cleaned by ultrasonication in a soap and ethanol bath subsequently. After that, the substrates were dried with compressed air. Next, UV–ozone treatment was done on the substrate to remove any organic residue from the FTO surface. HTL was deposited on the FTO according to the previously mentioned processes. After depositing the HTL, the substrates were transferred to the glovebox for the deposition of subsequent layers. Perovskite solution was prepared by mixing 1.1 M PbI  $  _{2}  $  , 0.22 M PbBr  $  _{2}  $  , 1 M FAI, and 0.2 M MABr in 1 mL of 4:1 dimethylformamide/dimethyl sulfoxide (DMF/DMSO) solvent (10% excess PbI  $  _{2}  $  /PbBr  $  _{2}  $   was taken). The mixture was kept in a hotplate at 70 °C until a clear solution was formed. After that 42.4 μL of 1.5 M CsI solution (in DMSO) was added to the previously prepared FA–MA–Pb–I–Br solution. This perovskite precursor solution was left in the hotplate at 70 °C for 30 min. Two-stage spin coating was used for perovskite coating; for the first 10 s, the speed was kept at 2000 rpm to facilitate the proper spreading of the solution, and the rotation speed for the next 30 s was set at 6000 rpm. For coating the film, 40 μL of perovskite precursor solution was dropped onto the substrate, and rotation was initiated instantly. Finally, 120 μL of antisolvent (chlorobenzene) was dropped on top of the substrate 5 s before the rotation being finished. After that, the substrates were transferred to the hotplate to be annealed at 100 °C for 30 min. Next, 20 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   of PCBM solution (in chlorobenzene) was used to spin-coat the PCBM layer at a speed of 1500 rpm for 40 s. The PCBM layer was annealed at 95 °C for 15 min. BCP layer was deposited by spin coating 0.5 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   of BCP solution (in Isopropyl alcohol) at 5000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, a 100 nm Ag layer was deposited by thermal evaporation to complete the device.

Characterization and Measurements: UV–vis spectroscopy was done by using a UV-3600 (Shimadzu) spectrophotometer. XRD characterization was carried out by a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation. The XRD of CuS and Al–CuS was carried out in GIXRD mode with the incident angle of 1°. The XRD of the perovskite film was done by using standard gonio scan mode. XPS and UPS were carried out by using AXIS Supra XPS instrument (Kratos Analytical Ltd, UK).
