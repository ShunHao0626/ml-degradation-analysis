# 1. Introduction

Methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI $ _3 $ ) films with three-dimensional (3D) bulk structures have been studied mainly as

In this study, both one-dimensional (1D) and three-dimensional (3D) methylammonium lead iodide  $ (\mathrm{MAPbI}_3) $  were successfully synthesized from the same starting precursor solution using a one-step in situ spin-coating process assisted by antisolvent washing with different ethereal solvents. Antisolvent washing with diethyl ether (DE) induced isotropic growth, resulting in dense 3D  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  films. However, washing with dibutyl ether (DB) having complementary properties promoted 1D directional growth to avoid steric hindrance in the DB/perovskite complex. Antisolvent DB having a lower vapor pressure than DE retarded the vaporization of dimethylformamide and facilitated the growth of larger grains with fewer defects through the slow crystallization. It was also found that long-chain induced 1D directional growth creates an internal compressive strain in the 1D NW film, which plays an important role in charge carrier dynamics. To compare the power conversion efficiency (PCE) and charge transport dynamics, planar solar cells based on both 1D and 3D perovskites were fabricated. 1D NW perovskite based devices achieved a longer lifetime and faster carrier transport owing to the limited ion migration in the presence of compressive strain, although the PCE  $ (15.73\%) $  of the 1D NW-based device was lower than that  $ (18.73\%) $  of the 3D bulk-based device due to inevitable pore incorporation. The improved charge carrier dynamics of 1D NW  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  can offer potential advantages in the development of optoelectronic devices.

In addition, perovskite-structured films with various dimensionalities and morphologies, such as zero-dimensional (0D) quantum dots (QDs) [8], one-dimensional (1D) nanowires (NWs) [9-20] and two-dimensional (2D) nanosheets [21] have gained special interests owing to the unique properties resulting from the low dimensionality. Among them, 1D NW perovskites reported to exhibit specific advantages such as their low number of crystal grain boundaries, low defect concentration, long carrier lifetime, and long carrier diffusion length [15,22]. However, there are limited reports on the reasons why 1D NW perovskites show superior properties compared to 3D counterparts, because there have been no reports for the synthesis of perovskite with same composition but different dimensions.

To date, two general methods have been reported for synthesizing perovskite NWs. One technique is the template-assisted synthesis of 1D NWs using anodic alumina [23] and nanofluidic channels [24] that serve as a template for guided 1D NW crystallization. The other technique is the synthesis of perovskite NWs by a solution method, which involves a two-step dissolution and recrystallization process [17]. The solution method is more convenient than the template method because it is simpler and is conducted at lower temperatures rather than using a complex template. However, the perovskite NWs reported thus far have several drawbacks because they were synthesized by a solution process that relied entirely on a two-step process. The reproducibility of the two-step process is very low because dissolution induced by the dimethylformamide (DMF) solvent severely damages the perovskite grains. In addition, the complex two-step process has the disadvantage of not being able to directly compare the characteristics of 1D and 3D structures because it is not possible to form 1D and 3D perovskites of the same composition due to the chemical changes between steps in the two-step process.

Herein, we propose a new technique based on a one-step solution process for the reproducible synthesis of 1D NW as well as 3D perovskites using the same starting precursor solution. The key step in the proposed one-step synthesis is an antisolvent washing stage, where the washing agents dibutyl ether (DB) and diethyl ether (DE) with different chain lengths and complementary properties are chosen to precisely control the perovskite morphologies. By the appropriate control of antisolvent agents, we successfully synthesized different perovskite films with the same composition yet different dimensionalities (3D or 1D) and compared the dimensionality dependent photovoltaic as well as charge transport dynamics. The long chain induced 1D NW growth technique offers another advantage of controlling the internal stress of the perovskite films. Because the directional growth of 1D NWs is dominated by the long chains of DB agent acting as templates, the final NWs was strained compressively, which plays an important role in the carrier transport dynamics. To compare the power conversion efficiency (PCE) and charge transport dynamics, planar PSCs with a device architecture of FTO/Li–SnO  $  _{2}  $  /perovskite (1D/3D)/spiro-MeOTAD/Au were fabricated and their PCEs were compared. Although the NW-based PSCs showed lower PCEs than devices with a 3D structure because of pores that provided as a shunt pathway and

recombination sites, the perovskite NWs exhibited superior charge-carrier dynamic properties such as a longer carrier lifetime and fast charge transfer owing to the limited ion migration of 1D NW perovskites in the presence of compressive strain, thereby providing important insights into photocarrier dynamics in 1D perovskite structures for optoelectronic applications.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1. Preparation of Li–SnO  $  _{2}  $  electron-selective layers

PSCs were fabricated on FTO-coated glass substrates (Pilkington, TEC-8), which were patterned using Zn powder and a dilute HCl solution, followed by cleaning with diluted Hellmanex, acetone, ethanol, and deionized water. To eliminate organic residues, the FTO-coated glass substrates were subjected to ultraviolet–ozone (UVO; Omniscience, UVC-150, wavelength: 184.9 nm, 253.7 nm) treatment for 15 min prior to use. Li–SnO  $  _{2}  $  was prepared as an electron-selective layer (ESL) on the FTO glass by spin coating an ethanolic solution of 0.1 M tin(II) chloride dihydrate and 1.7 mM lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (Li-TFSI) (Sigma–Aldrich) at 3000 rpm for 30 s [25]. After drying at 100 °C for 1 min, the sample was annealed at 185 °C for 3 h on a hotplate. The Li–SnO  $  _{2}  $  films were subsequently treated with UVO for 25 min.

# 2.2. Synthesis of 1D and 3D MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  solar cells

To synthesize 1D and 3D MAPbI $ _{3} $  perovskite, the perovskite precursor solution (50 wt.%) was prepared by dissolving lead(II) iodide (PbI $ _{2} $ ; TCI) and methylammonium iodide (MAI; GreatCellSolar) in 0.635 mL of DMF (Sigma–Aldrich) and then adding 70  $ \mu $ L of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma–Aldrich) to form the corresponding adducts [26]. The completely dissolved solution was spin-coated onto an Li–SnO $ _{2} $  ESL layer at 4000 rpm for 25 s, and 0.5 mL of DB or 0.5 mL of DE (Sigma–Aldrich) was slowly dripped onto the rotating substrate over a period of 10 s before the surface became turbid because of the rapid vaporization of DMF. The sample was subsequently annealed at 65  $ ^{\circ} $ C for 1 min and then at 100  $ ^{\circ} $ C for 2 min. A hole-selective layer was then coated onto the perovskite layer by spin coating 20  $ \mu $ L of a spiro-MeOTAD solution composed of 80 mg spiro-MeOTAD (1-Material), 28.5  $ \mu $ L of 4-tert-butyl pyridine, 17.5  $ \mu $ L of Li-TFSI (Sigma–Aldrich) solution (520 mg of Li-TSFI in 1 mL of acetonitrile), and 1 mL of chlorobenzene (Sigma–Aldrich) at 4000 rpm for 30 s. An Au electrode was evaporated under 10 $  ^{-6}  $  Torr using a thermal evaporation system.

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Structural properties of 1D perovskite NWs

Fig. S1 in the supporting information (SI) shows a schematic for the formation of 1D and 3D perovskite layers, all of which were synthesized under ambient conditions (20 °C and >45% relative humidity). First, the precursor solution prepared by dissolving PbI  $  _{2}  $  and MAI in DMSO/DMF solvent was spin-coated. To modify the morphology of the final perovskite layers, a controlled amount of solvent washing agent (DE or DB) was applied. Hereafter, MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  perovskite layers washed with DB and DE are designated as DBP and DEP, respectively. When the perovskite films were treated with a solvent washing agent, their color gradually changed to glossy black (DEP) or matte black (DBP), as shown in Fig. S2, indicating different crystallization routes of the perovskite films.

We performed XRD analysis to investigate the effect of different washing agents on the crystal structures and residual strain of the perovskite films. The XRD peaks of DEP/DBP at 14.16/14.42°, 20.06/20.27°, 23.55/23.76°, 24.54/24.79°, 28.50/28.71°, and 31.93/32.14° are indexed to the (110), (112), (211), (202), (220), and (310) planes, respectively [27]. Although similar XRD patterns are observed, all of the peak positions of the DBP films are slightly shifted (~0.1°) toward higher angles compared with the positions of the corresponding peaks in the patterns of the 3D DEP films (Fig. 1b). Such a shift of the XRD peak positions toward higher angles (lower d values) clearly suggests that the lattice of DBP was contracted, thereby suggesting that compressive micro-strain was induced during the directional growth. Basically, the long alkyl chains in DB act like templates during 1D directional growth, so the stress remains in the final 1D NWs. From the previous reports, it has been shown that the existence of residual strain/stress can stabilize the perovskite structure [28,29]. Accordingly, we quantitatively investigated the magnitude of the stress developed during the confined growth of 1D NWs in the presence of DB. We used the Halder-Wagner method, which is based on the equation  $ (\beta^{*}/\delta)^{2} = (1/L)(\beta^{*}/\delta^{2}) + (\varepsilon_{S}/2)^{2} $ , where  $ \beta^{*} = \beta \cos \theta/\lambda $ ,  $ \delta = 2 \sin \theta/\lambda $ ,  $ \beta $  is the full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of the XRD peak,  $ \theta $  is the Bragg angle,  $ \lambda $  is the Cu Kα wavelength (1.5406 Å),  $ L $  is the correlation length (corresponding to the crystalline film), and  $ \varepsilon_{s} $  is the stress parameter [30,31]. To obtain accurate  $ \varepsilon_{s} $  values, we determined the FWHMs by fitting the diffraction peaks with pseudo-Voigt functions and the detailed parameters are presented in Table S1. # 3.2. Formation mechanism of perovskite NWs

At the initial nucleation stage, the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  nuclei begin to form by solvent washing following the isotropic growth mechanism. When washed with an agent with short alkyl chains such as DE, methylammonium cations can be isotopically incorporated into corner-sharing  $ [\mathrm{PbX}_6]^{4-} $  octahedra to form 3D bulk structures, as is well known within the perovskite research community [26]. On the contrary, when washing is performed with an agent with long alkyl chains such as DB, isotropic grain growth is restricted because the long chains promote 1D directional growth, which leads to the formation of 1D NWs. Further 1D growth induces considerable stress in  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  as a result of the directional growth induced by long alkyl chains, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 3a. The growth mechanism of a 1D NW network is critically dependent on the length of alkyl chain in the washing agent because long alkyl chains can prevent the incorporation of perovskite crystals into the 3D bulk structures. In addition, the boiling point and vapor pressure of the washing agents can influence the crystal growth kinetics of perovskites by controlling the evaporation of DMF solvent. The formation of a 1D network structure can be explained by the following three-step growth mechanism (Fig. 3b). In step 1, DB with longer alkyl chains surrounds the perovskite crystal with the aid of van der Waals forces proportional to the alkyl chain length [33]. Such crystals surrounded by long alkyl chains indicate a strong steric hindrance effect between the crystals; thus, isotropic growth is hindered by imposed stress. In step 2, perovskite crystals continue to grow in one direction to avoid steric hindrance and impose stress via the long alkyl chains. In step 3, 1D NW growth is complete and residual DB is completely evaporated from the surface of the perovskite NWs during the subsequent heating process at  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . Following this mechanism, we can induce residual compressive strain inside the 1D NW without using a template, which has not been reported before.

# 3.3. Photovoltaic performance of perovskite NWs

To compare the influence of perovskite morphologies on their PV properties, we fabricated solar cells with an architecture of FTO/Li–SnO  $  _{2}  $  /MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  (DBP,DEP)/spiro-MeOTAD/Au; cross-sectional SEM images of the devices with different absorber layers (DEP and DBP) are shown in Fig. 4. The

device with DEP (Fig. 4a) is extremely dense, with few pinholes; however, the device with DBP (Fig. 4b) contains some inevitable pores due to the interconnected NW morphology, consistent with the results of a previous report [18]. Fig. 4c shows the current density–voltage (J–V) characteristics of one of the best-performing devices; the PV parameters of more than 30 devices fabricated using DEP and DBP are presented in Fig. S6. The device with DEP exhibited the highest PCE of 18.73%, with a short-circuit current density (  $  J_{sc}  $  ) of 22.25 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  , an open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ) of 1.088 V, a fill factor (FF) of 76.65%, and an average efficiency of 16.49 ± 1.32%. In the case of the DBP devices, the best PCE was 15.75%, with a   $  J_{sc}  $   of 22.12 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  ,   $  V_{oc}  $   of 0.955 V, FF of 71.05%, and average efficiency of 13.29 ± 2.95%.

To confirm the   $  J_{sc}  $   values obtained from the J-V curves, the external quantum efficiencies (EQEs) of the corresponding cells were characterized; the EQE results are presented in Fig. 4d. Using the EQE curves, we calculated the   $  J_{sc}  $   values of the devices based on DEP and DBP to be 21.84 and 19.44 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  , respectively; these values only slightly differ from those obtained from the J-V curves. The lower   $  J_{sc}  $   of the DBP-based devices is attributable to the pores acting as traps that cannot be completely filled by the HTL [35]. Interestingly, the incomplete pore filling led to a change of the device color. The dense DEP devices exhibited an orange color. However, the device based on DBP exhibited a green color, which might be related to the pore structure. To check for nonvisual color changes, we measured the absolute color using the CIE 1976 color meter, as shown in Fig. 4e. In the color map, DBP and DEP have coordinates of (0.1908, 0.4723) and (0.2148, 0.5159), respectively, which are displayed as green and orange on the CIE 1976 color meter.

# 3.4. Electrochemical characteristics of perovskite NWs

We calculated the active defect densities of both perovskite films using the electron-only structures of   $  SnO_{2}  $  /perovskites (DEP and DBP)/PCBM as shown in Fig. 5a. Fig. 5a shows the log plot of I-V of ETL-only devices based on DEP and DBP. We calculated the defect (trap) densities from the relation between the trap-filled limited voltage (  $  V_{TFL}  $  ) and trap density (  $  N_{t}  $  ),   $  N_{t} = 2\varepsilon\varepsilon_{0}V_{TRL}/eL^{2}  $  , where L is the film thickness,   $  \varepsilon  $   is the dielectric constant of the perovskite layer, and   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   is the dielectric constant of vacuum. The   $  V_{TFL}  $   values were 0.60 and 0.73 V (Fig. 5a), and the calculated trap densities were   $  7.8 \times 10^{15}  $   and   $  9.58 \times 10^{15}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $   for DBP and DEP based on electron-only structures, respectively, which imply that DBP contains lower active defects. The defect-less property of 1D

NW DBP can be attributed to the slow crystallization in the presence of the DB having long alkyl chains.

To investigate the electrochemical charge transport dynamics, we carried out EIS measurements using full cells with the perovskite materials with different morphologies. In general, EIS analysis is useful for gaining insight into electrical properties such as charge transfer, accumulation, and charge recombination in PSCs [37,38]. # 3.5. Optoelectronic characteristics of perovskite NWs

To confirm the perovskite film quality in DBP and DBP, SSPL analysis was performed using structures of glass/perovskite (DEP/DBP); the resultant SSPL spectra of DEP and DBP are shown in Fig. 6a. Apparently, the PL intensity of DBP is much higher than that of DEP, meaning that the excited carriers in DBP recombine in a radiative manner. The reduced non-radiative pathways in DBP are attributable to the reduction in the number of grain boundaries acting as defects compared with the number in its DEP counterpart [41]. In addition, the FWHM of the peak in the SSPL spectrum of DBP  $ (49.63\mathrm{nm}) $  is narrower than that of the peak in the spectrum of DEP  $ (54.58\mathrm{nm}) $ . This narrower PL linewidth emission also indicates that additional recombination centers in DBP are absent, resulting in better crystal quality [22]. The peak position of the PL signal in the spectrum of 3D DEP is blue-shifted compared with that in the spectrum of DBP, indicating a difference in bandgap between DEP and DBP. The PL emission spectra of DEP and DBP are centered at 771 and  $ 783~\mathrm{nm} $ , respectively,

corresponding to optical bandgaps of 1.60 and 1.58 eV, respectively (Fig. S8); these results indicate that the bandgap is modified only by the change in morphology. In general, the shortening of Pb–I bond length in the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  enhances the orbital overlap and band dispersion, which induces the decreased of the bandgap [42,43]. Therefore, the bandgap reduction in DBP might be closely related to the compressive strain of the DBP films, which agrees well with the computational data [40].

To characterize the recombination and charge-carrier dynamics of the DBP and DEP, we conducted FLIM and TRPL studies using structures of glass/perovskite and obtained the charge-carrier lifetimes in both structures. The FLIM results are shown in Fig. 6b and c, where the different colors correspond to different FLIM lifetimes calculated from the FLIM setup following the TTTR data acquisition method and where the brightness of the image corresponds to the total counts (or intensity) of the luminescence events at each pixel [44]. The 1D DBP film (Fig. 6c) has a longer lifetime, as indicated by red pixels, whereas the 3D DEP (Fig. 6b) has a shorter lifetime, as indicated by green pixels [22]. From the FLIM results, the carrier lifetime of DBP was calculated to be 241 ns, which is substantially longer than that (137 ns) of DEP (Fig. 6d), which is due to the reduction of I- ion migration in the presence of compressive strain of DBP.

To verify this effect, SSPL, TRPL, and FLIM studies were carried out for structures with a configuration of FTO/Perovskites (DEP,DBP)/HTL; the measurements were performed in two different directions, i.e., normal and parallel to the surface. # 4. Conclusions

In this study, using the same precursor solution, two highly reproducible perovskite layers, namely 3D dense perovskite films and 1D NW perovskite network films, were successfully synthesized via a one-step solution process combined with antisolvent washing. During the one-step process, the chain lengths and complementary properties of antisolvent (either DE or DB) played an important role in the formation of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  films with 3D or 1D morphologies. Perovskite films formed with DB washing experience preferential 1D growth to avoid strong steric hindrance in DB/perovskite complexes, which results in residual compressive stress on the perovskite NWs. To evaluate the PV properties of the perovskite films with different morphologies, planar solar cells with 3D and 1D  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  layers were tested. Although the 1D NW-based device showed a lower PCE (15.73%) than the 3D bulk-based device (18.73%), EIS analyses revealed that the 1D perovskite exhibited faster charge-carrier transport than the 3D bulk perovskite. Furthermore, FLIM and TRPL studies showed that the 1D NW perovskite exhibited a longer lifetime because of its lower grain-boundary density and faster carrier transport resulting from the reduction of I- ion migration in the presence of compressive strain; these characteristics should be beneficial to the performance of optoelectronic devices.
