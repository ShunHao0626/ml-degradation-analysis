# Ionic Liquid-Induced Ostwald Ripening Effect for Efficient and Stable Tin-Based Perovskite Solar Cells

Zhuojia Lin,  $  ^{||}  $   Yang Su,  $  ^{||}  $   Runying Dai, Gengling Liu, Jia Yang, Wangping Sheng, Yang Zhong, Licheng Tan,  $  ^{*}  $   and Yiwang Chen  $  ^{*}  $

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Tin-based perovskite solar cells (PVSCs) are regarded as the most promising alternative among lead-free PVSCs. However, the rapid crystallization for tin-based perovskite tends to cause inferior film morphology and abundant defect states, which make poor photovoltaic performance. Here, 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium bromide (BMIBr) ionic liquids (ILs) with strong polarity and a low melting point are first employed to produce the Ostwald ripening effect and obtain high-quality tin-based perovskite films with a large grain size. Meanwhile, the non-radiative recombination ascribed from defect states can also be effectively reduced for BMIBr-treated perovskite films. Consequently, a photoelectric conversion efficiency (PCE) of 10.09% for inverted tin-based PVSCs is attained by the Ostwald ripening effect. Moreover, the unencapsulated devices with BMIBr retain near 85% of the original PCE in

a  $ \mathrm{N}_2 $  glovebox beyond  $ 1200\mathrm{h} $  and about  $ 40\% $  of the original PCE after exposure to air for  $ 48\mathrm{h} $ .

KEYWORDS: perovskite solar cells, lead-free, ionic liquid, Ostwald ripening effect, stability

# 1. INTRODUCTION

Lead-based perovskite solar cells (PVSCs) have emerged as promising candidates for the next-generation photovoltaic semiconductor. However, the formidable toxicity for conventional lead-based PVSCs is the biggest obstacle for further large-scale production.  $  ^{1-5}  $   Thus, it is particularly urgent to search an alternative element of metallic lead. Among numerous low-toxic metal elements, tin (Sn) and germanium (Ge), located in the same main group with lead (Pb), are the most promising alternative elements.  $  ^{6,7}  $   However, GeI  $  _{2}  $   has poor solubility in polar solvents and the photoelectric conversion efficiency (PCE) for Ge-based PVSCs so far is only inferior at 0.57%.  $  ^{8,9}  $   Sn has a similar electronic configuration and ionic radius compared to Pb and will eventually degrade into friendly SnO  $  _{2}  $  , which makes Sn display greater potential to substitute for Pb.  $  ^{10,11}  $   However, tin-based PVSCs are up against the following bottlenecks: First is the severe p-type self-doping and device instability due to the irreversible trend from Sn  $  ^{2+}  $   to Sn  $  ^{4+}  $  ,  $  ^{12}  $   and second is the unsatisfactory photovoltaic performance originated from the inferior film morphology caused by faster crystallization.  $  ^{13,14}  $

Recently, numerous corresponding strategies have been reported to enhance the photovoltaic performance for tin-based PVSCs in response to the different challenges. For composition engineering, tin-based PVSCs based on   $  FA_{0.75}MA_{0.25}SnI_{3}  $   and   $  MA_{0.25}FA_{0.75}SnI_{2.75}Br_{0.25}  $   as the absorbing layers achieved PCE values of 8.12 and 9.1%, respectively.  $  ^{15,16}  $   Liu et al. reported the preparation of tin-based PVSCs by solvent

In this work, the Ostwald ripening effect induced by 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium bromide (BMIBr) ionic liquids (ILs) is first applied in fabricating tin-based PVSCs. During the thermal annealing of perovskite films, BMIBr IL domains generated by a lower melting point act as Ostwald ripening agents for dissolving the perovskite grains with a smaller size. With this facile strategy, the high-quality perovskite films possessing a larger grain size and fewer grain boundaries (GBs) can be fabricated, thereby reducing defect states and non-radiative recombination. Consequently, benefitting from the BMIBr-induced Ostwald ripening effect, a champion PCE of 10.09% has successfully been obtained for tin-based PVSCs with enhanced environmental stability.

# 2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

2.1. Materials and Solution Preparation. Formamidinium iodide (FAI, 99.9%), ethylenediamine dihydroiodide (EDAI  $  _{2}  $  , 98%), ethylammonium iodide (EAI, 98%), and 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium bromide (BMIBr, 98%) were purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (TCI). Tin(II) iodide (SnI  $  _{2}  $  , 99.99%), tin(II) fluoride (SnF  $  _{2}  $  , 99%), bathocuproine (BCP, 99%), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.9%), and chlorobenzene (CB, 99.8%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. [6,6]-Phenyl-C  $  _{61}  $  -butyric acid methyl ester (PC  $  _{61}  $  BM) and poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS, Clevios VP AI 4083) were acquired from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. All solvents and chemicals were purchased commercially and used as received without further purification. The precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 0.9 M FAI, 0.1 M EAI, 1 M SnI  $  _{2}  $  , 0.1 M SnF  $  _{2}  $  , and 0.01 M EDAI  $  _{2}  $   in a mixed solvent of 800   $  \mu  $  L of DMF and 200   $  \mu  $  L of DMSO. For the BMIBr-included precursor solution, the molar ratios of BMIBr and SnI  $  _{2}  $   were 1, 2, and 5%, respectively.

2.2. Device Fabrication. Cleaned ITO-coated glasses were treated with ultraviolet (UV)-ozone for 10 min. The hole transport layer (HTL) of PEDOT:PSS aqueous solution was spin-coated on the ITO substrates at 4000 rpm for 60 s and then annealed at 140 °C for 20 min in air. Afterward, the precursor solution was spun on the ITO/PEDOT:PSS substrates at 5000 rpm for 30 s. CB (150 mL) as the antisolvent was dropped at the ninth second after the spinning started, and the films were annealed on a hot stage at 90 °C for 10 min. Next, PCBM (20 mg/mL in CB) solution was deposited on top as an electron transport layer. Then, BCP (0.5 mg/mL) in isopropanol was spin-coated at 4500 rpm for 1 min. Finally, 100 nm-thick Ag was deposited to complete the preparation of PVSCs with an active area of 0.04 cm $  ^{2}  $ .

2.3. Characterization of Perovskite Films and Devices. The film morphology images were analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (JSM-6701F, JEOL Ltd., Japan). An X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern was recorded by a D8-Discover 25 diffractometer (Bruker). The photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectra were collected on Edinburgh FLS920 photoluminescence spectroscopy. The optical absorption was measured using an ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectrophotometer (SHIMADZU, UV-2600). The J-V curves and space-charge-limited current (SCLC) measurements were recorded under 100 mW/cm² (AM 1.5G standard) simulated illumination (Keithley 2400). The external quantum efficiency (EQE) was characterized by a QE-R system (Enli Tech.). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was investigated by nanoscope multimode (Bruker). The

electrochemical impedance spectra were measured with a Zahner electrochemical workstation. A Thermo Scientific ESCA Lab 250Xi was employed to test X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) of perovskite films coated on ITO substrates. The contact angle measurement was performed on a Krüss DSA100s drop shape analyzer.

Ostwald ripening, there needs to be a liquid domain between the perovskite grains.  $  ^{31}  $   Due to the strong polarity and low melting point characteristics of BMIBr ILs, they can dissolve all tin-based perovskite precursor composites during thermal annealing (Figure S1).  $  ^{32,33}  $   Moreover, part of the precipitated black perovskite has also been dissolved again by BMIBr ILs (Figure S2), suggesting that BMIBr can serve as the liquid domain required for the Ostwald ripening effect. Following the Gibbs–Thomson relation, smaller perovskite grains with higher chemical potential are preferred to dissolve in the liquid domain.  $  ^{34}  $   Such a dissolution process makes the concentration of dissolved components around the smaller perovskite grains higher than that of the dissolved components around the larger perovskite grains, which results in the mass transport from smaller perovskite grains to larger perovskite grains based on Fick's first law.  $  ^{35}  $   Consequently, the larger perovskite grains with lower chemical potential grow further with time, while the smaller perovskite grains disappear.  $  ^{36,37}  $   The top-view and cross-sectional view scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images, the perovskite grain size distribution, and X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of corresponding perovskite films under different thermal annealing times have been investigated to corroborate the influence of BMIBr-induced Ostwald ripening during the growth of perovskite films. As shown in Figure 2a,b, the unannealed perovskite films (without and with BMIBr) display similar morphologies containing smaller perovskite grains due to the excessively fast crystallization. Then, the increased thermal annealing time displays minimal impact on the pristine perovskite films. Surprisingly, the high-quality BMIBr-assisted perovskite films with a larger grain size as the thermal annealing time increased can be observed (Figure 2c), and the corresponding average grain sizes increase from 504 to 829 nm (Figure 2d). In addition, the cross-sectional view SEM image of BMIBr-assisted perovskite further supports the larger grain distribution with comparison to the pristine perovskite film (Figure 2e,f). To evaluate the emission characteristic and charge-recombination kinetics of perovskite films, the steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectra have been implemented.  $  ^{38}  $   The BMIBr-assisted perovskite film exhibits a stronger PL intensity in Figure 3a, implying that the excitons can be dissociated into free charges more efficiently and the defect state is reduced. Moreover, the carrier lifetime increases to 7.78 ns compared with the pristine perovskite film (4.96 ns), as shown in Figure 3b. The longer carrier lifetime demonstrates less non-radiative recombination.  $  ^{39,40}  $   For further confirmation, the space-charge-limited current (SCLC) measurements have been performed to calculate the defect state density (  $  N_{t}  $  ) (Figure 3c,d).   $  N_{t}  $   can be given from the following formula

$$
N _ {t} = \frac {2 \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{r}} \varepsilon_ {0} V _ {\mathrm{TFL}}}{q L ^ {2}}
$$

(1)

where   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   is the vacuum permittivity, q is the elementary charge,   $  V_{TFL}  $   is the trap filling-limited voltage, and the thickness and relative dielectric constant of perovskite film are represented by L and   $  \varepsilon_{r}  $  , respectively.  $  ^{41}  $   The hole and electronic defect state densities of BMIBr-treated perovskite films reduce to   $  9.82 \times 10^{15}  $   and   $  9.43 \times 10^{15}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  , respectively, which are both lower than those of the pristine perovskite film (  $  1.69 \times 10^{16}  $   and   $  1.57 \times 10^{16}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  ). These results are also consistent with the above PL spectra,  $  ^{42}  $   which are ascribed to the high-quality perovskite film by the BMIBr-induced Ostwald ripening effect. In addition, the carrier mobility (  $  \mu  $  ) can also be obtained by the Mott–Gurney law.  $  ^{43}  $

$$
J = \frac {9 \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{r}} \varepsilon_ {0} \mu V ^ {2}}{8 L ^ {3}}
$$

(2)

The hole (electron) mobility of perovskite film with BMIBr slightly increases from   $  1.08 \times 10^{-1}  $   (  $  3.01 \times 10^{-1}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $   V  $  ^{-1}  $   s  $  ^{-1}  $  ) to   $  2.37 \times 10^{-1}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $   V  $  ^{-1}  $   s  $  ^{-1}  $   (  $  3.92 \times 10^{-1}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $   V  $  ^{-1}  $   s  $  ^{-1}  $  ). The faster and more balanced carrier transport can be explained by the following facts: First, the defect states in typical PVSCs act as a barrier for the carrier transport and facilitate the recombination

between the holes and electrons. The BMIBr-induced Ostwald ripening effect can significantly reduce the defect states at GBs. Second, the carriers also prefer to travel through the perovskite grains rather than GBs.

The influence of the Ostwald ripening effect on the device performance has been investigated in the inverted tin-based PVSCs with the architecture of ITO/PEDOT:PSS/PVK (without and with BMIBr)/PCBM/BCP/Ag (Figure 4a). The photocurrent–voltage   $  (J-V)  $   curves of the corresponding devices are displayed in Figure 4b and Figure S9. The detailed parameters are summarized in Table 1 and Table S1. Clearly, the BMIBr-treated device exhibits a champion PCE of 10.09% along with an open-circuit voltage   $  (V_{\mathrm{oc}})  $   of 0.70 V, a fill factor (FF) of 72.36%, and a short-circuit current   $  (J_{\mathrm{sc}})  $   of 19.86 mA/cm², which are significantly higher than those of the reference device   $  (V_{\mathrm{oc}}:0.62\;\mathrm{V},\;\mathrm{FF}:68.16\%, J_{\mathrm{sc}}:18.30\;\mathrm{mA/cm^{2}},\;\mathrm{and}\;\mathrm{PCE}:7.70\%)  $  . Then, the steady-state measurements of devices have been characterized to verify the reliability of photovoltaic performance derived from the J-V curves. As shown in Figure 4c, a stabilized output efficiency of 9.74% and a photocurrent density of 16.89 mA/cm² have been achieved for the BMIBr-treated device at the maximum power point, while those of the reference device are 7.44% and 14.10 mA/cm², respectively. Meanwhile, the   $  J_{sc}  $   values extracted from the J-V curves are in accord with the integrated   $  J_{sc}  $   values derived from the external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra (Figure 4d). Compared to the reference device, the BMIBr-treated device achieves a higher EQE across the entire wavelength range, which is well matched with UV–vis absorption. In addition, the PCE distribution histogram for 20 devices is presented in Figure 4e and Figure S10, which manifests better reproducibility of photovoltaic performance by the assistance of BMIBr. Furthermore, the average PCE of the BMIBr-treated device increases from 7.22 to 9.63%. The higher   $  J_{sc}  $   mainly depends on superior optical absorption, whereas the increased   $  V_{oc}  $   originates from the better-matched energy level alignment and reduced defect states. In addition, the instability is another bottleneck restricting the development of tin-based PVSCs.  $  ^{50-52}  $   Thus, the stability of unencapsulated devices under different conditions has been also investigated. As shown in Figure 5a, the BMIBr-treated device stored in a   $  N_{2}  $   glovebox retains approximately 85% of the initial PCE up to 1200 h, whereas the reference device displays a PCE retention of 15% during the same time. For the long-term stability test of devices in air with about 50% relative humidity (Figure 5b), the PCE of the reference device undergoes a rapid

decrease and eventually collapses after 36 h. However, the BMIBr-treated device maintains nearly 40% of its initial PCE after 48 h. The better device stability (in a   $  N_{2}  $   glovebox or in air) can be attributed to the enhanced film quality and less defect states. To further prove the above statement, XRD measurements have been performed to monitor the degradation of perovskite films in air as a function of time. The pristine perovskite film has quickly degraded after being exposed to air for 8 h, and some new diffraction peaks assigned for oxidation of   $  Sn^{2+}  $   have been observed (Figure 5c).  $  ^{53-55}  $   However, introducing BMIBr can form stable   $  SnI_{2}-BMIBr  $   complexes, which is proven by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra in Figure S12. As a result, the BMIBr-assisted perovskite film has not exhibited any new diffraction peaks with a time of up to 12 h (Figure 5d). In addition, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra have been measured to ascertain the oxidation degree in perovskite films (Figure 5e,f). It is found that the ratios of   $  Sn^{2+}/Sn^{4+}  $   for the BMIBr-assisted perovskite film are significantly higher compared with the pristine perovskite film, which is due to the decreased GBs and formation of   $  SnI_{2}-BMIBr  $   complexes, thereby bringing about reducing oxygen erosion into perovskite grains via GBs.  $  ^{56-58}  $   Moreover, the photographs of corresponding perovskite films have been shown in Figure S13, which are in agreement with the above results. # 4. CONCLUSIONS

In summary, the Ostwald ripening effect induced by adding BMIBr ILs can greatly enlarge the grain size and decrease the GBs of tin-based perovskite films, resulting in the decrease of defect states as well as non-radiative recombination. Consequently, the champion PCE of inverted tin-based PVSCs has been achieved to 10.09% compared to the reference device (7.70%). Furthermore, the optimized device has better environmental stability, where aging in a   $  N_{2}  $   glovebox for 1200 h maintains almost 85% of the initial PCE and aging in air (about 50% relative humidity) for 48 h maintains nearly 40% of the initial PCE. Such a novel strategy of Ostwald ripening breaks through a new avenue to prepare high-quality tin-based perovskite film.
