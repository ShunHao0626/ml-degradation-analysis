# Fundamental Flaw in the Current Construction of the   $  TiO_{2}  $   Electron Transport Layer of Perovskite Solar Cells and Its Elimination

Yan Yan,  $  ^{§§}  $   Cheng Liu,  $  ^{§§}  $   Yi Yang, Guoxiang Hu, Vandana Tiwari, De-en Jiang, Wei Peng, Ajay Jha, Hong-Guang Duan, Friedjof Tellkamp, Yong Ding, Weidong Shi, Shouqi Yuan, Dwayne Miller, Wanhong Ma,  $  ^{*}  $   and Jincai Zhao

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: The top-performing perovskite solar cells (efficiency > 20%) generally rely on the use of a nanocrystal   $  TiO_{2}  $   electron transport layer (ETL). However, the efficacies and stability of the current stereotypically prepared   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs employing commercially available   $  TiO_{2}  $   nanocrystal paste are far from their maximum values. As revealed herein, the long-hidden reason for this discrepancy is that acidic protons (  $  \sim  $  0.11 wt %) always remain in   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs after high-temperature sintering due

to the decomposition of the organic proton solvent (mostly alcohol). These protons readily lead to the formation of Ti–H species upon light irradiation, which act to block the electron transfer at the perovskite/TiO  $  _{2}  $  interface. Affront this challenge, we introduced a simple deprotonation protocol by adding a small amount of strong proton acceptors (sodium ethoxide or NaOH) into the common TiO  $  _{2}  $  nanocrystal paste precursor and replicated the high-temperature sintering process, which wiped out nearly all protons in TiO  $  _{2}  $  ETLs during the sintering process. The use of deprotonated TiO  $  _{2}  $  ETLs not only promotes the PCE of both MAPbI  $  _{3}  $ -based and FA  $  _{0.85}  $  MA  $  _{0.15}  $  PbI  $  _{2.55}  $  Br  $  _{0.45}  $ -based devices over 20% but also significantly improves the long-term photostability of the target devices upon 1000 h of continuous operation.

KEYWORDS: perovskite solar cells,   $  TiO_{2}  $   electron transport layer, deprotonation, Ti–H species

# INTRODUCTION

Low-cost, hybrid metal-halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted enormous research interest in both household and space applications, which achieved a maximum certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 24.2%.  $  ^{1-6}  $   The current top-performing PSC devices (with PCEs > 20%) mainly rely on the use of   $  TiO_{2}  $   nanocrystals as the electron transport layer (ETL).  $  ^{4,7,8}  $   A transparent   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETL in either a mesoporous or planar structure with an appropriate thickness is vital for extracting photogenerated electrons from the light-harvesting perovskite layer and suppressing front-surface hole recombination.  $  ^{9-11}  $   The PCEs and the stability of PSCs have been pursued by optimizing the device structure, such as tandem, inverted planar, and trihalide mesosuperstructures, as well as developing new perovskite materials.  $  ^{12-16}  $   Since the well-established high-temperature (460–550 °C) preparation method for   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs has proven to be successful in creating mesoporous or planar structures and maintaining uniform crystallinity in tens of thousands of PSC devices,  $  ^{9-17,17-22}  $   the inherent status of   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs has seldom been considered as an optimization point for improving PCEs and the stability of PSCs. Until recently, Tan et al. employed a surface passivation strategy using chlorine-capped   $  TiO_{2}  $   nanocrystals as the ETL to develop high-efficiency flexible PSCs that required a relatively low temperature (<200 °C) to prepare the planar

However, experimental confirmation of these residual protons and their consequent effect in common   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs has not been reported. In liquid/solid systems, dissociated or bound protons on the oxide semiconductor surface can alter the interfacial electron transfer by redox reactions.  $  ^{26-28}  $   For example, under direct UV irradiation, generated protons from alcohol as a hole scavenger coupled with photoinduced electrons on   $  TiO_{2}  $   and ZnO nanoparticles induce concerted proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) reactions.  $  ^{26}  $   Moreover, in a typical   $  TiO_{2}  $   electrode-based dye-sensitized water splitting system, a significant decrease in efficiency was observed when a proton donor (e.g.,   $  HClO_{4}  $  ,   $  H_{2}O  $  , or ethanol) was intentionally added to the reaction system.  $  ^{27}  $   In our recent work, we discovered the formation of covalent Ti–H species at the liquid electrolyte/dye-  $  TiO_{2}  $   interface arisen from protons released from alcohol oxidation and electrons injected in the conduction band, and these Ti–H species are responsible for the drop in efficiency and the ultimate failure of the dye-sensitized solar cells.  $  ^{28}  $   We reason that protons are ubiquitous in   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs as well in the all-solid-state PSCs after the high-temperature removal of the organic components from the nanocrystal paste precursor. Furthermore, if protons are present, whether these residual protons are detrimental to the PCE and the stability of PSC devices and how the impact can be eliminated must be determined.

# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

We first verified whether protons are ubiquitous in the common high-temperature-prepared mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs (mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $  ) in PSC devices. The common commercial 18 nm   $  TiO_{2}  $   nanocrystal paste was used as the standard precursor to prepare mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs according to the conventional high-temperature sintering protocol (as described in Supporting Information). After removing   $  TiO_{2}  $   nanocrystals from FTO glasses on the as-prepared ETLs, we coated an equal amount of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   perovskite layer on the obtained   $  TiO_{2}  $   nanocrystals as the internal reference (denoted as   $  MAPbI_{3}/TiO_{2}  $  ) for the solid-state magic-angle-spinning nuclear magnetic resonance

(MAS-NMR   $  {}^{1}  $  H) characterization (Figure 1A). We also characterized both pristine TiO  $  _{2}  $   ETL and MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   as control samples (Figure 1B). In the spectrum of the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  /TiO  $  _{2}  $   sample, two significant peaks at 3.31 and 6.35 ppm accompanied by a less intense peak at 1.41 ppm (discussed later) were observed (Figure 1A). The peak at 3.31 ppm was readily assigned to the signal of the C–H species in MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ,  $  ^{28-31}  $   which was further confirmed by the observation of the same peak in the spectrum of pristine MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   (Figure 1B). However, we failed to observe the signal of N–H in the spectrum of pristine MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   at 6.4 ppm,  $  ^{29-31}  $   which is possibly due to the high dissociation of the amino group and the relatively low spin frequency (12 kHz) in our measurement.  $  ^{32}  $   Instead, the peak at 6.35 ppm was observed in the spectrum of the pristine TiO  $  _{2}  $   ETL sample without the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   coating (Figure 1B), which is consistent with the signal of inorganic protons in the form of TiO  $  ^{-}  $  -H  $  ^{+}  $   on TiO  $  _{2}  $   nanoparticles,  $  ^{33}  $   indicating that the corresponding species may not from the organic–inorganic hybrid MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   component. When using commercial 30 nm and homemade 15 nm TiO  $  _{2}  $   nanocrystal pastes to prepare the TiO  $  _{2}  $   ETLs under otherwise identical conditions, an identical signal at 6.35 ppm appeared similar to that when using 18 nm TiO  $  _{2}  $   nanocrystal paste (Figure 1A). Similar signal profiles were also observed when changing the sintering temperature in the range of 460–550 °C (Figure 1C). To directly verify the assignment of the 6.35 ppm peak to dissociable protons, we prepared a series of protonated TiO  $  _{2}  $   ETLs (denoted as H  $  ^{+}  $  -TiO  $  _{2}  $  ) by intentionally adding water solutions with different pH values (pH = 1–10) as the proton donor to the nanocrystal paste precursor. As expected, we observed a gradual change in the intensity of the 6.35 ppm peak relative to that of the 3.31 ppm peak (Figure S1). All of these observations confirm the assignment of the 6.35 ppm peak as the proton signal on TiO  $  _{2}  $   rather than the N–H signal of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $ , which should not be changed by modifying the surface of TiO  $  _{2}  $ , and indicate the presence of a substantial quantity of protons in TiO  $  _{2}  $   ETLs after high-temperature sintering. We argue that these residual protons on the   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETL come from the decomposition of organic ingredients including solvent ethanol (the major solvent), terpineol, and other organic auxiliary reagents in the   $  TiO_{2}  $   precursor paste. To verify the assumption, we used isotopically labeled ethanol-  $  d_{6}  $   as a solvent to prepare the   $  TiO_{2}  $   precursor paste. From the MAS-NMR   $  {}^{1}H  $   spectra, we found that the proton signal (  $  TiO^{-}-H^{+}  $  ) at 6.35 ppm on the pristine   $  TiO_{2}  $   sample was sharply decreased to less than 10% (Figure S3). This H/D replacement effect on the proton signal provided solid evidence that the ethanol solvent in   $  TiO_{2}  $   paste was the main proton source of normal high-temperature-prepared   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs.

To explore whether the acidic protons in   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs affect the performance of the resulting PSC devices, we first need to prepare a proton-free   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETL. Thus, we devised a deprotonation protocol by adding the ethanolic solution of strong proton acceptors, NaOH or sodium ethoxide (EtONa), into the   $  TiO_{2}  $   nanocrystal paste and following the conventional high-temperature sintering process to prepare deprotonated   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs (denoted as   $  NaOH-TiO_{2}  $   and   $  EtONa-TiO_{2}  $  ; for details of the methods, see Supporting Information). These strong proton acceptors are deemed to be easily bonded on the polar surface of   $  TiO_{2}  $   nanoparticles in the paste before high-temperature sintering, which can effectively suppress the formation of protons in   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs. Indeed, we found that the peak of protons at 6.35 ppm had completely disappeared from the MAS-NMR   $  {}^{1}H  $   spectra of both   $  NaOH-TiO_{2}  $   and   $  EtONa-TiO_{2}  $   ETLs (Figure 2A). Similar phenomena were observed when repeating this deprotonation protocol using commercial 30 nm and homemade 15 nm   $  TiO_{2}  $   nanocrystal pastes as the precursors (Figure S4) and when varying the

sintering temperature (460–550 °C) (Figure S5). All these results confirm that the use of proton acceptors ensures the as-prepared   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs without detectable protons.

Moreover, the PCEs of the target PSC devices are significantly improved using NaOH–TiO  $  _{2}  $  or EtONa–TiO  $  _{2}  $  ETL with the same light-harvesting MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  layer (Figure 2B,C). The optimized mass ratios of NaOH and EtONa added to TiO  $  _{2}  $  were 0.34 wt % (denoted as NaOH–TiO  $  _{2}  $  -0.34) and 1.34 wt % (denoted as EtONa–TiO  $  _{2}  $  -1.34), respectively. Compared to the control device with a normal TiO  $  _{2}  $  ETL (PCE = ~17.55%), the devices prepared with NaOH–TiO  $  _{2}  $  -0.34 and EtONa–TiO  $  _{2}  $  -1.34 ETLs possessed much higher PCEs, reaching 19.11 and 20.04%, respectively (Figure 2B). Both the J  $  _{sc}  $  and V  $  _{oc}  $  were increased with deprotonated TiO  $  _{2}  $  ETLs (Figure 2B). Further increasing the amount of base added slightly decreased the PCE (Figure 2C), which is reasonable for an excess Brønsted base loading would no doubt block the electron transfer. Moreover, from the SEM images, we observed no apparent difference between the structures of the target and control devices (Figure 2D,E) or in the morphologies of the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  perovskite layer (Figure 2F,G) and TiO  $  _{2}  $  ETLs (Figure 2H,I). X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra show that the structure of the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  layer was not changed after the deprotonation treatment of TiO  $  _{2}  $  ETLs (Figure S6). The absorption spectra of the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  layers coated on the normal TiO  $  _{2}  $  and EtONa–TiO  $  _{2}  $  -1.34 ETLs were also the same (Figure S7). The improved PSC performance from the deprotonation of the   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETL can be attributed to two possible factors: (i) improved electron migration efficiency inside the   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs or (ii) enhanced interfacial electron transfer at the perovskite/  $  TiO_{2}  $   interface. For the former, we directly measured the charge mobility inside both the normal and EtONa–  $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs by the space-charge-limited current (SCLC) method and observed no discernible difference (Figure S9). For the latter, we tracked the transient dynamics of the excited state in the   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   perovskite layer in contact with different   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs using a home-built femtosecond transient absorption (TA) spectrometer (for details of the setup and experimental conditions, see Supporting Information). Upon excitation at 515 nm (Figures 3A and S10), we observed bleaching of the signal from the intrinsic charge separation of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   on all samples at 760 nm.  $  ^{35,36}  $   Additionally, dual-band excited-state absorption (ESA) features were observed at 650 and 700 nm. The ESA at 650 nm can be attributed to the charge-transfer excited state (referred to as the hot-electron state), while the ESA at 700 nm is the charge-separated excited state.  $  ^{36}  $   When the   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   layer is in contact with   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs, an apparent population loss from the hot-electron state (650 nm) was observed (Figure 3B), suggesting that electron injection occurred at the   $  MAPbI_{3}/TiO_{2}  $   interface. Notably, when the   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   layer was in contact with NaOH–  $  TiO_{2}  $   or EtONa–  $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs, the corresponding population loss from the hot-electron state was much larger than that with the normal   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETL. This difference directly reflects the enhanced electron injection at the   $  MAPbI_{3}/TiO_{2}  $   interface with deprotonated   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs.

As mentioned in the above discussion (Figure 1), we always observed a less intense peak at 1.41 ppm in the MAS-NMR   $  {}^{1}  $  H spectra of both the pristine TiO  $  _{2}  $   ETL and MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  /TiO  $  _{2}  $   samples. This signal is from the covalent Ti–H species, as demonstrated in our previous study and other studies.  $  ^{28,37,38}  $   We argued that the Ti–H species was also formed at the all-solid-state perovskite/TiO  $  _{2}  $   interface with the substantial existence of protons. Accordingly, we monitored the amount of Ti–H species in MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  /TiO  $  _{2}  $   samples with normal TiO  $  _{2}  $   and EtONa–TiO  $  _{2}  $   ETLs during continuous 1 sun illumination (AM1.5, 100 mW/cm  $  ^{2}  $  ). Indeed, with the normal TiO  $  _{2}  $   ETL, the intensity of the Ti–H signal at 1.41 ppm was obviously increased, accompanied by a distinct decrease in the intensity of the acidic proton signal at 6.35 ppm (Figures 3C and S11). This result confirms that Ti–H species were arisen from PCET in the normal TiO  $  _{2}  $   ETL upon illumination. However, for the EtONa–TiO  $  _{2}  $   ETL (the proton signal at 6.35 ppm disappeared), the peak of the Ti–H species at 1.41 ppm was very weak and remained almost unchanged under identical illumination conditions (Figure 3D), indicating that the deprotonation of TiO  $  _{2}  $   ETLs truly minimized Ti–H formation.

We further used DFT calculations to simulate the proton adsorption and transfer at the   $  MAPbI_{3}/TiO_{2}  $   interface (for details of the computational methods, see Supporting Information). The DFT calculation result shows that the average hydrogen adsorption energy (  $  \Delta E_{H}  $  ) is -0.68 eV with 100% H (30 H atoms total) at the O sites (Figure 3E), while   $  \Delta E_{H}  $   is -0.26 eV with 50% H at O sites and 50% H at Ti sites (Figure 3F). The adsorption energies in both cases are negative, indicating that the adsorption of hydrogen species (regardless of the form, TiO–H or Ti–H) at the   $  MAPbI_{3}/TiO_{2}  $   interface is thermodynamically favorable. This means that once formed, the protons or hydrogen species should be firmly fixed at the   $  MAPbI_{3}/TiO_{2}  $   interface, which perfectly agrees with our experimental observation that protons are ubiquitous in the   $  MAPbI_{3}/TiO_{2}  $   samples (Figure 1). The formation of Ti–H species at the perovskite/TiO  $  _{2}  $   interface should be driven by the directional transfer of photoinduced electrons. In our previous work on the dye/TiO  $  _{2}  $   system, the Ti–H species can even be observed before the irradiation,  $  ^{28}  $   which is possibly caused by the exposure to ambient light during the preparation and storage. Recently, it was observed that a self-hydrogenated shell with the thickness of several nanometers was formed on TiO  $  _{2}  $   nanoparticles after the UV-induced photocatalytic hydrogen evolution reaction in an aqueous system.  $  ^{39}  $   From DFT calculations, the formation of the self-hydrogenated shell was proposed through the diffusion of photoreduced water protons.  $  ^{39}  $   In our all-solid-state perovskite/TiO  $  _{2}  $   PSC devices, due to the substantial existence of protons in the normal TiO  $  _{2}  $   ETL from the organic solvent in the paste, Ti–H species was easily formed even with the exposure of ambient light. The TiH  $  _{x}  $  -covered layer is an ionic conductor that achieves conduction through the transport of H  $  ^{-}  $   and H  $  ^{+}  $   ions,  $  ^{40}  $   whereas the pristine TiO  $  _{2}  $   crystal is an n-type electrical semiconductor that transports electrons between neighboring Ti  $  ^{3+}  $  /Ti  $  ^{4+}  $   atoms. Once formed, the stabilized Ti–H species on the TiO  $  _{2}  $   grain surface will block electron injection from the perovskite to the TiO  $  _{2}  $   ETL, by which the PCE of the PSC device is decreased. Moreover, it was reported that the protonation of the TiO  $  _{2}  $   surface generally turns the conduction band of TiO  $  _{2}  $   nanocrystals toward a more positive position,  $  ^{41}  $   which results in the formation of more trap sites and is unfavorable to the interfacial electron transfer. Thus, the use of our deprotonated TiO  $  _{2}  $   ETLs will lower the conduction band position, facilitate the electron transfer at the perovskite/TiO  $  _{2}  $   interface, and further increase the V  $  _{oc}  $   of PSC devices.

Finally, we repeated the deprotonation protocol and compared the PCEs and stabilities between the control and target PSC devices. The optimized EtONa–TiO  $  _{2}  $  -1.34 ETLs (commercial 18 nm TiO  $  _{2}  $   paste, 550 °C sintered) were used to assemble target devices. # CONCLUSIONS

It was unexpected that protons are still ubiquitous in   $  TiO_{2}  $   nanocrystal ETLs even after high-temperature sintering. Our work reveals a long-hidden factor constraining the efficiency and stability of   $  TiO_{2}  $  -based PSCs and thereby establishes a simple and robust method to suppress hydrogen terminals on the   $  TiO_{2}  $   ETLs, which significantly restores the deserved PCE and photostability of the resulting PSCs.

# EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

Experimental Instruments. The PCE and J-V curves of the PSC devices were measured with a Keithley 2400 source meter together with a sunlight simulator (XES-300T1, SAN-EI Electric, AM1.5), which was calibrated using a standard silicon reference cell. The J-V curves for all devices were measured by masking the active area with a black mask of   $  0.09 \, cm^2  $  . The IPCEs were determined as a function of wavelength from 300 to 900 nm (Enli Technology) with a dual xenon/quartz halogen light source and measured in the DC mode without using a bias light. The setup was calibrated with a certified silicon solar cell before measurements. MAS-NMR   $  {}^{1}H  $   spectra were recorded on a Bruker AVI 400 MHz NMR spectrometer. The chemical shifts were referenced to deuterated water (D  $  _{2}  $  O) at 4.800 ppm. All data were collected on a Bruker AVANCE III 400 MHz spectrometer. The MAS spin rate was 12 kHz with the one-pulse program. The pulse length was 4.5 us with a recycle delay of 5 s.

SCLC Measurements. The SCLC measurements were acquired using  $ \mathrm{Au / TiO_2 / FTO} $  devices with different  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  ETLs. The Au/  $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / FTO} $  devices were prepared following the same procedure used to prepare the corresponding PSC devices without the loading of perovskite and spiro-MeOTAD hole transport materials.

Femtosecond Time-Resolved Absorption Spectroscopy. All measurements were performed on a home-built TA setup in MPSD, Hamburg. The primary light source of the TA apparatus was a Pharos laser (light conversion, 1030 nm) operated at 1 kHz. A portion of the NIR-fundamental beam was converted into a visible pulse (515 nm) by a second-harmonic generation process. This 515 nm beam was attenuated to   $  \sim11\ \mu J\ cm^{-2}  $   pulses with a beam size of   $  >250\ \mu m  $   at the focus and used as the pump pulse for photoexcitation of the sample (which corresponds to a carrier density of   $  5\times10^{17}\ cm^{-3}  $  ). A white-light continuum was generated by focusing a small fraction of the attenuated 1030 nm pulse onto a 3 mm-thick sapphire crystal to attain a stable probe from 500 to 850 nm. To achieve a variable time delay between the pump and probe pulses, the path length of the pump beam was controlled through a retroreflector assembly placed over a 150 mm linear motorized translational stage from Newport. The probe spectra for each time delay were collected using a spectrograph (9055, Sciencetech) and a charge-coupled device linear image sensor (CCD; S11156-2048-02, Hamamatsu Photonics) that was read out at a repetition rate of 100 Hz. To obtain differential absorption signals, the detection system was synchronized to a chopper (Thorlabs) operating at 500 Hz. Time-resolved absorption spectra were measured with   $  \sim200  $   fs resolution. A comprehensive analysis of the TA spectra involving chirp correction and global analysis has been performed.

To avoid oxidation of the samples during measurements, all samples were kept in a cryostat (MicrostatHe2 from Oxford instruments) under vacuum (  $  \sim10^{-1}  $  Pa). To determine the stability of the films after the experiments, we repeated the same experiment at the same focal spot, and the results showed no change in the kinetics under experimental conditions. Measurements were also performed at different spots on the film, and these experiments showed similar kinetics.
