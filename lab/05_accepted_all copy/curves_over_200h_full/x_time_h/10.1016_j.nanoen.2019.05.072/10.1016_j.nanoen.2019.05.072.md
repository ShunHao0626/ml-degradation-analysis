# Abstract

Inverted perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have stood out from the promising candidates for portable power source applications. Their advantageous features such as high efficiency, low cost, light weight and flexibility can be retained when a robust and compact hole transport layer (HTL) is fabricated using a low-temperature technology. To maximize the mechanical feature, the integration of graphene-derived nanomaterials is a method of choice owing to their superior physical and mechanical properties. Herein, we report an innovative method of fabricating efficient PSCs by means of inserting conjugated polyelectrolyte PTFTS interlayers between the perovskite and graphene oxide (GO) HTL. Results show that the amphiphilic polymer interlayer facilitates the growth of perovskite grains, passivates interfacial trap-states, forms better energetic alignment, and increases intermolecular interaction between PTFTS and perovskite. More importantly, strong binding force between GO and PTFTS enables uniform interlayer films obtained from a simple dip-coating method. The facile processability of the PTFTS-modified GO has been successfully extended to scalable fabrication of flexible device  $ (1.08~\mathrm{cm}^2) $  with excellent device efficiency over  $ 17.0\% $ , the highest reported value so far for large-area flexible PSCs. This front-contact interfacial engineering offers an efficient strategy towards the widespread application of solution-processable graphene materials in practical perovskite devices.

# 1. Introduction

In the past several years, unprecedented improvement in power conversion efficiency (PCE) has been witnessed for perovskite solar cells (PSCs) based on organometal trihalide perovskites with intriguing and unique optoelectronic properties.  $  ^{[1-3]}  $   In addition to highly competitive PCEs for industrial engineered silicon solar cells, the appealing features of PSCs such as light weight,  $  ^{[4]}  $   low cost,  $  ^{[5]}  $   and low-temperature manufacturing  $  ^{[6]}  $   have intensively encouraged their entry into the established market. Aiming at portable applications, the scalable fabrication of PSC on flexible modules is becoming the focus of research.  $  ^{[7,8]}  $   Particularly, p-i-n planar architecture is very attractive for scale-up deposition on flexible plastic substrates without the need of sophisticated processing, such as extensive chemical doping and high-temperature annealing.  $  ^{[9,10]}  $   Although tremendous research efforts concerning the understanding and optimization of device performance on flexible plastics have been made, the performance of flexible PSCs with an area over   $  1cm^{2}  $   is still unsatisfactory, with the highest reported PCE around 15%.  $  ^{[11,12]}  $   Moreover, the performance degradation under harsh bending conditions prevents the practical application in flexible electronics.  $  ^{[13]}  $   To achieve an efficient flexible inverted PSC in large-area, several key issues need to be understood and overcome.  $  ^{[14]}  $   Among them, the development of efficient hole transport layer (HTL) underneath the perovskite remains undoubtedly a key challenge, because it not only boosts the efficiency but also imparts stability against mechanical bending and environmental effects. The situation becomes more challenging for cells with a comparatively large-area, where additional losses come from surface coverage of the upper perovskite absorber as well as charge transport at the interfaces.  $  ^{[15,16]}  $   Besides, low-temperature fabrication adds difficulties to the formation of high-quality front-contact compared with that on a rigid substrate. To overcome those challenges, the development of next-generation HTLs with better intrinsic properties will be pivotal.

The abundant carbon materials, especially 2D graphene, with excellent electronic, optical and mechanical properties have been widely applied in flexible optoelectronic

devices. $ ^{[17,18]} $  As a derivative of graphene, graphene oxide (GO) is regarded as a good candidate for the efficient and stable HTL due to its unique merits in optical transparency, compatibility with flexible substrates, outstanding thermal properties, and ion-blocking capability. $ ^{[19-21]} $  Additionally, perovskite grown on 2D layered materials exhibits high surface coverage and a high degree of crystallization carrying favorable (110) orientation, which together enable efficient hole extraction. $ ^{[22]} $  Notably, perovskite device based on GO HTLs show excellent light-soaking stability because GO possesses absorbability in the near-UV region (300–400 nm). $ ^{[23]} $  However, the PCEs of devices using GO or reduced GO as HTLs are still far behind other widely used competitors, such as inorganic nickel oxide (NiO $ _x $ ), $ ^{[24,25]} $  owing to the unsatisfactory p-type contact. The structural defects due to the oxygen groups in GO create surface inhomogeneity and reduce electron mobility. $ ^{[26]} $  Moreover, GO exhibits poor hole-selective capability and mismatched energy levels between its work function (WF) around -4.9 eV $ ^{[27]} $  and the valence band of MAPbI $ _3 $  (MA is methylammonium) at about -5.4 eV. In this regard, it is highly desired to develop new method to improve the front-contact interface of GO-based PSCs. Apart from chemical modification of GO, $ ^{[28]} $  an effective way to achieve high-quality interfacial contact is to introduce an interlayers between perovskite and GO. $ ^{[29]} $  It has been intensively shown that interfacial engineering of HTL surface using organic interlayer can significantly decrease interfacial loss processes by offering many benefits, including a higher WF, reduced trap sites and facilitated charge transfer. $ ^{[30,31]} $  However, most flexible PSCs, not merely GO-based devices, have been fabricated only with small device areas of ≤ 0.2 cm $ ^2 $  to avoid scalable issue. $ ^{[25,32-34]} $  This phenomenon arises from the conflict requirements on the surface energy of interlayer between crystallinity and surface coverage in perovskite films. We expect to achieve large-size perovskite crystal by depositing precursor solutions onto hydrophobic surface to suppress heterogeneous nucleation, while non-wetting interface normally results in poor and random surface coverage of the perovskite films containing numerous pinholes. $ ^{[35,36]} $  In addition to the scalable issue, few work presented a detailed investigation of the interfacial chemistry and the related charge transfer dynamics, which are critical for optimization of the front-contact modification strategy.

Herein we undertake thoroughly front-contact passivation by modifying GO film with

various conjugated polymer interlayers on a wide range of intrinsic properties, including wettability and energy level. This interfacial engineering enables us to reveal that ideal polymer interlayer should play a positive role in the following aspects, including (1) down-shifting the WF of GO to achieve a better alignment with perovskite absorber; (2) relatively hydrophilic surface to achieve full coverage and crystalline perovskite film; (3) providing sufficient electron blocking ability to avoid charge recombination and (4) forming strong intermolecular interaction with perovskite to enhance charge transfer. Based on this principle, we propose a precise approach to manipulate the surface properties of GO film by introducing an amphiphilic conjugated polyelectrolyte PTFTS interlayer with both triphenylamine and polar sulfonate groups for efficient HTL in PSCs. Therefore, the solution-processable and up-scalable interlayer ensures a high-performing and durable planar PSC on a large-area flexible substrate  $ (1.08~\mathrm{cm}^2) $ , yielding a champion efficiency of  $ 17.0\% $  with no appreciable hysteresis. Moreover, the unencapsulated devices also exhibit much desired photostability and environmental tolerance.

# 2. Material and methods

# 2.1. Photovoltaic Device Fabrication

The patterned ITO-coated glass or PEN substrates were cleaned by sequential sonication in soap solution, water, acetone and isopropanol for 20 min each and then dried under   $  N_{2}  $   flow and treated by oxygen plasma for 15 min. To prepare the p-i-n planar structure, an aqueous dispersion of GO (100   $  \mu  $  L, 1 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was spin-coated onto an ITO substrate at a rotation speed of 3000 rpm for 60 s, and the resulting GO film was heated at 130°C for 20 min to afford a thin GO film. After baking, the substrates were transferred into a   $  N_{2}  $  -filled glovebox for subsequent layers coating. To modify GO films with different interlayer, solutions containing different polymers (PTAA, PFNBr and PTFTS) at various concentrations were used. Typically, PTAA chlorobenzene solution (1 mg ml  $  ^{-1}  $  ), PFNBr isopropanol solution (0.5 mg ml  $  ^{-1}  $  ), and PTFTS solution (0.5 mg ml  $  ^{-1}  $  ) were spin-coated on GO substrates to achieve uniform ultrathin interlayers. For dip-coating of PTFTS, the GO substrates were immersed in isopropanol solution of PTFTS (1 mg ml  $  ^{-1}  $  ). After 10 min of incubation at room temperature, the substrates were rinsed with isopropanol followed by drying at 60 °C on a hotplate. Then, MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite

ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

precursor solution (1.2 M) dissolved in DMSO:DMF (1:4 in volume) mixed solvents were deposited on GO HTL using anti-solvent engineering method: the substrate was first spun at 1000 rpm for 12 s and then at 5000 rpm for 30 s. After 20 seconds, 200   $  \mu  $  L chlorobenzene was dropped onto the substrate. And the substrate was immediately annealed on a hotplate at 100 °C for 20 mins. After annealing, a solution of PC  $  _{61}  $  BM in chlorobenzene (25 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was spin-coated on top of the perovskite film at a rotation speed of 1500 rpm for 40 s. Finally, the samples were transferred to an evaporation chamber and BCP (8 nm) and Ag (120 nm) were deposited under vacuum.

# 2.2. Device Characterization

J-V characteristics were recorded in ambient environment using a Keithley 2400 source meter under the illumination of the solar simulator (Newport) at the light intensity of   $  100 \, mW cm^2  $  , with   $  0.1 \, V s^{-1}  $   sweeping rates. The power output of the lamp was calibrated using an NREL-traceable KG5-filtered silicon reference cell. The stabilized power output was measured by measuring the current while holding the voltage at the maximum power point, which was determined from the J-V curve in the reverse scan. All the devices were tested using a black metal aperture with a defined active area of   $  0.09 \, cm^2  $   and   $  0.25 \, cm^2  $   for the small devices, while   $  0.49 \, cm^2  $   and   $  1.08 \, cm^2  $   for the large   $  2 \, cm \times 2 \, cm  $   cells. The EQE spectra were obtained using a solar cell spectral response/QE/EQE measurement system with a chopping frequency of 100 Hz, and a Si-based diode for calibration. The dark J-V characteristics of the hole-only devices were also measured by a Keithley 2400 source and the trap state density was determined by the trap-filled limit voltage. Transient photovoltage measurements were performed at steady state under continuous illumination. The system was perturbed using a red light source, which was set to a suitably low level to the white diode array with light intensity equivalent to   $  100 \, mW cm^2  $  . The resulting voltage transient was recorded on a digital oscilloscope and the recombination lifetime was extracted from the decay curves using single-exponential fitting. The light-soaking stability was tested on a home-made illumination resistance test system equipped with a Class AAA solar simulator and a Keithley for automatic recording of the J-V curves. The light-soaking stability test was measured at continuous illumination with a 420 nm long-pass UV-filter under AM 1.5G at room temperature, measured in a   $  N_2  $  -filled glovebox. # 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. The Effect of Polymer Interlayer Modification on Surface Properites

The GO studied in this work were prepared using a modified Hummers method.  $  ^{[37]}  $   The morphology and quality of synthesized GO has been firstly characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) characterization. As shown in Fig. S1 (Supporting Information), the GO used here exhibits few layered sheets with a typical thickness of 2 nm. The GO films spin-coated on ITO substrates was then heated for 20 min at 130 °C to obtain HTL. At enough high transmittance, the resistances of the GO films vary from 17.2 Ω to 107.4 Ω with the film thickness increasing from 5 nm to 20 nm (Fig. S2, Supporting Information), respectively. And increase of the GO film thicknesses would lead to decreased sheet resistance, lower transmittance and increased charge recombination. To ensure the charge transport and coverage, the thin GO film with a thickness of 10 nm was adopted in this works.

Towards the demand of full perovskite coverage and high crystallinity, we first revealed possible changes of upper perovskite morphology as a function of surface energy of the interlayer. Here, a wide range of wetting and non-wetting polymers, including poly(bis(4-phenyl)(2,4,6-trimethylphenyl)amine) (PTAA), poly[9,9-bis((N,N,N-triethylammonium)-hexyl)-2,7-fluorene] dibromide (PFNBr) $ ^{[38]} $  and poly[N-(4-sulfonatophenyl)-4,4'-diphenylamine-alt-N-(p-trifluoromethyl)phenyl-4,4'-diphenylamine] sodium salt (PTFTS) $ ^{[39]} $  were chosen for investigation. The chemical structures of these polymers are shown in Scheme 1. Among them, PTAA is taken as a representative hydrophobic modifier, while conjugated polyelectrolyte PFNBr provides wetting surfaces. And the presence of hydrophobic backbone with trifluoromethyl groups in amphiphilic conjugated polyelectrolyte PTFTS makes it less hydrophilic when compared with PFNBr. As evidenced in contacting angles of Fig. 1a, the contact angles of perovskite precursor solutions on GO/PTAA, GO/PFNBr and GO/PTFTS are  $ 78^{\circ} $ ,  $ 21^{\circ} $  and  $ 44^{\circ} $ , respectively, whereas the contact angle on pristine GO is  $ 29^{\circ} $ . Previous works have verified that a non-

wetting surface would suppress heterogeneous nucleation and result in larger grain size. As shown in Fig. 1b, S3 and S4 (Supporting Information), we also performed top and cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) measurements of perovskite films deposited onto various underlayers. In line with previous findings,  $  ^{[35]}  $  the average grain size is largest for MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  films on hydrophobic PTAA interlayer and smallest on hydrophilic PFNBr interlayer, which presents a clear correlation between the substrate surface wetting capability and the grain size. And the introduction of polymer modifiers results in denser growth of upper perovskite layer without voids (Fig. S4, Supporting Information). Meanwhile, the X-ray diffraction peaks become stronger and sharper for perovskite films on hydrophobic surface, indicating better film crystallinity (Fig. 1c,d). For example, the average sizes of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  grains on GO/PTAA and GO/PTFTS are 800 nm and 550 nm, respectively. However, it is worth noting that the hydrophobic GO/PTAA underlayer impose a challenge in fabricating large-scale pinhole-free perovskite film on it, because the greatly polarity differences between the hydrophobic surface and the hydrophilic perovskite precursor result in a severe dewetting problem during processing.  $  ^{[40]}  $  As confirmed by optical images (Fig. S5, Supporting Information), the resulting MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  film on GO/PTAA do exist pinholes and voids in a wide area. In contrast, the amphiphilic properties of PTFTS not only ensure the good crystallinity of perovskite film but also remove the wetting problem of the perovskite film on the modified HTL. Since the underlayer may influence the packing structure of perovskite, the orientation of the films was examined by grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) measurement under synchrotron radiation. As shown in Fig. S6 (Supporting Information), the main perovskite peaks in the GIWAXS map show similar discrete Bragg spots for the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  films grown on GO and GO/PTFTS layers, indicating the interfacial interlayer do not cause crystal orientation change.

Not only the surface energy, but also electronic properties of the ITO/GO substrates would be changed by inserting these different polymers. The ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) results of the GO surfaces with and without polymer modification are presented in Fig. 2a, while the energy level alignments of pure polymers are show in Supporting Information (Fig. S7). In the figure, the left panel exhibits the photoemission cutoff to extract work function, while the right panel displays the top of the occupied

With the treatment of PTAA and PTFTS, the valence bands of modified GO films are 0.32 eV and 0.44 eV higher than that of bare GO film, respectively. Together with MAPbI $ _{3} $ , these energy alignments are summarized in Fig. 2b. It indicates that both PTAA and PTFTS treatment can precisely tune the electronic properties to obtain desired energy levels as design. $ ^{[41]} $  For PTAA modification, the polymer interlayer can form a graded HTL with GO, providing a large driving force for hole extraction (Fig. 2c). For PTFTS case, the HOMO of the interlayer is aligned well with that of the perovskite film, which enables an energy-lossless charge extraction at the interface. However, we found that the spectral shift of the GO/PFNBr film was negligible, which imply the insignificant charge interaction between them without interfacial dipole effect. This result is similar with previous works $ ^{[15,16]} $  using PFN derivates as an interlayer in HTL interface. In addition, we measured UV–vis absorption and transmittance spectra of GO/polymer in comparison with pristine GO film (Fig. S8, Supporting Information). After modification with polymer interlayer, all the modified GO films show stronger absorption in the UV range than that of bare GO film, but display similar high transparency in the visible wavelength range (400-800 nm). It indicates that the introduction of conjugated polymer may be beneficial for light-soaking stability by protecting the perovskite from UV radiation, and would hardly disturb light harvesting efficiency in solar cells.

# 3.2. The Effect of Interlayer Modification on Photovoltaic Performance

To directly evaluate the impact of polymer interlayer on device performance, we fabricated inverted planar PSCs with a glass/ITO/GO/polymer/MAPbI $ _3 $ /PC $ _{61} $ BM/bathocuproine (BCP)/Ag configuration (Scheme 1). For comparison, reference devices based on bare GO were also made. Fig. 4a presents the photovoltaic performance obtained by measuring the current density–voltage curve ( $ J-V $ ) under AM 1.5 G light illumination. For our champion GO device, we obtained an open-circuit voltage  $ V_{OC} $  of 1.05 V with a fill factor (FF) of 73.9 %, a short circuit current density ( $ J_{SC} $ ) of 19.22 mA cm $ ^{-2} $ , and a PCE of 14.91%, which is consistent with previous results for the GO devices. $ ^{[22,32]} $  Encouragingly, introducing all polymer interlayers led to considerable improvements of the cell efficiency (see Table 1 for device parameters). The optimized thickness of the interlayer films was no more than 5 nm, and further increase in the thickness caused a lost in device performance (Fig. S11, Supporting Information), due to the increase of interfacial resistance. In particular, the device containing PTFTS exhibited the best PCE of 18.82%, with a Voc of 1.10 V, a Jsc of 20.89 mA cm $ ^{-2} $ , and a FF of 0.819. Apart from enhanced FF and Jsc values, the significantly improvement of PCE is attributed to its increased Voc, which is responsible for the good matching of the HOMO levels between the perovskite and PTFTS. Meantime, the PSCs based on GO/PTAA showed PCE as high as 18.01%, which was comparable with that of PTFTS device. However, we found the device performance based on GO/PFNBr was slight improved to 15.54%, only with increased FF values. In

addition, we also fabricated the PSCs using bare polymers as HTLs to investigate their hole behaviors (Fig. S12 in Supporting Information). The device results confirm that the hydrophilic PFNBr would hardly affect the electronic properties of pristine GO, which is consistent with energy-level results.

The fabrication procedure was repeated in several batches to confirm the reliability and reproducibility of the modified method. The statistical results (inset figure) are collected from 40 samples for each condition, revealing that the average values of PCE (reverse scan) are also generally increased after the polymer modification. # 3.3. Microscopic Origins of Photovoltaic Efficiency

One straightforward reason for superior PCE of PTAA-based or PTFTS-based devices is the improved upper perovskite film quality, which influences the charge transport within the perovskite active layers. Besides, the reduced charge recombination at GO/MAPbI $ _3 $  interface due to front-contact passivation also contributes to an efficient electron extraction and thus the enhancement of  $ J_{\mathrm{SC}} $ . In order to accurately identify the passivation

$$

where   $  \varepsilon  $   and   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   are the dielectric constants of perovskite and vacuum permittivity, respectively, e is the elementary charge, and L is the thickness of the perovskite film. We further proceeded to illustrate how the polymer interlayers (PTFTS and PTAA) affect the charge carrier recombination dynamics within PSCs, consequently enhancing the performance via their impacts on Voc and FF. As shown in Fig. 4e, electrical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was measured under dark condition to understand the interfacial dynamics. Using equivalent circuit model, the recombination resistance  $ (R_{\mathrm{rec}}) $  is assigned to low-frequency arc and should be mainly associated with the charge recombination occurring at the interfaces; hence its value reflects the interface properties at the perovskite/HTLs. It is clear that the device based on both PTAA and PTFTS exhibits larger  $ R_{\mathrm{rec}} $  than that of GO and GO/PFNBr, which is attributed to their hole-selective transporting properties (Fig. 2c). On the other hand, measuring the  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  as a function of light intensity on GO-based device (Fig. S17, Supporting Information) with and without interlayers reveals a similar ideality factor for the PFNBr-containing device, verifying that the hydrophilic interlayers cannot completely change the overall nature of the

recombination process. In comparison, the smaller slope values of both PTAA- and PTFTS-modified devices than the control GO device indicate their less tendencies toward trap-assisted recombination. In addition, we performed transient photovoltage decay (TPV) measurement, which provide directly observation of charge recombination at the semiconductor/contact interface during device operation condition. Since no charges are collected in the outer circuit, the lifetime fitted from the decay curve is proportional to the charge recombination rate near the interface. Fig. 4f compares the interfacial charge recombination lifetime  $ (\tau_{\mathrm{n}}) $  from TPV for all GO-based devices. It is apparent that devices modified with PTFTS or PTAA show longer  $ \tau_{\mathrm{n}} $  than bare GO-based device, which reflects less charge recombination probability in optimized device. We further measured the decay curve as a function of white light intensity and summarized the fitted effective-lifetime values in the inset figure. In all the light intensities, the device modified with PTFTS has longer recombination lifetime compared to the reference device. The reduced interfacial recombination and longer charge lifetime therefore allow the photoexcited charges to be extracted more efficiently before they recombine during device operation, which manifests as a higher Voc and FF.

It is worth noting that PTFTS interlayer enables the devices to achieve slightly improved performance, in compared with the PTAA-based devices. It suggests that the incorporation of PTFTS would promote efficient charge extraction, which therefore lead to a reduced bimolecular recombination and hence increased carrier lifetime. From the structure view, the triphenylamine building unit in both conjugated polymers meet the request of efficient hole transfer with deep lying HOMOs. The results indicate that the polyelectrolytes unit with charged sulfonate in PTFTS can also play an important role in the interfacial modification. Next, we systematically investigated the structural and chemical interactions of the layers to understand this discrepancy. Not only the growth of overlying perovskite film is controlled by the surface features of polymer interlayer, charge-carrier characteristics would also be influenced by the chemical interactions between interlayer and GO layer. To address this point, we examined the interface chemistry of polymer interlayer in both sides using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). For the upper side, Fig. 5a presents the Pb 4f and I 3d spectra for a diluted  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite precursor (0.05 M) deposited on bare GO, GO/PTAA or GO/PTFTS.

The in-depth understanding of the interfacial interaction and recombination gives us experimental guidance on how the structure–property relationships of polymer interlayer play a crucial role for achieving high efficiency PSCs. Taken together, the device characteristics point to a schematical illustration of charge transport as shown in Fig. 5c. The studied interlayer in this work can be fallen into three categories. In the absence of charge-selective properties (PFNBr), both the electrons and holes can transport to the GO HTL and recombine there, leading to serious interfacial charge recombination and thus inferior charge collection. In the presence of p-type charge-transporting property and suitable energy levels (PTAA), incorporation of such interlayer allows efficient transport of holes at GO/perovskite interface. Furthermore, choosing an appropriate p-type dopant with proper chemical structure (PTFTS) for strong interfacial affinity is benefit for charge extraction thus resulting in effective hole injection from perovskite into to anode. # 3.4. Performance of Large-area Perovskite Solar Cell

Successful large-scale fabrication of the PSCs relies on reproducibility and large-area uniformity. Next, we examined the superiority of polymer interlayer in terms of large-area devices. Here, we selected PTAA and PTFTS as representative interfacial polymers for testing the area dependence of the PCE. Fig. 6a shows typical images of large device area  $ (1.08\mathrm{cm}^2) $  on a  $ 4\mathrm{cm}^2 $  substrate. From the images, we could easily discern that the introduction of a hydrophobic PTAA interlayer imposed a difficulty for achieving high surface coverage of perovskite films. The average PCE of large-scale devices based on bare GO was  $ 11.01 \pm 0.85\% $ , which is obviously lower and exhibits a larger standard deviation than that of  $ 16.69 \pm 0.59\% $  for the devices prepared from PTFTS-modified cells. Fig. 6b exhibits the reverse-scan  $ J-V $  curves of the PTFTS-treated PSC, exhibiting a Jsc of  $ 20.41\mathrm{mA cm}^{-2} $ , a Voc of  $ 1.09\mathrm{V} $ , and a FF of  $ 78.3\% $ , with a maximum PCE of  $ 17.42\% $  on a large-area over  $ 1\mathrm{cm}^2 $ . Typically, with PTAA interlayer, only one out of 30 large-area cells resulted in efficiencies above  $ 16\% $ , while this PCE could be easily achieved with 28 out of 30 cells if PTFTS was applied (Fig. S20, Supporting Information). # 3.5. Device Stability

In addition to the efficiency, the mechanical stability is also an important parameter for evaluation of the performance of the bendable devices. As shown in Fig. 6f, the large-area GO device demonstrates excellent resistance for the mechanical bending by maintaining  $ \approx 80\% $  of its initial PCE even after 1000 bending cycles (bending radii,  $ r = 10~\mathrm{mm} $ ), which are superior to their inorganic counterparts (such as  $ \mathrm{NiO_x} $ ).[49] Surprisingly, dip-coated PTFTS-based flexible devices show superior mechanical durability, with no more than  $ 10\% $  loss even after 1000 bending cycles. Thanks to its organic polymeric nature and non-covalent chemical interactions between perovskite and PTFTS, the presence of interlayer can protect the hybrid halide perovskite materials from fracture and delamination. And the main loss of the device performance after 500 bending cycles is the result of the decreased FF caused by the increased resistance that can be ascribed to the mechanical damage of the ITO electrode.[13] Therefore, mechanical stability would be further significantly improved by employing ITO-free electrode, thus setting a new standard for flexible PSCs.

In spite of huge advantages of high efficiency and scalability by introducing PTFTS interlayer, stability is another essential issue that needs to be evaluated for the commercialization. As displayed in Fig. 7a, we firstly compared the moisture stability of unencapsulated large-area devices with and without the PTFTS interlayer exposure to air (relative humidity range of 30–60%) at room temperature under the dark condition. PTFTS-treatment (dip-coated method) makes efficiency much more stable because of the fluorinated polymer interlayer, acting as a moisture barrier. And the device based on GO/PTFTS retained over 80% of its initial PCE after 500 h exposure, while the GO

ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

device without modification showed degradation by more than 60%. And we observed no perovskite decomposition even after 3 weeks storage, as seen from the XRD results (Fig. S23, Supporting Information). Furthermore, the light soaking test under AM 1.5G sun illumination was conducted to investigate the photostability of our GO-based PSCs. In order to eliminate the effect of moisture and UV radiation, the devices were put into glovebox filled with nitrogen for complete measurement with a 420-nm cutoff UV filter. The slightly increased PCE in the first tens of hours (Fig. 7b) may be caused by light-induced defect healing in the perovskite active layer. The PTFTS-modified solar cells retained 90% of their initial performance after continuous operation for 500 hours, while the efficiency of the control PSC degraded to □70% of the initial efficiency during this period with apparent drops in   $  V_{OC}  $   and FF (Fig. S24, Supporting Information). The photodegradation of device performance stems from a number of factors, among which the interfacial degradation induced by GO/MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   interface can be well suppressed by the PTFTS treatment. Besides, reduction of trap-assisted nonradiative recombination at HTL interface might also be a main reason for the enhancement of light stability. Finally, we investigated the UV-induced stability issue in our GO-based device, which is one of benefit for inverted structure. Especially for GO/PTFTS layer, its intense absorbability in the UV region suggests means less catalytic reaction and less decomposition of perovskite layer.  $  ^{[50]}  $   As shown in Fig. 7c, the control GO devices in this UV induced aging test, continuously irradiated under illumination at 5 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $   intensity, lost 15% of their initial efficiency after 100 h of exposure. Encouragingly, no obvious degradation of PTFTS-modified device performance could be founded after 100 h UV radiation. This result confirms emphasize on the unique role of PTFTS interlayer in preventing device degradation under illumination.

# 4. Conclusions

In summary, interfacial engineering by modification GO film with various functional polymer interlayers allowed us to identify the origin of recombination channels at the front-contact interface in inverted type planar PSCs. We also found that all the requirements to achieve efficient and reproducible large-area PSCs, including high

ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

crystalline perovskite grain-growth, uniform perovskite coverage and suitable energy alignment, could be fulfilled by applying an amphiphilic polymer with both triphenylamine and polar functional groups. In line with this discovery, we achieved a considerable improvement of the device performance by applying PTFTS as interfacial layer between GO HTL and perovskite absorber to suppress the recombination losses at the front-contact, which result in significant increase of  $  V_{\mathrm{OC}}  $  and FF. In addition, sulfonate charged PTFTS brings unexpected benefits of resulting in strong interaction between GO and PTFTS as the binding force, thus realizing the formation of uniform interfacial layer onto GO in a simple wet-chemical technique. As a result, the best PCEs for devices on an aperture area of  $  1.08~\mathrm{cm}^2  $  fabricated on rigid and flexible substrates were  $  18.2\%  $  and  $  17.0\%  $ , respectively. Moreover, our approach shows several significant advantages in enhancing the scalability, mechanical stability, and environmental stability of PSCs, which are key features for the commercialization of flexible devices. This work demonstrates how interfacial recombination losses can be unambiguously identified and suppressed through introduction of special molecule interlayer, and paves the way to advance the development of graphene materials for efficient flexible perovskite photovoltaics.

# Interfacial Engineering of Front-Contact with Finely Tuned Polymer Interlayers for High-performance Large-area Flexible Perovskite Solar Cells

Highlights:

1. For the first time, the interface between graphene oxide hole □ transport layer and perovskite layer can be delicately controlled using an amphiphilic conjugated polyelectrolyte to achieve a high-quality front-contact for inverted perovskite solar cell.

2. Sulfonate charged polyelectrolyte brings unexpected benefits of resulting in strong interaction between GO and interlayer as the binding force, thus realizing the scalable fabrication of uniform interfacial layer onto GO in a simple wet-chemical technique.

3. Benefiting from this front-contact modification strategy, efficient and stable flexible PSCs with an active area over  $ 1\mathrm{cm}^2 $  exhibit a record power conversion efficiency of  $ 17.0\% $ .

4. Introduction of functional polymer interlayers paves the way to advance the development of graphene materials for efficient flexible perovskite photovoltaics.
