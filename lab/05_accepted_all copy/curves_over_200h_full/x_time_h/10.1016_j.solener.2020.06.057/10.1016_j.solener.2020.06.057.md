# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite materials have attracted extensive attention due to their excellent optoelectronic properties, i.e., excellent light absorption (Stranks et al., 2013; Mei et al., 2014), high carrier mobility (Jeon et al., 2014; Heo et al., 2013; Wehrenfennig et al., 2014), low exciton binding energies (Mei et al., 2014; Oga et al., 2014; Snaith, 2013); long carrier diffusion length (Stranks et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2017), excellent ambipolar carrier transport properties (Oga et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2017), and the tailorable band gaps (Jacobsson et al., 2016; Burschka et al., 2013), contributing to excellent photovoltaic applications. In recent years, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of the PSCs have dramatically increased from  $ 3.8\% $  in 2009 to  $ 25.2\% $  in 2020 (Kojima et al., 2009; Jiang et al., 2019; Laboratory, 2020), which is unprecedented in the field of photovoltaics. Therefore, the PSCs are regarded as promising candidates for the high efficiency solar cells. Among various perovskite materials, formamidinium lead iodide  $ (\mathrm{FAPbI}_3) $ -based perovskite is used widely due to its suitable band gap  $ (1.48\mathrm{eV}) $  (Kim et al., 2019). However, apart from black  $ \alpha $ -phase perovskite,  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  has also a yellow non-perovskite  $ \delta $ -phase, whereas only the  $ \alpha $ -phase perovskite is available for PSCs (Han et al., 2016). A number of recent studies have identified that substituting a fraction of organic cation  $ \mathrm{FA}^+ $  with  $ \mathrm{MA}^+ $  and  $ \mathrm{Cs}^+ $  can

Recently, planar-structure perovskite solar cells processed by low-temperature solution method have attracted tremendous attention. However, the interfacial carrier recombination limits the further improvement in efficiency and causes serious hysteresis. Therefore, the effective interface passivation techniques are necessary to further improve the efficiency of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). In this work, we demonstrate that an ultrathin mixture layer of PMMA:PCBM with the optimization ratio of 1:2 can effectively passivate defects at the electron transport layer/perovskite interface, thus significantly inhibiting the interfacial carrier recombination, contributing to the improved open-circuit voltage  $ (\mathrm{V_{oc}}) $  and negligible hysteresis effect. The PMMA:PCBM mixture passivation layer elevates the  $ \mathrm{V_{oc}} $  of triple-cation perovskite  $ (\mathrm{Cs}_{0.05}(\mathrm{FA}_{0.85}\mathrm{MA}_{0.15})_{0.95}\mathrm{Pb}(\mathrm{I}_{0.85}\mathrm{Br}_{0.15})_3) $  solar cells by  $ 40\mathrm{mV} $ , with champion device achieving a  $ \mathrm{V_{oc}} $  of  $ 1.17\mathrm{V} $ . The maximum efficiency of the passivated PSCs is  $ 18.63\% $  with negligible hysteresis. Moreover, the interfacial passivation method improves the long-term stability of PSCs, which retains  $ 82\% $  initial performance after over  $ 768\mathrm{h} $  of storage at room temperature. This passivation approach successfully provides an avenue for further improving the efficiency and stability of PSCs.

Therefore, the FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  -based perovskite doped by Cs  $  ^{+}  $   and/or MA  $  ^{+}  $   is an excellent material for the light absorption layer of PSCs. Unfortunately, the intrinsic instability of PSCs (especially humidity instability) impede their commercialized applications in spite of the unparalleled growth rate in PCEs.

Hitherto, most reported PSCs are subject to a relatively low   $  V_{oc}  $   (far from the theoretical limit of 1.33 V) (Yang et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2014), and the notorious hysteresis effect in current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics. Recent researches have demonstrated that these issues are attributed to the existence of defects and trap states at the interfaces of electron-transport layer (ETL)/perovskite and/or perovskite/hole-transport layer (HTL) (Giordano et al., 2016; Correa-Baena et al., 2017; Ke et al., 2016). In general, defects and trap states lead to the interfacial carrier recombination. The detrimental carrier recombination would restrict the diffusion of carriers (Reid et al., 2016), reduce the charge carrier lifetime (Wang et al., 2017), lead to voltage loss (Tan et al., 2017), thus limiting the improvement of PCE and stability of PSCs. In addition, charge carrier recombination, ion diffusion and migration in the perovskite films are considered to be responsible for the serious hysteresis effect (Richardson et al., 2016). It is reported that perovskite films prepared by solution method are usually polycrystalline, which means numerous defects are formed at the surfaces and grain boundaries of perovskite in the fabrication process (Wang et al., 2016; Manser et al., 2016; Duan et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2014; Ouyang et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2020; Yao et al., 2020). In general, there are three main methods for effective defect-passivation to reduce the impact of interfacial recombination of PSCs. The first approach is introducing an excellent passivation material into the antisolvent during the preparation of perovskite thin films (Li et al., 2019; Bi et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2016). Accordingly, Bi et al. (2017) introduced a passivation material of polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) into the anti-solvent, resulting in smoother perovskite films with fewer defects and larger grains compared to the control films. This facile and effective interface strategy can simultaneously improve the PCE, suppress the hysteresis, and decelerate the degradation rate in performance. Li et al. (2019) have shown that adding dithienobenzodithiophene-based  $ \pi $ -conjugated polymers (PDTBDT-FBT) to anti-solvent can simultaneously passivate the defects and promote the transport of holes, thus leading to more homogeneous morphology and indistinct grain bounds of the perovskite films. Ultimately, the passivated devices exhibited a higher efficiency and a better stability when stored in air. The second approach is introducing halide compounds into the perovskite precursor, which can passivate defects of organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite efficiently (Li et al., 2019; Cao et al., 2018; Abdi-Jalebi et al., 2018). For instance, Li et al. (2019) added alkali halidesodium fluoride (NaF) into a precursor solution of triple-cation perovskite (CsFAMA) with a two-step preparation process. It is obvious that the fluoride ions can prevent the degradation of perovskite effectively. Therefore, the modified devices exhibited remarkable long-term stability, retaining  $ 90\% $  initial PCE after  $ 1000\mathrm{h} $  continuous illumination. Abdi-Jalebi et al. (2018) added potassium halide (KI) into the CsFAMA precursor, which could passivate the dangling bonds of the perovskite by exploiting the associated intermolecular interaction. After passivation, there was a significant increase in  $ \mathrm{V_{oc}} $  from  $ 1.05\mathrm{V} $  to  $ 1.17\mathrm{V} $ . The third approach is introducing a passivation layer between the charge transport layers and the perovskite film (Jiang et al., 2019; Richardson et al., 2016; Abdi-Jalebi et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2018). Liu et al. (2018) have proved that a thin alkaline halide (KCl) layer between the ETL and perovskite interface can simultaneous passivate defects of positive and negative charges by sufficient utilization of  $ \mathrm{K}^+ $  and  $ \mathrm{Cl^-} $ . The interfacial carrier lifetime was extended and the trap density was decreased by inserting the KCl passivation layer. Peng et al. (2017) have applied an ultrathin polymer-fullerene films to restrain interfacial carrier recombination at the mesoporous- $ \mathrm{TiO_2 / RbCsFAMA} $  perovskite interface, raising the  $ \mathrm{V_{oc}} $  to  $ 1.16\mathrm{V} $ . Jiang et al. (2019) adopted an organic halide salt, phenethylammonium iodide (PEAI), for the post-treatment between the perovskite and HTL interface, which can effectively restrain the surface defects of perovskite. As a result, a very high  $ \mathrm{V_{oc}} $  of  $ 1.18\mathrm{V} $  and a  $ 23.32\% $  certified PCE have been achieved. Although these investigations demonstrated that suppressing interfacial charge recombination is crucial for the improvement of  $ \mathrm{V_{oc}} $  and PCE, only a few literatures reported so far have prepared solar cells with  $ \mathrm{V_{oc}} > 1.16\mathrm{V} $ . Consequently, it is critical to take action to suppress the formation of defects and trap states to prepare high-efficiency PSCs.

In this work, we apply an ultrathin PMMA: PCBM mixture layer to passivate the defects and restrain the interfacial carrier recombination between the stannic oxide  $ (\mathrm{SnO}_2) $  and perovskite interface (the preparation temperature of the whole process is less than  $ 150^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ ). As is known, PMMA is an excellent insulated passivated material used as a passivation layer in PSCs (Bi et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2018). PCBM not only an excellent ETL material but also an interfacial passivation material in PSCs (Zheng et al., 2017), but its passivation effect is not as good as PMMA. In addition, the excellent passivation role of PMMA is

achieved with the sacrifice of conductivity. Therefore, the PMMA:PCBM mixed materials can effectively adjust the conductivity and passivation effect of the passivation layer. We take advantage of the synergistic effect of PMMA:PCBM to maximize the efficiency, resulting in an enhanced PCE of  $ 18.63\% $  ,a  $ \mathrm{V_{oc}} $  of 1.17 V and negligible hysteresis. The PMMA:PCBM passivation layer significantly enhances the  $ \mathrm{V_{oc}} $  of the PSCs, consequently increases the PCE by  $ \sim 12.5\% $  compared with the controlled device. In addition, the passivated PSCs exhibit an excellent long-term stability, retaining  $ 82\% $  initial PCE after over  $ 768\mathrm{h} $  of storage at room temperature without encapsulation. The results highlight the importance of defect passivation in the preparation of high-efficiency solar cells.

# 2. Experimental section

# 2.1. Materials

$ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  colloidal dispersion (15 wt% in  $ \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} $ ) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. Lead iodide  $ (\mathrm{PbI}_2, 99.9\%) $ , formamidinium iodide (FAI,  $ 99.9\% $ ), lead bromide  $ (\mathrm{PbBr}_2, 99.9\%) $ , methylammonium bromide (MABr,  $ >99.5\% $ ), cesium iodide (CsI,  $ 99.99\% $  and 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-di-p-methoxyphenylamine)-9,9'-spirobifluorene(Spiro-OMeTAD,  $ 99.8\% $  ) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp.N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, anhydrous  $ 99.8\% $  ), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO,  $ 99.8\% $  ), chlorobenzene (CB, anhydrous  $ 99.8\% $  ), ethyl acetate (anhydrous,  $ 99.8\% $  ) and acetonitrile (anhydrous,  $ 99.8\% $  ) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All reagents were employed as received without further purification.

# 2.2. Fabrication of halide perovskite solar cell

ITO glass was rinsed sequentially by detergent, deionized water, acetone, ethyl alcohol, and isopropanol. Before using, the ITO was cleaned by ultraviolet ozone (UVO) for 30 min. SnO₂ colloidal solution was diluted to the concentration of 2.5 wt% by deionized water. The SnO₂ ETL was fabricated by spin-coating the diluted SnO₂ colloidal dispersion at 4000 rpm for 30 s on ITO substrates and then annealed at 150 °C for 30 min in ambient air. Then, the substrates were transferred into a nitrogen glove box (the concentration of oxygen and moisture less than 1.0 ppm) immediately. To assess the passivation effect of ultrathin PMMA: PCBM films at the perovskite/ETL interface, PSCs with different passivation layers were prepared. The controlled device (no passivation layer) and devices with different ratios of PMMA: PCBM (w/w) were prepared, such as 1:0 (pure PMMA), 1:2 and 0:1 (pure PCBM). The pure PMMA precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 1 mg PMMA into 1 mL CB. The pure PCBM precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 1 mg PCBM into 1 mL CB. The PMMA: PCBM ratios of 1:2 (abbreviated as PMMA: PCBM hereafter) mixed precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 1 mg PMMA and 2 mg PCBM into 1 mL CB. The ultrathin passivation layer was fabricated by spin-coating the precursor solution at 4000 rpm for 30 s on ITO/SnO₂ substrates and then annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. The CsFAMA mixed halide perovskite precursor was prepared as reported in the literature (Bu et al., 2018). 1.105 M FAI (190.0 mg), 0.195 M MABr (21.8 mg), 1.19 M PbI₂ (548.6 mg), and 0.21 M PbBr₂ (77.0 mg) were dissolved in 1 mL mixture solvent of DMF/DMSO (the volume ratio is 4:1). In addition, a 2 M (520 mg/mL) CsI solution was pre-dissolved in DMSO. 34 μL CsI solution was added to the above-mixed perovskite precursor to form the specific proportion of Cs₀.₀₅(FA₀.₈₅MA₀.₁₅)₀.₉₅Pb(I₀.₈₅Br₀.₁₅)₃. The mixed perovskite precursor is stirring in 60 °C for 30 min, then in 28 °C for 120 min. The CsFAMA perovskite thin film was spin-coated on SnO₂ and SnO₂/PMMA: PCBM substrates by a two-step spin-coating program: a slow step at 2000 rpm for 10 s, and a subsequent fast step at 6000 rpm for 25 s. At the last 5th seconds, 100 μL ethyl acetate was rapidly dropped in the middle of the substrates and then the deposited films were annealed at 100 °C for 45 min. The

# 2.3. Device characterizations

The crystal structures of perovskite films were characterized by X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD, PA Nalytical Empyrean Xpert Materials Research diffractometer equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (  $  \lambda = 1.5406  $   Å)). Optical absorption spectra of perovskite films were measured by Shimadzu UV-1800 UV–Vis spectrophotometer. The surface morphology of as-deposited films and cross-sectional images of devices were characterized by JEOL JSM-7800F field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM). The atomic-force microscopy (AFM) was analyzed by a Bruker Dimension Icon AFM. The Incident Photon-to-Current Efficiency (IPCE) was characterized by a Newport-74125 system (Newport Instruments). The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra were obtained by a fluorescence spectrophotometer (Cary Eclipse, Agilent) at the excitation wavelength of 530 nm. The time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) were recorded by a combined fluorescence lifetime and steady-state spectrometer (FLS980, Edinburgh Instruments Ltd.). The J-V characteristics were achieved by an AM 1.5G solar simulator (Newport, 2612A). It is worth noting that reverse scan is from   $  V_{oc}  $   to   $  J_{sc}  $   (1.2 V → -0.1 V), and forward scan is from   $  J_{sc}  $   to   $  V_{oc}  $   (-0.1 V → 1.2 V). The dwell time and voltage steps were 30 ms and 10 mV, respectively. The active area of all devices was 0.11 cm² and the measurements of PCE were in the air with a relative humidity of 50–60% (the ambient temperature of ~30 °C).

# 3. Results and discussion

In this work, the structure of planar heterojunction PSCs is Indium tin oxide (ITO)/ $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $ /Different passivation layer/Perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD/Ag, where the perovskite represent  $ \mathrm{Cs}_{0.05}(\mathrm{FA}_{0.85}\mathrm{MA}_{0.15})_{0.95}\mathrm{Pb(I_{0.85}Br_{0.15})_3} $ . The energy level diagram of the PSCs is shown in Fig. 1(a), the conduction band minimum (CBM) of  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  (-4.5 eV) matches well with the CBM of the perovskite (-3.9 eV), which can ensure the efficient and fast transport of electron. Fig. 1(b) shows the cross-sectional SEM image of the mixed PMMA: PCBM passivated PSC device. The multilayered structures are formed with the individual thicknesses of  $ 30~\mathrm{nm} $ ,  $ 460~\mathrm{nm} $  and  $ 200~\mathrm{nm} $  for  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $ , CsFAMA and Spiro-OMeTAD, respectively.

Fig. 1(c) shows the J-V curves of the CsFAMA PSCs with different passivation layers under reverse scan. The dependence of the cell performance on the different passivation layers is shown in Fig. 1(d). The critical parameters of PSCs are summarized in Table 1, including   $  V_{oc}  $  , short current density (  $  J_{sc}  $  ), fill factor (FF), and PCE in the forward scan (from   $  J_{sc}  $   to   $  V_{oc}  $  ) and reverse scan (from   $  V_{oc}  $   to   $  J_{sc}  $  ). Compared to the controlled cell, the PCE of the cell introduced with pure PMMA and pure PCBM passivation layers improved to 17.43% and 17.80%, respectively. Moreover, when further adjusted the ratio of PMMA:PCBM, the PCE increased to 18.63%. As a result, the best ratio of PMMA:PCBM to acquire the champion performance is 1:2. In addition, to investigate the effect of the ratio of PMMA:PCBM on the device performance, the device with different concentration ratios of PMMA:PCBM was fabricated. The J-V curves under reverse scan with the different concentrations of PMMA:PCBM passivation layers are exhibited in Fig. S1. This figure shows that accurate regulating the ratio of PMMA:PCBM can optimize their synergy effect to achieve the highest cell efficiency.

Fig. 1(e) shows the J-V curves of the passivated champion PSCs and the controlled device. The controlled device, without any passivation layer, shows a PCE of 17.32%,   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.13 V,   $  J_{sc}  $   of 21.68, and FF of 70.83% received from a reverse scan at a rate of 40 mV s  $  ^{-1}  $  . However, the J-V curves suffer severe hysteresis when measured by a forward scan at the same rate, where the PCE of is thus 1.03% lower than that of reverse scan. On the contrary, the PMMA: PCBM passivated cell shows negligible difference between reverse and forward scan at a rate of 40 mV s  $  ^{-1}  $  , realizing a reverse scan (forward scan) PCE of 18.63% (18.21%),   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.17 V (1.17 V),   $  J_{sc}  $   of 22.73 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   (22.78 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ), and FF of 69.87% (68.52%). The increased   $  J_{sc}  $   of the PMMA: PCBM passivated cell was confirmed by the spectral response of IPCE spectrum, as shown in Fig. 1(f). Although both types of cells show almost the same shape of spectrogram, the PMMA: PCBM passivated cells have higher IPCE values in visible region (400–760 nm).

To investigate the influence of passivation layers on the morphology of perovskite, the top-view SEM images of perovskite with different passivation layers were characterized, as shown in Fig. 2(a-d). It was clear that all of the samples had the uniform dense morphology with white  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  phase distributed around the grain boundary. The particle size of perovskite was approximately  $ 300\mathrm{nm} $  for all samples, confirming that the passivation layer has mere influence on perovskite particle size. However, as shown in Fig. 2(a-d), the white  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  phase gradually decreased with the increase of passivation layers concentration. According to the previous report, the white  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  phase observed on the surface of sample was mainly from the decomposition of perovskite during the heating treatment (Liu et al., 2018). Therefore, it is inferred that the PMMA: PCBM passivation layer could effective inhibit the decomposition of perovskite, thus improving the stability of the PSCs. Moreover, the roughness of the PMMA: PCBM (1:2) passivated perovskite is increased to  $ 13.6\mathrm{nm} $  compared to  $ 12.5\mathrm{nm} $  for the controlled sample as evidenced by Atomic Force Microscope (AFM) measurements shown in Fig. 2(e-f). Due to the poor infiltration of perovskite precursor solution on the PMMA/PCBM passivated layer, the slightly higher roughness of the perovskite film is emerged. The roughness of perovskite films with passivation layers is shown in Fig. S2.

The XRD was used for investigating the composition of perovskite films. As shown in Fig. 3(a), the peak positions and intensity of all perovskite samples were almost identical, indicating the passivation layers have rarely influence for the phase and crystalline of perovskite. In particular, a weak diffraction peak located at  $ 12.6^{\circ} $  represented the  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  diffraction peak, other diffraction peaks originated from the perovskite phase (Zheng et al., 2017). As reported in previous literature, the formation of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  is due to partial decomposition of the perovskite layer (Jiang et al., 2016). Moreover, the relative peaks intensity of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  gradually decreased as the increases of the concentration of passivation layer. In addition, there is still residual  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  in the perovskite. It is reported in previous literature that the proper contents of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  can passivate the surface or grain boundary of perovskite layer and reduce the defects states of the perovskite, which is beneficial to improve the efficiency (Chen et al., 2014). The phenomenon suggested that the passivation layer could restrain the decomposition of perovskite to a certain degree, which proved that the passivation layer could effective passivate defects and increase the stability of perovskite (Jiang et al., 2017). The XRD patterns confirmed the same tendency about the quantity of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  as SEM results presented in Fig. 2.

To further investigate the properties of the CsFAMA perovskite film, UV–Vis spectroscopy has been applied to examine the light harvesting variation of perovskites upon the different passivation layers. It was found that the component of passivation layers influences the absorption and the bandgap slightly (Fig. 3(b)), which proved that the passivation layer had little effect on the crystallinity of perovskite. In addition, the transmittance of ITO/SnO  $  _{2}  $  substrates with/without passivation layers were measured to affirm whether the passivation layer affected the photon injection. As shown in Table 2, the un-passivated sample has a fast decay lifetime  $ \tau_{\mathrm{ave}} = 174.67 $  ns, whereas the CsFAMA films with the PMMA: PCBM (or PMMA) passivation layers have a slow decay lifetime  $ \tau_{\mathrm{ave}} = 222.73 $  ns (or  $ 597.52~\mathrm{ns} $ ). These results demonstrated that the PMMA: PCBM ultrathin passivation layer can effectively suppress the non-radiative recombination of the perovskite/ $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  interface by reducing the defects density and trap states (Peng et al., 2017). Moreover, with the introduction of the passivation layers, the emission peaks of PL were slightly redshifted, which could be attributed to the defect-passivation in CsFAMA by PMMA and PCBM. The similar passivation effect of perovskite with PCBM or  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  has been reported in the

literatures (Shao et al., 2014; Jiang et al., 2017).

were measured. Fig. 6 shows the distributions of performance parameters received from reverse scan measurements. The variation of   $  V_{oc}  $   are shown in Fig. 6(a). It is shown that the most significant impact of the passivation layers is the dramatic increase in   $  V_{oc}  $  . The significant improvement of   $  V_{oc}  $   can be attributed to the excellent passivation property of PMMA, which has been demonstrated by previous literatures (Yao et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2018). Moreover, the PMMA can also passivate defects/deep trap centers caused by the dangling bonds localized at the perovskite surface (Kong et al., 2016). As shown in Fig. 6(b–c), the   $  J_{sc}  $   and FF exhibit dependence on the passivation layer in some degrees. The average   $  J_{sc}  $   and FF for the pure PMMA and pure PCBM samples is lower than that of the controlled samples, which should be ascribed to the increased series resistance. Therefore, it is necessary to add PCBM (with higher electron mobility) into PMMA (with better passivation performance) to form PMMA: PCBM blend films to reduce the series resistance and increase the electronic transmission efficiency. Taking advantages of the synergy of PMMA: PCBM, the average   $  J_{sc}  $   and FF for the PMMA: PCBM samples is slightly higher than that of the controlled samples. In addition, since PCBM is considered to be absorbed in visible wavelengths (Brabec et al., 2010), the average   $  J_{sc}  $   and FF of the PCBM film is less than the value of controlled sample. The comprehensive results of the observed trends in   $  V_{oc}  $  , FF and   $  J_{sc}  $   are shown in Fig. 6(d), which show the PCEs distributions for different passivation layers. As a result, the average efficiency of cells increases from 16.3% for the controlled samples to 18.0% for the PMMA: PCBM passivated samples. The statistics of the PCE distribution demonstrate the reproducibility of our experiment and the passivating treatment can undoubtedly improve the performance of PSCs.

# 4. Conclusions

In summary, we have explored an effective and simple approach for the interfacial passivation of ETL/perovskite, where the defects are significantly reduced and the carrier recombination is suppressed. Accordingly, the efficiency and stability of devices were remarkably enhanced and the hysteresis was eliminated. By introducing an ultrathin PMMA: PCBM passivation layer to ETL/perovskite interface, we realize a dramatic increase in   $  V_{oc}  $   from 1.13 V (controlled device) to 1.17 V (passivated device), and obtain a maximum PCE of 18.63% with negligible hysteresis in planar PSCs. Without any encapsulation, these devices exhibit remarkable long-term stability, retaining 82% of their initial PCE after over 768 h of storage at room temperature. These results indicate that the effective defect passivation at the ETL/perovskite interface can contribute to the increase in   $  V_{oc}  $  , and the elimination of hysteresis. This passivation approach solves one of the main factors of limiting the improvement of efficiency: interfacial charges recombination, and illustrates the availability of fullerene-polymer passivation layer to improve the PCE in planar-structure PSCs.
