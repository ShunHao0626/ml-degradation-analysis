# ABSTRACT

Lead (Pb) $ ^{0} $  and iodine (I) $ ^{0} $  point defects generated during perovskite solar cell (PSC) fabrication and photoconversion form deep band energy levels as the carriers' recombination centers. These defects not only deteriorate device efficiency, but also facilitate chemical degradation with ion migration, resulting in restricted device lifetime. Herein, we present a novel type of phosphines as the point defects stabilizer for hybrid perovskite solar cells with enhanced performances. Three phosphines with varied side groups of tributyl, trioctyl and triphenyl are exampled as the dopants in perovskite films. The group dependent redox properties were observed in the perovskite film, dependent on their molecular weights and steric hindrances of phosphines. The partially oxidized tributyl phosphine (TBUP) with additional tributyl phosphine oxides (TBPO) is efficient in reduction of lead (Pb) $ ^{0} $  and iodine (I) $ ^{0} $  concentrations during the

Journal Pre-proofs

device fabrication and operation. The device with TBUP-TBPO pair showed enhanced power conversion efficiency (PCE) to 20.48% and maintain 91.7% of their initial PCEs after 500 h at 65 °C thermal annealing. Thus, this work presents an efficient route of utilize the phosphine species to reduce point defects in the perovskite film, which promoting further development of novel phosphorous additives with defects stabilization, interface passivation and encapsulation for low-cost solution processed PSCs.

Keywords: Phosphine organics; Redox; Perovskite solar cells; Lead defect; Stability

# 1. Introduction

With superior optoelectronic properties, such as long-range high carriers mobilities, large photon absorption coefficient, the balanced charge diffusion lengths, efficient photo-generated exciton dissociation and low density of deep trap states, the perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has drawn extensive attention and the power-conversion efficiency (PCE) has surge to over 25% [1–6]. This performance has been on par with that of polycrystalline silicon solar cells, but the poor stability under ambient operation conditions still hinders the applications of perovskite optoelectronics [7–10].

Hybrid perovskite are susceptible to various degradation factors, including moisture, oxygen, heat, electric field, and ultraviolet exposure   $  [11–14]  $  , which are the main causes of instable photovoltaic conversion for PSCs   $  [15]  $  . Many researches have exhibit effective elimination of external factors by encapsulation   $  [16–19]  $   and interface modification   $  [20]  $  , which has improved the stability of the device. However, even if the interface is well modified to block the corrosion of water and oxygen in the external environment, internal degradation stress such as electrical and thermal stress cannot be eliminated, which leads to decomposition of the perovskite   $  [21]  $  . In addition, various point defects such as atomic vacancies, interstitials, antisite substitutions, and higher-

Journal Pre-proofs

dimensional defects (dislocations, grain boundaries and precipitates) existing in perovskite films are also a major factor affecting the efficiency and stability of perovskite solar cells [22,23]. Recently, approaches have been proven to be useful to eliminate the defects in either modification of perovskite materials [24] or passivation at the material interfaces [25,26], which increase the efficiency and stability of perovskite solar cell devices.

Since the relative weak bonding energy of lead-iodine, the  $ \mathrm{Pb^{0}} $  and  $ \mathrm{I}^0 $  generated from  $ \mathrm{PbI_2} $  are the common generated point defects in perovskite film under internal stress such as electrical field and thermal shock [27]. This degradation of perovskite phase and performances is predictable with formed and point defects generating the carrier's recombination center [28]. Moreover, iodine element is evaporable under heating, which causes the several chemical degradations and result in the collapse of the perovskite lattice structure [29]. At the same time,  $ \mathrm{Pb^{0}} $ , as a primary deep defect state, will also severely degrade the performance of perovskite optoelectronic devices for enhanced carriers' recombination [30,31], as well as their long-term durability [32]. Works have been presented to eliminate either  $ \mathrm{Pb^{0}} $  or  $ \mathrm{I}^0 $  defects, such as optimizing film processing [33] and additive engineering [34,35]. These approaches are effective in both elevating the efficiency and improving stability, which directly suppressing the defects during fabrication.

To further enhance the performance of the devices, the additives are favorable to eliminate both   $  Pb^{0}  $   and   $  I^{0}  $   point defects and inhibit their formation during long-term operation. Recent work has present that using inorganic redox   $  Eu^{3+}-Eu^{2+}  $   pair additives into PSCs, which can effectively eliminate   $  Pb^{0}  $   and   $  I^{0}  $   and demonstrate notable stability improvement [36]. Organic phosphines have been widely used as additives for perovskite solar cells [37–39], in this work, we report a novel organic phosphine redox

Journal Pre-proofs

pair to stabilize the Pb-I to reduce the defect generation of   $  Pb^{0}  $   and   $  I^{0}  $   and achieved enhanced performance and long-term thermal stability for the device. The PCE of TBUP-TBPO-doped devices has been improved to 20.48% with negligible current density-voltage (J-V) hysteresis. The corresponding device has achieved enhanced thermal stability over 91% of the initial efficiency can be maintained after heating at 65°C for 500 hours comparing to 72% of the undoped sample. The improvement of efficiency suggests that the organic species can effectively suppress the Pb-I defects and provides measure for stability and performance improvement.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1.Materials

Unless specified, otherwise all chemicals were purchased from Aladdin, Alfa Aesar or Sigma-Aldrich and used as received. Formamidinium iodide (FAI), methylammonium bromine (MABr), and lead bromine  $ (\mathrm{PbBr}_2) $  were purchased from Lumtec, Taiwan. Lead iodide  $ (\mathrm{PbI}_2) $ , tributyl phosphine (TBUP), trioctylphosphine (TOP) and triphenylphosphine (PPh3) was purchased from TCI. Spiro-OMeTAD was purchased from Shenzhen Feiming Science and Technology Co., Ltd.

# 2.2. Preparation of the mixed perovskite precursor and Spiro-OMeTAD solution

The mixed perovskite precursor was prepared by dissolving 1.4 M mixture of metal lead salts which are composed of 0.85 PbI $ _2 $  and 0.15 PbBr $ _2 $ , and 1.3 M organic cation which are composed of 0.85 FAI and 0.15 MABr in a co-solvent of DMSO/DMF (4:1, by volume) followed by the addition of 30 μL CsI (pre-dissolved as a 2 mol/mL stock solution in DMSO) and 20 μL KI (predissolved as a 2 mol/mL stock solution in DMSO). For TBUP-doped perovskite precursor, different amounts of TBUP were added to 1 mL mixed perovskite precursor solution, and after mixing, 100 mL TBUP-doped perovskite precursor was exposed to dry air for different lengths of time. The Spiro solution was

Journal Pre-proofs

prepared by dissolving 73 mg Spiro-OMeTAD into 1 mL chlorobenzene followed by the addition of 18   $  \mu  $  L Li-TFSI (pre-dissolved as a 520 mg/mL stock solution in acetonitrile), 29   $  \mu  $  L FK209 (predissolved as a 300 mg/mL stock solution in acetonitrile) and 30   $  \mu  $  L 4-tert-butylpyridine.

# 2.3. Device fabrication

FTO glass was etched by a laser machine (Universal Laser Systems, VLS2.30), and followed by ultrasonic cleaning through detergent, pure water and ethyl alcohol for 20 min, respectively. They were then dried with dry-air gas flow and then treated by plasma for 5 min. The compact SnO₂ film was achieved by chemical bath deposition (CBD). 5 g urea was dissolved into 400 mL deionized water, followed by the addition of 100 μL mercaptoacetic acid and 5 mL HCl (37 wt%). Finally, SnCl₂·2H₂O was dissolved in the solution at 0.012 M and then stored in fridge for 3 days before use. The as cleaned FTO glass was soaked into the diluted SnCl₂·2H₂O solution (0.002 M) for 2 h at 70 °C and then washed by deionized water and dried by gas gun blowing. The CBD process was repeated for 3 times to achieve the desired thickness, followed by the annealing at 180 °C for 1 h. The perovskite absorber was deposited on the UV processed SnO₂ substrates (UV illuminated for 15 min) by spun a 25 μL mixed perovskite solution at 6000 rpm for 30 s with an accelerated speed of 1000 rpm, and 100 μL anti-solvents of ethyl acetate was dropped at the last 5th second. The films were then annealed at 120 °C for 45 min, and after cooling down, a 25 μL Spiro-OMeTAD solution was spun on the corresponding mixed perovskite films at 3000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, a 60 nm of gold was evaporated on the top of Spiro as the back electrode to complete the whole devices.

To verify the interaction between phosphine and   $  Pb^{0}  $   and   $  I^{0}  $   defects in perovskite films and whether different side chain groups have an effect on the redox process, we demonstrate here three organo-phosphine compounds, tributyl phosphine (TBUP), trioctyl phosphine (TOP) and triphenyl phosphine (PPh3) with tributyl, trioctyl and triphenyl groups that attached to the phosphine (Fig. 2f), as redox additives into perovskite film. Further characterization was carried out to confirm the conjecture of redox process of   $  PbI_{2}  $   with TBUP in the perovskite film. As shown in Fig. 2(b and d), compared with the 2.77 of the I/Pb intensity ratio in the pristine perovskite film, the ratio in the TBUP-TBPO-, TOP-TOPO-, PPh3-TPPO-doped film were increased to 3.28, 3.27 and 3.12, respectively, indicating less volatile  $ \mathrm{I}^0 $  in the corresponding perovskite film. Owing to the lower BE of  $ \mathrm{I}^- $  and the shifting of peak to the lower BE suggesting the increasing of  $ \mathrm{I}^- $ .

XPS was also used to investigate the effects of phosphine organics on   $  I^{0}  $   and   $  Pb^{0}  $   defects in perovskite films before and after oxidation (Figs. S4 and S5). Compared to the BE of I   $  3d_{5/2}  $   in phosphine-doped perovskite film without oxidation and pristine perovskite film, the shift of BE to the lower part indicated an increase of   $  I^{-}  $  in the oxidized phosphine-doped perovskite film. As shown in Fig. S5, compared to the partially-oxidized phosphine completely eliminates   $  Pb^{0}  $   defects, non-oxidized phosphine was only partially eliminated   $  Pb^{0}  $   defects, which may be because non-oxidized phosphine can only inhibit the oxidation of iodine and result in partial reduction of lead. This result is in concert with previous proposing that partially oxidized phosphine could benefit for defects inhibition.

Different side groups may affect the quality of perovskite crystallinity, which has

a vital significance for the enhancement of the device performance, since the crystallinity of the perovskite plays an important role in the optical absorption, charge transporting and device stability   $  [51–53]  $  . Thus, we compared full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) values of TBUP-, PPh3- and TOP-doped and pristine perovskite (Fig. 2e). Comprehensive consideration, we choose TBUP as the more suitable additive.

The crystal structure of the TBUP-doped perovskite film with different degrees of oxidation was characterized by XRD, as shown in Fig. 3(b). The (101), (012), (202), and (211) reflections of the phase are visible about  $ 2\theta $  values of  $ \sim 14^{\circ} $ ,  $ 20^{\circ} $ ,  $ 28^{\circ} $ , and  $ 32^{\circ} $ , respectively. It can be seen that after doping the partially oxidized TBUP, the intensity and  $ 2\theta $  values of several major diffraction peaks of the perovskite hardly change, which means that TBUP-TBPO does not affect the crystallization of the perovskite. However, the intensity of the diffraction peak of the lead iodide crystal varied with the degree of oxidation of the TBUP. When the oxidation time is below 10 s, the diffraction intensity of lead iodide increases with oxidation time increasing, but after oxidation for a long time (20 s), the diffraction intensity of lead iodide crystals decreases, which fully indicates that a suitable TBUP/TBPO ratio is beneficial to inhibiting the formation of  $ \mathrm{I}^0 $  and  $ \mathrm{Pb}^0 $ .

The photovoltaic performances of PSCs fabricated with TBUP with different oxidation times are shown in Fig. 3(c). Oxidation time had a significant effect on the performance of perovskite solar cells. Compared to the pristine device (19.54%), the TBUP-doped perovskite device oxidized for 5 s achieved the enhanced PCE of 20.48% with short-circuit current density ( $ J_{\mathrm{SC}} $ ), open-circuit voltage ( $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $ ), and fill factor (FF) of 22.97 mA/cm², 1.132 V, and 78.8%, respectively. The  $ J_{\mathrm{SC}} $  is consistent with the integrated current densities (22.3 mA/cm²) calculated by the external quantum efficiency (EQE) measurement (Fig. 3d). Compared to the pristine device, the  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  and FF of the 5s-oxidized TBUP-doped device were all improved, which is in line with the decrease in I⁰ and Pb⁰ defects. In order to rule out the effect of oxidation of perovskite precursor, the performances of PSCs fabricated from perovskite precursor exposed to dry air without TBUP was inserted in Fig. 3(c). The efficiencies demonstrated no changes along with times comparing to the control samples. This indicates the exposure

to dry air did not have a significant effect on the perovskite precursor and the enhancement of performance is majorly originated from the addition of TBUP oxidation.

Different TBUP/TBPO ratios, which come from varied exposure time of TBUP, will undoubtedly affect the defect conditions from the redox of I and Pb. We investigate the non-radiative recombination oxidation time of TBUP using PL and TRPL tests, as shown in Fig. 4(a and b). The PL intensity in the perovskite film has reached the highest value when TBUP-doped perovskite precursor was oxidized for 5 s, indicating the decrease of nonradiative recombination centers. It can be observed that the fast decay (  $  \tau_{1}  $  ) of the perovskite film has reached 36.12 ns when TBUP-doped perovskite precursor was oxidized for 5 s, which is higher than the others (Table S2). The slow decay (  $  \tau_{2}  $  ) increased from 66.23 ns in pristine perovskite to 336.85, 372.96, 147.09 and 82.15 ns for oxidized 0, 5, 10 and 20 s TBUP-doped perovskite film, respectively. The longest exciton lifetime indicated a reduction in defects. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements have been performed on PSCs which were fabricated by TBUP-doped perovskite precursor with different oxidized time [54,55]. As shown in Fig. 4(c), there are two different semicircles in the Nyquist plots. The one located at high frequency ranges and related to recombination of carriers. It is obviously that the   $  R_{rec}  $   (recombination resistance) in the 5s-oxidized TBUP-doped devices is much higher than pristine devices, confirming the defect induced recombination rate in perovskite devices with TBUP doping is effectively reduced.

To further understand the status of defects in the perovskite film, space-charge-limited-current (SCLC) technique was performed to estimate trap densities with the relation:  $  N_{\mathrm{defects}} = 2\varepsilon \varepsilon_0V_{\mathrm{TFL}} / eL^2  $  [56], where  $  \varepsilon  $  (28.8 F/m) and  $  \varepsilon_0(8.854\times 10^{-12}\mathrm{F / m})  $  are the dielectric constants of perovskite and vacuum permittivity, L (600 nm) is the thickness of the perovskite film and e ( $  1.6\times 10^{-19}\mathrm{C}  $ ) is the elementary charge. As shown in Fig. 5(a), the electron trap density in the 5s-oxidized TBUP-doped perovskite are calculated to be  $  4.87\times 10^{15}\mathrm{cm}^{-3}  $ , which is lower than pristine perovskite, whose electron trap density is  $  6.11\times 10^{15}\mathrm{cm}^{-3}  $ . We consider the reduced trap density as a result of decreasing concentration of  $  \mathrm{I}^0  $  and  $  \mathrm{Pb}^0  $ . We tested the device response under different light intensities. It can be obtained that both the pristine and 5s-oxidized TBUP-doped devices show a linear  $  V_{\mathrm{OC}}  $  versus light intensity relationship, as shown in Fig. 5(b). It is known that deviation of the slope from  $  kT / q  $  reflects defect-assisted recombination in the devices [57], where k is the Boltzmann constant, T is temperature and q is the electric charge. The pristine device shows a slope of  $  1.67kT / q  $ , while 5s-oxidized TBUP-doped devices show a smaller slope of  $  1.39kT / q  $ , which further confirmed that the recombination has been suppressed in the perovskite layer.

We tested the device stability at   $  65\ °C  $   for more than 500 h, the result is shown in the Fig. 5(c). We found that the efficiency of the pristine device decreased significantly with the passage of time during the first 50 h of heating at   $  65\ °C  $  , which could be caused by the rapid evaporation of accumulated   $  I^{0}  $   under heating at   $  65\ °C  $  , triggering a chain reaction to form a large amount of iodine vacancies. After 50 h, the rate of decline in efficiency slowed down and the device maintained 72% of the original efficiency after heating for 500 h. But for TBUP-doped samples, the rapid decline of efficiency at the initial stage of heating did not occur, which may benefit from that the accumulated   $  I^{0}  $   is rare and the formation of   $  I^{0}  $   and   $  Pb^{0}  $   is suppressed during the annealing process. Finally, after heating for 500 h, the TBUP-doped device still maintained over 91% of the original efficiency.

# 4. Conclusions

In conclusion, we present an organophosphorus compound to significantly reduce the point defects from  $ \mathrm{Pb}^0 $  and  $ \mathrm{I}^0 $  inside of the perovskite film. The TBUP with suitable redox properties was found as a proper additive by the selection of phosphine side chain

groups. The TBUP-TBPO inhibit the generation of   $  Pb^{0}  $   and   $  I^{0}  $   defects in a long-term operation. As a result, the PCE of the perovskite optoelectronic device with TBPO-TBUP increased from 19.54% to 20.48%, which was attributed to the increase in   $  V_{OC}  $   and FF due to the reduction of   $  Pb^{0}  $   and   $  I^{0}  $   defects. In addition, the thermal stability of the device is improved, the devices with TBPO-TBUP retained 91% of initial efficiency when heated at 65 °C for 500 h. Our findings provide inspiration measure for efficient and stable photovoltaic devices, and could also be appealing for new organic defect stabilizer with interface passivation and encapsulation.
