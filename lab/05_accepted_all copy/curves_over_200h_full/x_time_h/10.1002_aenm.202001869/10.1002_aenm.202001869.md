# Fully Solution Processed Pure   $  \alpha  $  -Phase Formamidinium Lead Iodide Perovskite Solar Cells for Scalable Production in Ambient Condition

Fu Yang,  $  ^{*}  $   Lirong Dong, Dongju Jang, Kai Cheong Tam, Kaicheng Zhang, Ning Li, Fei Guo, Cong Li, Charline Arrive, Mélanie Bertrand, Christoph J. Brabec, and Hans-Joachim Egelhaaf  $  ^{*}  $

# 1. Introduction

Manufacturing commercially viable perovskite solar cells still requires appropriate low-temperature and scalable deposition processes to be developed. While  $ \alpha $ -phase FAPbI $ _3 $  has higher thermal stability and broader absorption than MAPbI $ _3 $ , there still is no report of a pure  $ \alpha $ -phase FAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite film obtained by a scalable printing method. Moreover, spontaneous conversion of the  $ \alpha $ -phase to non-perovskite  $ \delta $ -phase under ambient conditions poses a serious challenge for practical applications. Herein, a scalable and fully solution based printing method for the fabrication of pure  $ \alpha $ -phase FAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite solar cells is reported. Through adding  $ N $ -methyl pyrrolidone and methylammonium chloride to the dimethylformamide based precursor solution to control the crystallization, and vacuum or air-flow assisted film drying, pure  $ \alpha $ -FAPbI $ _3 $  phase is obtained by doctor blading. The resulting  $ \alpha $ -FAPbI $ _3 $  film is highly stable, with no  $ \delta $ -FAPbI $ _3 $  phase being formed even after keeping it in an ambient atmosphere over a period of 200 days without encapsulation. In addition, a fully solution processed PSC with a PCE of 16.1% is processed by the vacuum assisted method, and 17.8% by the air-flow assisted method. Replacing silver with a printed carbon electrode provides a stable PCE up to 15% for the vacuum assisted and 16.4% for the air-flow assisted method, which is the highest performance of FAPbI $ _3 $  solar cells to date. Compared with MAPbI $ _3 $ , the fully printed FAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite devices exhibit a remarkable thermal stability in humid atmospheres which makes them a promising candidate for scalable production and commercialization.

The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of organometallic halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has rapidly increased from 3.9% to 25.2% within just a few years, which is mainly attributed to the high charge-carrier mobility, long charge carrier lifetime, high absorption coefficient, and excellent defect tolerance of the active layer material.  $  ^{[1]}  $   In addition, simple and low-cost methods for roll-to-roll (R2R) processing this material are available, which could significantly reduce the production cost for large-scale perovskite solar modules.  $  ^{[2]}  $   However, most of the impressive PCE results have been achieved for solar cells fabricated by spin-coating, which is limited with respect to film area and wastes most of the solution during spinning.  $  ^{[3]}  $   In addition, thickness and morphology of spin-coated perovskite films are determined by the continuous centrifugal force arising from spinning, which is difficult to be replicated for the scalable deposition processes and is thus not compatible with R2R manufacturing. Second, they need to maintain a high level of efficiencies also when going from small lab cells to large area modules. Last, but not least, they need to be compatible with high throughput production processes, either sheet-to-sheet (S2S), or, more preferably, R2R.[4] Blade coating is the most frequently employed process next to spin coating for fabricating perovskite films with well-defined thickness.[5,6] However, most perovskite films fabricated by blade coating method are methylammonium (MA) based perovskites, which usually are poor in light absorption and thermal stability.[5-7] In addition, for most perovskite solar cells reported as being blade coated only the perovskite film has been deposited by blade coating, whereas the charge transport layers and top electrodes were prepared by spin-coating and vapor deposition.[5-7] Compared to MA based perovskites, formamidinium (FA) based perovskites show enhanced stability and broader photon absorption. However, it is challenging to prepare high quality  $ \alpha $ -phase FA perovskite films with complete coverage of the substrate and without the formation of delta-phase FAPbI $ _3 $ , which is the more stable phase in ambient conditions. In addition, it is hard to obtain high quality films of this material by blade coating, as it is hard to control the nucleation and crystallization kinetics of the FA-based perovskites by this method.[8,9] Thus, the perovskite films of the most efficient FA based perovskite solar cells have been made by the antisolvent assisted spin-coating method.[10] Unlike MAPbI $ _3 $ , the  $ \alpha $ -phase of FAPbI $ _3 $  is not stable at room temperature and undergoes a thermodynamic phase transition to the perovskite  $ \delta $ -phase.[11] Multiple concepts have been used to stabilize the black  $ \alpha $ -phase of FA-based perovskite films prepared by the blade coating method, for instance, by adding mixed cations and anions (FA, MA, cesium (Cs), iodine(I), and bromine (Br) ions).[12] However, these additives will widen the bandgap of the film and reduce its thermal stability.[13] Due to these difficulties in the growth of FAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite films, there has been no report on the preparation of high quality pure  $ \alpha $ -phase FAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite films by blade coating. Therefore, exploiting a plausible and promising printing method that is compatible with upscalable fabrication of pure  $ \alpha $ -phase FAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite solar cells is challenging but urgently required.

In this work, we demonstrate the fabrication of pure  $ \alpha $ -phase  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  perovskite films by one-step blade-coating at room temperature in ambient air. Even though it has been reported that adding dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) is helpful to form the stable intermediate phase for achieving a pure  $ \alpha $ -phase  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  film by spin-coating, it is different for the blade coating process, where dominantly the non-perovskite  $ \delta $ - $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  phase film is achieved when using DMSO as the additive. However, through forming a more stable intermediate phase than that of DMSO, the additive  $ N $ -Methyl-2-pyrrolidon (NMP) avoids the formation of the  $ \delta $ - $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  phase, thus providing a pure  $ \alpha $ - $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  phase film. Moreover, through adjusting the content of the additives NMP and methylammonium chloride (MACl) in the  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  dimethylformamide (DMF) precursor solution, pure  $ \alpha $ -phase  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  perovskite films with high quality are obtained by the vacuum assisted blade coating method. Another central development of this work is a vacuum-based precrystallization procedure

performed in air, which is decisive to create intermediate phases by removing the excess solvent of the freshly printed wet films.  $  ^{[6]}  $   The resulting intermediate stage guarantees that the precursor deposition is kept separated from the subsequent thermal-annealing based crystallization, leading to the growth of high-quality FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite films in a controlled manner. In addition, a scalable processing strategy has been developed to prepare all the semi-conducting layers in the perovskite solar cells, including electron transport layer, perovskite layer, and hole transport layer. Through optimizing the blade-coating conditions, fully solution processed perovskite solar cells with PCEs up to 18% are obtained using a SnO  $  _{2}  $  /fullerene bi-layer as electron extraction layer and a double layer of the organic polymer PDCBT and tantalum doped tungsten oxide nanoparticles as hole extraction layer. Finally, by using carbon as the top electrode, we managed to obtain PSCs with a stable PCE of up to 15%, which is the highest performance of fully printed FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   solar cells so far.

# 2. Results and Discussion

To underline the significance of this work, we first revisit the performance of fully printed perovskite solar cells with carbon-based top electrodes from previous reports.  $  ^{[14]}  $   The collected data points are plotted in Figure 1a. All previous reports on fully printed perovskite solar cells are based on n-i-p device architectures, relying on a mesoporous bottom charge extraction layer, which is limited in   $  V_{OC}  $    $  ^{[15]}  $   and needs annealing high temperatures (over 400 °C), which is not viable in low temperature R2R coating. Furthermore, all of the fully printed devices are based on MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   as the main component in the active layer, which will suffer from light induced degradation and thermal instability. In contrast, the planar structure employed in this work can be achieved at temperatures acceptable in R2R coating. In addition, we have fabricated for the first time pure   $  \alpha  $  -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite layers by scalable printing methods, which is thermally more stable and shows a broader absorption than perovskite layers based on MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   and is thus more appropriate for commercializing PSCs. Figure 1c illustrates the processing strategy used in this work to prepare fully printed perovskite solar cells. For this purpose, we have transferred the processing method for an n-i-p architecture developed in our labs from spin-coating to blade-coating.  $  ^{[16]}  $   First, a thin SnO  $  _{2}  $   nanoparticle layer was blade coated on UV-ozone treated ITO glass. Subsequently, a fullerene derivative whose phosphonic acid anchoring group binds strongly to metal oxide surfaces was blade-coated on the annealed SnO  $  _{2}  $   layer to enhance its electron extraction properties. Next, the perovskite layer was blade-coated and treated in a vacuum chamber at a pressure of 1000 Pa for 90 s or under air-flow for several seconds to form a shining and transparent perovskite intermediate phase. The treatment time in the vacuum chamber is a bit longer than previously reported for the spin-coating method,  $  ^{[17]}  $   which could be due to different equipment designs and experimental conditions. High quality FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite was achieved by annealing this intermediate phase at 150 °C for 10 min. Finally, the hole transport layer and the carbon top electrode were blade-coated to finish the preparation of the fully printed perovskite solar cells. Solvent engineering, that is, adding co-solvents to the host solvent, is a common and effective preparation method for high quality perovskite films. In this work, DMSO, and NMP were explored as potential additives to FAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite precursor solutions as those two solvents are reported to be effective additives for achieving pure  $ \alpha $ -FAPbI $ _3 $  phase films. $ ^{[8]} $  When adding 10% (v/v) DMSO to the precursor solution of PbI $ _2 $  and FA in DMF, a blend of the yellow  $ \delta $ -phase FAPbI $ _3 $  and the black  $ \alpha $ -phase FAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite is formed, as identified in the microscope image and digital photograph in Figure 2a. However, when adding 10% (v/v) NMP to the precursor solution, almost exclusively black  $ \alpha $ -phase FAPbI $ _3 $  was obtained by annealing the perovskite intermediate film as shown in the microscope image in Figure 2b. These optical observations are confirmed by the XRD patterns of FAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite films displayed in Figure 2d. For the film prepared from the DMF/DMSO solvent mixture, there is a strong peak located at 11.8°, which is assigned to the  $ \delta $ -phase FAPbI $ _3 $ . In contrast, for the additive NMP, the peak at 11.8° has almost disappeared, while the strong peak located at 14.34° indicates that the film consists of almost pure  $ \alpha $ -phase FAPbI $ _3 $ . The UV–vis spectra in Figure S3, Supporting Information show that the absorption intensity does not increase by using either DMSO or NMP as the additive. The above results are in accordance with previous reports, which suggest that NMP has much more appropriate Lewis base properties than DMSO for the preparation of FA-based perovskites. $ ^{[18,19]} $  Compared to DMSO, NMP has a high hydrogen-bond-accepting ability, and the relative hardness of the Lewis acid and base is more matched to FAI and PbI $ _2 $ . $ ^{[20]} $  Therefore, the relatively weak interactions between DMSO and FAI result in an unstable FAI·PbI $ _2 $ ·DMSO adduct phase which is expected to induce conversion into  $ \delta $ -phase

$ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  at low temperature. On the other hand, NMP forms a stronger bond with FAI than DMSO, which yields the more stable  $ \mathrm{FAI}\cdot \mathrm{PbI}_2\cdot \mathrm{NMP} $  adduct resulting in  $ \alpha $  -phase  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  after annealing.[19] DFT Calculations have indicated that the intermediate phase,  $ \mathrm{FAI}\cdot \mathrm{PbI}_2\cdot \mathrm{NMP} $  has a significantly higher interaction energy (-2.413 eV) than the intermediate phase  $ \mathrm{FAI}\cdot \mathrm{PbI}_2\cdot \mathrm{DMSO} $  (-2.138 eV), corresponding to a more stable NMP adduct. The enhanced stability of the NMP adduct leads to the formation of a more uniform and pinhole-free  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  film.[19] The intermediate phases prepared by vacuum assisted blade coating of precursor solutions with different solvent additives were studied by X-ray measurements, as shown in Figure 2c. There are strong peaks located at  $ 8.63^{\circ} $  and  $ 9.14^{\circ} $  in the pristine film processed from  $ 10\% $  NMP which are assigned to the intermediate state of  $ \mathrm{FAI}\cdot \mathrm{PbI}_2\cdot \mathrm{NMP} $ .[21] The weak peak located at  $ 11.8^{\circ} $  can be indexed to the (121) plane of  $ \delta \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $ . For the film processed from DMF with  $ 10\% $  DMSO the peak of the (121) plane of  $ \delta \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  is quite strong and persists after thermal annealing. In order to check the versatility of the NMP additive with respect to different blade coating methods, we have fabricated the  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  perovskite film also by air flow assisted blade coating with  $ 10\% $  NMP in DMF. The XRD result shown in Figure S4, Supporting Information confirms that the  $ \delta \text{-FAPbI}_3 $  can be avoided by using the NMP additive also with this technique. In order to explore the perovskite film formation and crystallization dynamics, we apply the in-situ measurement of the steady PL measurement of the air-flow assisted blade coating process shown in Figure S13, Supporting Information. The air-flow was immediately applied after blade-coating the perovskite precursor solution. Figure 3 shows the SEM images of  $ \alpha $ -phase FAPbI $ _3 $  films obtained by blade coating of precursor solution with  $ 10\% $  NMP, which still suffer from poor crystal quality and a significant pinhole density. In order to reduce the pin-hole density and increase film quality, different amounts of MACl were used as co-additive into the precursor solution. MACl has been shown to improve the quality of perovskite films by reducing the crystallization rate. $ ^{[22]} $  As evident in Figure 3, adding MACl to the precursor solution significantly changes the morphology of perovskite films. At concentrations of  $ 30~\mathrm{mol}\% $  and  $ 40~\mathrm{mol}\% $  MACl additive (the molar ratio is given with respect to lead iodide), pinhole free perovskite films were obtained in which the size distribution of the crystals is homogenous throughout the sample. The crystal grain size increases substantially from  $ \approx 160~\mathrm{nm} $  in MACl-free films to over  $ 1.2~{\mu\mathrm{m}} $  in films processed from  $ 50~\mathrm{mol}\% $  MACl, underlining the importance of MACl for nucleation and crystal growth processes. MACl slows down the perovskite crystallization rate by forming intermediate phases. These intermediate phases quickly transform into the perovskite structure due to the facile sublimation of MACl during thermal annealing, resulting in high photovoltaic performance

in the corresponding perovskite solar cells.  $  ^{[22]}  $   However, at an additive content of 50 mol%, MACl pinholes were again detected (Figure 3f), which is due to the release of large amounts of excess MACl from the film during the annealing processes.  $  ^{[6]}  $   In addition, we also fabricated the perovskite films by the air-flow assisted blade-coating method shown in Figure 3g–I, which provides a large grain size perovskite film, suggesting again the importance of the co-additive concept using NMP and MACl for achieving high quality FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite films, independent of the quenching method.

As shown in Figure 4a, slightly enhanced absorption of perovskite films is observed upon adding MACl, which can be attributed to better surface coverage and the increased absorption near the bad gap associated with an improved crystallinity. In addition, the absorption edge of  $ \alpha $ -FAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite film is located at 838 nm, and is slightly red-shifted by adding the MACl additive. Increasing the MACl content to over 40% leads to a slight reduction of absorption, primarily due to the existence of large voids. Figure 4b displays the x-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of FAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite films prepared from DMF/NMP precursor solution containing 30 mol% MACl and subsequently annealed at 150 °C for 10 min. Two strong peaks at 14.3° and 28.6° are observed which are assigned to the (001) and (002) crystal planes of  $ \alpha $ -FAPbI3, respectively. No peak characteristic for  $ \delta $ -FAPbI3 at 11.8° was observed. The XRD results clearly prove that pure  $ \alpha $ -FAPbI $ _3 $  phase is formed from all precursor solutions containing the MACl and NMP additives. The   $  SnO_{2}  $   particle size is   $  \approx4  $   nm based on previous report,  $  ^{[24]}  $   which is much smaller than the   $  SnO_{2}  $   film thickness. # 3. Conclusion

High-quality pure  $ \alpha $ -phase FAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite films have been obtained at room temperature in air by the scalable technique of vacuum-assisted one-step blade-coating. The formation of the undesired  $ \delta $ -phase FAPbI $ _3 $  was successfully suppressed through modifying the DMF based precursor solution with the co-solvent NMP. Pin-hole free FAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite films with crystal grain sizes over  $ 1\mu \mathrm{m} $  were achieved by modifying the crystallization kinetics through the addition of MACl. These additives were also applied in the air flow assisted blade-coating method, which proves their versatility. The resulting FAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite film shows excellent stability in ambient atmosphere with nearly no degradation

in ambient atmosphere for 200 days. In order to demonstrate the potential of blade-coating as a scalable processing strategy, perovskite solar cells were fabricated by blade-coating all layers between the two electrodes, including electron and hole extraction layers, showing PCEs of  $ 16.1\% $  by the vacuum assisted method, and  $ 17.8\% $  by the air-flow assisted method. Replacing doped Spiro-OMeTAD by a bilayer of undoped polymer PDCBT and  $ \mathrm{Ta - WO_x} $  proved to be advantageous with respect to the long-term stability of the perovskite solar cells. At last, all printed devices were prepared by replacing the vapor-deposited Ag electrode by a doctor bladed carbon electrode, which yielded a PCE of  $ 16.4\% $  along with long-term stability. These results establish new guidelines in exploring scalable coating methods for high performance perovskite devices.

# 4. Experimental Section

Preparation of Perovskite Solar Cells: All reagents including were used without further purification. ITO (Indium Tin Oxide) Coated Glass (Liaoning Huite photoelectricTechnology Co.,Ltd.) substrates were ultrasonically cleaned sequentially for 15 min in acetone and isopropanol. Afterwards the substrates were treated under UV-Ozone box for 20 min to remove organic residues and to enable better wetting. An aqueous SnO $  _{2}  $  nanoparticle solution (Alfa Aesar) was used to prepare the ETL. The solution was diluted to 2.0 wt% SnO $  _{2}  $  and treated in the ultrasonic bath for 10 min followed by filtering using a 0.45 μm PTFE filter. The solution was then doctor bladed at 60 °C with 10 mm s $  ^{-1}  $  and a gap height of 100 μm. Subsequently the film was annealed at 150 °C for 30 min to form a compact layer. Then a C60-SAM layer was prepared by blade-coating a filtered 2 mg mL $  ^{-1}  $  C60-SAM solution in (1-butanol Aldrich, 99.8%) at 15 mm s $  ^{-1}  $  and a gap height of 250 μm, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 5 min. Equal molar amounts of FAI (Sigma, 98%) and PbI $  _{2}  $  (Sigma, 99%) were dissolved in anhydrous dimethylformamide (DMF, Aldrich, 99.8%), anhydrous 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP, Aldrich, 99.8%), or anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Aldrich, 99.8%) (DMF:NMP or DMF: DMSO, 9:1) to prepare 1 M FAPbI $  _{3}  $  precursor solution and stirred at room temperature for 1 h. Methylammonium chloride (MACl, Aldrich, 99.8%) was used as the additive in to the prevskite precursor solution with different molar ratio (0, 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%). The precursor solution was doctor bladed onto the substrate at 5 mm s $  ^{-1}  $  and a gap height of 120 μm. The coating temperature was set as low as 30 °C to prevent drying of the film before the vacuum step. The substrate with the still wet film was put immediately into a vacuum chamber at 1000 mbar for 1 min, where the DMF solvent gets extracted and brown perovskite intermediate forms. The intermediate film was subsequently annealed at 150 °C for 10 min to get the final FAPbI $  _{3}  $  phase. For the hole transport layer, a bilayer of P3HT and Ta-WO $  _{x}  $  was used. 20 mg mL $  ^{-1}  $  P3HT was dissolved in anhydrous chlorobenzene (Aldrich, 99.8%) and stirred at 80 °C for at least 2 h. The Ta-WO $  _{x}  $  nanoparticle dispersion comes as 2.5 wt% Ta-WO $  _{x}  $  (Avantama) in ethanol which was further diluted in anhydrous IPA (Aldrich, 99.8%) to have a 1:1 ethanol to IPA volume ratio. A gap height of 250 μm and a volume of 60 μL was used for doctor blading both the P3HT and Ta-WO $  _{x}  $  solutions. The coating temperatures and speeds for P3HT and Ta-WO $  _{x}  $  were 50 and 40 °C as well as 10 mm s $  ^{-1}  $  and 5 mm s $  ^{-1}  $  respectively. After coating the P3HT, the film was annealed at 80 °C for 5 min. The same was done after coating the Ta-WO $  _{x}  $  solution. For the PDCBT layer, PDCBT (1-Material Inc.) was dissolved in anhydrous chlorobenzene to form 5 mg mL $  ^{-1}  $  solution. A gap height of 150 μm and a volume of 40 μL was used for doctor blading. The solution was stirred at 80 °C for 30 min before using. The blade-coating temperature and speeds for PDCBT were 60 °C with 10 mm s $  ^{-1}  $ , following with annealed at 100 °C for 5 min. After that, Ta-WO $  _{x}  $  layer was prepared.

For using Spiro as hole transport layer, the spiro-OMeTAD layer was prepared by blade coating a chlorobenzene solution containing 180 mm spiro-OMeTAD, 60 mm tert-butylpyridine, 30 mm Li-TFSI (520 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  in acetonitrile), and 33 mm FK209 (300 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  in acetonitrile) at 60 °C with 10 mm s  $  ^{-1}  $ , along with a gap of 150 μm. Finally, a 100 nm thickness Ag counter electrode was deposited by thermal evaporation. For the carbon electrode, the carbon paste (Liaoning Huite Photoelectric Technology Co. Ltd.) was blade coated on the prepared film and annealed at 120 °C for 15 min. For this the electrodes pattern was cut out of an adhesive tape with a laser. The tape was then placed on the substrate with the sticky side down. The cutouts were filled with carbon paste by blade coating method. The tape was then removed carefully and the substrate annealed on a hot plate at 120 °C for 15 min. The resulted carbon electrode thickness is around 60 μm which is confirmed by cross section FE-SEM image.

Characterization: Optical microscope images were recorded using a MX51 from Olympus Co. Solar cells were characterized by measuring their current–voltage (J-V)-characteristics with an AAA solar simulator, which provides AM1.5G illumination and source measurement system from LOT-Quantum Design, calibrated with a certified silicon solar

module. The sweeping ranges for solar cells were -0.5 to 1.5 V in steps of 20 mV. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM): A FEI Helios Nanolab 660 was used to acquire scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images and to prepare FIB cross-sections. The final polishing with the ion beam was performed at 5 kV and 80 pA. X-ray powder diffraction (XRD): X-ray diffraction analysis was performed by classical ex situ Bragg–Brentano geometry using a Panalytical X'pert powder diffractometer with filtered Cu-Kα radiation and an X'Celerator solid-state stripe detector.
