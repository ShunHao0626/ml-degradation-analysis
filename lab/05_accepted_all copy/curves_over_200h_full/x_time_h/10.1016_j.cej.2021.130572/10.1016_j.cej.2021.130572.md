# 1. Introduction

In recent years, metal-halide perovskite materials with exceptional photoelectrical properties and low-cost solution fabrication have drawn worldwide interest from both academic and industrial community. The enormous progress has been achieved for organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs), in which the power conversion efficiency (PCE) has increased up from 3.8% to 25.5% [1,2]. However, with the hypostatic volatility of organic cations such as methylammonium (MA $  ^{+}  $ ) and formamidinium (FA $  ^{+}  $ ), organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite films usually suffer from compositional degradation under continuous erosion by heat, moisture or ultraviolet light, which greatly restricts their practical application [3–5]. It is well-known that cesium-based all-inorganic halide perovskites possess the analogous optoelectronic properties as the organic-inorganic counterparts, but exhibit superior thermal and photostability. Indeed, the inorganic monovalent cation Cs $  ^{+}  $  can not only supersede MA $  ^{+}  $  and FA $  ^{+}  $  ions to form thermally stable CsPbX $  _{3}  $  perovskites (>300 °C), but also has an adjustable bandgap (1.73

CsPbI $ _2 $ Br all-inorganic perovskite has attracted considerable attention due to its high thermal stability and suitable bandgap, while the unsatisfactory crystallization quality and moisture instability significantly hinder its further application. Herein, a novel multifunctional liquid additive, 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA), is introduced into the CsPbI $ _2 $ Br precursor to ameliorate the above issues. Our results demonstrate that the functional groups of HEMA can interact with perovskite precursor, thus improving crystallization and passivating defects at the same time, and leading to a champion power conversion efficiency of up to 16.13%. Importantly, HEMA molecule in perovskite films will polymerize to a hydrophobic polymer at grain boundaries through breaking C = C bonds during the high temperature annealing process (160 °C). Moreover, this multifunctional liquid additive strategy endows perovskite films with promoted moisture tolerance by crosslinking perovskite grains and blocking moisture penetration, thus the unencapsulated device maintains 78% of its initial efficiency after aging at 30% relative humidity for 1000 h. These results demonstrate that the additive engineering with multifunctional molecule is applicable strategy for developing CsPbI $ _2 $ Br all-inorganic perovskite solar cells with both high efficiency and stability.

# 2. Experimental methods

# 2.1. Material preparation

Cesium iodide (Csl, 99.9%), Lead iodide (PbI  $  _{2}  $  , 99.9%), Lead bromide (PbBr  $  _{2}  $  , 99.9%), and Molybdenum Trioxide (M  $  _{O}  $  O  $  _{3}  $  , 99.99%) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. The PM6 was purchased from Organtec Ltd. The zinc acetate dihydrate (>98%) and 2-Hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA, 96%) were purchased from Aladdin. N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.8%, anhydrous), chlorobenzene (CB, 99.9%, Aladdin), Ethanolamine (99.5%, Aladdin), methoxyethanol (>99.5%, Aladdin). All materials and reagents were used as received without further purification.

# 2.2. Perovskite precursor preparation

# 2.3. Devices fabrication

Glass/FTO substrates (1.5 cm*1.5 cm) were cleaned by sonication in deionized water, ethanol and isopropanol (IPA) and then dried in an oven. Then, the dry substrates were treated with UV-ozone for 20 min before used. The ZnO layer was prepared by dissolving 250 mg zinc acetate dihydrate and 69 μL ethanolamine in 2.5 mL 2-methoxyethanol, and was stirred at least 12 h before use, then was spin-coated (4000 rpm/40 s) on FTO and annealed at 150 °C for 30 min. Whereafter, the device was transferred to a nitrogen-filled glove box. The perovskite precursor was filtered by syringe before use. And then 40 μL perovskite precursor solution was deposited onto the FTO/ZnO substrates and spin-coated at 1000 rpm for 5 s and 3000 rpm for 30 s without any antisolvent treatment. Subsequently, the perovskite film was immediately annealed at 45 °C for 60 s and at 160 °C for 5 min. Hole transporting layer (HTL) was prepared by dissolving PM6 (6 mg) in 1 mL of chlorbenzene (CB) solution without any dopants, and stirring at 40 °C for 8 h. Then HTL was spin-coated on the perovskite layer at 1300 rpm for 40 s. Finally, a thin film with MoO₃ (10 nm) thermally evaporates with the evaporation rate under vacuum conditions 0.4 Å/s. Finally, 60 nm Ag electrodes were thermally evaporated (<6 × 10⁻⁴ Pa) to finish the device fabrication. The active area of the device in this work was 0.075 cm².

# 2.4. Characterization of perovskite thin films

X-ray diffraction patterns (XRD, Bruker D8 Advance instrument with Cu Kα X-ray radiation about 0.154 nm) were used to test crystal structure and diffraction angle range from 5° to 45°. The morphology characterization of the films was measured by SEM (JSM-7800F). FTIR spectroscopy were taken with IR spectrometer instrument (Thermo, Nicolet 6700). The surface morphology and potential of the perovskite films were tested by atomic force microscopy (AFM, Asylum Research Cypher, UK). PL and TR-PL spectra were recorded via using the Pico Quant Fluotime 450 with a 650 nm pulsed laser. The chemical states of ions were tested by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Kratos Axis Ultra DLD) using Al Kα as radiation and the C 1 s line at 284.6 eV was used for correction the all spectra.

# 2.5. Characterization of perovskite solar cells

The  $ J-V $  curves were collected by a calibrated solar simulator (Newport Inc.) with an AM 1.5G filter and Keithley 2400 source meter under  $ 100\mathrm{mW/cm^2} $  in glove box. The voltage scanned from  $ 2\mathrm{V} $  to  $ -0.2\mathrm{V} $  with dwell time of  $ 50~\mathrm{ms} $ . The space-charge-limited current (SCLC) measurements and dark  $ J-V $  curves were measured by the Keithley 4200 m under dark condition. The transient photo-current (TPC) decay and transient photo-voltage (TPV) decay were recorded by electrochemical workstation (Zahner, Germany) with a white light LED supplied  $ 80\mathrm{Mw/cm^2} $  light intensity to excite the perovskite solar cells. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) was tested by Newport EQE system and wavelength range from 300 to  $ 700\mathrm{nm} $ .

# 3. Results and discussion

The reactants CsI,   $  PbI_{2}  $  ,   $  PbBr_{2}  $   as well as HEMA molecules were first dissolved in DMF/DMSO solution, as shown in Fig. 1a. It is acknowledged that Lewis acids CsI,   $  PbI_{2}  $   and   $  PbBr_{2}  $   can be combined with the Lewis bases DMF and DMSO in the perovskite precursor solution [37]. Due to the addition of HEMA into the precursor solution, the

crystallization process of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   films could be significantly affected by the interactions between HEMA and perovskite component, leading to obtain high-quality films with less defects. Unlike the above mentioned solid additives (ABA and biuret), HEMA is a liquid organic additive, which combines multiple functional groups such as -OH, C = O and C = C in one molecule. During the thermal annealing process of as-prepared   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   films at 160 °C, the small molecule HEMA can break the C = C bonds and polymerize to a hydrophobic polymer. As illustrated in Figure S1, the HEMA molecules are changed from viscous liquid into transparent compact solid. Obviously, the fresh HEMA molecules are soluble in water while the obtained HEMA polymers are insoluble in polar water, this phenomenon is clearly observed from the incompatible layered solution for HEMA polymers (Figure S1b). The Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy is performed to verify the HEMA polymerization and their bonding interactions with perovskite. The full FT-IR spectra of HEMA molecule, HEMA with thermal treatment at 160 °C (HEMA-160 °C), and HEMA-  $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   are present in Figure S2a. Pure HEMA molecules exhibit characteristic stretching vibrations of C = O and C = C groups at 1708 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   and 1634 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  , respectively (Fig. 2a). For HEMA-160 °C sample, the peak from C = C groups almost disappear while the peak from C = O group always exists when treated after thermal annealing. The results strongly indicate that the effective polymerization of HEMA in perovskite films by breaking the C = C bond during the annealing process. In addition, the C = O and C-OH groups in HEMA can interact with   $  PbI_{2}  $   and   $  PbBr_{2}  $   in perovskite, as revealed in the FT-IR spectra in Fig. 2b and Fig. 2c. The stretching vibration frequency of C = O bond and C-OH in pure HEMA appears at 1708 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   and 3513 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  , while they shift to 1697 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   and 3501 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   upon interaction with   $  PbI_{2}  $   and   $  PbBr_{2}  $  . In order to further investigate how the HEMA molecules interacted with the perovskite precursors in solution, liquid state   $  {}^{1}H  $   nuclear magnetic resonance (  $  {}^{1}H  $   NMR) spectroscopy characterization was performed. Figure S2b illustrates the   $  {}^{1}H  $   NMR spectra of neat HEMA solutions in deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide-d6 (DMSO-  $  d_{6}  $  ) to the signals of HEMA. Indeed, the characteristic peaks at 4.81 ppm were assigned to the typical hydrogen signals of -OH in HEMA. As shown in Figure S2c, a clear upfield chemical shift was observed for the -OH peak when mixing the HEMA with CsI,   $  PbI_{2}  $ ,   $  PbBr_{2}  $   or   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $ , respectively. The peak shift in the   $  {}^{1}H  $   NMR spectra indicates the strong hydrogen bonding interaction of the -OH with the halide ions (I', Br') of perovskite components. These results suggest that C = O bond and C-OH can interact with uncoordinated ions, which usually act as defect sites such as   $  Pb^{2+}  $ , I' and Br', benefiting to reduce the trap states density from GBs. Moreover, C = O and C-OH groups can provide coordination sites for HEMA, and help the polymerized HEMA existing on the GBs of the perovskite film just as Fig. 1c illustrates, thereby enhance the interaction between HEMA and perovskite grains.

To elucidate the chemical bond state changes caused by HEMA additive strategy, we performed X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis on   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   films without (w/o) and with HEMA addition. The three main peaks of Cs 3d, Pb 4f, and I 3d are shown in Fig. 2d-f, respectively. The   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film incorporating HEMA additive exhibits the shift of the Cs, Pb, and I valence electrons when compared to the pristine   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film w/o HEMA additive, which indicates the effective interaction between HEMA and perovskite [38]. Furthermore, the peaks of metal Pb at 141.2 and 136.1 eV, an indication of the existence of Pb defects and iodide vacancies, are greatly weakened for the films with HEMA in comparison to the pristine   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film, implying the fewer traps in the films with HEMA additive[39]. XPS results also confirmed the presence of HEMA molecules in the perovskite film by detecting the O1s peaks corresponding to the characteristic element oxygen of the HEMA (Fig. 2g). In the case of the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film w/o HEMA, oxygen is also detected in view of the inevitably absorbed oxygen or moisture molecules in the air. From the EQE spectra, the integrated  $ J_{\mathrm{SC}} $  values for the PSCs w/o and HEMA additive are 14.91 and  $ 15.34\mathrm{mA/cm^2} $ , displaying a small deviation of the  $ J_{\mathrm{SC}} $  obtained from the  $ J-V $  curves. To estimate the efficiency reliability of each device, the stabilized power output (SPO) PCEs is conducted under the maximum power point (MPP) voltage bias. As shown in Fig. 4f, the  $ \mathrm{CsPbI_2Br} $  device with HEMA exhibits the improved output efficiency with  $ 15.13\% $  under MPP pointing  $ (1.05\mathrm{V}) $  for  $ 200\mathrm{s} $ , while the device w/o HEMA just gets the attenuated PCE and remains  $ 12.15\% $  under the same operation, which indicates the better operational stability for the devices with HEMA additive.

Besides, in order to further unveil the impact of HEMA additive on the trap-state density of   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   PSCs, pure electronic devices with FTO/ZnO/  $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $  /PCBM/Ag structure were fabricated. By measuring the dark current–voltage curves of electron-only devices, we extracted the trap-filled limit voltage (  $  V_{TFL}  $  ), that is, the voltage at the kink point when the current increases sharply (Fig. 4g). From the value of   $  V_{TFL}  $  , we can calculate the trap density(  $  n_{t}  $  ) according to the following equation [47]:

$$
n _ {\mathrm{t}} = 2 \varepsilon \varepsilon_ {0} V _ {\mathrm{TFL}} / e L ^ {2}
$$

Where  $ \varepsilon_0 $  is the vacuum permittivity,  $ e $  is the elementary charge,  $ \varepsilon $  is the relative dielectric constant of CsPbI $ _2 $ Br (8.5) and  $ L $  is the thickness of the perovskite. The trap density is directly proportional to the  $ V_{\mathrm{TFL}} $ , and trap density values of devices w/o and with HEMA additive are deduced to be  $ 1.56 \times 10^{15} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3} $  and  $ 8.69 \times 10^{14} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3} $ , respectively. The result

indicated that the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film with HEMA exhibited remarkably reduced trap density, which conformed the efficient passivation effects at GBs by the HEMA additive, as illustrated in Fig. 1c. Additionally, the charge carrier dynamics of PSCs were further investigated by measuring transient photovoltage (TPV) decay and transient photocurrent (TPC) decay. With the addition of HEMA, a slower decay time of TPV (Fig. 4h) can be observed, which indicates the decrease of nonradiative recombination. By contrast, the TPC decay time of the PSCs with HEMA was shorter than that of the PSCs w/o HEMA, as shown in Fig. 4i. A rapid TPC decay times suggests the faster carrier extraction and transmission. Simultaneously, the charge-carrier recombination of related devices was conducted by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) test. Nyquist plots (Figure S10) is recorded in the darkness at a bias voltage of 0.9 V, where the values of recombination resistance (  $  R_{rec}  $  ) and series resistance (  $  R_{s}  $  ) are respectively corresponding to the diameter of semicircle and the intercept on the   $  Z'  $   axis [41]. Compared with the devices w/o HEMA, the devices with HEMA feature larger   $  R_{rec}  $ , which indicates that bulk recombination are effectively suppressed. Consequently, it can be confirmed that the HEMA-based PSCs enables much more efficient charge transport and decreased recombination, and the overall improved PSCs-performances in   $  V_{OC}  $ ,   $  J_{SC}  $ , FF, and PCE could be mainly attributed to the better crystallization, favorable morphology, and effective defect passivation.

It is certain that apart from device efficiency, the long-term stability of PSCs is one of the most challenging problems in its future application. Recent studies have shown that energy-unstable surfaces and GBs provide channels for water erosion toward the inner part of the perovskite grains, leading to accelerated decomposition or phase transformation of inorganic perovskites   $  [48,49]  $  . In good consistence with the enhanced hydrophobicity, the   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   all-inorganic film with HEMA additive shows excellent moisture stability (under 50% RH, Figure S11). The   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   film w/o HEMA displays the obvious decomposition at the edge and inside after aging for 50 min. In contrast, the perovskite film with HEMA shows greatly improved humidity stability, with little decomposition under the same conditions, which benefits from the hydrophobic HEMA polymer formed during thermal annealing process (Fig. 1b and S1). For the further study of the effect of HEMA additives on the stability of the device, we put the non-encapsulated devices in the nitrogen atmosphere glove box (Fig. 5a) and in air with humidity of 30% (Fig. 5b) to test the PSCs stability. As shown in Fig. 5a, the device with HEMA additive exhibit better storage stability, and almost no efficiency loss after monitoring for 1000 h. However, the device w/o HEMA additive suffers from a decrease and retains 91% of its initial efficiency. Actually, water vapor is inevitable in ambient air, thus the moisture stability is also evaluated by aging the devices in atmosphere with 30% RH. As shown in Fig. 5b, the moisture stability of the device w/o HEMA additives is poor, which drops down to 28% of the initial efficiency after 680 h. While the device with HEMA additive presents prominent tolerance to humidity, and maintains 78% of its initial PCE after under the same conditions. These elevated stabilities were attributed to the HEMA additive in perovskite films, in which the HEMA molecules could polymerize to a compact hydrophobic polymer at GBs and then block the channels for moisture penetration. On the other hand, due to the multiple functional groups (C = O and -OH), the HEMA could provide anchor points and cross-link the neighboring grains to make the perovskite crystals more robust and steady.

# 4. Conclusions

In summary, we have successfully introduced the multifunctional liquid additive HEMA into perovskite to fabricate efficient and stable   $  CsPbI_{2}Br  $   PSCs. It was found that HEMA with functional groups of C = O and -OH, could significantly improve film crystallinity, passivate defects, reduce carrier recombination, as well as prolong carrier lifetime. Importantly, HEMA molecule in perovskite films will polymerize to a hydrophobic polymer at GBs through breaking C = C bonds. In addition,
