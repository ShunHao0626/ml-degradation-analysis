# Stable Perovskite Solar Cells with Bulk-Mixed Electron Transport Layer by Multifunctional Defect Passivation

Ningning Ma, Jizhong Jiang, Guoxin Wang, Deyu Wang, Yiqi Zhang, Yufei Wang, Yaxi Wang, Yongcheng Ji,  $  ^{*}  $  Wei Wei, and Liang Shen  $  ^{*}  $

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Multifarious electron transport layers (ETLs), especially fullerene derivatives, have been applied in organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite (OIHP) devices owing to their superior optoelectronic properties. However, a PCBM Lewis acid molecule can only passivate the iodine-rich defect sites, which cannot solve the problem of uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and water oxygen erosion due to the high volatility of halide   $  I^{-}  $  and the hydrophilicity of organic cation   $  MA^{+}  $  . Herein, we introduce a Lewis base, TBA-Azo with an electron-donating Azo moiety, and hydrophobic long alkyl chains into the PCBM layer to form a multifunctional bulk-mixed electron transport layer (MBE). PCBM of MBE can combine with iodine-rich trap sites at the surface and grain boundaries of perovskite. TBA-Azo molecules of MBE can passivate uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   by forming Lewis adducts and isolate water/oxygen at the perovskite surface with hydrophobic alkyl chains. It results in a decrease of trap

densities with 1 order of magnitude, effectively inhibiting both bimolecular and trap-induced recombination and thus elongating the carrier lifetime. The passivation of MBE can effectively improve the open-circuit voltage from 1.05 to 1.10 V. Furthermore, three long carbon chain structures of TBA-Azo in MBE can improve the water-resistant ability of OIHP devices, which can maintain 90% of the original PCE after 500 h at the humidity of   $  50 \pm 10\%  $  . We believe that the MBE with multifunctional defect passivation provides a strategy for simultaneously achieving high-performance and high-stability OIHP optoelectronic devices.

KEYWORDS: bulk-mixed electron transport layer, multifunctional defect passivation, water repellence, perovskite solar cells

# INTRODUCTION

The organic–inorganic halide perovskite (OIHP) semiconductors possess the unique and outstanding optoelectronic properties, such as the large absorption coefficient, high carrier mobility, long carrier lifetime, and carrier diffusion length, contributing the state-of-the-art optoelectronic devices with a widely promoted and low-cost solution process route.  $  ^{1-8}  $   However, the solution-processed fabrication technology inevitably gives rise to defects at the surface and grain boundary of polycrystalline films, inducing severe charge recombination.  $  ^{9-14}  $   The high density of trap states at the surface and grain boundaries dramatically impair the efficiency and stability of the optoelectronic devices.  $  ^{15-17}  $   Moreover, the high volatility of hydrophilic organic cations MA  $  ^{+}  $   and halides I  $  ^{-}  $   particularly affect the stability of the device, as well as low thermal stability.  $  ^{18-21}  $   Therefore, defect passivation and stability improvement become critical and decisive strategies toward commercialization.

Fullerenes and their derivatives, especially PCBM, are widely used as ETL to suppress hysteresis and passivate the iodine-rich trap sites on the surface/grain boundaries of perovskite.  $  ^{22-27}  $   PCBM with excellent electron accepting capability has been proved to be an effective passivation agent as a Lewis acid for the preparation of high efficiency perovskite solar cells

In our previous article, we demonstrated that single component TBA-Azo, as a Lewis base, can effectively passivate the under-coordinated Pb ions at the perovskite surface by combining N=N in azobenzene for tin-lead near-infrared photodetectors.  $  ^{40}  $   On this basis, a multifunctional ETL was mixed with a Lewis acid (PCBM) and a Lewis base (TBA-Azo) to passivate different types of defects. Here, we doped a Lewis base TBA-Azo into the PCBM layer to form a multifunctional bulk-mixed electron transport layer (MBE). PCBM can combine with iodine-rich trap sites at the perovskite grain boundary and surface. TBA-Azo molecules simultaneously can passivate uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   by forming Lewis adducts and isolate water and oxygen at the perovskite surface. The MBE can be employed to achieve a high open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) from 1.05 to 1.10 V for PSCs, relying on passivating defects and suppressing nonradiative charge recombination. Time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) and trap density of states (tDOS) revealed that the lone pair electrons of N in the Azo moiety can passivate defects at the perovskite grain surface with undercoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   by forming a Lewis adduct. Simultaneously, the TBA-Azo doping devices show a good stability compared with the pristine one (without TBA-Azo doping), which is ascribed to the high hydrophobicity of alkyl chains. The MBE with Lewis base TBA-Azo contributes the performance improvement with reduction of nonradiative recombination and improvement of hydrophobicity, which provide potential insights into the design of high-performance devices toward energy applications.

# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

As we all know, the imperfections in OIHP materials play an important role in the process of charge recombination in PSCs, which heavily influences the resulting device energy conversion efficiency and stability. There are many kinds of defects on the surface or grain boundary of perovskite crystals, such as

undercoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ions, undercoordinated halides ions, lead clusters, I or MA vacancies in the bulk of materials, and occasionally a few intrinsic point defects, etc.  $  ^{2}  $   Strategies such as surface passivation or the use of processing additives have been explored to minimize nonradiative recombination losses at interfaces and thus improve the open-circuit voltage of devices.  $  ^{39,41}  $   However, most of the current studies only solve a specific defect on the perovskite surfaces. PCBM has been proven that it can passivate the iodine-rich trap sites on the perovskite grain boundaries and surface, reducing hysteresis and inhibiting ion migration. PCBM molecules are absorbed at the defect positions along the perovskite grain boundary, passivating these defect states as shown in Scheme 1a. It is generally believed that a large number of undercoordinated Pb ions still exist on the surface of the perovskite, especially the iodine vacancies caused by annealing. Lewis bases using coordination bonding have been frequently employed to tackle this issue due to their functional groups to the intentional passivation.  $  ^{37,38}  $   In our previous article, we demonstrated that single component TBA-Azo, as a Lewis base, can effectively passivate the undercoordinated Pb ions at the perovskite surface by combining N=N in azobenzene. To investigate the effect of MBE on optoelectronic performance of PSCs, the inverted planar heterojunction perovskite devices were constructed with a structure of indium tin oxide (ITO)/poly(triarylamine) (PTAA)/MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  /PCBM with/without TBA-Azo/C  $  _{60}  $  /BCP/Cu as detailed in the Experimental Section (Scheme 1a). All the perovskite active layers are spun from a simple one-step solution process, and PTAA is used as the hole transport layer.

Here, we report an MBE, which mixes TBA-Azo with PCBM molecules. The Lewis base TBA-Azo contains an electron-donating azo moiety and three hydrophobic long alkyl chains, as shown in Figure S1. The purpose of selecting the Azo moiety is to passivate the crystal defects at the surface of the perovskite grains by forming coordination bonds between the undercoordinated lead ion and the lone pair of N (Scheme 1b). Comparing with the regular passivation molecules, the Azo moiety of TBA-Azo tends to combine with the perovskite grain surface, which exposes the alkyl chains to form a moisture-repelling barrier. The optimal device was obtained by varying the TBA-Azo solution concentration (0.5–10 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ). As shown in Figure S2, the perovskite films treated with MBE (TBA-Azo 0.5 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) show a champion performance.

The device of TBA-Azo/PCBM delivers a PCE of 18.6%, with a  $ V_{OC} $  of 1.07 V,  $ J_{SC} $  of 23.78 mA cm $ ^{-2} $ , and fill factor (FF) of 73.12%. The experimental results show that the performance of the device is slightly improved compared with the pristine (Figure 1a,b). Only the devices with MBE can achieve the best performance. The experiments indicate the addition of TBA-Azo in PCBM is correlated with the defect of the perovskite grain boundary. TBA-Azo is introduced as a passivation molecule to passivate uncoordinated lead ions on the surface or grain boundary of perovskite. It does not participate in the crystal structure of perovskite, so it generally has no effect on the morphology and crystal structure. The length of a TBA-Azo molecule is  $ \sim $ 2.6 nm. It is obtained by simulation in our previous research. $ ^{42} $  Theoretically, the TBA-Azo molecule can enter the grain boundary of perovskite.

To investigate the passivation mechanism of MBE on perovskite defects, we used XPS (X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy) to examine whether the TBA-Azo molecules enter into grain boundaries with PTAA/MAPbI $ _3 $ /PCBM:TBA-Azo films. First, we remove the MBE on the perovskite surface and analyze the internal elements of perovskite. The deconvolution of the high resolution shows that the N 1s peak can be resolved into two components centered at 399.76 and 400.51 eV representing N-H and N=N in the TBA-Azo structure, respectively (Figure 1c). The existence of N=N indicates that TBA-Azo molecules enter into the perovskite grain boundaries. To investigate the carrier recombination dynamics, transient photovoltages (TPV) were measured. The charge recombination lifetime was increased from 52  $ \mu $ s in the pristine to 308  $ \mu $ s for TBA-Azo doping (Figure 1d), suggesting that TBA-Azo doping effectively inhibits charge carrier recombination.

TRPL curves for perovskite polycrystalline films with/without TBA-Azo doping were applied to analyze the carrier recombination process. As shown in Figure 2a, the perovskite film with TBA-Azo had a decay time of 10.8 ns derived from a double exponential decay function fitting, almost 3 times longer than that of the pristine film (3.7 ns). It suggested that the TBA-Azo is in favor of the transporting of electrons, which can be attributed to the reduction of nonradiative recombination centers. We used the thermal admittance spectroscopy (TAS) to measure tDOS of PSCs. Compared with the pristine one, the density of defect states of TBA-Azo doping is almost 1 order of magnitude lower in the whole trap depth region as shown in Figure 2b. This result further implies that the TBA-Azo doped in PCBM can effectively suppress nonradiative recombination.

The recombination kinetics were measured by the light intensity (I) dependence of the short-circuit current density   $  (J_{\mathrm{SC}})-V_{\mathrm{OC}}  $   characteristics to further understand the influence of trap-assisted recombination on device performance. The relationship between light intensity and   $  J_{SC}  $   can be represented by   $  \alpha  $  . When   $  \alpha  $   is closer to unity, the bimolecular recombination becomes less.  $  ^{43}  $   Figure 2c depicts that both the pristine and TBA-Azo treated devices show a linear relationship, where   $  \alpha=0.968  $   and 0.985, respectively. The results indicated that the bimolecular recombination is reduced with the introduction of TBA-Azo. Figure 2d shows the logarithmic variation of   $  V_{OC}  $   with light intensity. It is reported that deviation of the slope from nkT/q reflects trap-assisted charge recombination behavior in the devices, where n is the ideality factor, k is the Boltzmann constant, T is temperature, and q is the electric charge. It has been reported that polycrystalline perovskite films contain a high density of defects at the surface and grain boundaries, which will further influence the efficiency of PSCs.  $  ^{40}  $   In this article, we tried to prepare a bulk-mixed electron transport layer by mixing PCBM and TBA-Azo molecules. PCBM molecules are used to enter the perovskite grain boundary and combine with iodine-rich trap sites. At the same time, TBA-Azo is introduced to passivate under-coordinated Pb ions of the perovskite film surface by forming a Lewis adduct. The above results show that TBA-Azo mixed with PCBM can effectively suppresses charge carrier recombination and then enhance the electron extraction. On the basis of the above discussion, we applied TBA-Azo to the inverted PSCs with the structure of ITO/PTAA/MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  /PCBM with/without TBA-Azo/C  $  _{60}  $  /BCP/Cu to evaluate the effects of passivation on optoelectronic device performance. The J-V curves of the pristine and TBA-Azo doping devices were measured under the standard illumination of AM 1.5G, 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $   shown in Figure 3a. Apparently, there is no obvious hysteresis behavior between the pristine and TBA-Azo one with forward and reverse scan (Figure S3). We summarized the corresponding photovoltaic parameters in Table S1 of the Supporting Information. Figure 3b shows the external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra of the PSCs with/without TBA-Azo. The magnitude of the integral current (23.2 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) is consistent with the measured value (23.40 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) (Figure S4).

As shown in Table 1, the pristine device delivers a modest PCE of  $ 18.3\% $ , with a  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  of  $ 1.05\mathrm{V} $ ,  $ J_{\mathrm{SC}} $  of  $ 23.6\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $ , and fill factor (FF) of  $ 73.8\% $ , whereas the champion device based on the TBA-Azo-treated device exhibits a PCE of  $ 19.6\% $  with a higher  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  of  $ 1.10\mathrm{V} $ , a  $ J_{\mathrm{SC}} $  of  $ 23.40\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $ , and FF of  $ 76.0\% $ . The enhanced PCE mainly comes from the improving FF and  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $ , which demonstrates the ability to passivate defects and thereby suppress nonradiative recombination with TBA-Azo doping in PCBM. To confirm the reliability and repeatability of the performance enhancement obtained by employing TBA-Azo, PCEs of 20 individual pristine and TBA-Azo-treated solar cells were statistically analyzed. Statistic PCE distributions are presented in Figure 3c, which indicated that

the MBE can increase the reproducibility of PSCs. The maximum power point (MPP) tracking under the standard AM 1.5G illumination shown in Figure 3d depicts a stabilized photocurrent and PCE for the champion PSCs with TBA-Azo.

The static water contact angle between the perovskite film and TBA-Azo is larger, as shown in Figure 4a. We attribute the enhanced stability to the increased hydrophobicity of TBA-Azo, in which the hydrophobic carbon chain tails tend to form a water-repelling barrier on the perovskite grain surface. We also investigated the operational stability and water repellence ability of the PSCs with MBE under the humidity level of  $ 50 \pm 10\% $  (room temperature). As shown in Figure 4b, the devices of TBA-Azo doping showed a better stability, which could maintain  $ 90\% $  of the origin PCE after  $ 500\mathrm{h} $  without encapsulation, while the pristine one can only contain the same level in less than  $ 200\mathrm{h} $ . To show the unique water repellence ability of the MBE, we put the TBA-Azo doping devices into water with the illumination of  $ 1.5\mathrm{G} $  light intensity. As Figure 4c shows, the photograph was taken when the device was soaked into water for  $ 5\mathrm{min} $ . The electric meter shows the real-time  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  of the devices to prove the outstanding water repellence ability. Compared with the value in Figure 3a, the lower  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  might probably be due to the decrease of light intensity in water.

In addition, to monitor the water repellence ability of MBE, we specially prepared different concentrations of TBA-Azo solution (0, 0.2, 0.5 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) mixed with PCBM for spin coating on the surface of the perovskite layer. The decomposition of different concentrations of TBA-Azo doping devices in air was compared. As shown in Figure S5, the perovskite layer of the device without TBA-Azo doping completely decomposes after a week, while the decomposition degree of the perovskite layer is relatively reduced with the increase of TBA-Azo concentration. The amazing results indicated that TBA-Azo shows a good potential to improve the hydrophobic ability, which is attributed to the long carbon chain structure.

# EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

Materials and Precursor Preparation. PTAA, PCBM, and   $  PbI_{2}  $   (99.99%) were purchased from XI'an Polymer Light Technology Corp, and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.9%) and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Methylammonium iodide (MAI) was purchased from Greatcell Solar. Toluene and isopropanol were purchased from Beijing Chemical reagent. In addition,   $  C_{60}  $   and BCP were purchased from Luminescence Technology. To prepare   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   precursor solution, MAI and   $  PbI_{2}  $   were dissolved in a mixed solvent of DMF and DMSO = 9:1 mixed solution. The concentration of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   in DMF and DMSO is 1.0 mol L  $  ^{-1}  $  . To obtain the hole transport layer, 0.5 mg of PTAA was dissolved in 1 mL of toluene. Moreover, the TBA-Azo were then prepared in the dichlorobenzene solution (contained 20 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   PCBM) with the ratios of 0.5, 2, 5, 10 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  . The PSCs devices with the TBA-Azo are called TBA-Azo doping devices (with TBA-Azo) and the pristine devices are without the TBA-Azo doping.

Device Fabrication. The ITO glass was completely cleaned for 15 min with acetone, ethanol, and deionized water, respectively, by ultrasound. The glass was then exposed to UV treatment for 10 min. The prepared PTAA solution was spin-coated onto the precleaned ITO at 4000 rpm for 30 s; then ITO with PTAA solution films were annealed on the heating table at 100 °C for 20 min. Then the substrate was transferred to the coater for perovskite active layer deposition and further equipment manufacturing. Thereafter, toluene was coated on top of the perovskite film. The perovskite film was annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. MBE solution would be then spin-coated on at 5000 rpm for 30 s and be annealed at 90 °C for 30 min. We use the vacuum evaporation method to deposit   $  C_{60}  $  (  $  \sim  $  30–40 nm), BCP (10 nm), and Cu (100 nm) copper, respectively, to complete the device preparation process.

Characterizations and Measurements. For current density–voltage characteristic curves, air mass 1.5 G simulated irradiation with an Oriel 300 W solar simulator of an intensity of 100 mW cm  $  ^{2}  $  was employed with a computer-programmed Keithley 2601 source meter. The J-V scan rate is 0.2 V s  $  ^{-1}  $ . The incident photon-to-current efficiency (IPCE) spectra were obtained with a Pharos Technology QEM1000. The TRPL curves were recorded by using the steady state and transient state fluorescence spectrometer (FLS920, Edinburgh Instrument). The unencapsulated devices were kept in air with the humidity of 50 ± 10% at room temperature for the long-term stability and light-induced degradation.
