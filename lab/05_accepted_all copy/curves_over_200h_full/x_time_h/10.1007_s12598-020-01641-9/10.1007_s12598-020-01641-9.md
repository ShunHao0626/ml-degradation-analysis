# Electrodeposited transparent PEDOT for inverted perovskite solar cells: improved charge transport and catalytic performances

Cong-Tan Zhu, Ying Yang  $  ^{*}  $    $  ^{ID}  $  , Fei-Yu Lin, Yuan Luo, Shu-Peng Ma, Liu Zhu, Xue-Yi Guo

Received: 12 August 2020/Revised: 16 September 2020/Accepted: 21 September 2020  
© GRINM Bohan (Beijing) Publishing Co., Ltd 2021

Abstract The acidic, corrosive effect of sodium polystyrene sulfonate (PSS) in poly 3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene:sodium polystyrene sulfonate (PEDOT:PSS) limits the stability of inverted perovskite solar cells (PSCs) based on the ITO/PEDOT:PSS/perovskite/PCBM/BCP/Ag structure. In this work, a poly 3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene (PEDOT) hole transport layer (HTL) with high hole mobility and good catalytic performance was prepared by electrochemical cyclic voltammetry (CV) method for inverted PSCs. By controlling the CV cycles (from 1 to 5 cycles) and EDOT monomer solution concentration (from 0.5 to  $ 2.0\mathrm{mmol}\cdot \mathrm{L}^{-1} $ ) of electrochemical deposition, the thickness, morphology, optical and electrochemical properties of PEDOT could be accurately adjusted. The optimal photovoltaic performance with current density ( $ J_{\mathrm{sc}} $ ) of  $ 22.19\mathrm{mA}\cdot \mathrm{cm}^{-2} $ , open circuit voltage ( $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $ ) of  $ 0.94\mathrm{V} $ , fill factor (FF) of 0.65 and photoelectric conversion efficiency of  $ 13.56\% $  was obtained when deposition of PEDOT with 1 CV cycle and EDOT concentration of  $ 0.5\mathrm{mmol}\cdot \mathrm{L}^{-1} $ . At this point, the perovskite showed good crystallization, optimal optical, charge transport and recombination performance, resulting in better  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  and photoelectric conversion efficiency (PCE) compared to the devices with higher CV cycle numbers and 3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene (EDOT) concentration. For comparison with spin-coated PEDOT:PSS, the device with electrodeposited PEDOT showed improved  $ J_{\mathrm{sc}} $  and comparable  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $ , which may

result from its better charge transport and catalytic ability. The device with spin-coated PEDOT:PSS showed photoelectric conversion efficiency of 12.25%, which was lower than that based on electrodeposited PEDOT (13.56%) with 1 CV cycles and 0.5 mmol·L  $  ^{-1}  $  EDOT concentration. And the device with electrodeposited PEDOT as HTLs showed more excellent air stability. In ambient air ((32 ± 5) °C and RH: 70% ± 20%), it still maintained more than 80% of the initial photoelectric conversion efficiency after 1000 h. In comparison, the photoelectric conversion efficiency of the device with PEDOT:PSS decreased to 20% of the initial value after storage for 500 h. From this study, a facial and low-cost way to prepare PEDOT HTL with high performances that better than the traditional PEDOT:PSS has been explored, which is expected to eliminate the acidic, corrosive effect of PSS in PEDOT:PSS.

Keywords Electrochemically cyclic voltammetry; Inverted perovskite solar cells; Electrodeposited PEDOT; Hole mobility; Tafel analysis

# 1 Introduction

Hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted great attention in recent years   $  [1]  $  . The power conversion efficiency of PSCs has exceeded 25%   $  [2]  $  , which shows great potential in photovoltaic field. Compared to the traditional n-i-p PSCs, the inverted p-i-n PSCs have the following unique advantages: (i) negligible hysteresis effect and better environmental stability due to the immobilization of iodine ions or vacancies in the bulk of the perovskite layer by PCBM molecules   $  [3, 4]  $  ; (ii) applicable in roll-to-roll flexible devices with no concerns about the high-temperature treated nanocrystal electron transport layer   $  [5–7]  $  .

Typical hole transport layer (HTL) in inverted PSCs includes poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly (styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS) [8], PTAA, nickel oxide and some small molecule HTLs [9–12]. PEDOT:PSS has the advantages such as high transparency and good flexibility [13]. However, the electrical conductivity and moisture stability of PEDOT:PSS is relatively low. This is because of the presence of non-conductive and hydrophilic PSS [14, 15]. Inefficient charge transport in PEDOT:PSS results in charge accumulation at the interface and inferior photovoltaic performances [16, 17]. Despite these undesired effects, PSS is still widely used in inverted perovskite solar cells as additive because it stabilizes the PEDOT in aqueous solution and increases its work function [18]. Approaches improving the PEDOT:PSS layer to achieve higher photovoltaic performances in inverted PSCs mostly focused on doping [19–23] and interfacial modification [24–27]. Seldom studies have reported the single PEDOT layer without PSS for inverted PSCs.

Compared to PEDOT:PSS, single PEDOT shows better electrical conductivity, electrochemical stability and transparency, which has important application prospects in the fields of organic light-emitting diode and solar cells   $  [28, 29]  $  . However, PEDOT is not stable in the aqueous solution due to its hydrophobic nature. This results in a poor film formation and inferior interfacial properties, which increases pinholes in perovskite layer and reduces the charge transport in PSCs. Except for preparing PEDOT layer from aqueous solution, it can also be obtained from electro-polymerization   $  [30, 31]  $  , which reduces the problem of solubility and the need of PSS. Furthermore, electrodeposition method offers an effective way with good reproducibility to form uniform PEDOT layer with good interfacial contact and tunable electronic properties   $  [32]  $  . The use of electrodeposited PEDOT has shown good results in organic and dye-sensitized solar cells   $  [10, 31]  $  . Qiao and his colleagues have introduced electrochemical deposited PEDOT into dye-sensitized solar cells (DSSCs) to investigate their stability at high temperatures   $  [10]  $  . Somboonsub et al.   $  [29]  $   have prepared nanofibrous PEDOT films by electrochemical deposition and applied them in DSSCs and the device obtained 5.2% of photoelectric conversion efficiency (PCE). Li et al.   $  [33]  $   have electrodeposited PEDOT on a silicon substrate to prepare silicon/organic solar cells. However, in inverted PSCs, the electrodeposition of PEDOT has seldom been explored.

Herein, we prepared electrodeposited PEDOT HTL by electrochemical cyclic voltammetry (CV) method and used it to replace the conventional PEDOT:PSS HTL in inverted perovskite solar cells. After adjusting the CV cycles and 3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene (EDOT) concentration, the thickness of PEDOT film was controlled. By reducing the film thickness, the PEDOT posed a higher catalytical and

charge transport ability of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  and the photovoltaic performances of the inverted PSCs were greatly enhanced. Moreover, compared to traditional PEDOT:PSS HTL, the electrodeposited PEDOT contributed more significantly to improving the carrier transport in the device, leading to better photoelectric conversion efficiency of devices with electrodeposited PEDOT (13.56%) and PEDOT:PSS HTL (12.25%). This facial electrodeposited method is universal and applicable to catalytical performance control of the PEDOT and the crystallization control of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ , which is crucial for high performance of inverted PSCs without PSS.

# 2 Experimental

# 2.1 Electrochemical synthesis of PEDOT

The PEDOT film was prepared using a three-electrode system using an Ag/AgCl reference electrode, Pt as counter electrode and conductive glass (ITO) as working electrode. The supporting electrolyte solution contained 0.5/1.0/1.5/2.0 mmol·L  $  ^{-1}  $  EDOT monomer, 2.5/5.0/7.5/10.0 mmol·L  $  ^{-1}  $  LiClO  $  _{4}  $  and 2.5/5.0/7.5/10.0 mmol·L  $  ^{-1}  $  sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS). The electrochemical polymerization was performed by CV. The parameters of the CV were set as follows: the highest potential of +1.20 V, the lowest potential of -0.10 V, the starting potential of -0.10 V, the stop potential of -0.09 V and the scanning speed of 0.1 V·s  $  ^{-1}  $ . The CV cycles number is set to 5 gradients, which were 1–5 in order.

# 2.2 Fabrication of inverted perovskite solar cells

Fluorine-doped tin oxide (ITO) substrates with a resistance of   $  \sim15\ \Omega  $   per square were cleaned with a detergent and deionized water, followed by ethanol. The cleaned ITO substrates were dried with vacuum drying box and kept for 30 min. Then, the PEDOT film was polymerized on the ITO substrates with different CV cycles by electrochemical CV, which was thermally annealed at   $  140\ ^{\circ}C  $   for 30 min under an ambient atmosphere. As the controlled device, PEDOT:PSS was spin-coated at   $  6000\ r\cdot min^{-1}  $   for 40 s on ITO substrate and then annealed at   $  120\ ^{\circ}C  $   on a hotplate for 20 min. To prepare the perovskite film, the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   precursor solution composed of   $  PbI_{2}  $   and   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $   with a molar ratio of 1:1 was stirred in a mixture of DMSO:DMF (1:4 in volume ratio) at   $  60\ ^{\circ}C  $   for 12 h. The HTL substrates were transferred into a   $  N_{2}  $  -filled glove box, where   $  1.2\ mol\cdot L^{-1}\ CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   precursor solution was applied onto the ITO/PEDOT(or PEDOT:PSS) substrates by two consecutive spin-coating steps at 4000 and   $  6000\ r\cdot min^{-1}  $   for 10 and 20 s, respectively. # 2.3 Characterization and measurements

The surface morphologies, transmittance and chemical structure of electrochemical CV polymeric PEDOT film and perovskite film were measured by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, Quanta 200 FEG, FEI Co.), ultraviolet–visible spectrophotometer (UV–Vis, Hitachi, Japan) and Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR, Thermo Nicolet Corporation, Nexus 670, America), respectively. The roughness (RMS) of PEDOT films was measured by atomic force microscope (AFM, Bruker Dimension Icon), and the hole mobility test was carried out on electrochemical workstation (PGSTAT302N, Metrom, AUT86802, Switzerland) in the dark. The steady state photoluminescence (PL) spectra of PEDOT/MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  films were measured by using FLS1000 with a 466-nm pulse (Edinburgh Instruments, United Kingdom, 200–1700 nm full-range steady-state spectrum). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of perovskite films were measured by using X-ray diffractometer (Brooke, Germany D8 Advance). The current density–voltage (J–V) characteristics of PSCs were measured in an ambient atmosphere using a Keithley 2400 source meter under AM 1.5G solar illumination at 100 mW·cm  $  ^{-2}  $  (Newport 91160S). The active area of the cell was 0.1657 cm  $  ^{2}  $ . The external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra of PSCs were measured in spectral response measurement system (QE-R, Guang Yan, China). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed on an electrochemical workstation (PGSTAT302N, Metrom, AUT86802, Switzerland) in the dark with a bias near the corresponding  $  V_{oc}  $  of individual cells. And the Tafel curve was measured by electrochemical workstation in the hole-only PEDOT/MAPbI  $  _{3}  $ /PEDOT under AM 1.5 G at 100 mW·cm  $  ^{-2}  $  light illumination.

# 3 Results and discussion

# 3.1 Formation of electrodeposited PEDOT

As shown in Fig. 1a, the thickness and morphology of PEDOT are controlled by CV cycles in a solution

containing EDOT, SDS and  $ \mathrm{LiClO_4} $ . The oxidation peak at  $ +0.88\mathrm{V} $  is observed in CV curves in Fig. 1b, at which the polymerization of EDOT happens [34]. Generally, the polymerization of PEDOT is considered to be a cationic radical polymerization which is a stepwise process, as shown in Fig. 1c [35]. The EDOT monomer is oxidized to radical cation, and then two radical cations remove two protons to form a dimer. The dimer is further oxidized to a dimer radical cation, which is coupled with the monomer radical cation to form a trimer. And eventually PEDOT is formed [36].

# 3.2 Morphology and optical properties of electrodeposited PEDOT

All the electrodeposited PEDOT and spin-coated PEDOT:PSS film for surface, cross-sectional SEM and AFM characterization (Fig. 2) were deposited on ITO. Clear grain boundaries are observed in electrodeposited PEDOT film with CV cycles from 1 to 2 (Fig. 2a, b), which may result from the ITO. After increasing the CV cycles from 3 to 5 (Fig. 2c–e), the grain boundaries generally become blurry with an appearance of fibrous structure resulting from PEDOT. Meanwhile, the PEDOT surface becomes full of cavities when the CV cycles reache 5 (Fig. 2e). From the cross-sectional SEM images (Fig. 2a'–e'), it is found that the film thickness increases from 60 to 135 nm as CV cycles increase from 1 to 5. The linear increase in film thickness may result from the stepwise growth of the PEDOT on ITO. From AFM analysis (Fig. 2a'–f' and Table 1), it is noticed that the roughness (RMS) of the PEDOT increases from 3.00 to 8.60 nm with CV cycles increasing. The morphology and roughness change of the PEDOT film could be due to the formation of polymer clusters between grain boundaries which loosen the contact of grains and make the surface rough. Compared to the electrodeposited PEDOT films, spin-coated PEDOT:PSS film shows relatively thinner film thickness (Fig. 2f', RMS = 1.66 nm). The PEDOT film with CV cycle of 1 exhibits comparable surface morphology and roughness with PEDOT:PSS film.

To verify the presence of PEDOT, FTIR was applied to characterize the electrodeposited product (Fig. 3a). Generally, PEDOT has structures such as C = C, C–S, C–O–C and polythiophene skeleton, as shown in Fig. 3a. The vibrations at 1320 and 1450 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  are attributed to the C–C and C = C stretching of the quinoidal structure of the thiophene ring [37]. The vibrations at 1050, 1140 and 1185 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  are assigned to the C–O–C stretching modes of ethylenedioxy. The peaks at 620, 840 and 982 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  correspond to the C–S bond stretching in the thiophene ring [37]. FTIR spectra therefore indicate that the PEDOT thin films are successfully prepared by electrodeposition methods.

Sunlight penetrates through the PEDOT HTL to the perovskite layer, and thus, a high optical transparency of the HTL is required to facilitate the light absorbance of perovskite. Figure 3b shows the transmittance spectroscopy of PEDOT films with different CV cycles. The light transmittance of the ITO/PEDOT structure is maintained between  $ 70\% $  and  $ 80\% $ , which guarantees solar energy collection and light capture rate of the devices. In addition, as the number of CV cycles increases, the transmittance decreases. The main reason for the difference in light transmission is related to different thickness of PEDOT films with different CV cycles. Because of the thinnest thickness of the spin-coated PEDO:PSS film  $ (20\mathrm{nm}) $ , it shows the highest transmittance among all the electrodeposited PEDOT films.

The hole mobility of the electrodeposited PEDOT and spin-coated PEDOT:PSS HTLs was measured using space charge limited current (SCLC) method on a hole-only device structured as ITO/PEDOT or PEDOT:PSS HTLs/

Ag with current density-photovoltage (J-V) curves, as shown in Fig. 3c, where the abscissa   $  V_{app}  $   is the applied voltage,   $  V_{bl}  $   is the built-in electric field and   $  V_{s}  $   is the voltage loss. The hole mobility is calculated using the Mott-Gurney law expressed by   $  J = (9\mu\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon V^{2})/(8d^{3})  $  , where   $  \mu  $   is the hole mobility,   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   is the free space permittivity (  $  8.85 \times 10^{-14}  $   C·V  $  ^{-1}  $  ·cm  $  ^{-1}  $  ),   $  \varepsilon  $   is the dielectric constant (the value of organic materials is generally 3), V is applied voltage and d is the thickness of HTL [38]. It is found that the hole mobility decreases from to   $  73.13 \times 10^{-3}  $   to   $  2.99 \times 10^{-3}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $  ·V  $  ^{-1}  $  ·s  $  ^{-1}  $   when CV cycles increase from 1 to 5 (Table 1). And the hole mobility of all the electrodeposited PEDOT film is higher than that of PEDOT:PSS film (  $  6.46 \times 10^{-5}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $  ·V  $  ^{-1}  $  ·s  $  ^{-1}  $  ). Higher hole mobility indicates better carriers transport, which will benefit for charge collection. This means that electrodeposited PEDOT is expected to promote the hole mobility effectively of the HTL for improving the performances of PSCs.

# 4 Conclusion

In conclusion, a PEDOT HTL with high hole mobility and improved electrocatalytic performances could be obtained by electrochemical polymerization deposition. From controlling the CV cycles and EDOT concentration of deposition, the thickness, morphology, optical and electrochemical properties of PEDOT could be adjusted accurately to match inverted perovskite solar cells. The optimal photovoltaic performance with   $  J_{sc}  $   of 22.19 mA·cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ,   $  V_{oc}  $   of 0.94 V, FF of 0.65 and photoelectric conversion efficiency of 13.56% was obtained when deposition of PEDOT with 1 CV cycle and 0.5 mmol·L  $  ^{-1}  $   EDOT. At this point, the perovskite crystal size of perovskite is 564 nm and the hole mobility and charge transport showed great improvement. Compared to the solar cell based on spin-coated PEDOT:PSS, the device with electrodeposited PEDOT showed improved   $  J_{sc}  $   and comparable   $  V_{oc}  $  , which may result from its better charge transport and catalytic ability. And the device with PEDOT as HTLs showed more excellent air stability. In ambient air ((32 ± 5) °C and RH: 70% ± 20%), it still maintained more than 80% of the initial photoelectric conversion efficiency after 1000 h. In comparison, the photoelectric conversion efficiency of the device with PEDOT:PSS as the hole transport layer decreased to 20% of the initial value after storage for 500 h. In all, from electrochemical polymerization deposition method, we find a facial and low-cost way to prepare PEDOT HTLs with high performances that shows better performance to the traditional PEDOT:PSS, which is expected to eliminate the acidic, corrosive effects of PSS in PEDOT:PSS.
