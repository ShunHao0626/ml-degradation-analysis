# Simultaneous defect passivation and hole mobility enhancement of perovskite solar cells by incorporating anionic metal-organic framework into hole transport materials

Jindan Zhang  $  ^{a,b}  $  , Shanshan Guo  $  ^{a}  $  , Mengqi Zhu  $  ^{a}  $  , Chi Li  $  ^{a}  $  , Jingan Chen  $  ^{a}  $  , Lizhen Liu  $  ^{a}  $  , Shengchang Xiang  $  ^{a,b}  $  , Zhangjing Zhang  $  ^{a,b,*}  $

Originated from the defects of hybrid halide perovskite and the intrinsic low hole mobility of hole transport materials (HTM), the perovskite degradation and interface carrier recombination hampered the stability and power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs. Herein, we construct a dual-functional layer HTM-FJU-17 by incorporating the   $  (\mathrm{Me}_{2}\mathrm{NH}_{2})^{+}  $  -encapsulated indium-based anionic metal-organic framework (FJU-17) as a "capsule" into HTM. The FJU-17 capsule would passivate the organic cation vacancies with releasing   $  (\mathrm{Me}_{2}\mathrm{NH}_{2})^{+}  $  ion, while its anionic framework can stabilize the positively charged oxidized HTM to enhance hole mobility. As a result, PSCs exhibit suppressed charge recombination with PCE improvement from 18.32% to 20.34%, and stable device is obtained with 90% retaining of the original PCE after 1000 h in ambient condition. This work demonstrates the promising potential of using the dual-functional layer based on the large family of anionic MOFs/HTMs to manufacture PSC devices with high performance and simplified preparation processes.

# 1. Introduction

Although the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) swiftly increased from  $ 3.8\% $  to  $ 25.2\% $  in 10 years due to the development of composition engineering, perovskite film growth control and perovskite/transport layer interface engineering [1-10], the commercialization of PSCs still requires further advancement of efficiency and stability, which have been proved to be significantly influenced by perovskite defects and the performance of hole transport materials (HTM). In a polycrystalline perovskite film, defects such as halide anion vacancies and organic cation vacancies are ubiquitous, especially at surface and grain boundaries [11-14]. These defects cause charge recombination and perovskite decomposition, greatly impairing the photovoltaic performances and stability of PSCs [15-18]. To passivate and mitigate defects, introducing additional functional layers on top of perovskite film has been regarded as an efficient strategy [19,20]. For instance, the organic ammonium cations with larger molecule, which are difficult to evaporate and loss during the thermal annealing process, are widely used to fabricate a two-dimensional

layered structure to suppress the formation of organic cation vacancies [21,22]. However, the usually unmatched energy level and low hole mobility [22,23] of the additional layers will impede the carrier extraction at perovskite/HTM interface, limiting the enhancement of PSCs efficiency. On the other hand, the performance of HTMs is another key factor for efficient PSCs [24–26]. Unfortunately, the low hole extraction and transport ability of HTM itself cannot meet the demands of high performance PSCs in the current stage, as observed in the most widely used HTM, spiro-OMeTAD [27,28]. And lithium bis(trifluoromethyl sulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) is usually doped into HTM to compensate the positively charged oxidized HTM (such as spiro-OMeTAD  $  ^{+}  $  ) with its counter ions, so as to stabilize rather high quantities of holes and enhance the hole conductivity of the HTM [29,30]. However, the lithium ion migration toward the interface of perovskite/HTM will decrease the efficiency and stability of PSCs [31]. To date, it is still very challenging and difficult to simultaneously fulfill high PCE and stability of PSCs, because the independent design on passivating layers of perovskite films and dopants of HTMs is hard to achieve the requirements of both defect passivation and hole mobility enhancement,

even accompanied with the complicated preparation process of the devices. Therefore, the new strategy to address the problems of perovskite defects and the performance of HTMs at the same time is highly desirable, to the best of our knowledge, which has not yet been reported.

Based on the structure-designable and function-controllable nature, the metal-organic framework materials (MOFs) have attracted more and more attentions   $  [32–36]  $  . And recently, due to their various porous structures, oxidation capacity and good chemical stability, several MOFs have been approved to be excellent charge-transport layers or interlayers in PSCs   $  [37–47]  $   to promote perovskite crystallinity   $  [38–43]  $  , oxidize the HTM   $  [44,45]  $  , protect moisture invasion   $  [44,46]  $   or other effects   $  [43,47]  $  . However, the research on their potential in PSCs has been significantly left behind others applications. In particular, the development of MOF-modified HTM is still at a very early stage   $  [44,45]  $  , and it is important but barely reports to take account of the effect of the MOF in HTM layer on vacancy passivation of the adjacent perovskite layer.

Herein, we use a   $  (\mathrm{Me}_{2}\mathrm{NH}_{2})^{+}  $  -encapsulated indium-based anionic MOF (FJU-17 [48], Scheme 1a) as a “capsule” into HTM to develop a dual-functional layer (namely, HTM-FJU-17 DFL) (Scheme 1b). Compared with traditional additive layers, the HTM-FJU-17 DFL passivates perovskite defects by filling the organic cation vacancies with releasing   $  (\mathrm{Me}_{2}\mathrm{NH}_{2})^{+}  $   ions, which is efficient because the ordered reticular framework of the MOF enables uniform distribution of   $  (\mathrm{Me}_{2}\mathrm{NH}_{2})^{+}  $   ions. Meanwhile, the HTM-FJU-17 DFL enhances hole mobility via compensating and stabilizing the oxidized spiro-OMeTAD  $  ^{+}  $   with the anionic framework of FJU-17 capsule, as well eliminates lithium ion migration due to the strong binding interaction between Li ion and the anionic framework. As a result, suppressed charge recombination, enhanced PCE and improved stability PSCs are obtained simultaneously. As expected, the hole mobility can increase from   $  3.11 \times 10^{-4}  $   to   $  1.05 \times 10^{-3}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $  (Vs)  $  ^{-1}  $  , and PSC devices with HTM-FJU-17 show improved PCE from 18.32% to 20.34%, as well as enhanced stability with 90% retaining of their original PCE after 1000 h storing at condition air.

# 2.1. Materials and methods

All reagents and solvents used were commercially available without further purification. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) was carried out with a PANalytical X'Pert3 powder diffractometer equipped with a Cu sealed tube ( $ \lambda = 1.541874 $  Å) at 40 kV and 40 mA over the  $ 2\theta $  range of 5-30°. Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) (excitation wavelength 300 nm) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) were conducted with Edinburgh Instruments LTD (FLS 980). UV–Vis absorption spectra were measured using a PerkinElmer model Lambda 950 spectrometer. The  $ J-V $  curves were measured under illumination at AM 1.5 G (100 mW cm $ ^{-2} $ ) using a solar simulator (Newport, Oriel Class A, 91,195 A, equipped with a 300 W xenon lamp) and a source meter (Keithley 2420). The light intensity was adjusted by an NREL-calibrated Si solar cell, and active area of solar cells was 0.09 cm $ ^{2} $ . The alternating-current (AC) impedance measurements were recorded with Zahner Pro2 electrochemical work station from 1 Hz to 10 $ ^{5} $  Hz at 0.9 V in dark, and fitted by the Z-view software. The used equivalent circuit is shown in inset in Fig. 1i. The Rs is the series resistance and Rct is the charge transport resistance. The fitting results are expressed with solid lines in the EIS spectra.

# 2.2. Synthesis of FJU-17  $ \left[(\mathrm{CH}_3)_2\mathrm{NH}_2\right] $  [In(L)]·4.5DMF·16H2O)

The synthesis procedure of FJU-17 and ligand 4,4',4'',4'''-(1,4-Phenylbenis (pyridine-4,2,6-triyl))-tetrabenzoic acid (H $  _{4}  $ L) was reported in the previous work[46]. H $  _{4}  $ L (10 mg, 0.0143 mmol), In(NO $  _{3}  $ ) $  _{3}  $ ·xH $  _{2}  $ O (6.1 mg, 0.0205 mmol), DMF (1 mL) and HNO $  _{3}  $  (0.20 mL, 2.7 M in DMF) were added to a vial, and the solution was stirred at 80 °C for 4 h. After cooling to room temperature, the precipitate was washed and dried at room temperature. Then the target product was obtained by solvent exchange with chlorobenzene.

# 2.3. Device fabrication

PSCs were prepared with FTO/TiO $ _2 $ /CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ PbI $ _3 $ /HTM/Ag structure. Etched Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass was washed by acetone, ethanol and deionized water in ultrasonic bath for 20 min, respectively. The 0.15 M titanium tetraisopropanolate in isopropanol solution was spin-coated on FTO substrate at 2500 rpm for 25 s. After thermal annealing at 450 °C for 30 min in air, a TiO $ _2 $  paste (Dyesol DSL 18NR-T) in isopropanol (1:8, mass ratio) was spin-coated on the substrate at 5000 rpm for 25 s, followed by annealing at 550 °C for 30 min.

The perovskite solution was prepared by dissolving 159 mg   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $   and 461 mg   $  PbI_{2}  $   in 1 mL mixed solvent of DMF and DMSO (volume ratio 4:1). Then, the solution was coated onto the mp-  $  TiO_{2}/bl-TiO_{2}/FTO  $   substrate by two consecutive spin-coating steps, at 1000 and 5000 rpm for 10 s and 20 s, respectively. During the second spin-coating (5000 rpm) step, 150   $  \mu  $  L chlorobenzene was dropped onto the substrate after 15 s. The intermediate phase substrate was then put on a heating plate at 100 °C for 10 min. The HTM was deposited on the top of the perovskite layer by spin coating at 4000 rpm for 30 s and heated again. The pristine HTM was prepared by dissolving 72.9 mg 2,2',7,7'-Tetrakis(N,N-di(4-methoxyphenyl)amino]-9,9'-spirobifluorene(Spiro-OMeTAD), 29   $  \mu  $  L 4-Tert-butylpyridine (TBP), and 17.5   $  \mu  $  L solution of 520 mg lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide in 1 mL acetonitrile. Different amount FJU-17 (HTM-FJU-17, 0.25 mg, 0.5 mg, 1 mg, 2 mg) were added into pristine HTM to form mixture solution, and the HTM with FJU-17 are named HTM-FJU-17 DFL. Finally, Ag was thermally evaporated on top of the device to form the metal cathode.

The PXRD patterns (Fig. S1b) demonstrate the synthesized FJU-17 powder is structural integrity with high purity after solvent exchange. Fig. 1a presents the morphology of HTM-FJU-17 film. It clearly reveals that FJU-17 particles are homogenously distributed in the film without any obvious aggregation in the field of vision. To determine the impacts of HTM-FJU-17 DFL on PSCs performances, typical PSCs with structure of FTO/TiO $ _2 $ /perovskite/HTM-FJU-17/Ag (Fig. S3) are tested; and PSCs with pristine HTM are used as reference devices. Very encouragingly, the density-voltage characteristics (Fig. 1b, Table 1) show that the short-circuit current ( $ J_{SC} $ ), open-circuit voltage ( $ V_{OC} $ ) and fill factor (FF) values of the devices increase with HTM-FJU-17 incorporation, resulting in performance improvements with average PCE from 17.77% to 19.50% and the maximum PCE from 18.32% to 20.34% (with an amount of 0.5 mg, Fig. 1e, S4, Table S1). The  $ J-V $  hysteresis measurements (Fig. 1b, Table S2) reveal suppressed hysteresis on HTM-FJU-17 devices, with hysteresis index (HI) decreasing to 0.220 from an original value of 0.305. The improvement performances in  $ J-V $  and hysteresis curves indicate the more efficient carrier extraction and reduced recombination due to the incorporation of HTM-FJU-17. And the initiation position in dark current is changed from 0.85 to 0.95 V (Fig. 1c), indicating the charge recombination is restrained with HTM-FJU-17. PCE distribution of 20 devices in Fig. 1d exhibits the reproducibility of devices with HTM-FJU-17 and declares a significant upshift trend for HTM-FJU-17 based PSCs compared with reference devices.

The higher incident photo-to-current conversion efficiency (IPCE) for the HTM-FJU-17 device throughout the entire wavelength range in Fig. 1f can be ascribed to the carrier recombination suppressing of the HTM-FJU-17. The integrated currents in IPCE are 22.10 and  $ 20.68\mathrm{mA} $   $ \mathrm{cm}^{-2} $  for PSCs with HTM-FJU-17 and pristine HTM, respectively, and the discrepancy comparing with  $ J_{SC} $  values obtained from J-V curves is probably due to the capacitive current from J-V hysteresis [52,53]. To further elucidate the interfacial charge transport and recombination behaviors,  $ J - V $  curves are analyzed based on the single heterojunction solar cell theory. The series resistance  $ R_{S} $  can be obtained by fitting the linear part of the  $ \mathrm{-dV / dJ} $  vs  $ (J_{SC} - J)^{-1} $  curve. By using HTM-FJU-17 DFL,  $ R_{S} $  reduces from 3.11 to  $ 0.22\Omega \mathrm{cm}^2 $  (Fig. 1g), and the reverse saturated current density  $ J_0 $  derived from the linear fitting result of the  $ \ln (J_{SC} - J) $  vs  $ V + R_{S}J $  plot (Fig. 1h) decreases from  $ 1.12\times 10^{-5} $  to  $ 8.80\times 10^{-9} $   $ \mathrm{mA\cdot cm^{-2}} $ . The decreased  $ R_{S} $  and  $ J_0 $  values suggest enhanced carrier transportation and suppressed recombination in HTM-FJU-17 DFL-included devices, thus supporting the positive effect of multifunctional FJU-17 capsule on the device performance. Also, the charge transport resistance (Rct) extracted by fitting the electrochemical impedance spectrometry data (Fig. 1i and Table S3) states an enhanced charge transport across the perovskite/HTM interface for the Rct value decreases from 192 to  $ 151\Omega $  with HTM-FJU-17 DFL incorporation.

To confirm the hole transport enhancement effect of the FJU-17 on HTM, the hole mobility (Fig. 2b) of HTM-FJU-17 are measured. And the space-charge-limited current (SCLC) measurement declares that the HTM-FJU-17 DFL (0.5 mg) has a higher hole mobility of  $ 1.05 \times 10^{-3} $  cm $ ^{2} $ (Vs) $ ^{-1} $  than  $ 3.11 \times 10^{-4} $  cm $ ^{2} $ (Vs) $ ^{-1} $  for the pristine HTM. Therefore, these results prove that the HTM-FJU-17 has the ability to reinforce the hole transport process, and thus, suppress carrier recombination in PSCs. As shown in Fig. S9a and b, the water contact angle of HTM-FJU-17 film is larger than that of pristine HTM, indicating that the HTM-FJU-17 is more hydrophobic. Impedance results (Fig. S9c) show that the conductivity of HTM film increases with the temperature rises after introducing FJU-17, and would keep stable and conductive even at 100 °C due to the conduction characteristics of MOF materials. Therefore, the corporation of FJU-17 into HTM would stabilize the spiro-OMeTAD and improve the PSCs stability against moisture and thermal.

The evaluations of passivation effect and stability are determined later. As shown in Fig. S10, the interaction of FJU-17 and perovskite is determined by XRD and XPS spectra. In XRD spectra, both fresh samples show the characteristic peaks of perovskite films, however, a   $  PbI_{2}  $   diffraction peak at   $  12.8^{\circ}  $   is observed in perovskite with pristine HTM sample after 120 h heating at   $  85^{\circ}C  $ , indicating the degradation of the 3D perovskite film. While no apparent   $  PbI_{2}  $   diffraction peak is observed in the sample of perovskite with HTM-FJU-17, suggesting that the HTM-FJU-17 have positive effects on perovskites crystallinity with enhanced thermal stability. And XPS measurement is performed on aging perovskite films (120 h) to further confirm the existence of the passivation effect and the interaction between the FJU-17 and   $  PbI_{2}  $ . As shown in Fig. S10c and d, all the peaks assigned to Pb 4f and I 3d shifts toward lower binding energy with FJU-17 incorporation. The shift to lower binding energy indicates the changed chemical environment of lead. According to the atomic ratios extracted from XPS data (Table S5), the atomic ratio of N/Pb remains 66% (decreases to 0.66 from 0.99) for the   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   film with pristine HTM after aging, while the value of the HTM-FJU-17 based sample maintains 72% (from 0.94 to 0.68). These results confirm that the   $  Me_{2}NH_{2}  $   cations from FJU-17 have passivated the organic vacancies, and have a strong interaction with   $  Pb^{2+}  $ .

Furthermore, the ambient, thermal and light-soaking stability of encapsulated PSCs based on HTM-FJU-17 DFL during a longer time was measured, respectively (Fig. 2f, S13–15). It is worth noting that although the addition of MOFs in HTM can improve the performance of PSCs, the complexity and cost of the fabrication process will increase [58–61]. Therefore, more works need to be done in the further improve the HTM properties and simplify the device fabrication process.

# 4. Conclusion

We demonstrated the incorporation of an indium-based anionic framework FUJ-17 containing   $  (\mathrm{Me}_{2}\mathrm{NH}_{2})^{+}  $   counterions as a capsule-type additives into HTM to construct a dual-functional layer for efficient and stable PSCs. HTM-FJU-17 has multifunctional effects on suppressing organic cation vacancies and facilitating hole mobility of PSCs simultaneously. And PSCs show suppressing charge recombination resulting

in the average PCE improved from 17.77% to 19.50% and a maximum PCE of 20.34% with the multifunctional HTM-FJU-17 DFL. The hole mobility of HTM-FJU-17 increase from   $  3.11 \times 10^{-4}  $   to   $  1.05 \times 10^{-3}  $     $  cm^{2}(Vs)^{-1}  $  , and the HTM-FJU-17-included PSCs exhibit remarkable long-term stability, retaining in   $  \sim100\%  $   at maximum power point tracking for 1 h and   $  \sim90\%  $   of their original PCE after 1000 h in air condition. These findings provide a new approach for HTM functional design to suppressing charge recombination and improving stability simultaneously for PSCs.
