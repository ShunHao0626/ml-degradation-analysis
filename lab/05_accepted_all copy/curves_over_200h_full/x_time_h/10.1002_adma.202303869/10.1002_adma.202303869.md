High-performance perovskite solar cells (PSCs) typically require interfacial passivation, yet this is challenging for the buried interface, owing to the dissolution of passivation agents during the deposition of perovskites. Here, we overcome this limitation with in-situ buried interface passivation – achieved via directly adding a cyanoacrylic acid-based molecular additive, namely BT-T, into the perovskite precursor solution. Classical and ab-initio molecular dynamics simulations reveal that BT-T spontaneously may self-assemble at the buried interface during the formation of the perovskite layer on a nickel oxide hole transporting layer. The preferential buried interface passivation results in facilitated hole transfer and suppressed charge recombination. In addition, residual BT-T molecules in the perovskite layer enhance its stability and homogeneity. We report a power-conversion efficiency (PCE) of 23.48% for 1.0 cm  $  ^{2}  $  . # 1. Introduction

Metal halide perovskites have attracted intensive research attention for optoelectronic applications in light of their excellent optoelectronic properties, including tunable bandgaps, strong light absorption, and long carrier diffusion lengths, combined with low-cost solution-processing.  $  ^{[1-4]}  $   In just a decade, the power-conversion efficiency (PCE) of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has reached a certified value of 25.8%,  $  ^{[5]}  $   which is competitive with that of crystalline silicon (c-Si) photovoltaics (PV). However, record-efficiency PSCs are lab-made small cells with an active area of merely   $  \sim0.1~cm^{2}  $  ; scaling up PSCs to large scales devices causes inferior device performance and stability – a challenge that hampers the commercialization of this emerging PV technology.  $  ^{[6,7]}  $

Efficient and stable PSCs require uniform and defect-less perovskite thin films at large areas, which can improve charge transport, suppress non-radiative energy loss, and minimize device degradation pathways.  $  ^{[8-11]}  $   Besides that, improvements in the performance and stability of PSCs also rely on the selection of efficient charge-transport layer (CTL) and passivation of perovskite/ CTL interfaces.  $  ^{[12-17]}  $   Compared with efficient but expensive and delicate organic hole transport layers (HTLs), such as 2,2'7,7'-tetrakis-(N, N-di-p-methoxyphenylamine)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-MeOTAD) and poly[bis (4-phenyl)(2,4,6-trimethylphenyl)amine] (PTAA),  $  ^{[18, 19]}  $   inorganic HTLs of nickel oxide (NiO  $  _{x}  $  ) are more favorable for fabricating highly stable PSCs, since they are low cost, stable and readily scalable.  $  ^{[20]}  $   In particular, inverted-structure PSCs with NiO  $  _{x}  $   HTLs have delivered some of the best-reported stability results under a variety of stressors, including the 85°C-85% relative humidity damp heat test, temperature cycling from -40°C to 85°C, and operational stability under continuous illumination.  $  ^{[21]}  $   Currently, PSCs with NiO  $  _{x}  $   HTLs still demonstrate lower efficiencies than those with organic HTLs.  $  ^{[22, 23]}  $   McGehee et. al. found that suppressing the redox reactions at the

$ \mathrm{NiO_x} $ -perovskite interface can enhance the open-circuit voltage ( $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $ ) of as-fabricated cells. $ ^{[24]} $  For the modification of interfaces in PSCs, great efforts have been devoted to the passivation of the top surface of perovskite films, which can be feasibly accomplished by treating them with ammonium halide solutions. $ ^{[25, 26]} $  Nevertheless, the passivation of the buried interface, i.e., the interface between the perovskite and bottom CTL, $ ^{[27]} $  remains a challenge: in prior, the buried interface passivation was demonstrated ex-situ in PSCs via the treatment of bottom CTLs before the perovskite deposition. $ ^{[8]} $  This approach, unfortunately, may cause the dissolution of passivating agents in the perovskite film during its subsequent deposition, eventually reducing the effectiveness of passivation.

We reason that in-situ buried interface passivation – established via directly adding molecular additives into the perovskite precursor inks – could resolve this issue and simplify the cells fabrication.  $  ^{[9, 28, 29]}  $   However, typical molecular additives mainly passivate the film surface and grain boundaries of perovskite films. It thereby required the rational design of modifiers for sufficient buried interface passivation: ideally, a molecular additive should exhibit stronger interaction with the bottom CTL than that with perovskites, such that they would self-assemble preferably at the buried interface during perovskite film formation.

Here, we report the use of a cyanoacrylic acid-based molecular additive as an in-situ buried interface modifier for inverted-structure PSCs with   $  NiO_{x}  $   HTLs. Our computational and experimental investigations reveal that this modifier can be anchored spontaneously onto the buried interface during perovskite deposition. This approach suppresses non-radiative charge carrier recombination and facilitates interfacial charge transfer, enabling the demonstration of a PCE of 23.48% for PSC with an active area of   $  1\ cm^{2}  $  . The encapsulated cell retained 95.4% of its initial performance after 1,960 h of operation under one-sun equivalent light illumination at   $  65^{\circ}C  $  .

# Organic dye as the in-situ buried interface modifier

We focused on inverted-structure PSCs with solution-processed   $  NiO_{x}  $   as the bottom HTL. We sought herein to endow the   $  NiO_{x}  $   with a molecular surface modifier – during the processing of the perovskite layer – that would passivate the buried interface, thereby improving the device performance and stability of PSCs. Here, we synthesized 2-cyano-3-(4-(7-(4-(diphenylamino)phenyl)benzo[c][1,2,5]thiadiazol-4-yl)phenyl)acrylic acid (BT-T) – a donor-acceptor organic molecule used in dye-sensitized solar cells (DSSCs).  $  ^{[30]}  $   BT-T comprises a triphenylamine electron donor and a benzothiadiazole-containing electron acceptor with cyanoacrylic acid as the anchoring group (Fig. 1a). It has been shown that the deprotonated cyanoacrylic acid group binds strongly to Ti ions.  $  ^{[31]}  $   Since the electrostatic potential (ESP) calculations indicate a high negative charge density at the cyanoacrylic acid side of the BT-T molecule (Fig. 1b), we reasoned that this molecule might be anchored onto   $  NiO_{x}  $   and perovskites via similar metal ion coordination. Density functional theory (DFT) computations of the highest-occupied-molecular-orbital (HOMO) and lower-unoccupied-molecular-orbital (LUMO) of the deprotonated BT-T molecule (Fig. 1c) further reveal that the carboxyl-group (COO-group) acts as a preferential channel for the hole transport between the HTL and   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   surfaces. The HOMO of the deprotonated BT-T molecule is a relevant orbital for the hole transport and the   $  FAPbI_{3}/BT-T/NiO_{x}  $   band alignment turns out to be favorable (Fig. S1).

Considering the full system is crucial to understanding the interaction of BT-T at the buried interface. DFT calculations of the   $  FAPbI_{3}/BT-T/NiO_{x}  $   interface show a clear tendency of the deprotonated BT-T molecule to bind almost entirely flat on   $  NiO_{x}  $   (Fig. 1f and Fig. S3) as a consequence of the high   $  NiO_{x}  $   surface reactivity due to unsaturated oxygen dangling bonds. Almost perfect planarity of BT-T was retained at finite temperatures, as confirmed by classical MD simulations at ambient conditions (Fig. S4). This preferential binding mode reduces the buried interface volume substantially, promoting optimal contact through BT-T between   $  NiO_{x}  $   and   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   (with an average   $  NiO_{x}-FAPbI_{3}  $   distance of about 8 Å) (Fig. S4). Both ab-initio and classical MD show that the carboxyl group of the molecule is anchored via one oxygen to the   $  NiO_{x}  $   surface, while the other oxygen stands almost perpendicular concerning the   $  NiO_{x}  $   surface; in addition, ab-initio MD reveals that the CN-group is simultaneously bonded to undercoordinated Pb on the   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   surface (Fig. 1e and Fig. S5).

# Characterization of the perovskite/NiO  $  _{x}  $  interface

Since DFT calculations indicate the strong binding of BT-T onto the buried interface, we sought to investigate experimentally the buried interface passivation with the BT-T additive. Perovskites with a stoichiometry of   $  Cs_{0.05}(FA_{0.87}MA_{0.13})_{0.95}\mathrm{Pb(I_{0.87}Br_{0.013})_{3}}  $   were prepared on   $  NiO_{x}  $  -coated ITO substrate. We firstly employed time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) to reveal the distribution of BT-T molecules throughout the entire thickness of the perovskite layer (Fig. 2a). We used I and NiO as the indicators for the perovskite and NiO  $  _{x}  $  layer, respectively; and sulfur as the tracing element for the BT-T molecule. The results indicate that BT-T distributes predominantly at the perovskite/NiO  $  _{x}  $  interface (i.e., buried interface), a condition for preferable buried interface passivation. This inhomogeneous distribution agrees well with the DFT calculations, where BT-T was found to have stronger interactions with NiO  $  _{x}  $  than perovskite surfaces.

To confirm BT-T chemically anchored on the   $  NiO_{x}  $   form a robustness of self-assembly of the monolayer (SAM), we then performed X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements. Fig. S6 shows that the ratio of S: Ni and N: Ni increased after increasing the concentration of BT-T in the spin-coating solution. After washing, the ratio of S: Ni and N: Ni in the different concentrations tend to be consistent, confirming that the chemically anchored BT-T monolayer was preserved at the buried interface because it could not be dissolved by the perovskite solution. Furthermore, we used angle-resolved XPS (AR-XPS) to confirm that BT-T is allowed to be located on the   $  NiO_{x}  $   surface and terminated as a single monolayer. In AR-XPS, the maximum probing depth is   $  3\lambda\cos\theta  $  , where the   $  \theta  $   is take-off angle and   $  \lambda  $   is the electrons of the material inelastic mean free path.  $  ^{[34, 35]}  $   At   $  0^{\circ}  $  , all composition information is the same as normal XPS. The sample was titled at   $  0^{\circ}  $  ,   $  30^{\circ}  $  , and   $  75^{\circ}  $   take-off angles, respectively. The probing depth is varied from approximately   $  \sim8  $   nm at   $  0^{\circ}  $   to only   $  \sim2  $   nm at   $  75^{\circ}  $  , which means the compositional information is closer to the   $  NiO_{x}  $   surface at a higher angle. Here we directly performed AR-XPS to characterize the component information of the   $  NiO_{x}/BT-T  $   surface after using a mixed solvent of DMSO and DMF (volume ratio of 4:1) to rinse up BT-T. As Fig. S7 shows, from   $  0^{\circ}  $   to 75, there is a slightly increased in the ratio of N: Ni and S: Ni, from 0.14 to 0.25 and 0.07 to 0.10, respectively, which indicates that BT-T is likely forming a surface layer of   $  NiO_{x}  $  .

# Structural properties of perovskite films

Next, we investigated how the BT-T additive affects the morphology and crystalline structure of perovskite films. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the perovskite films with and without BT-T are shown in Fig. 3a and b. Compared to the control film, the film with BT-T exhibited an enlarged average grain domain size (Fig. S13). Cross-sectional SEM (Fig. S14) displays that perovskite films containing BT-T deposited on  $ \mathrm{NiO_x} $  substrates exhibited the preferred perpendicular orientation of grains with respect to the substrate. This is accompanied by enlarged grains/domains compared to the pristine ones deposited on  $ \mathrm{NiO_x} $  and  $ \mathrm{NiO_x} $ -BT-T substrates. The improved film formation is ascribed to the retarded crystallization due to the Lewis acid-base interaction between the carboxylate functional group ( $ \mathrm{C = O} $ ) and under-coordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2 + }} $ , which was probed by FTIR (Fig. S15) and XPS (Fig. 3f). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the control and BT-T films (Fig. 3c) reveal the inhomogeneity of perovskite films, as evidenced by the coexistence of perovskite and  $ \mathrm{PbI_2} $  phases. We found that the BT-T addition reduced the formation of  $ \mathrm{PbI_2} $ . This improved phase homogeneity of perovskite films is beneficial for the performance of PSCs.[8]

The film uniformity was further studied using Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) (Fig. S16). The BT-T film displays a reduced variation in the contact potential difference (CPD) among grains, despite having a similar surface roughness to the control. This better film uniformity is explained by more-homogenous crystallization: BT-T was observed from the FTIR spectra (Fig. S17) to form complexes with perovskite precursor constituents (such as  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and MAI). These interactions induce a delayed and thus uniform crystallization process, driven by the lower volatility of BT-T than that of DMSO.

# PV performance and stability of PSCs

We fabricated 1 cm $  ^{2}  $ -sized PSCs using NiO $  _{x}  $  as the HTL, solution-processed PCBM/BCP bilayer as the ETL, and perovskites with a stoichiometry of Cs $  _{0.05}  $ (FA $  _{0.98}  $ MA $  _{0.02}  $ ) $  _{0.95}  $ Pb(I $  _{0.98}  $ Br $  _{0.02}  $ ) $  _{3}  $  as the absorbing layer without and with the BT-T additive. The optimal concentration of BT-T in the precursor solution was 0.24 wt%. Compared to the control devices, BT-T-passivated PSCs showed improved PV performances with a higher average PCE of 22.40 % (among 25 devices) than that of the control (20.72%; Table S4, Fig. 19 and 20). Notably, the average V $  _{OC}  $  was improved from 1.10 V to 1.15 V with the BT-T addition. This we ascribe to the improved band alignment and suppressed non-radiative energy loss, seen in the UPS and PL studies. We achieved a champion PCE of 23.48% for the 1-cm $  ^{2}  $  BT-T-passivated PSC, with a J $  _{SC}  $  of 24.60 mA cm $  ^{-2}  $ , an FF of 0.816, and a V $  _{OC}  $  of 1.170 V. The J-V curves of the champion device are shown in Fig. 4a and Fig. S21 with negligible hysteresis from reverse and forward scans. We measured the external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra of the devices without and with BT-T (Fig. 4b). The integrated J $  _{SC}  $  values match well with those from the J-V scans. We further obtained a stabilized PCE of 23.21% from the steady-state power output measurements (Fig. S22), a high value among previously-reported 1 cm $  ^{2}  $ -sized inverted-structure PSCs (Table S5). # 3. Conclusion

We have introduced a cyanoacrylic acid-based molecular additive for   $  NiO_{x}  $  -based inverted PSCs using an in-situ buried interface passivation strategy, in which small amounts of molecular additives are into the perovskite precursor solution. The computational and experimental investigations reveal that this modifier can be anchored spontaneously onto the buried interface and grain boundaries of perovskite films during the perovskite deposition. This approach satisfies bottom contact, facilitates interfacial charge transfer, as well as improves the morphology and crystalline structure of perovskite films. As a result, a remarkable PCE of 23.48% for PSCs with an active area of   $  1\ cm^{2}  $   is realized. The encapsulated cell retained 95.4% of its initial performance after 1,960 h of operation under 1-sun equivalent light illumination at 65°C (ISOS-L-2I protocol) and 91.5% of initial performance following 100-hour annealing at 100 °C. This work proves that the rational design modifier to in-situ passivates buried interface is a facile and effective strategy to boost the performance and stability of inverted-structure PSCs.
