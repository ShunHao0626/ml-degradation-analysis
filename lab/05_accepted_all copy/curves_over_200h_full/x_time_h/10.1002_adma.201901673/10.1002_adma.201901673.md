Inert long-chain organic cations not only prevent moisture from penetrating and attacking an inorganic layers, but also enhance the structural stability via strong van del Waals interaction between capping organic cations and the   $  [PbI_{6}]^{-}  $  units.  $  ^{[9]}  $   Unfortunately, the insulating organic cations inhibit the charge transport between adjacent highly conductive inorganic layers.  $  ^{[6,10]}  $   Thus, low-dimensional RPPs usually suffer from poor efficiency. For example, low-dimensional   $  (\mathrm{PEA})_{2}(\mathrm{MA})_{n-1}\mathrm{Pb}_{n}\mathrm{I}_{3n+1}  $  -based perovskite solar cells were reported to achieve a PCE of 4.73% at n = 3.  $  ^{[6]}  $   Similarly, a low PCE of 4.02% at n = 3 was reported for   $  (\mathrm{BA})_{2}(\mathrm{MA})_{n-1}\mathrm{Pb}_{n}\mathrm{I}_{3n+1}  $  -based perovskite.  $  ^{[10]}  $

Low-dimensional Ruddlesden–Popper perovskites (RPPs) exhibit excellent stability in comparison with 3D perovskites; however, the relatively low power conversion efficiency (PCE) limits their future application. In this work, a new fluorine-substituted phenylethlammonium (PEA) cation is developed as a spacer to fabricate quasi-2D   $  (4\mathrm{FPEA})_{2}(\mathrm{MA})_{4}\mathrm{Pb}_{5}\mathrm{I}_{16}  $   (n = 5) perovskite solar cells. The champion device exhibits a remarkable PCE of 17.3% with a   $  J_{sc}  $   of 19.00 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , a   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.16 V, and a fill factor (FF) of 79%, which are among the best results for low-dimensional RPP solar cells (  $  n \leq 5  $  ). The enhanced device performance can be attributed as follows: first, the strong dipole field induced by the 4-fluoro-phenethylammonium (4FPEA) organic spacer facilitates charge dissociation. Second, fluorinated RPP crystals preferentially grow along the vertical direction, and form a phase distribution with the increasing n number from bottom to the top surface, resulting in efficient charge transport. Third, 4FPEA-based RPP films exhibit higher film crystallinity, enlarged grain size, and reduced trap-state density. Lastly, the unsealed fluorinated RPP devices demonstrate superior humidity and thermal stability. Therefore, the fluorination of the long-chain organic cations provides a feasible approach for simultaneously improving the efficiency and stability of low-dimensional RPP solar cells.

For example, replacing linear n-BA with branched iso-BA cations of 2D perovskites can obtain a high PCE of 10.63%, but the devices suffered from the large electrical hysteresis.  $  ^{[15]}  $   NH  $  _{4}  $  SCN has been used as the additive to regulate the vertical orientated growth of (PEA)  $  _{2}  $  (MA)  $  _{n-1}  $  Pb  $  _{n}  $  I  $  _{3n+1}  $   crystals, resulting in a high PCE of 11% at n = 5.  $  ^{[13]}  $   Doping a small amount of cesium cations yielded a high PCE of 13.7%.  $  ^{[7]}  $   So far, low-dimensional RPPs with efficiency over 14% are still rarely reported. It is well known that replacing hydrogen atoms with fluorine can increase the hydrophobicity of organic materials.  $  ^{[20]}  $   Furthermore, fluorination of organic functional groups has been proven as an effective method to modify the energy levels of molecules, optimize the film morphology, and eventually increase the PCE of organic solar cells.  $  ^{[21,22]}  $   Thus, it is a natural choice to use fluorinated organic cations as a spacer to enhance the stability and efficiency of perovskite solar cells. In early days, Mitzi et al. adopted fluorinated 2D perovskite to fabricate thin-film transistors and found that fluorine substitution on the organic cation of perovskite modifies the crystal structure, resulting in different transport properties.  $  ^{[23]}  $   The theoretical calculation also predicted that a fluorine-substituted organic spacer may distort the crystal structure of perovskite.  $  ^{[24]}  $   Very recently, during our paper submission process, several latest publications also experimentally demonstrate that the fluorination of organic spacer cations can impact on the perovskite crystal structure and photovoltaic performance.  $  ^{[25-27]}  $

In this work, we substituted the fluorine atom on the para-position on the conjugated phenyl ring of PEA cations to obtain 4-fluoro-phenethylammonium (4FPEA)-based perovskite, and then fabricated the inverted (p–i–n) planar solar cells through a facial one-step spin-casting process at room temperature. Surprisingly, replacing PEA with fluorinated 4FPEA bulky cation dramatically increased the RPP device efficiency from 11% to over 17%, which is mainly benefitted from the significant increase of   $  f_{sc}  $   and fill factor (FF). To the best of our knowledge, this remarkable PCE value is among the highest efficiency of the low-dimensional PEA  $  _{2}  $  MA  $  _{n-1}  $  Pb  $  _{n}  $  I  $  _{3n+1}  $  -based RPP solar cells (  $  n \leq 5  $  ). Fluorinated quasi-2D RPP solar cells also demonstrated the pronounced device stability due to the hydrophobicity nature of fluorinated long-chain cations. Moreover, we systematically investigate how the fluorinated organic cation improves the device operation by combing multiple structure, morphology, and photophysics characterizations. Our results highlighted that employing fluorinated organic spacer cations may open the new avenues for highly efficient and stable perovskite solar cells.

Rather than a homogeneous single phase of 3D perovskite, RPPs actually comprise multiple phases with different energy bandgaps, which self-organize into quantum well structures.  $  ^{[32]}  $   Thus, we carried out optical spectroscopy to investigate the phase distribution and band alignment in our RPP films. Figure 4a represents the UV–vis absorption spectra of 4FPEA- and PEA-based RPP films. Both RPP films show multiple strong absorption peaks at   $  \approx570  $  , 610, 646 nm corresponding to n = 2, 3, 4 phases, and a low broad absorption band at the long-wavelength region assigning to larger   $  n = \infty  $   phases.  $  ^{[14]}  $   This result verifies that our RPP films are the mixture of low n and high n phases. Moreover, in comparison to   $  (\mathrm{PEA})_{2}(\mathrm{MA})_{4}\mathrm{Pb}_{5}\mathrm{I}_{16}  $  , 4FPEA-based RPP film exhibits the stronger absorption intensity at low-dimensional phase (n = 2), suggesting a large amount of low-dimensional phase (n = 2) in   $  (4\mathrm{FPEA})_{2}(\mathrm{MA})_{4}\mathrm{Pb}_{5}\mathrm{I}_{16}  $   RPP film. Additionally, three excitonic absorption peaks (n = 2, 3, 4) of   $  (4\mathrm{FPEA})_{2}(\mathrm{MA})_{4}\mathrm{Pb}_{5}\mathrm{I}_{16}  $   film slightly redshift by 2–3 nm, and the onset of the absorption edge also redshifts from 751 nm for PEA-based RPP to 765 nm for 4FPEA-based RPP films as shown in Figure S8 (Supporting Information). To unravel how different n phases are spatially arranged, we compared the photoluminescence (PL) spectra excited from the front side (film side) and back side (glass side), as shown in Figure 4b. Under front excitation, PEA- and 4FPEA-based RPPs mainly represent a single PL emission with the peaks located at 765 and 771 nm, respectively, arising from the lowest bandgap emission of high n phase. The solubility of PEA and 4FPEA organic cations in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) solvent was tested to be 6.1 and 4.4 M, respectively. Due to lower solubility, 4FPEAI is more likely to precipitate at the initial nucleation stage, and then forms more portion of small n phase on the substrate earlier. The residue solution leaves over more short-chain MA cations. Subsequently, the higher stoichiometric ratio of MA to 4FPEA cations facilitates the large n phase nucleation, leading to the formation of large RPP phases on the upper surface. Correspondingly, this spatial phase distribution leads to a cascade energy band diagram as illustrated in the schematic in Figure 4d. This type-II band alignment of mixed phases plays critical roles in achieving highly efficient perovskite solar cells. In this energy alignment, the photogenerated exciton can be effectively dissociated because of the energetically favorable band offset. Then, the dissociated electron and hole can be separately transferred in the opposite directions, and ultimately collected by the electrodes. Specifically, the holes are continuously transferred upstream from large n to small n, while the electrons are transferred downstream from small n to large n. Additionally, the gradually reduced energy bandgap of multiple phases from small n to large n value is beneficial for light harvesting in analog with the tandem structure.

Next, we investigate the charge dynamics in the operating RPP devices including charge dissociation, transport, and recombination, in order to reveal the fluorine substitution effect on the improved device efficiency. Recently, fluorination has been proven as an effective molecule design strategy to facilitate the charge dissociation and charge transfer, thus improving the photovoltaic performance of organic solar cells.  $  ^{[35,36]}  $   Similarly, due to the strong polarity of fluorine atoms, fluorine substitution on PEA cations essentially induces a strong molecule

dipole moment for charge separation. First, we employed the density function theory (DFT) to calculate the directionality and amplitude of the local dipole moments shown in Figure 5a. Our calculation reveals that the dipole moment values of 4FPEA and PEA organic cations are 1.98 and 1.26 D, respectively. The intrinsic high polarity of organic cation 4FPEA would change the surrounding dielectric environment, which can be experimentally verified by a dielectric constant (  $  \varepsilon  $  ) measurement. As shown in capacitance–voltage characteristics in Figure 5b, the dielectric constant of 4FPEA RPP was extracted from the device geometry capacitance and calculated to be 29.74, which is larger than that of PEA RPP (  $  \varepsilon = 24.1  $  ). To further confirm the large dipole moment of fluorinated RPP, the surface potentials of 2D perovskite films were measured by Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM). We evenly measured 12 points on the films to ensure the film uniformity and accuracy of the measurement. The average surface potential of   $  (4\mathrm{FPEA})_{2}\mathrm{MA}_{4}\mathrm{Pb}_{5}\mathrm{I}_{16}  $   film is 224.36 mV, which is substantially larger than that of nonfluorinated   $  (\mathrm{PEA})_{2}(\mathrm{MA})_{4}\mathrm{Pb}_{5}\mathrm{I}_{16}  $   film (91.28 mV). Due to the combined quantum and dielectric confinement effect, low-dimensional RPP exhibits more excitonic characteristics with larger binding energy as compared to the 3D perovskite. The excitonic nature of RPP is beneficial for charge recombination, but not in favor of the charge dissociation. In particular, quantum confinement can be changed by tuning n number, whereas dielectric confinement effect arises from the dielectric difference in the organic cation barrier (  $  \varepsilon_{barrier}  $  ) and the inorganic well (  $  \varepsilon_{well}  $  ), described with an enhancing factor as   $  \frac{\varepsilon_{well}-\varepsilon_{barrier}}{\varepsilon_{well}+\varepsilon_{barrier}}  $  . As   $  \varepsilon_{barrier}  $   increases, the large dipole moment of 4FPEA cations essentially decreased the dielectric confinement effect, and thereby reduced the exciton binding energy. Recent publications also found that introducing the polar organic substituents substantially reduces the exciton binding energy of 2D perovskite films.  $  ^{[25,37]}  $   The reduced exciton binding energy can also be reflected by the corresponding redshift of excitonic peaks and onset of the absorption edge of the 4FPEA-based RPP film shown earlier. As a result, the strong dipole field and reduced excitonic binding energy of   $  (4\mathrm{FPEA})_{2}(\mathrm{MA})_{4}\mathrm{Pb}_{5}\mathrm{I}_{16}  $   facilitate the charge dissociation at the edge states of the layered perovskite, leading to the enhanced   $  J_{sc}  $  .

The electron mobilities for 4FPEA- and PEA-based perovskite are   $  3.4\times10^{-3}  $   and   $  2.64\times10^{-3}\;\mathrm{cm}^{2}\;\mathrm{V}^{-1}\;\mathrm{s}^{-1}  $  , respectively. Thus, the ratio   $  \mu_{e}/\mu_{h}  $   (  $  \approx0.83  $  ) of 4FPEA-based RPP indicates a well-balanced charge transport, resulting in a remarkable high FF of 79%. In summary, we have successfully employed the fluorinated PEA cation to improve the efficiency and stability for low-dimensional RPP solar cells. A remarkable high efficiency of 17.3% was achieved by a one-step spin coating process. This PCE value is known as one of the best efficiencies for low-dimensional 2D RPP (  $  n \leq 5  $  ) so far. The fluorinated devices also demonstrated the good environmental stability, which can retain beyond 93% of its initial efficiency after 500 h storage in relative humidity (55–65%) air without any encapsulation. We assigned the improved device performance to the critical role of fluorination on the RPP film morphology, defect densities, mixed phase distribution, and charge dissociation dynamics. First, the RPP with fluorinated PEA spacer cations increased crystallite size, tailored crystals growth toward preferential vertical orientation, and formed compact film morphology with the low trap-state density, leading to enhanced charge transport. Second, introducing fluorinated cation into 2D RPP yields a unique phase distribution with the majority of small n phases located at the bottom, and large phases self-assembled on the top surface of the RPP film, which is in favor of the light absorption and charge separation in the RPP solar cells. Third, the strong dipole moment of the fluorine atom and large dielectric constant reduce the exciton binding energy, and thereby facilitate the charge dissociation in RPP devices. Lastly, the prolonged device environmental stability can be attributed to the hydrophobic nature of fluorinated cation and the resulted strong van der Waals interaction with lead halide octahedrons. Our work demonstrates a facial approach to modulate the low-dimensional RPP film morphology, phase distribution, and eventually enhance the device performance by using fluorinated organic spacer cations. We believe this effective strategy can be applied to other related device applications such as perovskite light-emitting diodes, photodetectors, perovskite-based ferroelectrics and spintronics.

# Experimental Section

Materials and Chemicals: Lead iodide (PbI $ _2 $ , ≥99.99%), methyl ammonium iodide (MAI, 99.5%), PTAA (molecular weight:

1000–10 000 g mol  $  ^{-1}  $  ), and PCBM (99.5%) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. and used without any purification. PEA (  $  \geq  $  98%), 4FPEA (  $  \geq  $  98%), and hydroiodic acid (HI, analytical reagent,   $  \geq  $  47.0%, with H  $  _{3}  $  PO  $  _{2}  $   stabilizer) were purchased from Aladdin. Isopropyl alcohol (IPA,   $  \geq  $  99.9%), DMF (  $  \geq  $  99.9%), and chlorobenzene (CB, 99.8%) used in the experiments were anhydrous reagents and purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. PEI (M  $  _{w}  $   = 25k, M  $  _{n}  $   = 10k) was purchased from the same vendor.

Synthesis of Organic Ammonium Cations: About 10 mL of HI was added dropwise into the mixture of ethanol and PEA under ice bath condition. The solution was stirred for 2 h before the solvent was removed by a thermal plate at 60 °C and white powder was obtained. The precipitates were subsequently washed by ether and recrystallized in isopropyl alcohol three times. The final products were dried under vacuum at 60 °C overnight and then transferred into a glove box for further use. The synthesis of 4FPEAI was carried out in the same procedures.

Device Fabrication: Patterned ITO substrates were cleaned using an ultrasonic bath in detergent water followed by sequential washing with acetone and alcohol for 30 min. After drying, clean ITO substrates were treated by oxygen plasma before spin-coating hole-transport materials. PTAA with 6 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  in chlorobenzene solution was spin-coated on ITO substrates at 5000 rpm for 30 s followed by annealing on a hot plate at 100 °C for 10 min. The (4FPEA)  $  _{2}  $  MA  $  _{n-1}  $  Pb  $  _{n}  $  I  $  _{3n+1}  $  (n = 3, 4, 5) precursor solutions were prepared by mixing 4FPEAI, MAI, PbI  $  _{2}  $ , and NH  $  _{4}  $  SCN with different molar ratios of 2:4:n:x in DMF with Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  molar concentration of 1 M. NH  $  _{4}  $  SCN additives were added to improve the film morphology and promote the crystal vertical orientation.  $  ^{[13]}  $  The optimized NH  $  _{4}  $  SCN molar ratio was found to be 2.5 (2.5 refers to 4FPEAI:MAI:PbI  $  _{2}  $ :NH  $  _{4}  $  SCN = 2:4:5:2:5) for n = 5. The mixed precursor solution was stirred for 2 h followed by spin-coating at the speed of 5000 rpm for 45 s. Then, the perovskite films were annealed at 100 °C for 15 min. Most of NH  $  _{4}  $  SCN additives were removed after thermal treatment verified by Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra shown in Figure S1 (Supporting Information). In the following step, the solution of PCBM (25 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  in CB) was spin-coated on top of perovskite film at 3000 rpm for 60 s. A concentration of 0.7 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  PEI in isopropyl alcohol was spin coated at 5000 rpm for 30 s before thermal evaporation. Finally, 80 nm of silver was thermally evaporated as the top contact at the evaporation rate of 0.2 Å s  $  ^{-1}  $ .

Characterizations: The current density–voltage (J–V) curves of PSCs were measured under illumination by an Air Mass 1.5 Global (AM 1.5) solar simulator (Enli Technology Co., Ltd.) with an irradiation intensity of 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  . EQE measurements were carried out under ambient conditions using an EQE system (Enli Technology Co., Ltd.). SEM images of the perovskite films were obtained by using Nova NanoSEM 450 at the accelerating voltage of 10 kV. The PL spectrum and lifetime measurements of perovskite films were conducted on Edinburgh fluorescence spectrometers (FLSP920). The surface potential of perovskite films was measured with a Kelvin probe microscope (KP020, KP Technology). XRD spectra were recorded by an X-ray diffractometer (Bruker D8 Advance). GIWAXS measurement was performed at the 8ID-E beamline at the Advanced Photon Source, Argonne National Laboratory. The X-ray with the wavelength of 1.1385 Å was radiated at an incident angle of 0.13°. C–V spectra were recorded by an Agilent 4294A impedance analyzer at 1 kHz, and AC oscillating amplitude was 50 mV. The DFT calculations were performed at the B3LYP/6-31 g(d, p) level using Gaussian 09 program. For the humility stability test, the unencapsulated devices were stored in lab ambient condition with a humidity of 55–65% and room temperature (25–30 °C) under dark conditions. The thermal stability test was conducted by continuously annealing unencapsulated devices at 55 and 85 °C in a nitrogen atmosphere.
