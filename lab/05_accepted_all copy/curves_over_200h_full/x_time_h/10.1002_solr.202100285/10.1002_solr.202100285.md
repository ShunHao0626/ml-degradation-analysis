# Bottom Interfacial Engineering for Methylammonium-Free Regular-Structure Planar Perovskite Solar Cells over 21%

Shibing Leng, Luyao Wang, Xin Wang, Zhanfei Zhang, Jianghu Liang, Yiting Zheng, Jinkun Jiang, Zhiang Zhang, Xiao (Xiao) Liu, Yuankun Qiu, and Chun-Chao Chen*

Formamidinium cesium (FACs) perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with the exclusion of methylammonium (MA) cations often have greatly improved device stability; however, their inferior performance compared with MA-based devices has impeded the real application. Among various device engineering strategies, bottom interfacial engineering is a promising method to simultaneously achieve the passivation of interfacial defects and the crystallization control of perovskite. Herein, a simple and effective bottom interfacial design is presented to improve the efficiency and stability of FACs PSCs by capping o-phenanthroline derivatives on the ZnO electron transporting layer (ETL). The most efficient modifier, 4,7-Dichloro-1,10-phenanthroline (Cl-phen), can improve the crystallinity of the perovskite film by chlorinated surface and passivate the defects of ZnO by reducing surface hydroxyl groups and oxygen vacancies. In addition, Cl-phen modified ZnO shows better energy alignment with FACs perovskite and increases the built-in electric field cascade by 80 mV. As a result, a champion device efficiency of 21.15% is obtained using ZnO/Cl-phen bilayer ETL. The stability has also been improved using ZnO/Cl-phen bilayer ETL, in which 91.5% of initial PCE is retained after 1500 h of storage at ambient environment (RH: 40–50%) without encapsulation.

# 1. Introduction

Organic/inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted much attention for their rapid improvements in efficiency from 3.8% to 25.5% in a decade.  $  ^{[1-5]}  $   Methylaminium lead iodide (MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ) was the first material to be studied and PSCs based on MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   with high efficiency (>20%) have been successfully fabricated. However, it was later discovered that the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite has insufficient thermal stability because of the degradation of MA iodide at 80 °C.  $  ^{[6]}  $   In contrast, formamidinium lead iodide (FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ) perovskite has better thermal stability and a narrower bandgap (1.48 eV), which is more suitable for high-efficiency PSCs, but pure FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   easily turns into

There are relatively few reports of FACs perovskite, compared with perovskite containing MA. The major reason is the lag in performance because it is relatively difficult to obtain high-quality FACs perovskite films. As MA cation is very effective for perovskite film formation, it often allows high crystallinity and device performance in FAMA or triple-cation PSCs.[8,17] By adding the same proportion of Cs as phase stabilizer to replace MA, the obtained FACs-based perovskite film often exhibits lower crystallinity, resulting in lower efficiency. By increasing the ratio of Cs, or introducing other cations and halogen ions, the crystallinity of the perovskite film can be improved.[6,18-21] Turren et al. introduced  $ 5\% $  of Ru in the FACs perovskite system, obtaining a stable PSC with an efficiency of  $ 20.35\% $ .[6] Gao and coworkers introduced CsCl and  $ \mathrm{PbBr}_2 $  to the FACs perovskite for phase stabilization and improved the crystallinity and grain size, achieving an efficiency of  $ 20.50\% $ .[20] However, the addition of other ions will reduce the purity of  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  and widen the perovskite's bandgap, which is not conducive to the further improvement of efficiency. In addition to the change in the composition of the perovskite, passivation of FACs perovskite and the substrate also has an important influence on the quality of perovskite film. Wen-Hua Zhang's group showed that D-penicellamine (PA) effectively passivated

photoinactive phase (yellow phase) at room temperature.  $  ^{[7,8]}  $   Introducing MA to the FA-based perovskite is the most common approach to stabilize the   $  \alpha  $  -phase (black phase) of perovskite, forming FAMA double cation perovskite (FA  $  _{1-x}  $  MA  $  _{x}  $  PbI  $  _{3}  $  ).  $  ^{[9-11]}  $   Some researchers further introduced cesium (Cs) to the FAMA perovskite to form triple-cation perovskite.  $  ^{[12-16]}  $   High-efficiency PSCs basically use these two component systems. However, these PSCs suffer from intrinsic instability introduced by the MA cation. In 2015, Park and his coworkers found that Cs alone can stabilize   $  \alpha  $  -phase of FA-based perovskite and the FACs perovskite they obtained showed enhanced photo- and moisture stability along with photovoltaic performance of 16.5%.  $  ^{[17]}  $   Stability is now the most important goal beyond efficiency in the field of PSCs. The FACs binary cation system perovskite provides a suitable bandgap and improved stability, which can be the ideal system for future PSCs with high efficiency and stability.

Zin oxide (ZnO) is a promising candidate for electron transporting layer (ETL). Compared with the traditional bottom layer for FACs PSCs,   $  TiO_{2}  $  ,   $  [6,20,25]  $   ZnO has an electron mobility several orders of magnitude higher than that of   $  TiO_{2}  $  .   $  [26]  $   The exceptional mobility can accelerate the transport of photogenerated electron and avoid charge accumulation at the interface. In addition, to control the crystallinity of   $  TiO_{2}  $   film, multistep processes including high-temperature annealing are often required, while ZnO can be easily crystallized in simple solution processes and low temperature. However, hydroxyl groups on the ZnO surface usually cause severe charge recombination at the interface resulting in instability of PSC. Several approaches have been applied to alleviate the surface defects problem of ZnO.   $  [27-36]  $   High-temperature annealing over 400°C can remove hydroxyl groups and organic residuals of ZnO.   $  [29]  $   But high-temperature sintering will make ZnO losing the advantages of low-temperature preparation. Deposition of small molecules or polymer layer can block the direct interaction of ZnO and perovskite and suppress oxygen vacancies of ZnO surface.   $  [32,34,37-39]  $   However, most of the researches focus on reducing the harmful reaction between ZnO and MA-based perovskite, such as FAMA and triple-cation systems.   $  [40,41]  $   For FACs perovskite, research on the surface treatment of zinc oxide is still lacking.

In this work, we used 4,7-Dichloro-1,10-phenanthroline (Clphen) and 1,10-bathophenanthroline (Bphen) to modify the surface of ETL prepared by ZnO nanoparticles at low temperature. Such approach can significantly reduce the content of surface hydroxyl groups on ZnO surface, thus suppressing charge recombination induced by oxygen defects on the interface. Moreover, the modified ZnO improves the film quality of the FACs perovskite. Furthermore, the grain size of FACs perovskite grown on Cl-phen modified surface can increase by over 50%. In parallel, PbI  $  _{2}  $  content has been suppressed after Cl-phen modification. In addition, the energy-level gap between conduction band minimum (CBM) of ZnO and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of FACs perovskite was reduced from 0.29 to 0.06 eV after Cl-phen modification. Thus, the built-in electric field cascade was increased from 1.00 to 1.08 V, which is conductive to the electron extraction on the interface. As a result, Cl-phen modified PSC attained an efficiency of 21.15% and enhanced device stability for retaining 91.5% of initial PCE after 1500 h of storage at ambient environment (RH: 40–50%).

# 2. Results and Discussion

Zinc oxide is proved to be the most suitable electron transporting layer for FACs perovskite in terms of energy level.  $  ^{[23,40]}  $

Low-temperature preparation is also a great advantage of zinc oxide, which allows ZnO to be applied to flexible substrates. However, the surface of zinc oxide prepared at low temperature often has a lot of defects,  $  ^{[34,42,43]}  $  hampering the charge transport through the interface and the stability of the device. The most typical defects are oxygen defects such as hydroxyl groups on the surface. An electron transporting layer with less defects and better compatibility is highly desirable to improve the device performance. Herein, we used a simple method to modify the surface of zinc oxide by depositing small molecule Cl-phen (or Bphen) on the ZnO layer, forming an inorganic/organic bilayer. The schematic is shown in Figure 1a. ZnO layer was prepared by spin coating of a 5 nm-size ZnO nanoparticle dispersion; the morphology of nanoparticles is shown in Figure S1, Supporting Information. The modification of Bphen and Cl-phen greatly reduces the oxygen defects on the surface of the zinc oxide layer, as shown in Figure 1b. We conducted XPS measurement on pristine ZnO film and modified films. The O 1s spectra can be spitted into two peaks. The lower peak at 530.1 eV referred to bonding oxygen atoms, while the higher peak at 531.5 eV related to oxygen vacancies and other oxygen-deficient defects.  $  ^{[34,44,45]}  $  The intensity of the higher O 1s peak of the control group is equivalent to that of the lower O 1s peak, which confirms that there are many oxygen defects such as hydroxyl groups on the surface of pristine ZnO layer. After modification, the intensity of higher O 1s peak significantly reduced, suggesting the reduction of oxygen-deficient defects. Other results of XPS measurement further demonstrate the interaction between small molecules (Cl-phen and Bphen) and ZnO, as shown in Figure S2a-c, Supporting Information. From Figure S2a, Supporting Information, we can see that Zn  $  2p_{3}  $  and Zn  $  2p_{1}  $  peaks of the pristine ZnO film are located at 1021.3 and 1044.4 eV, respectively. When Cl-phen and Bphen are coated on ZnO layer, the Zn  $  2p_{3}  $  and Zn  $  2p_{1}  $  peaks negatively shift by 0.1 and 0.3 eV, respectively, indicating the connection of zinc and the small molecules. As shown in Figure S2b, Supporting Information, a peak of N 1s at around 399.7 eV is observed from both small molecules modified samples, while no obvious signal is observed in the pristine ZnO film. The additional N 1s peak is detected from the small molecules. From Figure S2c, Supporting Information, signal of chlorine was only detected in Cl-phen modified sample, the Cl  $  2p  $  spectra can be deconvoluted into Cl  $  2p_{3/2}  $  at 199.0 eV and Cl  $  2p_{1/2}  $  at 200.7 eV. Furthermore, Cl-phen and Bphen are both detected on the modified film from the XPS measurement, indicating both molecules were successfully loaded on ZnO layer. The shift of the Zn  $  2p  $  peaks indicates that there is an interaction between small molecules and Zn. Significant decrease in defect peak intensity of O 1s spectra proves the surface passivation of oxygen defects by the introduction of small molecules.

Figure 1c is a schematic diagram of small molecules passivating the surface of ZnO. After passivation, the film quality of FACs perovskite grown on the interface is significantly improved. Figure 1d shows the energy levels of each layer of the device. After Cl-phen passivation, the CBM of the modified ZnO is closer to that of FACs perovskite, facilitating electron transport the interface, thereby improving solar cell performance. These two points will be further elaborated in the following discussion.

The change in interface energy level will cause variety of the built-in potential (  $  V_{bi}  $  ) in the device. To investigate the   $  V_{bi}  $   change of FACs PSCs after ETL modification, we measured Mott–Schottky plots in Figure 4a and it can be calculated from the following equation  $  ^{[51]}  $

$$
\frac {1}{C ^ {2}} = \frac {2}{A ^ {2} q \varepsilon \varepsilon_ {0} N _ {\mathrm{d}}} \times (V _ {\mathrm{bi}} - V)
$$

(1)

where C is the capacitance of depletion layer, A is the area of electrode, q is the electronic charge,   $  \varepsilon  $   is the sample dielectric constant,   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   is the vacuum permittivity,   $  N_{d}  $   is the donor density, and V is the applied voltage. The   $  V_{bi}  $   of control, Bphen, and Cl-phen modified PSCs can be evaluated from Figure 4a, that is, 1.00, 0.98, and 1.08 V, respectively, which is also consistent with the results of UPS measurement. The Cl-phen modified PSC has a higher   $  V_{bi}  $ , which means the dark recombination is inhibited and photogenerated carriers can be extracted more efficiently, eventually leading to higher   $  V_{oc}  $   in the later J-V testing.

To further understand the recombination in the devices, we operated control and Cl-phen modified solar cells as a light-emitting diode (LED). The external quantum efficiency (EQE) of the EL of the solar cells can be used to estimate the recombination in the solar cells.  $  ^{[3,52]}  $   The results are shown in

Figure 4b. The relationship between radiative recombination emission efficiency and   $  V_{oc}  $   can be described by the following expression

$$
\mathrm{V} _ {\mathrm{OC}} = \frac {k _ {\mathrm{B}} T}{q} \ln \left(\mathrm{EQE} _ {\mathrm{EL}} \frac {J _ {\mathrm{ph}}}{J _ {\mathrm{em,0}}} + 1\right)
$$

(2)

where   $  J_{em,0}  $   is the current of reemitted photons at room temperature and   $  EQE_{EL}  $   is quantum efficiency when the injection current in the dark is equal to the photocurrent (  $  J_{ph}  $  ) of the device under light illumination.  $  ^{[53]}  $   It can be seen from Figure 4b that the EL efficiency of Cl-phen modified device is 3.075%, which is 5 times of that of control device. So, the   $  V_{oc}  $   improvement after Cl-phen treatment can be calculated to be 41.4 mV based on Equation (2).

As a result, the introduction of Cl-phen to ZnO reduces the nonradioactive recombination in the device and increases the  $  V_{\mathrm{oc}}  $  and built-in electric field cascade. Both modifiers can reduce the trap state density in the device and increase the interfacial recombination resistance. Cl-phen comprehensively improves the charge transport capability of the interface.

To investigate the influence of the addition of Cl-phen and Bphen on the overall performance of solar cells, we prepared 30 PSC devices for each ETL. The PCE distribution is shown in Figure 5b. The efficiency of control devices is around 16% and is relatively discrete. In comparison, Cl-phen modified devices have more concentrated efficiency at around 20%. As shown in Figure 5a, the best control device yielded PCE of 18.41%,   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.12 V,   $  J_{sc}  $   of 21.7 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and FF of 75%. Both modified devices increase the   $  J_{sc}  $   by around 1 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  . We assumed the increase in   $  J_{sc}  $   should come from the decrease in recombination in the device, including the decrease in recombination at the interface and in the perovskite layer. The addition of Cl-phen and Bphen facilitated the charge transport at the interface. On the contrary, perovskite grown on modified ETL is larger in grain size with fewer grain boundaries, and therefore has less recombination. The EQE spectra in Figure 5c also reveal the change in   $  J_{sc}  $  . Both Bphen and Cl-phen modified devices have higher EQE in full wavelength from 300 to 800 nm, while the appearance of each spectra is alike as the same FACs perovskite is adopted in all

devices. The integrated   $  J_{sc}  $   of control, Bphen, and Cl-phen modified device is 21.33, 22.38, and 22.64 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , respectively. Furthermore, the   $  V_{oc}  $   of Cl-phen modified devices is generally 0.4 V higher than that of other devices. This result is also consistent with the data calculated from EL efficiency. From UPS and Mott-Schottky plots we learn that the energy level of Cl-phen decorated ETL is matched well with that of FACs perovskite. Such improvement usually contributes to higher   $  V_{oc}  $  . Finally, our champion device is based on Cl-phen modified ZnO with PCE of 21.15%,   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.16 V,   $  J_{sc}  $   of 22.9 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and FF of 80%. From Figure 5 d, its hysteresis effect is obviously suppressed compared with the control device.

At last, we investigated the stability of devices based on different ETLs, as shown in Figure 5e. It can be seen from the stabilized PCE plots that the current density of control device declines after 100 s of illumination, while the output efficiency of the bilayer ETL-based devices is stable. Then we conducted atmospheric stability tests on three different devices. The control device only maintained 55% of initial efficiency after 1500 h storage in air (RH = 40–50%). In comparison, Cl-phen and Bphen modified devices retained 91.5% and 85% of initial efficiency, respectively, under the same condition. There are three reasons for the improved stability after using modified ETL. First, Cl-phen and Bphen reduce the direct contact between ZnO and perovskite, avoiding the corrosion of ZnO on organic cations in the perovskite. Second, the perovskite grown on the modified ETL has improved crystallinity and reduced grain boundaries, so the stability of perovskite layer is improved. Third, the addition of Cl-phen and Bphen increases the hydrophobicity of the bottom interface, making the overall device less prone to water degradation. As a result, the coverage of small molecules for the passivated and stabilized surface of nanoparticle-made ZnO can further contribute to the long-term stability of MA-free PSCs (Table 1).

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, we developed a novel inorganic/organic bilayer electron transporting layer for MA-free PSC by introducing o-phenanthroline derivatives (Cl-phen and Bphen) onto low-temperature prepared zinc oxide. Both modifiers significantly suppressed the oxygen defects of the original ZnO surface (proved by XPS measurement) and increased the electron transporting capability of the ZnO layer. Moreover, the surface of ZnO became smoother and more hydrophobic after modification, so the FACs perovskite grown on the bilayer ETL has increased grain size and crystallinity. From UPS, the Cl-phen modified ZnO can achieve improved energy alignment with FACs perovskite, leading to increased   $  V_{bi}  $   and reduced nonradioactive recombination in the device. The extraction of charges from FACs perovskite to ZnO/Cl-phen bilayer ETL has also been greatly improved from TFT and SCLC measurement. By using ZnO/Cl-phen bilayer ETL, a peak PCE of 21.15% was achieved with enhancement in   $  J_{sc}  $  ,   $  V_{oc}  $  , and FF. The stability of the MA-free device has also been improved. The Cl-phen modified devices retained 91.5% of initial efficiency after placing in the ambient air for 1500 h. This work presents an effective and simple strategy to enhance the efficiency and stability of MA-free PSCs by bottom interfacial engineering. We believe that by

ZnO Nanoparticles Synthesis: The ZnO nanoparticles were synthesized as described in previous reports.  $  ^{[56]}  $   Zinc acetate dehydrate (2.95 g) and KOH (1.5 g) were dissolved into 125 and 65 mL absolute methanol, respectively, stirring under 60 °C for 30 min. Then the heated KOH solution was slowly added into zinc acetate solution within 3 min. It was remained stirring at 500 rpm for 3 h at about 60 °C. After cooling to room temperature, ZnO nanoparticles were separated from the reaction solution by natural sedimentation about 2.5 h, which were subsequently washed three times with methanol. Eventually, ZnO sediment was dispersed into methanol, preparing a ZnO solution with a concentration of 10 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  .

Solar Cell Fabrication: Patterned ITO glass was cleaned sequentially through sonication in ethanol, detergent, deionized water, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol. Prior to deposition, the ITO was treated with UV-ozone for 15 min. The thin pristine ZnO film was formed by spin coating onto the substrates from the as-prepared ZnO nanoparticles dispersion at 3000 rpm for 30 s; the sample was then annealed in air at 120 °C for 30 min. After cooling to room temperature, the ITO/ZnO substrate was cleaned with UV-ozone for 15 min to improve the surface wetting in the following steps in the glove box filled with nitrogen. To modify the ZnO layer, a solution of Cl-phen or Bphen in IPA (0.2 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was spin-coated on the ITO/ZnO substrate at 5000 rpm for 30 s and then wait for 30 min for the evaporation of residual IPA. The (FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  )  $  _{0.9}  $  (CsPbBr  $  _{3}  $  )  $  _{0.1}  $  mixed precursor solution was made by mixing 1.3 M PbI  $  _{2}  $ , 1.26 M FAI, 0.14 M PbBr  $  _{2}  $ , and 0.14 M CsI in DMF and DMSO (4:1, v/v). The dissolved hybrid solution was spin-coated on the (modified) ZnO layer at 1000 rpm for 10 s and then at 6000 rpm for 40 s. In the second step, 0.1 mL of anisole was added on the substrate 10 s before finishing the spinning procedure. The film was then annealed at 100 °C for 30 min. The HTL solution was prepared from a solution of Spiro-OMeTAD in chlorobenzene (72.3 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $ ) containing Li-TFSI solution (17.5 μL; prepared as a 520 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  solution in acetonitrile), and TBP (28.8 μL). It was spin-coated on the perovskite film at 3000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, an Ag film (100 nm) was deposited, as the counter electrode, through thermal evaporation on top of the Spiro-OMeTAD layer. The device size was 0.168 cm  $  ^{2}  $ ; the accurate active cell area was 0.1 cm  $  ^{2}  $ , using a nonreflective mask, when measuring.

Characterization: XRD patterns were obtained using a Rigaku Ultima IV diffractometer and Cu Kα radiation. SEM images were obtained using a JEOL JSM-7800 F Prime scanning electron microscope. UV-vis absorption spectra were recorded using a Lambda 35 UV-vis spectrometer. Topographic AFM images were recorded using a Bruker Dimension Icon system operated in tapping mode under ambient conditions. Steady-state fluorescence and TRPL spectra were recorded using a FLS1000 PL spectrometer with excitation at a wavelength of 460 nm. Current density–voltage (J-V) curves of the photovoltaic devices were measured using a Keithley 2400 source meter. Photocurrents were measured under AM1.5 G illumination at 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  using an Abet Technologies Sun 2000 solar simulator, calibrated with a standard VLSI Si reference solar cell (SRC-1000-TC-K-QZ). EQE spectra were recorded using an Enlitech LQE-50-FL IPCE measurement system. The light intensity was calibrated using a silicon standard. The active area of each cell was 0.1 cm  $  ^{2}  $ . The J-V measurements were recorded in ambient air without encapsulation; both reverse (1.2 V → -0.1 V) and forward (-0.1 V → 1.2 V) scans were performed using the same step of 0.02 V s  $  ^{-1}  $ . Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed using a Dong hua DH7000 electrochemical workstation. UPS was performed using an AXIS Ultra DLD instrument with the emitting UV energy at 21.22 eV.
