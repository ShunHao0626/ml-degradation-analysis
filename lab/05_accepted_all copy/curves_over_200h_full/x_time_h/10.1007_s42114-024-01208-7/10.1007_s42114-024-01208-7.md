# Guanine-assisted defect passivation for high-efficiency n-i-p planar perovskite solar cells

Xiaohui Li  $  ^{1,2}  $   · Haogang Meng  $  ^{1,2}  $   · Yongxiang Mai  $  ^{1,2}  $   · Jianyao Tang  $  ^{1,2}  $   · Fu Yang  $  ^{3}  $   · Putao Zhang  $  ^{1,2}  $   · Shengjun Li  $  ^{1,2}  $

# Abstract

Perovskite film is a polycrystalline film, coupled with its own soft lattice ionic material characteristics, and the rapid crystallization during the preparation process will inevitably produce a large number of defects within the film and at the interface. However, passivation using Lewis base and Lewis acid compounds can effectively suppress carrier recombination caused by these defects, greatly enhancing the overall performance of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). In order to achieve a better passivation effect, we employed guanine, a purine derivative, as a Lewis base by incorporation, which not only inhibits water degradation and ion migration and reduces trap defects by coordinating with uncoordinated ions, but also improves carrier transport by less non-radiative recombination. The guanine-modified devices exhibited significant enhancements compared to the original devices, with the corresponding devices exhibiting efficiencies of more than 23% (0.04 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ). The PSCs of the unencapsulated guanine-modified devices, storage in air at 25 ± 5 °C and 5–10% RH for 1200 h, retained 85% of the initial power conversion efficiency (PCE), which is much higher than that of the untreated devices, yielding satisfactory stability.

Keywords Guanine · Passivation · Perovskite

# 1 Introduction

Since the organic–inorganic hybrid perovskites have been applied to solar cells as photosensitive materials, it has triggered vigorous exploration by scientists around the world because of its excellent properties, such as suitable band gap, bipolar transmission characteristics, and low-temperature solution method preparation process   $  [1-7]  $  . The certified

PCE of its monojunction PSCs has risen from 3.8% all the way up to 26.41% (26.21% certified) [8]. This achievement brings them nearly on par with conventional monocrystalline silicon solar cells. Despite continuous improvements, the stability of perovskite and the presence of defects such as vacancy defects, antisite defects, and gap defects that cause hysteresis have never been completely resolved. In the past decade, researchers have proposed many strategies to reduce defects and widely adopted crystallization modulation methods [9–12]. The introduction of additives can affect the chemical environment in the precursor solution, which in turn affects the crystallization process of the perovskite, thereby altering the quality of the perovskite film. The common ones include compounds of hydrohalic acid containing Cl ions, thiocyanate (  $  SCN^{-}  $  ), acetate (  $  CH_{3}COO^{-}  $  ,   $  AC^{-}  $  ), formate (  $  HCOO^{-}  $  ) related compounds as well as some molecules containing elements such as O, N, S, and P [13–19], and Lewis bases usually happen to be some molecules containing these elements such as amine molecules and thiophene, and due to the fact that these elements contain on the lone pair of electrons, they can coordinate with   $  Pb^{2+}  $  , inhibiting   $  Pb^{0}  $   defects [20–24]. In previous research, a small molecule named 1,3,7-trimethylxanthine was introduced into PSCs [27]. By taking advantage of the carboxyl groups in various chemical environments and increasing the activation energy to induce molecular orientation, it facilitated the growth of high-quality films with superior electronic properties. Despite this, addressing the impact of moisture, oxygen, and other environmental factors on the performance of PSCs in practical applications is also extremely important. We chose a purine derivative as Lewis base to be introduced into the perovskite precursor, where the C = O contained not only binds to uncoordinated Pb $  ^{2+}  $ , but also guanine molecules form strong chemical bonds with the Pb-I framework, inhibits water degradation and ion migration, and reduces the interfacial defects, while less non-radiative complexation improves the carrier transport. with the efficiency of the corresponding devices exceeding 23% (0.04 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ). Unencapsulated PSCs retained 85% of their initial PCE after undergoing storage in air at 25 ± 5°C and 5–10% relative humidity for 1200 hours, the device retained 85% of its initial PCE.

# 2 Results and discussion

Therein, by incorporating guanine into the perovskite precursor solution, we are able to control the crystallization process, thereby enhancing the quality of perovskite and improving the efficiency of perovskite films. Guanine, a derivative of purine, comprises a fused pyrimidine-imidazole ring structure featuring conjugated double bonds. This structural configuration facilitates its bonding with perovskite. The integration of guanine into the perovskite precursor is elaborated upon in the experimental section. For differentiation purposes, the untreated sample is designated as the control, whereas the optimized sample is termed guanine. To elucidate the enhancement of perovskite crystals, Fig. 1a presents a structural diagram of guanine, accompanied by simulations of its electron cloud distribution and molecular electronegativity. The guanine molecule's distinct functional groups possess specific electron cloud densities, enabling them to passivate various charged defects on the perovskite grain surface, thereby demonstrating a versatile defect passivation capability. Electrostatic surface potential (ESP) images show negative charge densities predominantly around the O element, illustrating the nucleophilic nature of these sites and confirming their Lewis base properties [28]. In the surface where  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  is terminal, one end of  $ C = O $  binds to uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  for interactions. Hybridized states formed by overlap between wave functions connect the trapped states to the band-edge states of the perovskite surface plate, thus reducing charge trapping [29]. Simulations with density functional theory (DFT) show that the binding energy of guanine to periodic perovskite crystals is  $ -1.354\mathrm{eV} $ . Perovskites with Lewis acid defects, such as uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  ions, can interact electrostatically with the electron-rich carbonyl  $ C = O $  groups on guanine molecules. This interaction occurs as surplus electrons are donated to the 6p orbital of the  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  ions [30, 31]. Figure 1c shows the electron transport layer as well as the preparation of the perovskite film, where the addition of guanine results in larger and more aligned film grains, and the uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  interacts with  $ C = O $  thereby reducing film defects and aiding in the electron transport.

The guanine is dissolved in the perovskite precursor solution and spin-coated onto the perovskite film to investigate the effect of defect passivation; we studied the effect of guanine on the morphology properties of perovskite films. # 3 Conclusion

In summary, we introduce a purine derivative into the precursor solution and use it as a Lewis base, demonstrating that PSCs improve both device performance and stability.

This strategy effectively eliminates   $  Pb^{0}  $  , coordinating guanine with uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   to achieve Lewis acid–base binding, thereby reducing the formation of defects. It can enhance the hygroscopic property, passivate the defects of the absorption layer, improve the carrier extraction/transport, significantly inhibit the non-radiative recombination, and significantly enhance the   $  V_{OC}  $  . As a result, the PCE of the device increased from 20.13 to 23.06%. After 1200 h of storage in air at   $  25\pm5  $   °C and 5–10% RH, the device retained 85% of its initial PCE.
