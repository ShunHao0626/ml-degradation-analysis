# Reducing Energy Disorder in Perovskite Solar Cells by Chelation

Yiting Jiang, Jiabin Wang, Huachao Zai, Dongyuan Ni, Jiayu Wang, Peiyao Xue, Nengxu Li, Boyu Jia, Huanjun Lu, Yu Zhang, Feng Wang, Zhenyu Guo, Zhaozhao Bi, Haipeng Xie, Qian Wang, Wei Ma, Yingfeng Tu,  $  ^{*}  $   Huanping Zhou,  $  ^{*}  $   and Xiaowei Zhan  $  ^{*}  $

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: In inverted perovskite solar cells (PSCs), the fullerene derivative   $  [6,6]  $  -phenyl-  $  C_{61}  $  -butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) is a widely used electron transport material. However, a high degree of energy disorder and inadequate passivation of PCBM limit the efficiency of devices, and severe self-aggregation and unstable morphology limit the lifespan of devices. Here, we design a series of fullerene dyads FP-Cn (n = 4, 8, 12) to replace PCBM as an electron transport layer, where   $  [60]  $  fullerene is linked with a terpyridine chelating group via a flexible alkyl chain of different lengths as a spacer. Among three fullerene dyads, FP-C8 shows the most enhanced molecule ordering and adhesion with the perovskite surface due to the balanced decoupling between the chelation effect from terpyridine and the self-assembly of fullerene, leading to lower energy disorder and higher morphological stability relative to PCBM. The FP-C8/C60-based devices

using   $  Cs_{0.05}FA_{0.90}MA_{0.05}PbI_{2.85}Br_{0.15}  $   as a light absorber show a power conversion efficiency of 21.69%, higher than that of PCBM/C60 (20.09%), benefiting from improved electron extraction and transport as well as reduced charge recombination loss. When employing   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   as a light absorber, the FP-C8/C60-based devices exhibit an efficiency of 23.08%, which is the champion value of inverted PSCs with solution-processed fullerene derivatives. Moreover, the FP-C8/C60-based devices show better moisture and thermal stability than PCBM/C60-based devices and maintain 96% of their original efficiency after 1200 h of operation, while their counterpart PCBM/C60 maintains 60% after 670 h.

# INTRODUCTION

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted extensive research enthusiasm as a promising photovoltaic technology during the last decade, thanks to their facile and low-cost fabrication process and rapidly improved power conversion efficiency (PCE).  $  ^{1-7}  $   The champion PCEs of PSCs were obtained using a regular (n–i–p) device structure, while the inverted (p–i–n) structure possesses superior operating stability, thanks to the removal of ionically doped hole transport materials.  $  ^{8-11}  $   However, the efficiency of inverted PSCs is still lagging behind that of their regular counterpart because of higher energy disorder at the interface between the perovskite and the organic transport layer and even in the bulk of organic materials, which leads to lower carrier mobility, more severe nonradiative energy loss, and inferior performance of devices.  $  ^{12,13}  $   Although significant progress in reducing the energy disorder of organic hole transport materials has been achieved,  $  ^{14-16}  $   the study of organic electron transport materials is insufficient, which gradually becomes the short board of Cannikin's law.

In inverted PSCs, [6,6]-phenyl-  $ \mathrm{C_{61}} $  -butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) is a widely used electron transport material owing to several advantages, such as well-aligned energy levels with the perovskite and good electron extraction/transporting abil

The lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy level of PCBM and FP-Cn were estimated by cyclic voltammetry measurements (Figure S13). The LUMO energy level of PCBM was measured to be -4.20 eV, which is consistent with that reported before.  $  ^{39}  $   From FP-C4, FP-C8 to FP-C12, the LUMO energy levels downshifted from -4.08, -4.15 to -4.17 eV. The conduction band (CB) of the perovskite was measured to be -4.13 eV by Tauc plot and UPS measurements (Figure S14), while the LUMO level of FP-C8 was closer to the CB of perovskite, implying better electron extraction ability (Figure 1b).  $  ^{40,41}  $

To investigate the film-forming ability of these fullerene derivatives, the quality of different ETL films grown on the perovskite layer were investigated with scanning electron microscopy (SEM) measurements, and the SEM images of pristine perovskite and FP-Cn films on the perovskite are shown in Figures S15 and 1c–f, respectively. Obvious cracks and holes were observed in the film of FP-C4 since FP-C4 has limited solubility due to the short alkyl chain length (Figure S16). Interestingly, the FP-C8 film was more compact and denser than FP-C12 and PCBM films. According to atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements, FP-C8 film showed the smoothest surface morphology with the smallest root-mean-square roughness among PCBM and FP-Cn (Figure S17); the FP-C8 film also showed the largest water contact angle (Figure S18). These results revealed that FP-C8 has a better film-forming ability on the surface of perovskite films.

Energy Disorder Characterization. The crystallinity and molecular ordering of PCBM and FP-Cn series films were studied by grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS). As shown in Figures 2a,b and S19a,b, both PCBM and FP-Cn series showed a ring with uniform intensity at about   $  1.4 \AA^{-1}  $  , which suggests the isotropic packing structure without preferred orientation owing to the spherical geometry of fullerene.  $  ^{42}  $   Nevertheless, by analyzing the scattering peaks along the out-of-plane direction (Figures 2c and S19c), we found that from PCBM, FP-C4, FP-C8 to FP-C12, the full width at half-maximum (FWHM) becomes narrower, and the crystalline coherence length (CL) calculated by the Debye–Scherrer equation increases from 24.40 to 27.72 Å (Table S1). The CL of the FP-Cn series was larger than that of PCBM, owing to improved molecular ordering caused by the   $  \pi-\pi  $   interaction of terpyridine and van der Waals interactions of the long alkyl chain. The longer the alkyl chain, the larger the CL in the FP-Cn series, suggesting a better decoupling effect from longer flexible chains, which enables the better formation of the crystals. Moreover, in these fullerene derivatives, the FP-C8 film showed a slightly shorter molecular stacking distance (d-spacing) (Table S1). The stacking distance of FP-C8 was smaller than that of PCBM, which can be attributed to the stronger intermolecular interaction caused by the terpyridine group. However, the d-spacing of FP-C12 was larger than that of FP-C8 due to the too long alkyl chain of FP-C12. The limited solubility of FP-C4 decreases the film quality and crystallinity, leading to a larger d-spacing.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) experiments were carried out to probe the interaction between FP-C8 and perovskite (Figure 2d). The C≡N stretching vibration peaks of FP-C8 at 1583 and 1566 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   $  ^{43}  $  moved toward 1595

and 1572 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  when FP-C8 was mixed with PbI  $  _{2}  $ , suggesting chelation between the terpyridine of FP-C8 and PbI  $  _{2}  $ . Furthermore, we employed X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to study the interaction (Figure 2e,f). Two Pb 4f peaks located at 143.48 and 138.62 eV of the pristine perovskite shifted toward a lower binding energy region when the perovskite was coated with an FP-C8 film (Figure 2e), indicating the formation of coordination bonds between the terpyridine of FP-C8 and the undercoordinated Pb cations of the perovskite. On the other hand, the N 1s peak of FP-C8 moves toward a higher binding energy direction after combining with the perovskite, suggesting the electron donor role of terpyridine in the perovskite surface (Figure 2f). Furthermore, we conducted density functional theory (DFT) calculations to support the chelation hypothesis. As shown in the “Calculation of Adsorption Energy” part and Figure S20, FP-C8 exhibited lower adsorption energy (-0.79 eV) on undercoordinated Pb cations sites compared with I anions (-0.33 eV), confirming that the terpyridine group chelates with undercoordinated Pb cation defects. In addition, an FP-C8-coated perovskite film showed the same UV–vis absorption spectrum as the PCBM-coated one (Figure S21), and the perovskite layers before and after being coated with PCBM or FP-C8 exhibited the same X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Figure S22), implying that a coated PCBM or FP-C8 film does not influence the crystal properties of the perovskite. These results revealed that FP-C8 can chelate with the exposed metal cations of the perovskite surface and the interfacial anchoring effect leads to ordered molecular orientation and formation of a dense and stable ETL film (Figure 2g) compared with PCBM.

To further investigate the molecular assembly in the fullerene derivative film on the surface of perovskites, the dipole moment of FP-Cn derivatives was calculated using Materials Studio with the classical simulation method, as shown in the "Calculation of Molecular Dipole Moment" part and Figure S23. According to previous works, a larger dipole moment may lead to more ordered molecular orientation and preferred assembly on the interface.  $  ^{27,44}  $   In their conformations with the lowest total energy, the dipole moments of FP-C4, FP-C8, and FP-C12 were 24.81, 23.12, and 20.40 D, respectively, which were much larger than that of PCBM (4.86 D) reported before,  $  ^{27}  $   suggesting better intermolecular stacking. However, FP-C4 with the largest dipole moment showed worse film quality and crystallinity due to limited solubility.

To understand the effects of molecular ordering and film quality on the energy disorder of the ETL, we qualitatively and quantitatively analyzed the energy disorder difference between PCBM and FP-C8. Relative to PCBM, the FP-C8 thin film showed a sharper absorption onset (Figure S24), indicating that the energy disorder of the FP-C8 film was smaller than that of PCBM.  $  ^{45}  $   Furthermore, we investigated the temperature dependence of electron mobility in PCBM and FP-C8 films by employing the extended Gaussian disorder model (EGDM).  $  ^{46,47}  $   In the EGDM, the distribution of electronic density of states (DOS) follows a Gaussian curve in organic semiconductors. The charge transport in amorphous organic semiconductors is a hopping process between the localized sites of DOS, and the broadening width of DOS is assumed to be the degree of energy disorder;  $  ^{47,48}  $   thus, the energy disorder of materials can be investigated through carrier mobility measurement. The energy disorder of the ETL broadens the DOS and leads to a band tail with additional hopping sites that extend into the forbidden gap.  $  ^{36}  $   In an ETL film with a large degree of energy disorder, the nonequilibrium photogenerated carriers tend to relax down to these additional hopping sites, which downshifts the electron quasi-Fermi level and gives a smaller   $  V_{OC}  $   in devices. The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) decay were used to investigate the charge extraction ability of different ETLs. After being coated with the PCBM or FP-C8 film, the PL intensity of the perovskite film is markedly decreased (Figure 5a), indicating efficient charge transfer between the perovskite and ETL. Compared with PCBM, FP-C8 quenches PL more efficiently, suggesting superior electron extraction ability. The PL lifetimes of TRPL measurements were fitted with a biexponential decay function (Figure 5b), acquiring a fast decay ( $ \tau_1 $ ) component and a slow decay ( $ \tau_2 $ ) component (Table S6).  $ \tau_1 $  represents charge carrier transport at the perovskite interface, while  $ \tau_2 $  is ascribed to nonradiative recombination decay. $ ^{39} $  The pristine perovskite film showed an average decay time ( $ \tau_{avg} $ ) of 374.22 ns with a dominant percentage of  $ \tau_2 $  ( $ A_2 = 93.23\% $ ), suggesting that the nonradiative decay mechanism is the main origin for the reduction of the PL intensity. After being coated with the ETL, the lifetime of the perovskite film is dramatically decreased with an increased  $ \tau_1 $  ratio, indicating the strengthened electron extraction mechanism. In addition, the  $ \tau_{avg} $  (24.84 ns) of the FP-C8-based perovskite film was notably shorter than that of PCBM (66.39 ns) and accompanied by an increased  $ A_1 $  value from 52.54 to 53.41%, implying that a reduced energy disorder of FP-C8 is beneficial to electron extraction at the interface of perovskite/ETL, which is consistent with the PL measurements. Furthermore, we investigated the impact of the linker length of FP-Cn derivatives on their electron extraction ability. FP-C8 possesses superior electron extraction ability relative to FP-C4 and FP-C12 due to not only better alignment of LUMO energy levels but also extended band bending on the interface caused by a

relatively larger dipole moment of FP-C8 (Text S2, Figure S41, and Table S6).

Furthermore, the effects of different ETLs on nonradiative charge recombination were investigated. The PSCs were operated as light-emitting diode (LED) devices under dark and applied bias voltage for electroluminescence (EL) measurements. # CONCLUSIONS

Three fullerene derivatives of FP-Cn (n = 4, 8, 12) with a long alkyl chain end-capped by a chelating group terpyridine were designed and synthesized and used as ETLs in PSCs. FP-C8 showed the best photovoltaic performance among the FP-Cn series and was better than the benchmark molecule PCBM. FP-C8 showed better film-forming ability and enhanced molecule ordering and adhesion with the perovskite surface than PCBM, benefiting from the chelation effect of terpyridine and van der Waals intermolecular interaction of the long alkyl chain. FP-C8 showed higher electron mobility, lower energy disorder, and higher morphological stability than PCBM. In PSCs, the FP-C8 ETL showed more efficient electron extraction and transport than PCBM due to better aligned LUMO energy level and elevated quasi-Fermi level. The FP-C8/C60-based devices showed a lower trap concentration and reduced charge recombination due to more efficient defect passivation relative to the PCBM/C60 counterpart. As a result, the inverted planar PSCs using   $  Cs_{0.05}FA_{0.90}MA_{0.05}PbI_{2.85}Br_{0.15}  $   as the light absorber based on the FP-C8/C60 ETL showed a PCE of 21.69%, higher than that of PCBM/C60 (20.09%), with negligible hysteresis and good reproducibility. The   $  FAPbI_{3}  $  -based PSCs with FP-C8/C60 exhibited an even higher PCE of 23.08%, which is the champion efficiency of inverted PSCs with solution-processed fullerene derivatives. Moreover, the FP-C8/C60-based devices maintained 96% of their initial PCE after 1200 h of operation, accompanied by improved
