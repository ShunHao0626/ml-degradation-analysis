# 1. Introduction

Due to their attracting advantages of excellent optoelectronic properties and simple solution-chemistry processing, organic-inorganic hybrid perovskites (such as  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  and  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $ ) have been considered as the most promising light-harvesting material for solar cells application [1-7]. Through the interface and composition engineering and the optimization of fabrication procedure, the organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have recently achieved a recorded conversion efficiency exceeding  $ 25\% $  [8]. Nevertheless, the organic-inorganic hybrid perovskites contain the organic components such as  $ \mathrm{MA}^+ $  and  $ \mathrm{FA}^+ $ . The intrinsic hygroscopicity and thermal instability of the organic component greatly deteriorate the stability of hybrid PSCs and hinder the practical deployment of hybrid cells [9,10]. Therefore, completely replacing organic components with inorganic cations (such as  $ \mathrm{Cs}^+ $ ) in perovskites has been developed and considered as one of the most promising strategies to enhance the stability of PSCs [11-16].

Among the all-inorganic perovskites, the I-rich inorganic perovskites

All-inorganic   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite has been regarded as a promising candidate for perovskite solar cells (PSCs) considering its suitable bandgap and high stability. However, the poor quality of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   film greatly impedes the performance improvement of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $ -based PSCs. Herein, we report a facile strategy to improve the quality of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film by introducing cellulose acetate (CA) into the perovskite precursor solution. The interaction between the oxygen functional groups of CA and   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and the increase of the precursor viscosity induced by CA addition can slow down the crystallization rate of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite, which allows the perovskite films have enough time to form high-quality crystal structure. By optimizing the amount of CA (the optimal amount of CA is 2.5 wt%), a high-quality   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film with high crystallinity, large grain size, and smooth surface is obtained. As consequence, the carbon-based PSC with a structure of FTO/  $  TiO_{2}  $ /perovskite/carbon using the   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film with 2.5 wt% CA achieves a champion efficiency of 7.52%, which is increased by 40.8% compared with the cell based on pure   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film. In addition, the PSC based on   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   film with CA exhibits an excellent long-term stability under both ambient condition and high-humidity environment.

Unfortunately, the I-rich inorganic perovskites can easily transform to undesirable non-perovskite phase at ambient condition due to their inappropriate Goldschmidt tolerance factor. In contrast, all-Br inorganic perovskite,   $  CsPbBr_{3}  $ , has an excellent phase stability at ambient condition [26–30]. The obtained  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  PSCs achieved a high efficiency up to  $ 11\% $  [38], demonstrating a great promise for the practical application in the future. Nevertheless, the efficiency of  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  PSC is still lower than that of the other inorganic PSCs and far from the theoretical value. This is mainly attributed to the poor-quality  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  perovskite film with apparent pinholes and high density of defects, resulting in severe nonradiation recombination and low charge extraction efficiency. Although it had been reported that there are benign pinholes in  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  perovskite film [39], a large quantity of pinholes and defects still induce the trap states within perovskite film, which can remarkably deteriorate the photovoltaic performance of PSCs. Therefore, it is highly desirable to improve the quality of  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  perovskite film for enhancing the photovoltaic performance of  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  PSCs.

Generally, the crystallization process plays a key role in determining the quality of perovskite film. A high-crystallinity, pinhole-free, and homogenous perovskite film can be obtained by carefully controlling the crystal nucleation and the crystalline grain growth processes. Zhang et al. employed the antisolvent strategy to precisely control the   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   crystals growth for preparing the high-quality inorganic perovskite film. The fabricated   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   PSC achieved a high conversion efficiency of 9.17% [40]. Meanwhile, the light-processing strategy and preheating-assisted process have also been applied to manipulate the   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   crystal growth, which largely promoted the quality of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite films and boosted the efficiency of the corresponding PSCs [41–43]. Recently, polymer additives, such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) [44], polyethyleneimine (PEI) [45], polyvinylpyrrolidine (PVP) [46], and poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) [47], have been introduced into the inorganic perovskite precursor solutions for improving the quality and suppressing the defects of inorganic perovskite film. The improvement of perovskite quality induced by introducing polymer additive is generally attributed to the fact that the functional groups of polymers (such as -C=O, -NH-) can coordinate with the metal ions in perovskite precursor solution and the polymer chain dissolved in precursor solution can affect the diffusion of precursor ions, which effectively regulate the crystallization process of inorganic perovskite film and lead to forming the high-quality inorganic perovskite films.

In this work, cellulose acetate (CA) is employed as a polymer additive to be added into  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  precursor solution for improving the quality of  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  perovskite film. The interaction between the oxygen functional groups of CA and metal ions in precursor solution and the increase of the viscosity of precursor solution induced by CA addition can slow down the crystallization rate of perovskite, which facilitates the formation of the high-quality perovskite crystal with low defect density. By optimizing the amount of CA additive, a pinhole-free  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  perovskite film with high crystallinity and largely reduced defect was prepared by a one-step spin-coating process. We fabricate the PSCs based on CA-modified  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  perovskite film and carbon electrode with a structure of  $ \mathrm{FTO / TiO_2 / perovskite / carbon} $ . The fabricated PSC achieved a remarkably improved photovoltaic performance compared with the device based on pure  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  film with the efficiency being increased from  $ 5.34\% $  to  $ 7.52\% $ . Moreover, the carbon-based PSC with CA-modified  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  perovskite film shows an excellent long-term stability under both the ambient condition and high-humidity environment.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1. Materials

CsI (99.8%),   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (99.9%), DMSO (99.9%), and Cellulose acetate (39.8 wt% of acetyl, 3.5 wt% of hydroxyl) were purchased from Alfa Aesar. All of them were used as received without further purification. Titanium diisopropoxide bis (acetylacetonate) was purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Carbon paste was purchased from Shanghai New

Materials Co. Ltd.

# 2.2. Preparation of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   films and carbon-based PSCs

FTO glass substrates were cleaned under sonication and then treated under UV-  $  O_{3}  $  according to the procedure reported previously [27]. A compact  $  TiO_{2}  $  (c-  $  TiO_{2}  $  ) layer was deposited by spin-coating a 0.15 M ethanol solution of titanium diisopropoxide bis (acetylacetonate) on pre-cleaned FTO substrate at 4500 rpm for 45 s and sintered at 450 °C for 30 min. Then, a commercial  $  TiO_{2}  $  paste (Dyesol 18NR-T) diluted in ethanol with weight ratio of 1:15 was spin-coated on the c-  $  TiO_{2}  $  layer and subsequently annealed at 450 °C for 30 min. 260 mg of CsI and 370 mg of  $  PbBr_{2}  $  were added into 1 mL DMSO and stirred at 70 °C till completely dissolved. The different amounts of CA (0, 1 wt%, 2.5 wt%, and 5 wt% with respect to  $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $ ) were added into the prepared  $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $  perovskite precursor solution. Then, the precursor solution was spin-coated onto  $  FTO/TiO_{2}  $  substrate at 1000 rpm for 5 s and then 3500 rpm for 30 s. The obtained samples were annealed at a temperature of 200 °C for 15 min to form  $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $  perovskite film. Finally, the carbon paste was coated onto the perovskite film by doctor-blade method, followed by heating at 80 °C for 15 min. All above procedures were carried out under ambient condition without humidity control.

# 2.3. Measurements and characterization

The morphology characteristics of perovskite films were investigated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi S-4800) and atomic force microscopy (AFM, Park system XE-70). X-ray diffraction (XRD) data of perovskite were measured by a Rigaku Smartlab X-ray diffractometer with Cu Ka radiation. The UV–Vis absorption spectra were measured by a UV-3600 UV–Vis-NIR spectrophotometer (Shimadzu). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were measured using a Scimitar FTS 2000 FTIR spectrometer. The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolution PL (TRPL) measurements were carried out on an FLS 920 fluorescence spectrophotometer with an excitation wavelength of 450 nm. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were carried out on a Solartron1255/1287 system under dark in the frequency range from   $  10^{6}  $   to 1 Hz at an amplitude of 5 mV and a bias of 0.9 V. The capacitance–voltage measurements were performed on an electrochemical workstation (Versa-STAT 3, Ametek). The current density–voltage (J-V) curves of cells were measured under standard AM 1.5 illumination using a computer-controlled Keithley 2400 source meter at a scan rate of 50 mV s  $  ^{-1}  $   from -0.05 V to 1.3 V and from 1.3 V to -0.05 V. The active area of the cells is defined to be 0.09 cm  $  ^{2}  $   using a metal mask. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectrum was recorded by a Zolix-SCS-10 system equipped with a 150 W Xe lamp and a monochromator with a step of 5 nm. All measurements were carried out in air.

# 3. Results and discussion

In this work, CA was introduced into the   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite precursor solution and the   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite films were prepared by a facile one-pot spin-coating process. CA contains a large quantity of oxygen functional groups such as C-O-C and -COO-, which can coordinate with   $  Pb^{2+}  $   owing to the empty orbit of   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and the lone pair electron on oxygen. FTIR spectra shown in Fig. S1 indicate that the stretching vibrations of C = O and C-O-C groups in CA at   $  1749\ cm^{-1}  $   and   $  1029\ cm^{-1}  $   are shifted to   $  1739\ cm^{-1}  $   and   $  1017\ cm^{-1}  $   in CA-  $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   sample, respectively, illuminating the interaction effect between the oxygen functional groups in CA and   $  Pb^{2+}  $  [44]. This interaction between the oxygen functional groups and   $  Pb^{2+}  $   can retard the   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   crystal growth because the breakage of coordination bond require the extra energy. In addition, the introduction of CA can increase the viscosity of perovskite precursor solution, which retards the diffusion of perovskite precursor ions and also results in the decrease of perovskite

The time-dependent photographs of CsPbIBr $ _2 $  precursor films during the annealing process at 200 °C (as shown in Fig. S2) demonstrate that the crystallization rate of CsPbIBr $ _2 $  with CA is obviously lower than that of pure CsPbIBr $ _2 $ . XRD measurements were carried out to characterize the crystal structure of as-prepared   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite films. Fig. 1 shows XRD patterns of the obtained   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite films without and with CA. All   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   films display clear diffraction peaks at   $  15.02^{\circ}  $  ,   $  21.34^{\circ}  $  , and   $  30.24^{\circ}  $  , corresponding to (100), (110), and (200) planes of   $  \alpha  $  -phase   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite crystal, respectively. There is no other impurity peak in Fig. 1, indicating a pure perovskite structure of as-prepared   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   films. Therefore, it is speculated that the added CA did not participate into the   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite crystal but distributed at the grain boundaries and/or interfaces area. In addition, no diffraction peak assigning to CA is observed in XRD patterns, suggesting that a small amount of CA remain in the final perovskite film. Fig. 1 also shows that the main diffraction peak intensity of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film in the presence of CA up to 2.5 wt% is obviously stronger than that of pure   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   sample, suggesting a remarkable enhancement of crystallinity and reduction of grain boundary scattering upon CA introduction. Furthermore, the full width at half maximum (FWHM) value of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film with 2.5 wt% CA is lower than that of pure perovskite film, indicating that the larger crystal grain can be obtained after CA introduction. Nevertheless, the excessive addition of CA can impair the crystallinity of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film, as evidenced by the intensity reduction of diffraction peaks for   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film with 5 wt% CA.

To investigate the effect of CA addition on the morphology of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film, SEM measurements were carried out.   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite films were deposited on the   $  FTO/TiO_{2}  $   substrate, and top-view SEM images of the as-prepared   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite films without and with CA are displayed in Fig. 2. The pure   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film shows a rough surface with large amounts of voids and obvious grain boundaries (Fig. 2a), which can induce the serious short circuit and unfavorable charge recombination. The introduction of CA greatly improves the morphology of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film. By introducing a small amount of CA (such as 1 wt%), as shown in Fig. 2b, the void-free   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film is obtained. More impressively, when 2.5 wt% CA is introduced, the obtained   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film (Fig. 2c) displays a compact, pinhole-free, and smooth surface with large-size grains and blurry grain boundaries. Fig. 3a compares the UV–Vis absorption spectra of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite films without and with CA. All   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   films exhibit the similar absorption onset of about 605 nm, corresponding to the bandgap of 2.05 eV. It is well known that CA shows a negligible absorption in the visible region. Fig. 3b displays steady-state PL spectra of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite films without and with CA deposited on the glass substrate. Clearly, the introduction of CA greatly enhances the PL intensity of perovskite film. The   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film with 2.5 wt% CA shows the highest PL intensity, which is about 3 times stronger compared with that of pristine   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   sample. Generally, the stronger PL intensity demonstrates a suppressed nonradiative recombination, suggesting a high-quality perovskite film [48,49]. In addition, a slight blue shift can be observed from Fig. 3b for   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film with CA, which suggests that the CA addition can decrease the defect and trap density of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   inorganic perovskite film [50]. Fig. 3c displays TRPL spectra of pure   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   and   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   with 2.5 wt% CA. The PL lifetimes are obtained by fitting PL decay curves with a bi-exponential function. Obviously, the   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   with 2.5 wt% CA shows a much longer PL lifetime of 41.7 ns in comparison with 21.3 ns for the pure   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $ , which demonstrates that the CA addition decreases the non-radiative recombination sites and increases the lifetime of carrier in the perovskite film. The space charge-limited current (SCLC) curves of the electron-only devices with a structure of FTO/  $  SnO_{2}  $ /perovskite/PCBM/carbon were measured in the dark. The trap density (  $  N_{trap}  $  ) of perovskite film can be calculated using the equation   $  N_{trap} = 2V_{TFL}\varepsilon\varepsilon_{0}/eL^{2}  $ , where   $  V_{TFL}  $   is the trap-filled limit voltage,   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   is the vacuum permittivity,   $  \varepsilon  $   and L are the relative dielectric constant and the thickness of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite, respectively. Clearly, Fig. 3d indicates that the   $  V_{TFL}  $   value of the   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film with CA is lower than that of the pure   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film, suggesting an obviously decreased defect and trap density [51].

The remarkably improved quality of  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  perovskite film upon CA addition can be attributed to the relatively slow crystallization rate derived from the interaction between  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2 + }} $  and the oxygen functional groups of CA and the increase of the viscosity of the precursor solution. The slow crystallization rate allows the perovskite film has enough time to form perfect perovskite crystal structure with large-size crystal grain and low density of defects. Furthermore, the oxygen functional groups of CA can passivate the undercoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2 + }} $ , which effectively suppress the  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2 + }} $  induced defects and traps in  $ \mathrm{CsPbIBr_2} $  perovskite film.

The relatively higher   $  V_{bi}  $   can enhance the driving force to dissociate the charge carriers and extend the depletion region to suppress the charge recombination [55]. This result is in good agreement with the data of EIS and dark current measurements. Overall, the above discussions indicate that the PSC fabricated by   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film with 2.5 wt% CA has faster charge extraction and transport and lower charge recombination, which endows it with superior photovoltaic performance.

# 4. Conclusion

In summary, the quality of   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film is greatly improved by introducing an appropriate amount of CA. The interaction between the oxygen functional groups of CA and   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and the viscosity increase of the precursor solution derived from CA addition slow down the crystallization rate and passivate the uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $ , which lead to forming the void-free   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film with high crystallinity, large grain size, smooth surface, and low density of defect and trap. These favorable characteristics can enhance the charge extraction and transport and reduce the charge recombination in PSCs. As a result, the champion device based on   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film with 2.5 wt% CA achieves a greatly improved PCE of 7.52% in comparison with the 5.34% for the cell based on pure   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   perovskite film. In addition, the PSC based on   $  CsPbIBr_{2}  $   with 2.5 wt% CA displays an excellent long-term stability under both the ambient condition and high-humidity environment. Therefore, this facile strategy is expected to be helpful for developing the highly efficient and stable all-inorganic PSCs.
