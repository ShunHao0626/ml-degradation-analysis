# ABSTRACT

In the fabrication of inverted perovskite solar cells (PeSCs), it is necessary to select an appropriate p-type semiconductive material as hole transport layer (HTL), which will determine the crystallization quality of the subsequent perovskite films and hole charge extraction at the perovskite/HTL interface. Herein, a low-cost, low-temperature processed, and hydrophobic organic small molecule, N,N-bis(3-methylphenyl)-N,N-bis(phenyl) benzidine (TPD), is utilized as HTL in fabricating inverted PeSCs. It achieves a peak power conversion efficiency of 19.77% by optimizing the thickness, which is about 1.4 times higher than the PeSCs employing poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene)-poly(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS) as HTL. Compared with PEDOT:PSS-based PeSCs, the outstanding performance of PeSCs-based TPD derives from that of the employment of TPD as the HTL produced perovskite films with larger grain size and higher crystallinity. Owing to the hydrophobicity of TPD, TPD-based PeSCs exhibited longer stability. After storage of 700 h, the PeCE of TPD-based PeSCs retains 84.1%.

Owing to the excellent optoelectronic characteristics, such as tunable bandgap, high absorption coefficient, long life of charger carries, and ambipolar charge transport ability, perovskites have emerged as hot light harvesting materials for the next generation of solar cells.  $  ^{1-9}  $   In the past decade, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of perovskite solar cells (PeSCs) has been greatly improved to 25.5%.  $  ^{10}  $   In the construction of PeSCs, the inverse structure has attracted special attention due to its advantages of low-temperature annealing process (  $  \sim  $  100°C), which is beneficial for the cast savings and flexible substrate application.  $  ^{11-15}  $   In the inverted architecture, also named as the p-i-n planar structure, the hole-transport layer (HTL) plays a key role as the p-type layer for affecting the crystallization of the perovskite layer, which is deposited on the HTL and extracting hole from perovskite to the anode.

In the fabrication of inverted PeSCs, poly (3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly (styrenesulfonic acid) (PEDOT:PSS) was usually adopted as HTL for its excellent conductivity, high visible transparency, and outstanding film-forming property.  $  ^{16-19}  $   Nevertheless, the hydrophilic property of PEDOT:PSS will promote the moisture into the perovskite layer from atmosphere. On the other hand, the acidic nature of PEDOT:PSS can corrode the underlying indium tin oxide (ITO)

substrates. Both of these characteristics will lead to the erosion of interface between PEDOT:PSS and perovskite, which will cause the degradation of device performance.  $  ^{[17]}  $   Furthermore, since PEDOT:PSS has a low work function of 4.9–5.2 eV, it may lead to a severe energy loss of inverted PeSCs, causing the poor open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ).  $  ^{[18]}  $   Besides, p-type inorganic hole transport materials (HTMs), such as   $  NiO_{x}  $  ,  $  ^{[19]}  $     $  CuO  $  ,  $  ^{[20]}  $     $  CuSCN  $  ,  $  ^{[21]}  $     $  V_{2}O_{5}  $  ,  $  ^{[22]}  $   and CuI,  $  ^{[23]}  $   have been employed as HTL to build invert PeSCs since they hold the advantages of high mobility, good thermal stability, and low raw material cost. However, certain inorganic HTMs need be prepared by using harsh methods, such as pulsed laser or processing at high temperatures, which require annealing at 500°C.  $  ^{[24]}  $   This is unfavorable to the preparation on the flexible plastic substrates. As the other alternatives to PEDOT:PSS, non-wetting polymers have been employed to enhance device performance since they have unique merits such as suitable energy level, low temperature preparation, and no corrosion for ITO substrates. For example, Xu et al. employed poly(N,N'-bis(4-butylphenyl)-N,N'-bis(phenyl)benzidine) (poly-TPD) as HTL to fabricate inverted PeSCs with a maximum PCE of 18.1%.  $  ^{[18]}  $   Liu et al. reported high efficient inverted PeSCs with a champion PCE of 20.7% by using poly(bis(4-phenyl) (2,4,6-trimethylphenyl)amine) (PTAA) as HTL.  $  ^{[23]}  $   Yang et al.

Hence, it is necessary to find suitable p-type materials with hydrophobicity, low temperature preparation, and low price as HTL to fabricate inverted PeSCs.

Recently, p-type organic small molecules, used as HTL in the field of organic light emitting diodes (OLEDs), have been found to meet the above requirements. For example, the PCE of 18.12%, 18.8%, and 19.96% were achieved by adopting m-MTDATA,  $  ^{26}  $  4,4'-cyclohexylidenebis[N,N-bis(4-methylphenyl) benzenamine] (TPAC),  $  ^{27}  $  and N,N'-bis(naphthalen-1-yl)-N,N'-bis(phenyl)benzidine (NPB),  $  ^{13}  $  respectively. It means that off-the-peg and low-cost organic small molecules used as HTL in OLEDs could also be employed as HTL in fabricating highly efficient inverted PeSCs. Herein, a hydrophobicity, low-temperature processed, and low price organic small molecule N,N'-bis(3-methylphenyl)-N,N'-bis(phenyl) benzidine (TPD) is in front of our eyes. Compared to the expensive price of polymer (>\$900 per 1 g), the price of TPD is as low as \$67 per 1 g (Sigma-Aldrich). Meanwhile, according to the literatures, the smooth and flat TPD film can be prepared by solution casting although most TPD films are prepared by vacuum thermal evaporation in the past.  $  ^{28,29}  $  The conjugate structure of TPD guarantees its high hole conductivity without further doping.  $  ^{30}  $  All these merits give it the potential to be HTL material for building inverted PeSCs.

Hence, in this work, we fabricate high-performance and stable inverted PeSCs by using TPD as HTL and use PEDOT:PSS as a benchmark. After optimizing the concentration of TPD solution to control thickness, the champion device reached a high PCE of  $ 19.77\% $  together with a short circuit current density  $ (\mathrm{J_{sc}}) $  of  $ 22.92\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $ , a  $ \mathrm{V_{oc}} $  of  $ 1.083\mathrm{V} $ , and a fill factor (FF) of  $ 80.1\% $ , superior than the device based on PEDOT:PSS, which displays a maximum PCE of  $ 14.82\% $ , a  $ \mathrm{J_{sc}} $  of  $ 21.19\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $ , a  $ \mathrm{V_{oc}} $  of  $ 0.90\mathrm{V} $ , and an FF of  $ 77.61\% $ . Besides, the stability of TPD-based PeSCs is much higher than that of the PEDOT:PSS-based devices for the hydrophobicity of TPD.

The ITO-coated glass substrates were sequentially cleaned in an ultrasonic bath successively with detergent, de-ionized water, acetone, and isopropanol for 15 min. Then, all the substrates were exposed to ultraviolet ozone for 15 min after blown dry with nitrogen. For the devices with PEDOT:PSS HTL, PEDOT:PSS was spin-coated on ITO substrates with a thickness of 40 nm. For the devices employing TPD HTL, TPD was dissolved in chlorobenzene (CB) first. TPD solutions with different concentrations (0.5–10 mg ml  $  ^{-1}  $  ) were spin-coated on ITO substrates subsequently. Then, for all samples, perovskite films were deposited onto the HTL by a one-step spin-coating method. Then, the CB solvent of phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PC  $  _{61}  $  BM) was spun on the perovskite film. Finally, the obtained substrates were transferred to vacuum thermal equipment by depositing 4,7-diphenyl-1, 10-phenanthroline (Bphen) and then Ag cathode. The preparation process and characterization methods are detailed in the supplementary material.

To investigate why TPD-based PeSCs perform better than that based on PEDOT:PSS, the microstructure of perovskite films cast on PEDOT:PSS and TPD is characterized by scanning electron microscope (SEM). Figure 3 shows top-view SEM images of perovskite films deposited on the PEDOT:PSS layer and TPD layer. There exist no pinholes for perovskite films deposited on both PEDOT:PSS and TPD layer, as shown in Figs. 3(a) and 3(b). The perovskite film is entirely covered on these HTLs. However, with TPD as the underlying layer, larger perovskite grains are produced relative to PEDOT:PSS, as shown in Figs. 3(a) and 3(b), which is also proven by grain size distributions of perovskite films upon PEDOT:PSS layer ( $ \sim $ 200 nm) and TPD layer ( $ \sim $ 300 nm), as shown in Figs. 3(c) and 3(d). The larger

grain size of TPD-based perovskite film means lower grain boundary density, leading to fewer grain boundary defects, which is beneficial to the device performance.

In the meantime, the water contact angle (WCA) test indicates that the hydrophobicity of TPD is much higher than that of PEDOT:PSS, causing the larger grain size of the perovskite grown on TPD, as shown in Fig. 4. $ ^{32,33} $  The WCA of TPD is about  $ 75^{\circ} $ , while that of the PEDOT:PSS film is  $ 25^{\circ} $ . Meanwhile, the same volume of the perovskite precursor solution  $ (10\mu l) $  is dropped onto the HTL films. It could be found in Fig. 4(c) that the perovskite precursor solution can be rapidly spread on the PEDOT:PSS film but not on the TPD film, which also demonstrates the hydrophobicity of the TPD film. The hydrophobicity of the TPD film will benefit the long stability of the TPD case. Hence, long-term device stability was tested at ambient conditions as shown in Fig. 4(d) and stored in the glovebox when they were not tested. After almost one-month-long storage, the relative efficiency of the device with the PEDOT:PSS HTL degraded to  $ 62.3\% $  of its initial PCE, whereas the device with TPD HTL maintained the relative PCE of  $ 84.1\% $ . It means that the devices with TPD-based devices perform better than those with PEDOT:PSS HTL in terms of long-term stability. All the results mean that the TPD film is stable and universal to the inverted PeSCs.

In summary, highly efficient and stable  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -based inverted PeSCs have been fabricated with hydrophobic, low-temperature processed, and cheap organic small molecule TPD as HTL. The peak PCE of TPD-based PeSC approaches  $ 20\% $ , and the stability is also excellent, which is remarkably higher than PEDOT:PSS-based devices. It is due to the better energy level match between TPD and  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  layer, larger grain sizes of perovskite layers, and more efficient hole extraction. Hence, TPD has the potential as the HTL for commercial application in inverted PeSCs. Besides, it also means that ready-on and mass-produced organic small molecule materials working for OLED can be transplanted into the fabrication of high-performance perovskite solar cells, which can undoubtedly save research and development costs and time.
