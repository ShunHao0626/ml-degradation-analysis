# Super-flexible bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)-amide doped graphene transparent conductive electrode for photo-stable perovskite solar cells

Jin Hyuck Heo, Dong Hee Shin, Dae Ho Song, Do Hun Kim, Sang Jin Lee, and Sang Hyuk Im*

Super-flexible bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)-amide (TFSA)-doped graphene transparent conducting electrode (GR TCE)-based FAPbI $ _{3-x} $ Br $ _{x} $  perovskite solar cells with 18.9 % power conversion efficiency (PCE) for rigid device and 18.3 % for flexible one are demonstrated because the TFSA-doped GR TCE reveals high conductivity and high transmittance. The unencapsulated TFSA-doped GR TCE-based cell maintained ~95 % of its initial PCE under continuous light soaking of 1 Sun in 60 °C/30 % relative humidity for 1000 h. In addition, the TFSA-doped GR TCE-based flexible perovskite solar cells show excellent bending stabilities, maintaining PCEs of ~ 85, ~75, and ~35% of their original values after 5000 bending cycles, at R= 12, 8, and 4 mm, respectively.

In the past several decades, electronic devices have usually focused on higher speed, larger capacity, and smaller size, but these days, people are looking for more comfortable electronic devices that are flexible, foldable, or even wearable. To operate such electronic devices, the flexible power generation systems should be developed. From these aspects, the perovskite solar cell is a promising candidate for flexible power generator because its certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) is over 22 % due to its unique properties such as high absorption coefficient, long charge carrier's diffusion length, and small exciton binding energy and it has sufficient flexibility. $  ^{1}  $

Currently, indium tin oxide (ITO) has been used as transparent conducting electrodes (TCEs) in various optoelectronic devices due to the small sheet resistance and good transmittance in visible region (10-50   $  \Omega/cm^{2}  $   at   $  \sim90\%  $   transmittance).  $  ^{2}  $   However, the ITO TCEs are inherently brittle so their applications to flexible, foldable, and wearable optoelectronic devices are limited. Accordingly, it is strongly required to develop flexible TCEs for super flexible perovskite solar cells. For the flexible TCEs, carbon-based conductors

(carbon nanotubes and graphene),  $  ^{3}  $   conducting polymers,  $  ^{4}  $   metal-based conductors (nanowires and grids),  $  ^{5}  $   and oxide-metal-oxide (OMO) conductors  $  ^{6}  $   have been studied to date. Among them, the graphene (GR) conductor is a promising candidate for super-flexible TCE because it has high transparency, good conductivity, super flexibility, chemical stability, and convenient adjustment of Fermi level by chemical doping.

Recently, Sung et al. fabricated 17.1 % GR TCE/rigid glass substrate-based perovskite solar cells by reducing the sheet resistance of pristine single-layer GR TCE to ~500 Ω/cm² with p-type MoO₃ modification and 16.8 % GR TCE/PEN (polyethylene naphthalate) flexible substrate-based perovskite solar cells.⁷ We (Im et al.) also reduced the sheet resistance of single-layer GR TCE to ~70 Ω/cm² by p-type chemical doping with AuCl₃ and attained 17.9 % GR TCE/rigid glass substrate-based CH₃NH₃PbI₃ (MAPbI₃) perovskite solar cells.⁸ Very recently, we reported 17.9 % GR TCE/PET (polyethylene terephthalate) flexible substrate-based CH(NH₂)₂PbI₃₋ₓBrₓ(FAPbI₃₋ₓBrₓ) perovskite solar cells composed by PET substrate/3-aminopropyl triethoxysilane adhesion layer/AuCl₃-doped single-layer GR TCE/PEDOT:PSS (poly-(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrenesulfonic acid))/FAPbI₃₋ₓBrₓ/PCBM (phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester)/Al.⁹ Although the work function and the sheet resistance of single-layer GR TCE could be modified by chemical doping with AuCl₃, the optical loss by the absorption of AuCl₃ cannot be avoided.⁸,⁹ Unlike MoO₃ or AuCl₃ chemical doping, Tongay et al. reported that the bis (trifluoromethanesulfonyl)-amide[(CF₃SO₂)₂NH)] (TFSA) doped GR had low-resistance, high transparency, and long-term stability.¹⁰ Since then, the TFSA doped GR has been used to field effect transistors.¹¹

Here, we firstly used the TFSA doped GR to p-type TCE for perovskite solar cells. We fabricated TFSA-doped GR TCEs on a rigid glass and a flexible polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) substrate as model TCEs. We systematically checked the optical and electrical properties of TFSA-doped GR TCEs with respect to the doping concentration of TFSA and measured photovoltaic properties of TFSA-doped GR TCE-based

# <Figure 1>

Figure 1(a)-(d) are the atomic force microscopy (AFM) images showing the effects of TFSA doping concentration  $ (\mathsf{n}_{\mathsf{D}}) $  on the surface topologies of TFSA-doped GR TCEs. The root-mean-square roughness (rms,  $ R_{\mathrm{q}} $ ) of the pristine GR TCE ( $ n_{\mathrm{D}} = 0\mathrm{mM} $ ) was  $ \sim 0.332\mathrm{nm} $ , which is consistent with the previous report. $ ^{12} $  As the  $ n_{\mathrm{D}} $  increased from 0 to  $ 30~\mathrm{mM} $ , the  $ R_{\mathrm{q}} $  was gradually increased to 0.378, 0.405, and  $ 0.421\mathrm{nm} $  for  $ n_{\mathrm{D}} = 10, 20, $  and  $ 30~\mathrm{mM} $ , respectively. Unlike to the particulate dopants, the TFSA-doped GR TCEs had very smooth and even surface. To confirm the TFSA doping on GR TCE, we measured Raman spectroscopy of each sample as shown in Fig. 1(e). Two distinctive Raman peaks at  $ \sim 1580 $  and  $ \sim 2700~\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $  are the representative G and 2D band of GR TCE. $ ^{13} $  With increasing  $ n_{\mathrm{D}} $ , both G and 2D bands were blue-shifted by phonon softening due to the charge transfer from TFSA dopant to GR TCE as reported in previous literatures. $ ^{10,14} $  The intensity ratio of G to 2D peaks (I(G/2D)), another parameter to estimate the doping strength, increases gradually with doping concentration ( $ n_{\mathrm{D}} $ ) as listed in Table 1 because the doped material changes the lattice parameter and thereby changes the total charge number in the GR TCE. $ ^{15} $  The I(G/2D) of pristine GR TCE was 0.48, which indicates the graphitic structure is single-layer GR. $ ^{13} $  The TFSA-doping on the GR TCE was further confirmed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) as shown in Fig. 1(f) because the TFSA-doped sample ( $ n_{\mathrm{D}} = 20~\mathrm{mM} $ ) showed a clear N 1s peak in the XPS spectrum.

To check the electrical properties of TFSA-doped GR TCE with the doping concentration (  $  n_{D}  $  ), we measured work function and hole mobility of each sample from Kelvin probe force microscopy and van der Pauw method as shown in Fig. 1(g). The work functions of TFSA-doped GR TCEs are monotonically decreased from   $  -4.52 \pm 0.047  $   eV to   $  -4.92 \pm 0.026  $   eV with   $  n_{D}  $   as summarized in Table 1. This indicates that the TFSA-doped GR TCEs are p-type, consistent with the previous reports.  $  ^{[11,14]}  $

The   $  n_{D}  $  -dependent mobility of GR TCEs was estimated from the Hall measurement in dark room. The pristine GR TCE (  $  n_{D} = 0  $   mM) had the mobility of 2650 cm  $  ^{2}  $  /Vs and the 30 mM TFSA-doped one had slightly reduced mobility of 1894 cm  $  ^{2}  $  /Vs. The transmission spectra of TFSA-doped GR TCEs with various   $  n_{D}  $   in Fig. 1(e) indicate that the optical transmission loss is only ~1 % by TFSA chemical doping unlike conventional chemical dopants such as MoO  $  _{3}  $   and AuCl  $  _{3}  $  . At 550 nm-wavelength region, the transmittance of TFSA-doped GR TCEs was 97.18, 96.96, 96.80, and 96.48 % for   $  n_{D} = 0  $  , 10, 20, and 30 mM, respectively. The sheet resistance (  $  R_{s}  $  ) of pristine GR TCE was 650 ± 51 Ω/cm  $  ^{2}  $   and it was sharply decreased to 108 ± 45 Ω/cm  $  ^{2}  $   as   $  n_{D}  $   increases to 30 mM. In addition, we checked the long-term stability of the TFSA-doped GR TCEs by measuring the   $  R_{s}  $   with storage

time in air atmosphere as shown in Fig. 1(i). All TFSA-doped GR TCEs maintained their initial   $  R_{s}  $   values for 20 days.

To check whether the doped GR is suitable for a TCE, it is essential to analyze the sheet resistance (  $  R_{s}  $  )/transmittance (T). The ratio of DC conductivity/optical conductivity (  $  \sigma_{DC}/\sigma_{op}  $  ) is generally used as a parameter to evaluate the performance of a TCE. It can be calculated from the equation (  $  T = \{1 + (Z_{o}/2R_{s})(\sigma_{op}/\sigma_{DC})\}^{-2}  $  ).  $  ^{[16]}  $   Higher values of   $  \sigma_{DC}/\sigma_{op}  $   indicate better TCE properties with high T and low   $  R_{s}  $  . The   $  \sigma_{DC}/\sigma_{op}  $   values of the TFSA-doped GR TCEs were over   $  \sim35  $   and reached a maximum (  $  \sim100  $  ) at   $  n_{D} = 20  $   mM, much larger than the minimum industry standard (  $  \sigma_{DC}/\sigma_{op} = 35  $  ) for transparent conducting oxide (TCO) replacement materials.  $  ^{[16]}  $   In our previous report, we obtained the maximum   $  \sigma_{DC}/\sigma_{op}  $   value of   $  \sim39  $   for AuCl  $  _{3}  $   doped GR TCE.  $  ^{[8]}  $   Therefore, the TFSA-doped GR TCEs will be very promising TCE for perovskite solar cells.

Figure 2(a) is a schematic illustration of inverted (p-i-n type) FAPbI $ _{3-x} $ Br $ _x $  perovskite solar cell comprised to Glass or PDMS substrate/TFSA-doped GR TCE/PEDOT:PSS/FAPbI $ _{3-x} $ Br $ _x $ /PCBM/Al. It should be noted that the device is fabricated by low temperature (< 150 °C) solution process except Al metal electrode which is deposited by thermal evaporation in vacuum. Therefore, this device architecture is suitable to fabricate the perovskite solar cells of the rigid glass-based and the flexible PDMS-based TFSA-doped GR TCE. Figure 2(b) is the energy band diagram of corresponding FAPbI $ _{3-x} $ Br $ _x $  perovskite solar cell. Upon illumination of light, the free charge carriers or loosely-coupled electron-hole pairs are generated in the FAPbI $ _{3-x} $ Br $ _x $  perovskite layer. The electrons and holes are then transferred/transported to the PCBM ETL (electron transporting layer) and PEDOT:PSS HTL (hole transporting layer), respectively due to proper band alignment. The electrons and holes are then moved to Al and TFSA-doped GR TCE, respectively. Figure 2(c) is a cross-sectional SEM (scanning electron microscopy) image of representative inverted planar type FAPbI $ _{3-x} $ Br $ _x $  perovskite solar cell consisting of ~ 70-nm Al, ~ 40-nm PCBM, ~390-nm FAPbI $ _{3-x} $ Br $ _x $ , and ~ 30 nm PEDOT:PSS on TFSA-doped GR TCE/glass substrate.

To check the correct measurement of   $  J_{sc}  $  , the EQE (external quantum efficiency) spectra of each sample was measured as shown in Fig. 3(i). The EQE is significantly enhanced by doping of TFSA on the GR TCE in   $  n_{D} = 10 \, mM  $   sample compared to the pristine sample (  $  n_{D} = 0 \, mM  $  ). Apparently, the EQE values are evenly improved over a broad wavelength in a range from 300 to 850 nm. The EQE is a product of light harvesting efficiency (  $  \eta_{Ihe}  $  ), charge separation efficiency (  $  \eta_{Ics}  $  ), and charge collection efficiency (  $  \eta_{cc}  $  ) so the improvement of EQE values by TFSA doping is originated by the improved   $  \eta_{cs}  $   and/or   $  \eta_{cc}  $   because the absorption spectra were similar in all samples. The EQE spectra for   $  n_{D} \geq 20 \, mM  $   were saturated similar to the   $  J_{sc}  $   variations with the   $  n_{D}  $  .

To understand why the TFSA-doped GR TCE-based perovskite solar cells have the enhanced performance, we measured the diffusion coefficient (  $  D_{n}  $  ) and charge carrier's life time (  $  \tau_{n}  $  ) of each device by analysis of intensity modulated photocurrent spectroscopy (IMPS) and intensity modulated photovoltage spectroscopy (IMVS) spectra, respectively. Figure 3(j) and (k) indicate that by TFSA doping on GR TCE, the charge carriers generated in FAPbI  $  _{3-x}  $  Br  $  _{x}  $  perovskite solar cell are more effectively extracted out and the recombination between charge carriers within device is also more reduced than the pristine GR TCE-based device. From the measured data of  $  D_{n}  $  and  $  \tau_{n}  $ , we calculated the diffusion length (  $  L_{n} = (D_{n} \times \tau_{n})^{0.5}  $  ) of TFSA-doped GR TCE-based perovskite solar cell at  $  n_{D} = 0  $ , 10, 20, and 30 mM as listed in Table 2. The  $  L_{n}  $  of pristine (  $  n_{D} = 0  $  mM) sample (~215 nm) was greatly enhanced to ~355 nm in  $  n_{D} = 20  $  mM sample by the TFSA chemical doping on GR TCE and was saturated at over  $  n_{D} = 20  $  mM. From above results, we can conclude that the significantly enhanced device performance is attributed to the improved series resistance and shut resistance in the TFSA-doped GR TCE-based perovskite solar cells due to the efficient charge extraction and the reduced recombination of charge carriers. Figure 3(l) shows that the hysteresis with respect to the scan direction of perovskite solar cells is reduced with increase of  $  n_{D}  $  because the  $  \Delta PCE  $  (PCE of reverse scan condition–PCE of forward scan condition) was

reduced with   $  n_{D}  $  . The J-V hysteresis with respect to the scan direction can be reduced by balancing the electron and hole flux.  $  ^{[17]}  $   Accordingly, the reduced hysteresis might be attributed to the improved hole conductivity of TFSA-doped GR TCE/PEDOT:PSS layer because the electron conductivity of PCBM/Al is relatively high.

# Conclusions

We successfully fabricated highly efficient TCO-free FAPbI $ _{3-x} $ Br $ _{x} $  perovskite solar cells with excellent photo-stability by introduction of TFSA-doped GR TCEs with high transmittance and low R $ _{s} $ . The R $ _{s} $  of TFSA-doped GR TCE was greatly decreased from 650 ± 51 to 108 ± 45  $ \Omega $ /cm $ ^{2} $  when n $ _{D} $  increased from 0 to 30 mM, but its transmittance was not significantly reduced. At n $ _{D} $  =20 mM sample, the TFSA-doped GR TCE had the optimized/saturated R $ _{s} $  = 116 ± 49  $ \Omega $ /cm $ ^{2} $  and transmittance = 96.80 %, resulting in a maximum  $ \sigma_{\text{DC}}/\sigma_{\text{op}} $  = 99 ± 7. As a results, the TFSA-doped GR TCE/glass-based inverted planar type rigid FAPbI $ _{3-x} $ Br $ _{x} $  perovskite solar cells exhibited the highest PCE of 18.3 % for forward scan and 18.9 % for reverse scan condition and average PCE of 17.4±0.81 %. When the PCE of unencapsulated rigid device was tracked with continuous

light soaking time for 1000 h in 60 °C/30 % relative humidity under 1 Sun (100 mW/cm²) illumination, the TFSA-doped GR TCE-based perovskite solar cell exhibited 5.3 % degradation compared to its initial value, whereas the AuCl₃-doped GR TCE-based one had 13.1 % degradation. Similarly, the TFSA-doped GR TCE/PDMS-based flexible solar cells exhibited the best PCE of 17.8 % for forward scan and 18.2 % for reverse scan condition at nD = 20 mM sample. Its maintained PCEs of ~ 85, ~75, and ~35% of their original values after 5000 bending cycles, at R= 12, 8, and 4 mm, respectively. In addition, to improve the flexible stability of device, we treated the APTES coupling agent at TFSI-doped graphene and fabricated flexible perovskite solar cells by using APTES treated TFSI-doped graphene TCE. The APTES treated TFSA-doped GR TCE/PDMS-based flexible solar cells exhibited the best PCE of 18.0 % for forward scan and 18.3 % for reverse scan condition. Although PCE was similar to before APTES treatment, flexible stability was significantly improved than before APTES treatment. APTES treated devices maintained PCEs of ~ 89.5, ~82.2, and ~50.2 % of their original values after 5000 bending cycles, at R= 12, 8, and 4 mm, respectively.
