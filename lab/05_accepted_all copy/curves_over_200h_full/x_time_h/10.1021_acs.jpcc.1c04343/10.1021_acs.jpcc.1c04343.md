# Mechanism of Enhancement in Perovskite Solar Cells by Organosulfur Amine Constructed 2D/3D Heterojunctions

Zhen He, Yi Zhou, Cai Xu, Yingjie Su, Anmin Liu, Yanqiang Li, Liguo Gao,  $  ^{*}  $  and Tingli Ma

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Constructing a 2D/3D heterojunction perovskite that could remain highly efficient and improve the stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) is explored. In this paper, we compared 2D/3D heterojunction perovskite films passivated by organosulfur and commonly used organic amine salts. Results indicated that the perovskite film passivated by organosulfur amine salts had a lower trap state density, longer carrier lifetime, improved energy level alignment, and higher thermal and moisture stability. An approximate 21% power conversion efficiency (PCE) was obtained in the champion device. After the comparison, the passivation mechanism of the organosulfur 2D/3D heterojunction was proposed.

# INTRODUCTION

In the past decade, organic-inorganic lead halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have achieved rapid development with a certified record efficiency of up to 25.5%.  $  ^{1}  $   This attractive improvement is mainly attributed to the advantages of three dimensional (3D) halide perovskite materials, including suitable band gap, large light absorption coefficient, wide absorption spectrum, and long carrier life.  $  ^{2-5}  $   Compared with other photovoltaic devices, including silicon-based solar cells, inorganic thin-film solar cells, and organic solar cells, the instability of traditional 3D perovskite materials limits the commercialization of PSCs.  $  ^{6,7}  $   Therefore, the low-dimensional perovskite materials with enhanced stability have been extensively developed, in which the large-volume organic amine groups can provide steric hindrance for the adsorption of water molecules on the surface of the perovskite and the migration of internal ions.  $  ^{8,9}  $   Despite the enhanced stability, the reported power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs based on low-dimensional perovskite materials still lags far behind those of conventional PSCs. This is mainly because of the introduction of large organic amines. One reason is that the inserted large organic amines cause quantum and dielectric limiting domain effects, which will increase the exciton binding energy and widen the band gap of perovskite materials. The other reason is that the large organic amines decrease the conductivity and deteriorate the charge transfer performance, resulting in unsatisfactory carrier extraction and PCE.  $  ^{10}  $

To retain the high efficiency and improve the stability of PSCs, strategies of constructing 2D/3D perovskite heterojunctions with the large organic amines at the surface of 3D perovskite were investigated.  $  ^{11}  $   The large organic amines

# EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

Materials. FTO (15 Ω/□) substrate was ordered from Yingkou OPV Tech New Energy Co., Ltd. Tetrabutyl titanate (≥99.0%), diethanolamine (≥99.5%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, >99.9%), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, for GC-HS, ≥99.9%), chlorobenzene (standard for GC, ≥99.9%), and isopropyl alcohol (IPA, ≥99.9%) were purchased from Aladdin. TiO₂ paste (30 NRD), CsI (99.9%), PbBr₂ (99.9%), MABr (99.5%), FAI (99.5%), PbI₂ (99.9%), phenethylammonium iodide (PEAI), and thienylmethylamine iodide (TMAI) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. Spiro-OMeTAD (99.86%) was obtained from Liaoning Advanced Election Technology Co., Ltd. Anhydrous ethanol (99%) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. The target material for the high vacuum evaporation electrode was purchased from Alpha-Aisha.

Fabrication of Perovskite Solar Cells. The fabrication of c&m-TiO $ _2 $  substrates was the same as the process in our previous work. $ ^4 $  The trication perovskite material Cs $ _{0.05} $ (FA $ _{0.83} $ MA $ _{0.17} $ ) $ _{0.95} $ Pb(I $ _{0.83} $ Br $ _{0.17} $ ) $ _3 $  was composed of FAI (1.19 M), PbI $ _2 $  (1.31 M), MABr (0.21 M), PbBr $ _2 $  (0.21 M), and CsI (0.07 M), which were dissolved in a mixed solvent with a volume ratio of DMSO:DMF = 1:4. The perovskite precursor solution was spin coated on c&m-TiO $ _2 $  substrates, in a two-step procedure (first step: 1000 rpm for 10 s, second step: 5000 rpm for 30 s). During the 15th second of the second step, 150  $ \mu $ L of chlorobenzene, employed as antisolvent technology, was dropped onto the revolving perovskite surface. Subsequently, the substrate was annealed at 100  $ ^{\circ} $ C for 60 min.

For the control perovskite film, it was annealed on a hot plate at 100 °C for 15 min. PEAI-2D/3D and TMAI-2D/3D perovskite films were first cooled to room temperature and then reacted with the corresponding amine salt solution for 30 s. Next, the solution was spin-coated at 5000 rpm for 30 s and annealed on a hot plate at 100 C for 15 min. Then, the hole transporting layer was spin-coated on the perovskite substrates by hole transport material solution at 3500 rpm for 30 s which was obtained by adding 72.3 mg of spiro-OMeTAD, 28.8 μL of 4-tert-butylpyridine, 17 μL of Li⁺ salt, and 20 μL of cobalt(III) salt into 1 mL of chlorobenzene. The Au electrode was prepared by a high vacuum evaporation technology.

Characterization. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were carried out on XRD-7000S (Shimadzu), and the test conditions were 2–60° (2 theta), 4°·min  $  ^{-1}  $  . The UV–vis absorption spectra were measured by a LAMBDA 950 (PerkinElmer, U.S.A.). Steady-state PL spectra were obtained from an optically triggered streak camera system (C5410, Hamamatsu) with a 517 nm laser at a 400 V bias and 240 nm·min  $  ^{-1}  $  . Both top-view and cross-sectional scanning electron microscope (SEM) images were obtained from FEI Nova NanoSEM 450. The space charge limited current (SCLC) was collected on electron-only devices with a bias range of 0.001 to 10 V. Both X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) were obtained from the ESCALAB250Xi. The UPS employed a He I (hν = 21.2 eV) as the irradiation source, and the Fermi level was calibrated by Au. Time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectra were recorded with an Edinburgh FLS1000 fluorescence spectrometer under an excitation source at 375 nm. Nyquist plot measurements were conducted with an electrochemical workstation (Zennium Zahner, Germany) under AM 1.5G 100 mW·cm  $  ^{-2}  $   (Peccell-L15 solar simulator, Peccell, Japan) as well as a 0.6 V forward bias voltages of AC perturbation ranging from 100 mHz to 4 MHz. The obtained impedance spectra were fitted with the Zman software in terms of the appropriate equivalent circuit model. # RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONS

XRD results showed that the diffraction peaks of   $  5.44^{\circ}  $   and   $  6.06^{\circ}  $   represented the (020) crystal plane of the pure 2D perovskite material   $  PEA_{2}PbI_{4}  $   and   $  TMA_{2}PbI_{4}  $ , as shown in Figure S1. Therefore, the emerged same peak location after the organic amine passivation speculated that 2D perovskite material was formed on the 3D perovskite, thereby realizing a 2D/3D perovskite heterojunction, as shown in Figure 1b. However, the band gap of the passivated 2D/3D heterostructure film was almost the same as the 3D perovskite film without passivation (control), as ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption spectra shown in Figure S2. Compared with the control, the slight redshift of steady-state photoluminescence

(PL) deduced the crystal of the 2D perovskite film formed after passivation, as shown in Figure 1c.  $  ^{24,25}  $   These passivated 2D/3D heterostructure films could be also confirmed by SEM images. It showed that the grain boundaries were obvious in the control film, as shown in Figure 1d. However, after the passivation of both PEAI and TMAI, the perovskite grain boundaries were significantly reduced, as shown in Figure 1e,f. The passivated morphology is beneficial to suppress the charge recombination induced by grain boundaries, which would be deeply discussed later.  $  ^{13}  $

To understand the mechanism of the organosulfur amine salt TMAI-2D/3D heterojunction, a SCLC test was performed on electro-only devices (FTO/compact and mesoporous-  $  TiO_{2}  $   (c&m-  $  TiO_{2}  $  )/Perovskite/  $  PC_{61}BM/Au  $  ), as shown in Figure 2a. According to the equation   $  N_{\mathrm{t}} = (2V_{\mathrm{TFL}}\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{r}})/(qL^{2})  $  , the trap state density   $  N_{t}  $   can be obtained, where   $  V_{TFL}  $   is the trap-filled limit voltage, L is the thickness of the perovskite film,   $  \epsilon_{i}  $   is the relative dielectric constant of perovskite material, and   $  \epsilon_{0}  $   is the vacuum permittivity. For electron-only devices prepared in the same batch,   $  N_{t}  $   and   $  V_{TFL}  $   have a positive proportional relationship; that is, the smaller   $  V_{TFL}  $   means a smaller   $  N_{t}  $  .  $  ^{26}  $

Compared with control, the PEAI-2D/3D perovskite revealed a decreased   $  V_{TFL}  $   from 0.88 to 0.65 V. It was attributed to conversion of part of the excess   $  PbI_{2}  $   in the 3D perovskite layer into the 2D perovskite, which effectively reduced the trap state density. But the trap state density was further reduced in the TMAI-2D/3D perovskite film with a lowest   $  V_{TFL}  $   (0.34 V). To demonstrate the mechanism, XPS was performed on the Pb 4f of different perovskite films, as shown in Figure 2b. Compared with the control, the binding energies of the main peaks (Pb   $  4f_{7/2}  $   and Pb   $  4f_{5/2}  $  ) in PEAI-2D/3D film remained the same. However, this binding energy was changed in the TMAI-2D/3D samples. Meanwhile, the S 2p signals (Figure S3) in TMAI-2D/3D perovskite shift compared the TMAI ammonium salt. These results indicated that the S of TMAI has interactions with the uncoordinated Pb of perovskite (Pb…S), which is referred as Lewis adducts.  $  ^{27}  $   These Lewis adducts significantly passivate the defects induced by uncoordinated Pb, resulting in the lower trap state density. That means TMAI not only converts   $  PbI_{2}  $   into 2D perovskite but further passivates defects via Pb…S interactions. TRPL decay spectrum further confirmed the passivation effect of TMAI in Figure 2c. It was fitted by a biexponential decay function, as shown in Table S1. The   $  \tau_{1}  $   and   $  \tau_{2}  $   represented the trap-assisted nonradiative recombination at the perovskite film by different passivation and radiative recombinations of free carriers, which were mainly associated with the charge transfer process across the perovskite interface.  $  ^{28}  $   The largest   $  \tau_{1}  $   and   $  \tau_{2}  $   mean the lowest trap state density and longest lifetime in the TMAI-2D/3D heterojunction.

To demonstrate that the passivation had a positive effect on the photovoltaic performance, devices have been constructed as FTO/c&m-TiO $ _2 $ /Perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD/Au, as in the cross-sectional SEM image shown in Figure S4. Compared with the control, organic amine salt passivated 2D/3D heterojunction PSCs showed better photovoltaic performance. After passivating with both PEAI and TMAI, the open-circuit voltage ( $ V_{oc} $ ) and fill factor (F.F.) of the devices have increased significantly, as shown in Figures 3a and S5. However, the short circuit current ( $ J_{sc} $ ) of the device passivated with organic amine salts decreased. We speculated the large organic amines deteriorated the carrier transport and extraction, resulting in unsatisfactory  $ J_{sc} $ . The corresponding incident photon-to-electron conversion efficiency (IPCE), as shown in Figure 3b, verified the  $ J-V $  measurements. The TMAI-2D/3D device exhibits negligible hysteresis, which is attributed to the effective passivation, as shown in Figure S6. $ ^{29} $  Figure 3c illustrated the time evolution of the maximum output power of each device under the AM 1.5G light source. Results indicated that the TMAI-2D/3D and PEAI-2D/3D device could maintain a stable maximum output power within 600 s and had good reliability, whereas the control device demonstrated slightly reduced PCE after continuous output for about 250 s. $ ^{30} $  The PCE of 20 independent devices for each condition shows that the devices have good reproducibility in Figure 3d.

In order to deeply understand the influence of these organic amine passivated 2D/3D heterojunction PSCs, ELA was demonstrated by UPS (Figure S7). As shown in Figure 4a, the valence band (VB) of the TMAI-2D/3D perovskite film was higher than that of the 3D perovskite, which could form a type-II cascade alignment and was conducive to hole transport. Meanwhile, the high conduction band (CB) position of  $ \mathrm{TMA}_2\mathrm{PbI}_4 $  also effectively blocks the electrons generated by the bottom 3D perovskite layer from transporting to the hole

To confirm the improved charge extraction in the 2D/3D perovskite heterojunction devices, we characterized the devices by approaches of transient photocurrent and photovoltage decay.  $  ^{31,32}  $   Fitting the transient photovoltage (TPV) decay in Figure 4b with a single exponential decay function, the carrier lifetime of different devices can be extracted. The TMAI-2D/3D device performed the longest recombination time (  $  \tau_{r}  $  ) within 85.6   $  \mu  $  s, higher than those of PEAI-2D/3D (70.7   $  \mu  $  s) and the control (56.6   $  \mu  $  s). Then, the transient photocurrent (TPC) spectrum in Figure 4c was also utilized to analyze the charge transport in the devices. The fitting result of the TPC curves revealed that the TMAI-2D/3D based device demonstrated the smallest charge transport time (  $  \tau_{t}  $  ) within 7.8   $  \mu  $  s, while its PEAI-2D/3D and control counterparts are 9.4 and 10.7   $  \mu  $  s. The smaller the   $  \tau_{r}  $  , the faster the charge transports. The fitting results of electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) in Figure 4d show two arc rings, where a small high-frequency arc represents the charge transfer resistance (  $  R_{tr}  $  ), and a large low-frequency arc represents the charge recombination resistance (  $  R_{rec}  $  ). TMAI-2D/3D heterojunction PSCs showed the smallest   $  R_{tr}  $   and the largest   $  R_{rec}  $ , indicating the effective extraction and transfer of carriers at the interface between the perovskite and HTL. It is consistent with the results discussed above.  $  ^{33}  $

The thermal stability of the device is an important concern, where thermal stress can cause distortion of the perovskite lattice and induce ion migration.  $  ^{34}  $   The stability test showed that the TMAI-2D/3D heterojunction PSCs exhibited good thermal and long-term environmental stability, as shown in Figure 5. In   $  N_{2}  $   atmosphere at 85 °C, TMAI-2D/3D heterojunction PSCs still retained 90% of their initial PCE at 288 h (Figure 5a), while their counterparts based on the control and PEAI-2D/3D lost 85% and 35% of their initial PCE, respectively. The corresponding humidity test (65% RH) was carried out on unencapsulated devices at room temperature, as shown in Figure 5b. We hypothesized the mechanism that two main reasons caused the excellent thermal stability of TMAI-2D/3D heterojunction PSCs. One is the 2D/3D perovskite heterojunction formed by 2D amine salt passivation, which can provide a barrier for ion migration caused by thermal stress, which is also the main reason why the thermal stability of the PEAI-treated device is better than that of the control (Figure 5c). XPS results of I 3d in Figure S8 demonstrated enlarged binding energy due to the deceased electron cloud density of I atoms, indicating the formation of the S…I interaction.  $  ^{35}  $   The XPS results in Figure 2b showed that S atoms were a donor group, which could provide electrons to the Pb acceptor, resulting in increase of the electron cloud density of the Pb atom and reduction of the binding energy. To approve our suspicions, XPS measurements were conducted in the Spiro-OMeTAD layers of each device to detect I 3d and Pb 4f signals. Results in Figure S9 prove that, after annealing, I and Pb anions have immigrated into the spiro-OMeTAD layers from perovskite in control and PEAI-2D/3D devices, while the I 3d and Pb 4f signal is scarcely blank in the TMAI-2D/3D device. Above all, both Pb…S and S…I strong interactions induced by TMAI can further stabilize the perovskite lattice and suppress the ion immigration induced by thermal stress.  $  ^{36}  $

In addition to thermal stability, moisture resistance was carried out on the semicell device (FTO/c&m-TiO  $  _{2}  $  /Perovskite), as shown in Figure S10. The improvement of the moisture resistance of PSCs by the perovskite film is consistent with its hydrophobicity, and the order of the contact angles is TMAI (91.4°) > PEAI (79.1°) > control (58.9°). The large organic amine passivation becomes a hydrophobic surface, which can provide a strong barrier to the adsorption of water

on the perovskite lattice. From the XRD results in Figure S11, it is demonstrated that TMAI could prevent the moisture in the environment. For the control perovskite film, the peak of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  increased dramatically. The PEAI treated 2D/3D heterojunction perovskite film was much better, where the peak of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  slightly increased. However, there is no obvious change of the  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  peak in the TMAI treated 2D/3D heterojunction perovskite film. It indicated that TMAI has good hydrophobic properties. Therefore, 2D/3D heterojunction devices can remain stable in a high RH environment.

# CONCLUSION

The organic amine salt passivation could lower the trap state density, longer carrier lifetime, and improved energy level alignment of the 3D perovskite material. Results indicated that the perovskite film passivated by organosulfur amine salts was much better than the commonly used counterpart, which was induced by the strong Pb…S to form Lewis adducts. A type-II cascade alignment was formed in organosulfur amine salt passivated 2D/3D heterojunction PSCs, which facilitated the carrier transport. Results showed that the thermal stability, moisture stability, and PCE of PSCs based on organosulfur amine salt passivated 2D/3D heterojunction was higher than the control and commonly used counterpart passivation. The mechanism was that the organosulfur amine salts not only provided a barrier for ion migration but also had strong Pb…S and S…I interactions, which can further stabilize the perovskite lattice and help PSCs resist thermal stress-induced degradation.
