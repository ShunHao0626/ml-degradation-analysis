# Trap State Passivation by Rational Ligand Molecule Engineering toward Efficient and Stable Perovskite Solar Cells Exceeding 23% Efficiency

Lihua Zhu, Xian Zhang, Mengjia Li, Xueni Shang, Kaixiang Lei, Boxue Zhang, Cong Chen,* Shijian Zheng, Hongwei Song, and Jiangzhao Chen*

The nonradiative recombination losses resulting from the trap states at the surface and grain boundaries directly hinder the further enhancement of power conversion efficiency (PCE) and stability of perovskite solar cells. Consequently, it is highly desirable to suppress nonradiative recombination through modulating perovskite crystallization and passivating the defects of perovskite films. Here, a simple and effective multifunctional additive engineering strategy is reported where 11 Maleimidoundecanoic acid (11MA) units with carbonyls (carboxyl and amide) and long hydrophobic alkyl chain are incorporated into a perovskite precursor solution. It is revealed that improved crystallinity, reduced trap state density, and inhibited ion migration are achieved, which is ascribed to the strong coordination interaction between the carbonyl groups at both sides of 11MA molecules and  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} $ . As a result, improved efficiency and stability are achieved simultaneously after introducing 11MA additive. The device with 11MA additive delivers a champion PCE of  $ 23.34\% $  with negligible hysteresis, which is significantly higher than the  $ 18.24\% $  of the control device. The modified device maintains around  $ 91\% $  of its initial PCE after aging under ambient conditions for  $ 3000\mathrm{h} $ . This work provides a guide for developing multifunctional additive molecules for the purpose of simultaneous improvement of efficiency and stability.

The traditional solution method with fast crystallization and high-temperature annealing process would inevitably lead to a variety of defects in perovskite bulk and at the surfaces and grain boundaries (GBs).  $  ^{[7-9]}  $   Most defects in the bulk of perovskite films are shallow-level defects, while most defects at the surface and GBs of perovskite

films are deep-level defects, which is detrimental to device performance through capturing carriers.  $  ^{[6,10,11]}  $   Moreover, these defects would provide pathways for ion migration, which results in efficiency and stability losses.  $  ^{[6,12,13]}  $   In addition, water and oxygen would preferentially attack the surface and GBs of perovskite

# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic lead halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted extensive attention due to their high optical absorption coefficient, high carrier mobility, low exciton binding energy,

long carrier diffusion lengths, adjustable bandgaps, and low-cost fabrication.  $  ^{[1-3]}  $   Until now, the certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) has reached 25.5%,  $  ^{[4]}  $   making the PSC a promising candidate for the next-generation thin-film solar cells.  $  ^{[3,5]}  $   However, the presently obtained record PCE is still far from Shockley-Queisser limit efficiency.  $  ^{[6]}  $   In addition, the currently achieved stability is much lower below the standard of commercial application. It is well known that poor perovskite film quality is one of the main reasons for efficiency and stability losses. Consequently, it is highly expected to minimize the trap-assisted nonradiative recombination losses via improving perovskite film quality.

In the past, various strategies have been developed to improve the perovskite film quality, such as perovskite composition modulation,  $  ^{[18]}  $   precursor solvent, and antisolvent engineering,  $  ^{[19]}  $   deposition substrate modulation,  $  ^{[20]}  $   additive engineering,  $  ^{[21]}  $   etc. Among them, additive engineering has been demonstrated to be a feasible and effective strategy in modulating perovskite crystallization and passivating defects. Presently reported most additive molecules only could either modulate perovskite crystallization  $  ^{[22-24]}  $   or passivate defects.  $  ^{[7,25-27]}  $   Therefore, a simultaneous realization of crystallization modulation and defect passivation is still a huge challenge but important. Chen et al. incorporated bidentate ligand bidentate ligand 2,2'-bipyridine (Bpy) and tridentate ligand 2,2':6',2''-terpyridine (Tpy) into perovskite precursor solution, which resulted in the simultaneous achievement of improving crystallization and passivating defects.  $  ^{[21]}  $   Zheng et al. demonstrated the dual functions of crystallization control and defect passivation through introducing 3-(decyldimethylammonio)-propane-sulfonate inner salt (DPSI) inner salt into perovskite precursor solution.  $  ^{[28]}  $   Except for crystallization modulation and defect passivation, properly increasing the hydrophobicity of additive molecules are also necessary in order to improve the moisture stability of perovskite films and the final devices. The above works encourage us to further develop more effective multifunctional additive molecules to increase both efficiency and stability.

To date, a variety of additives have been developed, mainly including Lewis acid molecules,  $  ^{[29-32]}  $   Lewis base molecules,  $  ^{[21,33-36]}  $   and organic and inorganic salts.  $  ^{[37-39]}  $   Among the reported additives, Lewis base additives have been demonstrated to be very effective in modulating crystallization and passivating defects.  $  ^{[21,40]}  $   To tune the chemical interaction degree between additive molecules and perovskite compositions, appropriate functional groups containing N, O and/or S electron donors should be incorporated into additive molecules. The additive molecules containing -COOH have been widely demonstrated to control perovskite crystal growth and passivate film defects because carbonyl group as Lewis base in additive molecules can coordinate with   $  Pb^{2+}  $   as Lewis acid.  $  ^{[7,41-43]}  $   Apart from -COOH, carbonyl group was also introduced into additive molecules by amide functional group.  $  ^{[44,45]}  $   In addition, it should be stressed that multiple-active-site ligand molecules should be developed to maximize the potentials of defect passivation effect. Moreover, the development of ligand molecules containing not only functional groups acting as electron donors but also hydrophobic functional groups (e.g., benzene ring, long alkyl length, etc.) is highly desirable for the sake of simultaneous enhancement of efficiency and stability. In one word, it is highly desirable to achieve crystal growth modulation and defect passivation via rational engineering of multifunctional additive ligand molecules.

In this work, we developed a simple and effective additive engineering strategy via incorporating multifunctional molecule 11MA with carbonyl (carboxyl and amide) and long hydrophobic alkyl chain into perovskite precursor solution. It is demonstrated that the addition of 11MA leads to improved crystallization, increased carrier lifetimes, reduced defect density, and improved film stability, which are translated into improved

device efficiency and stability. The modified device achieves a remarkably increased PCE of 23.34% along with negligible hysteresis.

# 2. Results and Discussion

Figure 1a shows the chemical structure of 11MA, which consists of -COOH, long alkyl chain, and maleimide. As shown in Figure 1b, the -COOH and maleimide were expected to be capable of modulating crystal growth and passivating defects through coordinating with  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} $ . In addition, a hydrophobic long alkyl chain was expected to be able to improve the moisture stability of perovskite films. In a word, 11MA additive molecules were expected to enhance both PCE and stability. The shift of -C=O peaks proves the interaction between -COOH and perovskite film. The Pb 4f peaks arise from the perovskite films, and the C 1s peak originates from the atmospheric carbon dioxide, 11MA (with carboxyl, amide, and alkyl) and A-site organic component  $ (\mathrm{FA}^{+},\mathrm{MA}^{+}) $  in perovskite films. The effective incorporation of 11MA can be first confirmed by the appeared C=O, O-C=O, O-C-O bond signal in the C 1s spectra (Figure 1f) for the modified perovskites film. The interaction between 11MA and the perovskite film can also be confirmed by a shift in the binding energy of the  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} $  in Figure 1g. To figure out the reduced trap-assisted recombination with 11MA modification, we perform the electron trap density (  $  N_{t}  $  ) of the perovskite film from the J-V curves of the electron-only device to confine the defect passivation effect (Figure 4f).  $  ^{[1]}  $   Usually, the measured J-V curves can be divided into the ohmic region, trap-filled limit region (TFL), and child region. Under low voltage conditions, ohmic contact occurs inside the device. In the TFL section, when the bias exceeds trap-filled limit voltage (  $  V_{TFL}  $  ), which equal to kink-point voltage, the current increases sharply, indicating the completion of defect filling. The   $  N_{t}  $   can be obtained from the bias proximity   $  V_{TFL}  $   in the TFL region using the following formula

$$
V _ {\mathrm{TFL}} = \frac {e N _ {\mathrm{t}} L ^ {2}}{2 \varepsilon \varepsilon_ {0}}
$$

(1)

where  $ \varepsilon $  and  $ \varepsilon_0 $  are the dielectric constant of perovskite film and vacuum, respectively,  $ L $  is the thickness of the perovskite film,  $ e $  is the elementary charge. Calculation analysis indicated that the value of  $ V_{\mathrm{TFL}} $  of control and 11MA modified perovskite films is  $ 0.164\mathrm{V} $  and  $ 0.137\mathrm{V} $ , respectively. Consequently, the trap density of the control and target perovskite films were calculated to be  $ 2.54\times 10^{15}\mathrm{cm}^{-3} $  and  $ 2.12\times 10^{15}\mathrm{cm}^{-3} $ , respectively, which indicated

that 11MA could effectively passivate the defects in perovskite films in good agreement with increased carrier lifetimes as we discussed above. We further measured the charge carrier mobility by four-probe Hall effect measurements (Figure S4, Supporting Information). Our results indicated that the mobility of perovskite films was slightly increased from  $ 13.8~\mathrm{cm}^2\mathrm{V}^{-1}s^{-1} $  to  $ 17.2~\mathrm{cm}^2\mathrm{V}^{-1}s^{-1} $  after 11MA modification, which was consistent with the enhanced carrier lifetimes and reduced defect density.

Previous investigations indicated that controlling the nature of the perovskite film could mitigate nonradiative recombination and achieve high   $  V_{OC}  $  .[48,52] Ordinarily, the   $  V_{OC}  $   depends logarithmically on the light intensity. The relationship between   $  V_{OC}  $   and light intensity is plotted in Figure 5a, and the ideality factor n is introduced as a prefactor n in the following equation.

$$
\delta V _ {\mathrm{oc}} = n \left(\frac {K _ {\mathrm{B}} T}{e}\right) \ln (I) + \text { constant }
$$

(2)

where e is the elementary charge, T is temperature and   $  K_{B}  $   is the Boltzmann constant, and   $  V_{OC}  $   stands for the obtained   $  V_{OC}  $   under different light intensities (I). It was found that the control PSCs exhibited a slope of 1.91   $  K_{B}T/e  $  , while the 11MA modified device showed a much smaller slope of 1.32   $  K_{B}T/e  $  . This indicated that defect-assisted nonradiative recombination was effectively suppressed after 11MA modification, which was in good accordance with increased   $  V_{OC}  $   and FF. Also, the lower leakage dark current (Figure S5, Supporting Information) suggested improved charge transport and decreased recombination loss in the 11MA modified PSCs.  $  ^{[53]}  $

$$
\frac {1}{C ^ {2}} = \frac {2}{\varepsilon \varepsilon_ {0} e A ^ {2} N} (V _ {\mathrm{bi}} - V)

(3)

where C is the measured capacitance, A is the active area, V is the bias,   $  \varepsilon  $   is the static permittivity,   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   is the permittivity of free space, e is the elementary charge, and N is the doping density of the donor that represents carrier density in PSCs. It was found that the 11MA modified device showed a larger slope (  $  2.75 \times 10^{16}  $  ) than the control device (  $  2.51 \times 10^{16}  $  ), indicating the decreased trap states density and reduced nonradiative carrier recombination. In addition, recombination resistance was significantly increased after 11MA modification due to the effective defect passivation as reflected by the reduced defect density, which should contribute to enhanced   $  V_{OC}  $  .

Moreover, we analyzed the density of states (DOS) in the perovskite film using impedance spectroscopy. The capacitance is independent of the interfacial capacitance but is determined by the perovskite film itself, revealing that a high capacitance value stands for a high DOS.[7,56] It has been demonstrated that the distribution of the DOS can be derived from the chemical capacitance using the equation

$$
\mathrm{DOS} = \frac {C _ {\mu}}{L e ^ {2}}
$$

(4)

where   $  C_{\mu}  $   is the chemical capacitance, which reflects the capability of a photovoltaic device to accept or release additional carriers due to the carrier accumulation in perovskite films. L is the thickness of perovskite film. e is the elementary charge. Thus, the   $  C_{\mu}  $   is proportional to the DOS. The DOS of the PSCs was calculated through the above formula by fitting the curves in Figure 5c. As shown in Figure 5d, the 11MA modified device possessed a relatively smaller DOS in the order of   $  10^{15}  $  , which was lower than   $  10^{17}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $   for the control device, providing evidence for reduced trap states. In summary, from the optical and electrical points of view, these results verified that the defects in perovskite film were passivated effectively by 11MA.

We further investigated the effect of 11MA modification on the moisture stability of perovskite films. Specific measurement was exhibited by storing the films in a petri dish with water vapor. Figure 6a depicts the pictures of morphological changes. We can observe that the control film gradually turned yellow, indicating the decomposition of the perovskite material.

Nevertheless, the modified film remained black under the same conditions, revealing that the modified film possessed excellent humidity resistance. XRD patterns further confirmed this result after recording 3 and 6 days (Figure 6b). The control film exhibited a higher PbI $ _2 $  peak at 12.7° and a lower perovskite peak at 14.2° after 3 days. Furthermore, the control film exhibited a disappeared perovskite peak at 14.2° and a higher PbI $ _2 $  peak (at 12.1°) after 6 days, demonstrating the severely decomposed control film. As for the 11MA modified film, we can still observe a dominant perovskite diffraction peak at 14.2° after 6 days, indicating the integrity of the film crystal structure. Meanwhile, the recorded UV–vis absorption intensity (Figure 6c) of the control film dropped sharply over time, while the 11MA modified films presented a slight reduction at the wavelength range of ≈300–600 nm. By fitting the declining trend of the absorption curve (Figure S6, Supporting Information), we can find that the degradation rate of the control film is higher one order of magnitude than that of 11MA modified perovskite film. From the corresponding topography SEM images in Figure 6d,e, we can observe that the control films decompose seriously with obvious crystal structure fracture and substrate appearance, while the modified films still possess complete and flat morphology. Therefore, the introduction of 11MA into perovskite precursors could significantly enhance its ambient stability. The improved stability may be attributed to the following terms: 1) The long-alkyl-chain of 11MA for enhancing the hydrophobicity of the films, confirmed by the increased water contact angle (Figure 6f,g) from 65.02° to 85.02° for water-resistance. 2) Effective defect passivation reduces the active sites of degradation.

We also tracked the devices' photos under RH condition of   $  60 \pm 5\%  $   for 56 days (Figure 6h). It is evident that the control devices completely decomposed after 500 h. On the contrary, the 11MA modified device did not exhibit apparent decomposition after 56 days. From the corresponding normalized PCEs versus time under RH condition of   $  35 \pm 5\%  $  , 11MA modified devices could maintain   $  \approx91\%  $   of their initial performance after 3000 h (Figure 6i).

# 3. Conclusions

In summary, we have developed a rational ligand molecule, namely 11MA, which has been demonstrated to be able to improve perovskite crystallization, improve the moisture stability of perovskite film and the device, and passivate the trap states at the surface and/or GBs of perovskite films. It was revealed that improved crystallization and defect passivation are due to the strong chemical interaction between -C=O in 11MA and undercoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $ . The long hydrophobic long alkyl chain in 11MA molecules should be responsible for the markedly increased moisture resistance of the perovskite films and thereby devices. This strategy enabled us to obtain a device with an impressive PCE exceeding  $ 23.0\% $  with reduced voltage loss and inhibited hysteresis. The unencapsulated modified device maintains around  $ 91\% $  of its initial PCE after aging under ambient conditions for  $ 3000\mathrm{h} $ . Our work opens up a new paradigm toward speeding up the commercialization of high efficiency and stable PSCs.

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials: The   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloidal solution (15% in   $  H_{2}O  $   colloidal dispersion) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. Formamidinium iodide (FAI, 99.5%), methylammonium bromine (MABr, 99.9%) were purchased Greatcell solar Materials Pty Ltd. Lead iodide (PbI  $  _{2}  $  , 99.99%), Cesium iodide (CsI) (99.9%) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp, China. Lead bromide (PbBr  $  _{2}  $  , 99.9%), Chlorobenzene (99.99%), N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) (>99.9%), and dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) (>99.8%) were purchased from Macklin. Spiro-OMeTAD (99.5%) was purchased from Youxuankeji. FK 209 Co(III), 4-tert-butylpyridine (t-BuPy), acetonitrile (ACN) (>99.9%), and lithiumbis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (Li-TFSI), 11-M aleimidoundecanoic acid (CAS:57079-01-3, chemical structure: C15H23NO4) were obtained from Aladdin. All chemicals were used without further purification.

Preparation of the Perovskite Precursor: The   $  Cs_{0.05}(FA_{0.83}MA_{0.17})_{0.95}PbI_{0.83}Br_{0.17}  $   mixed perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving   $  PbI_{2}(1.1\;\mathrm{M})  $  ,   $  PbBr_{2}(0.2\;\mathrm{M})  $  , FAI (1.0 M), and MABr (0.2 M) in a mixed solvent of DMF/DMSO (4:1, by volume). Then adding 50   $  \mu  $  L CsI (pre-dissolved as a 1.5 mM stock solution in DMSO) to 1 mL above perovskite solution to obtain   $  Cs_{0.05}(FA_{0.83}MA_{0.17})_{0.95}PbI_{0.83}Br_{0.17}  $   perovskite precursor solution.

Preparation of the Spiro-OMeTAD Solution: The Spiro-OMeTAD solution was prepared by dissolving 73 mg Spiro-OMeTAD into 1 mL chlorobenzene, followed by the addition of 17.5 μL Li-TFSI (pre-dissolved as a 520 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  stock solution in ACN) and 29 μL FK209 (pre-dissolved as a 300 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  stock solution in ACN) and 28.8 μL 4-tert-butylpyridine.

Device Fabrication: FTO glass substrates (sheet resistance = 8 Ω per sq, thickness = 2.5 mm, AsadeionizedCo.) were cleaned by glass detergent (1 vol% in deionized water), deionized water, acetone, and ethanol for 15 min, in sequence. The cleaned substrates were treated with UV ozone for 30 min to remove the last traces of organic residues. SnO₂ films: The SnO₂ colloidal dispersion was diluted by deionized water (1:6 by volume), and then spin-coated onto the clean FTO glass substrates at 5000 rpm for 30 s, then annealed at 150 °C for 30 min. After it was cooled to room temperature, ultraviolet ozone treatment was performed for 15 min. Then, the perovskite layer was deposited on the SnO₂ layer through spin-coating of the mixed perovskite precursor solution in a two steps program at 1000 and 6000 rpm for 10 and 30 s, respectively. During the second step, 400 μL of chlorobenzene was poured at 25 s. The prepared films were then annealed at 100 °C for 60 min. Wait until cooling to room temperature, a 60 μL Spiro-OMeTAD solution was spun on the corresponding perovskite films was deposited by spin-coating at 3000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, 80 nm of gold was evaporated as the back electrode to form the whole device.

Characterization: The J-V characterizations were measured by a Keithley 2400 source measurement unit under simulated AM 1.5 irradiation (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) with a standard xenon-lamp-based solar simulator (7ISO503A, SOFN INSTRUMENTS).
