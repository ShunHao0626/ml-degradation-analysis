# Barium Acetate as an Additive for high Performance Perovskite Solar Cell

Yueming Wang,   $  ^{a,b}  $   Yulei Wu,   $  ^{b}  $   Sheng Fu,   $  ^{b}  $   Changjian Song,   $  ^{b}  $   Li Wan,   $  ^{b}  $   Wenxiao Zhang,   $  ^{b}  $   Xiaodong Li,   $  ^{b}  $   Weiguang Yang,   $  ^{a}  $   Weijie Song,   $  ^{b}  $   and Junfeng Fang   $  *^{b,c}  $

Defects within the halide perovskite films limit the efficiency and stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Here, the barium acetate   $  \left(\mathrm{BaAc}_{2}\right)  $   is employed as an additive in   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   for high performance PSCs. The   $  BaAc_{2}  $   can control the crystallization and thus produce high-quality perovskite film, which is attributed to interaction between the acetate ions and perovskite precursor. Both low defect density and the movement of energy levels contribute a significant increasement in open circuit voltage   $  \left(V_{oc}\right)  $  . Consequently, the efficiency up to 19.82% with a high   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.13V is obtained based on the optimizing   $  BaAc_{2}  $   concentration device. Moreover, the compact perovskite crystal with suppression of ions migration endows the   $  BaAc_{2}  $   doped PSCs obvious enhanced thermal stability. The device with   $  BaAc_{2}  $   additive maintained over 90% of its original power conversion efficiency (PCE) after thermal aging at 90 °C for 400 hours.

# Introduction

Metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have generated enormous attention in the photovoltaic field due to their merits of high absorption coefficient, long charge carrier diffusion lengths, tunable direct bandgap and simple fabrication process.  $  ^{1-5}  $   The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs has rocked from 3.8% in 2009 to certified 25.2% in 2019.  $  ^{6,7}  $   Despite the excellent performance has been achieved in PCE, the stability is still the main issue that limits the commercialization of PSCs.  $  ^{8}  $   The PSCs suffer from serious degradation under thermal stress, moisture, oxidation, light illumination, etc.  $  ^{9,10}  $   Massive researches have been taken to improve the efficiency and stability of the PSCs, including device architecture optimization,  $  ^{11,12}  $   solvent engineering,  $  ^{13}  $   interfacial engineering,  $  ^{14}  $   defect passivation,  $  ^{15}  $   structural and materials engineering,  $  ^{16}  $   low-dimensional perovskite based devices development and so on.  $  ^{17}  $   Of all factors, the perovskite films influence the most importantly device performance.

A great deal of approaches, such as vapor phase deposition,  $  ^{18}  $   two-step sequential deposition,  $  ^{19}  $   and importing additives into perovskite precursor  $  ^{20}  $   have been reported in the past few years to improve the perovskite film quality. However, the solution-preparation perovskite films still suffer from the poor crystallization.  $  ^{21, 22}  $   Numerous grains boundaries (GBs) and pinholes

enriching massive defects seriously influence the performance of PSCs seriously.  $  ^{3,4}  $   In addition, the GBs serve as channels for moisture penetration and then lead to perovskite decomposition.  $  ^{23}  $   It should also be recognized that the trap states at GBs are significant sources for non-radiative recombination.  $  ^{24,25}  $   Furthermore, ions migration is easy along the GBs, which has negative impact on device performance and long-term stability.  $  ^{26,27}  $   Therefore, passivation of GBs is an effective strategy for device performances and stability improvement.

Additive engineering has been demonstrated as a feasible method to obtain high-quality perovskite films and enhance the performance of PSCs.  $  ^{28}  $   Polymers,  $  ^{29,30}  $   inorganic acids,  $  ^{31}  $   fullerene,  $  ^{32,33}  $   organic halide salts,  $  ^{34,35}  $   ionic liquids  $  ^{36}  $   have been reported to be added into perovskite for device performance enhancement. Based on lewis-base theory, molecules containing carbonyl groups can interact with   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and   $  MA^{+}  $  ions and control the perovskite crystallization thus yield uniform films with less GBs.  $  ^{25,37}  $   For instance, our group reported trimethylolpropane triacrylate (TMTA) can in-situ cross-link in perovskite films after thermal post-treatment. The operational stability of devices obviously improved for the carbonyl groups in TMTA chemically anchoring to GBs.  $  ^{38}  $   In addition, carbonyl groups contained additives, such as polymers, small molecules, quantum dots can also be used for GB passivation and the control of morphology.  $  ^{39-41}  $   Metal acetate salts are usually used to form high quality perovskite films. Tang et al employed lead acetate as an additive, which can act as a crosslinking agent to control the crystallization process. Moreover, the lead acetate additive finally generate   $  PbI_{2}  $   in the perovskite films and passivate defects at GBs.  $  ^{42}  $   Europium acetate has been approved to effectively reduce defects in perovskite.  $  ^{43}  $   It was reported that liquid zirconium acetate used as an additive in   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   PSCs can capture free iodide ions, leading to high-efficiency and high-stability devices.  $  ^{44}  $   Alkaline-earth metal cations doped perovskites have been systematically

In this work, Barium acetate (BaAc $ _2 $ ) was introduced into the MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite precursor solution as an additive to fabricate devices with an inverted structure of ITO/P3CT-N/MAPbI $ _3 $ /PC $ _{61} $ BM/C60/BCP/Ag. The BaAc $ _2 $  containing carbonyl group can control the nucleation and growth of perovskite, thus leading to high crystallinity films with large grain size. The barium ions enter the crystal lattice of perovskite, resulting in lattice expansion. Furthermore, the energy levels of MAPbI $ _3 $  with BaAc $ _2 $  shift toward vacuum level relative to the pristine MAPbI $ _3 $ . The BaAc $ _2 $  finally can form Bal $ _2 $  in the perovskite layers and effectively passivate the GBs. The pristine device exhibited a PCE of 18.99% with a  $ V_{oc} $  of 1.09 V. The BaAc $ _2 $  added device achieved a PCE of 19.82% with a  $ V_{oc} $  of 1.13 V, which is remarkably improved compared with the pristine device. In addition, the BaAc $ _2 $  added device retained over 90% of its initial PCE after thermal annealing for 400 h under 90 °C. However, the control device maintained less than 50% of its initial PCE under the same condition. A very small amount of BaAc $ _2 $  can effectively promote both efficiency and stability of methylammonium lead trihalide perovskite solar cells.

# Experimental Section

# Materials

P3CT-N was synthesized in our lab according to previous report.  $  ^{46}  $     $  PbI_{2}  $   (99.99%), MAI (99.99%) and Bathocuproine (BCP, 99.9%) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%) and Chlorobenzene (CB, 99.8%) were procured from Sigma-Aldrich. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.8%) was procured from Alfa Aesar. Barium Acetate (  $  BaAc_{2}  $  , 99.99%) was obtained from Aladdin (China).   $  PC_{61}BM  $   (6, 6-Phenyl C61 Butyric Methyl Ester) was purchased from American Dye Source.

# Device fabrication

The ITO substrate (20mm*20mm) was sequentially cleaned through ultrasonication in detergent, deionized water, acetone and isopropanol for 15 min respectively. The cleaned substrates were dried under   $  N_{2}  $   flow and then treated in   $  O_{2}  $   plasma for 3 min. The P3CT-N solution (1 mg/mL in methanol) was deposited on ITO substrate at 4000 r.p.m for 30 s and then annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. The perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 230.5 mg MAI and 668.4 mg   $  PbI_{2}  $   in 1 mL DMF:DMSO (4:1 vol ratio) solvent. For the   $  BaAc_{2}  $   containing solution,   $  BaAc_{2}  $   powders were added into the solution directly. The perovskite layers were prepared by one-step spin coating method in a glove box filled with   $  N_{2}  $ . The perovskite precursor solution was dropped on ITO/P3CT-N substrate and spin coated at 4800 r.p.m for 20 s. 300 μL CB was dropped on the center of the substrate at 8s after the start-up. The film then was annealed on a hot plate at 60 °C for 1 min and 80 °C for 5 min. After the substrate cooled down to room temperature, the   $  PC_{61}BM  $   solution (10 mg/ml in CB) was spin-coated on the perovskite film at 2000 r.p.m for 40 s to form electron transporting layer. Finally, 20 nm

C60, 8 nm BCP and 100 nm Ag was deposited by thermal evaporation in a vacuum chamber using a metal shadow mask. The device area was 9 mm $  ^{2}  $ .

# Characterization and measurements

The  $ J - V $  curves were measured using Keithley 2440 sourcemeter with an Oriel So13A solar under simulated AM 1.5G irradiation (100  $ \mathrm{mW / cm^2} $ ) in a glove box filled with  $ \mathbf{N}_2 $ . The devices were measured by scanning from  $ -0.2\mathrm{V} $  to  $ 1.2\mathrm{V} $  for forward scan (from  $ 1.2\mathrm{V} $  to  $ -0.2\mathrm{V} $  for reverse scan) with a dwell time of  $ 50~\mathrm{ms} $ . The external quantum efficiency (EQE) was measured through a Newport quantum efficiency measurement system (ORIEL IQE 200TM) in atmosphere. The light intensity at each wavelength was calibrated with one standard Si/Ge solar cell. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images were recorded by field emission scanning electron microscope (Hitachi, S-4800) with an accelerating voltage of  $ 8\mathrm{kV} $ . Steady Photoluminescence (PL) spectra were recorded by a Fluorolog-Horiba fluorometer with excitation wavelength at  $ 466\mathrm{nm} $ . Time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) were conducted with excitation wavelength at  $ 466\mathrm{nm} $  and emission wavelength at 770 nm. XRD patterns of the films were analyzed using a powder X-ray diffractometer (Bruker AXS D8 Advance, Germany) with Cu Kα radiation. Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) was performed using a Kratos AXIS ULTRA DLD XPS/UPS system and was carried out using He I radiation at  $ 21.2\mathrm{eV} $  from a discharge lamp operated at  $ 20\mathrm{mA} $ , a pass energy of  $ 5\mathrm{eV} $ , and a channel width of 25 meV. UV-vis spectra were recorded on a GS54T spectrophotometer (Shanghai Lengguang Technology Co., China).

Comparing with the interior of grains, GBs containing a lot of defects are shortcuts for ion moving. Therefore, massive defects at GBs can be passivated to alleviate ion migration. Thermal stability is an important issue for long-term stability PSCs. MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   suffers from decomposition under heat due to its intrinsic phase instability and ion migration. Thermal stable perovskite compound is essential to thermal stable solar cells. To evaluate the thermal stability of BaAc  $  _{2}  $   added PSCs, devices without encapsulation were exposed to continuous heating at 90 °C under nitrogen atmosphere. The pristine device declined to less than 50% of initial PCE after 100 hours. # Conclusions

In summary,  $ \mathrm{BaAc_2} $  was added in  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  active layer in inverted planar PSCs.  $ \mathrm{BaAc_2} $  could interact with methylamine ion and retard the crystallization of perovskite, thus high quality perovskite films with large grains were obtained. The  $ \mathrm{Ba^{2+}} $  can enter the perovskite crystal lattice and result in lattice expansion. The generated  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and  $ \mathrm{BaI}_2 $  located at GBs could passivate defects and suppress ion migration. Both the shift of energy level toward vacuum and the decreased trap states in  $ \mathrm{BaAc_2} $ -incorporated perovskite films contribute to an obvious raise in  $ V_{oc} $ . As a result, a champion PCE of  $ 19.82\% $  with a high  $ V_{oc} $  of  $ 1.13\mathrm{V} $  was achieved in  $ \mathrm{BaAc_2} $  added device. At the same time, the PSCs with  $ \mathrm{BaAc_2} $  retained over  $ 90\% $  of initial efficiency after 400 hours continuous thermal aging, which shows remarkable improved thermal stability compared to the pristine devices.
