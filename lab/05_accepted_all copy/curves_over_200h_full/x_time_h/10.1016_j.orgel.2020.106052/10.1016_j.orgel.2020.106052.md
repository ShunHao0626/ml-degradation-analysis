# 1. Introduction

Hybrid perovskite semiconductors have attracted significant attention due to their excellent optoelectronic properties including tunable bandgaps, high light absorption coefficients, and long charge diffusion lengths   $  [1]  $  ,   $  [-3]  $   which enable them to have very promising applications in next-generation photovoltaic technology. In the past few years, the power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of single-junction perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have dramatically increased from the initial 3.8% in 2009 to the certified 25.4%   $  [4]  $   in 2020, which are attributed to the extensive investigation progress in material composition optimization   $  [5–7]  $  , device physics   $  [8–10]  $   and interface engineering   $  [11–13]  $  .

A typical PSC is composed of a perovskite layer as a light absorber sandwiched between a hole transport layer (HTL) and an electron transport layer (ETL). The interfaces at perovskite/HTL (or ETL) have crucial impacts on both device efficiency and stability   $  [14–16]  $  . They are mainly ascribed that (1) the defect density at the interfaces can be >100 times larger than the bulk perovskite films, implying that the interfaces

As a result, the champion device using BP:PTAA produces the higher power conversion efficiency of  $ 20.49\% $  than the control device of  $ 18.26\% $ . The resulting charge recombination and accumulation at the interfaces also destabilize perovskite crystal structures [20]. In addition, some strategies including low dimension contact layers and secondary growth technology, have been applied to effectively reduce nonradiative recombination at interface [21,22]. It indicates that there is much room for improving the perovskite/PTAA interface in terms of the band alignment engineering, trap passivation, and charge transport [30–33]. Recently, 2D black phosphorus (BP) has shown huge potential for application in optoelectronics due to its outstanding tunable optoelectronic properties and high carrier mobility [34–37]. It is reported that BP could serve as an effective HTM layer [38], improve perovskite crystallinity [39] and suppress Pb  $  ^{0}  $  formation in PSCs [40]. In addition, Huang et al. employed 2D BP nanosheets as a thickness-controllable dual-functional interfacial layer in PSCs, where the charge extraction was largely improved at both ETL/perovskite and perovskite/HTL interfaces, yielding an enhanced PCE of 19.83% from 16.95% of the control device [41].

In this work, we introduce BP into PTAA solution as a HTL to achieve the highly efficient planar p-i-n PSCs. Multiple beneficial effects of the BP:PTAA layer have been fully discussed, including enhanced charge conductivity, formation of high-quality perovskite film with less defect states, and improved charge extraction efficiency with decreased energy barrier at interface. These improvements lead to the suppressed non-radiative recombination at perovskite interface and in bulk film. The BP:PTAA realizes a high PCE of 20.49% with a   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.106 V, a   $  J_{sc}  $   of 22.43 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  , and a FF of 82.6%, superior to 18.26% of the control device. Furthermore, the unencapsulated device shows the excellent stability, where it can maintain over 80% of its initial efficiency after 800 h storage under 45% relative humidity at room temperature, in contrast, the control device quickly degrades with the loss over 40% of its initial value for the same period.

To evaluate the hole extraction efficiency at perovskite/BP:PTAA interface, steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) are further measured. In Fig. 2c, the improved hole extraction from perovskite to BP:PTAA is evidenced by the smaller PL intensity and stronger PL quenching of the perovskite/BP:PTAA. Fig. 2d shows the corresponding TRPL spectra consisting a fast decay component  $ (\tau_{1}) $  and a slow decay component  $ (\tau_{2}) $  obtained by fitting the decay curves with a bi-exponential function:  $ Y = A_{1}\exp (-t / \tau_{1}) + A_{2}\exp (-t / \tau_{2}) $ , where  $ \tau_{1} $  is attributed to the quenching of photo-excited carriers at interface and  $ \tau_{2} $  originates from the trap-assisted recombination in the film.  $ A_{1} $  and  $ A_{2} $  are the relative amplitudes of the two processes. The fitted parameters are summarized in Table S1. The  $ \tau_{1} $  and  $ \tau_{2} $  of the perovskite/BP:PTAA are 48.46 and 7.73 ns, respectively, shorter than 66.99 and 11.29 ns of the perovskite/PTAA. The reduced carrier lifetimes demonstrate the more efficient charge transfer at perovskite/BP:PTAA interface, boosting the device performance of the PSCs.

We characterize the device performance using BP:PTAA as HTL by fabricating inverted planar PSCs with the configuration of ITO/BP:PTAA (PTAA)/MAPbI $ _3 $ /PCBM/BCP/Ag. The cross-sectional SEM image proves the compact and uniform perovskite films without cracks between the layers as illustrated in Fig. S6. Fig. 3a and Table S2 depict the J-V curves of the devices with the different BP concentrations measured under AM 1.5 G illumination at 100 mW cm $ ^{-2} $ . The optimal BP concentration producing the champion device is 0.4% w/w BP:PTAA. Fig. 3b presents the J-V curves of the champion devices with and without BP in both forward and reverse scan. The device with BP:PTAA features an enhanced PCE of 20.49% (19.68%) with a V $ _{oc} $  of 1.106 (1.105) V, a J $ _{sc} $  of 22.43 (22.43) mA/cm $ ^2 $ , and a FF of 0.826 (0.794) in reverse (forward) scan, which is remarkably superior to the PCE of 18.26% (17.16%) of the control device with a V $ _{oc} $  of 1.09 (1.082) V, a J $ _{sc} $  of 20.76 (20.72) mA/ cm $ ^2 $ , and a FF of 0.807 (0.765). The hysteresis index of the J-V curves largely decreases from 0.045 to 0.018 after introducing BP:PTAA. The enhancements of all photovoltaic parameters are expected to mainly originate from the improved charge transport at the perovskite/BP: PTAA interface and the formation of the high-quality perovskite film. The devices with BP:PTAA exhibits the excellent reproducibility as evident from the V $ _{oc} $ , J $ _{sc} $ , FF and PCE distributions obtained from 20 devices shown in Fig. 3b and Fig. S7. Moreover, the device performance using the BP:PTAA is comparable with the device with 2,3,5,6-Tetrafluoro-7,7,8,8-tetracyanoquinodimethane (F4TCNQ):PTAA (Fig. S8).

Fig. 3c shows the EQE spectra and integrated photocurrent of the devices. A higher photon-electron conversion efficiency in the EQE spectra over the whole absorption region of 300–800 nm wavelength is observed for the device with BP:PTAA, in line with the increased   $  J_{sc}  $  . The integrated photocurrent densities of the control device and the device with BP:PTAA are 19.72 and 21.29 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  , respectively, which are very close to the values of the measured J-V curves. The champion device with BP:PTAA produces a high steady-state photocurrent density of 20.98 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $   and a high stabilized efficiency of 20.15% at continuous operation at a maximum power point voltage of 0.96 V for 600s (Fig. 3d).

Fig. 4b displays the dark J-V curves of the hole-only device with the structure of ITO/BP:PTAA (PTAA)/MAPbI $ _3 $ /MoO $ _3 $ /Ag to evaluate the trap density and charge mobility by the space charge limited current (SCLC) method. We calculate the trap density according to the equation: N $ _{trap} $  = 2εrεqV $ _{TFL} $ /qL [2], where V $ _{TFL} $  is the trap-filled limit voltage, and L is the thickness of MAPbI $ _3 $  film. ε $ _0 $  and ε $ _r $  are the vacuum permittivity and relative permittivity of MAPbI $ _3 $ , respectively. The hole trap density is remarkably deduced from 1.29 × 10 $  ^{16}  $  for pristine PTAA to 9.6 × 10 $  ^{15}  $  cm $  ^{-3}  $  for BP:PTAA, confirming the formation of high-quality perovskite film grown on the BP:PTAA. The charge mobility (μ) is further estimated via the Mott-Gurney law: J = 9εrεqμV [2]/8 L $  ^{3}  $ . The calculated mobilities of the pristine PTAA and BP:PTAA are 5.57 × 10 $  ^{-4}  $  and 1.51 × 10 $  ^{-3}  $  cm $  ^{2}  $  V $  ^{-1}  $ s $  ^{-1}  $ , respectively. The improvement in charge mobility means the improved charge transport and the reduced charge recombination.

Fig. 4c shows the electrical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) of the full PSCs. The equivalent circuit consists sheet resistance  $ (\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{s}}) $ , charge transfer resistance  $ (\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{ct}}) $  and chemical capacitance  $ (C_{\mathrm{ct}}) $ . The  $ \mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{s}} $  is assumed to be the same because of the same device structure [42]. The  $ \mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{ct}} $  value decreases from  $ 1610\Omega $  of the control device to  $ 929\Omega $  of the device with BP:PTAA. The smaller  $ \mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{ct}} $  indicates the higher charge transfer ability for charge collection. The ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) is employed to investigate the surface electronic structures of the pristine and BP doped PTAA films. The work function (WF) is determined by the secondary electron cutoff in the left panel, and the position of the highest occupied molecular orbitals (HOMO) referenced to the Fermi level  $ (\mathrm{E_F}) $  is derived from the valence band region in the right panel as shown in Fig. 4d. After BP doping, the WF of the BP:PTAA film increases from 4.5 to  $ 4.65\mathrm{eV} $ . The  $ \mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{F}} $  is shifted by 70 meV towards the HOMO level indicating that the BP:PTAA film becomes more p-type. The more p-type with the higher hole concentration (2.54  $ \times 10^{18}\mathrm{cm}^{-3} $  for pristine PTAA,  $ 5.58\times 10^{18}\mathrm{cm}^{-3} $  for BP doped PTAA) has been demonstrated by Hall effect measurement (Table S5), which enables more efficient hole transfer between the perovskite and BP:PTAA layer. The ionization potential (IP) that refers to the energy difference between the vacuum level and the HOMO is the 5.13 and  $ 5.21\mathrm{eV} $  for the pristine and doped PTAA, respectively. It is expected that the difference of the IP values maybe result from the surface dipole caused by BP. Considering that the IP value of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  is  $ 5.6\mathrm{eV} $  [43], [-45] the increased IP of the BP:PTAA decreases the hole extraction barrier at perovskite/HTL interface, favoring the device performance.

To further understand the relationship between charge recombination and device performance, we plot light intensity (P) dependent J-V properties. As shown in Fig. 4e, the device with BP:PTAA has a smaller slope of   $  1.36 \, k_{B}T/q  $   for   $  V_{oc}  $   versus the logarithm of light intensity than   $  1.92 \, k_{B}T/q  $   of the control device, revealing that the defect-assisted recombination is effectively suppressed in the operational device due to the reduced defect states in the perovskite film. The dependency of the   $  J_{sc}  $   on the light intensity is expressed by   $  J_{sc} \propto P^{\alpha}  $   in Fig. S9. The exponent   $  \alpha  $   increases from 0.86 for the control device to 0.98 for the modified device, suggesting the reduced biomolecular recombination attributed to the improved charge transfer at perovskite/BP:PTAA interface.

As a comparison, the control device losses over  $ 40\% $  of its initial value at the same storage process. # 3. Conclusion

In summary, we have demonstrated that the BP:PTAA is an effective hole transport layer for the high-performance perovskite solar cells. The BP doping increases the conductivity and hydrophobicity of the PTAA film. The BP:PTAA layer significantly improves the charge extraction at perovskite interface and thus reduces the interfacial carrier recombination. The high-quality perovskite film with enlarged grain size is formed coated on BP:PTAA, leading to the suppressed trap-assisted recombination. As a result, the PCE of the device largely increases from 18.26% (the control device) to 20.45%, with a stabilized power output of 20.15% at continuous operation. The device stability is simultaneously enhanced attributed to the more hydrophobicity of BP:PTAA and the perovskite layer with less defect states.

# 4. Experimental section

# 4.1. Materials

Indium tin oxides (ITO, ≤10 Ω/□) substrates etched by laser were purchased from YouXuan Technology Ltd. Methylammonium iodide (MAI, 99.5%), Lead iodide (99.99%), Poly(triarylamine) (PTAA, 99%), [6,6]-phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM, 99.5%) and Bathocuproine (BCP, 99.9%) were obtained from Xi'an Polymer Light. Anhydrous dimethylformamide (DMF), Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), Chlorobenzene (CB) and isopropanol (IPA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The black phosphorus nanosheets dispersed in NMP (0.5 mg/mL, 1–10 layers) were purchased from Klamar reagent. All materials were used as received without further purification.

# 4.2. Device fabrication and characterization

The perovskite solar cells were all fabricated on ITO substrates. The ITO substrates were ultrasonically cleaned for  $ 20\mathrm{min} $  with detergent,

deionized water, acetone, ethanol, and IPA, sequentially, then were treated with UV-ozone irradiation for 20 min before spin coating. The control PTAA solution was prepared by dissolving 2 mg PTAA in 1 mL CB. The BP dispersion was added into the PTAA solution (weight ratios of BP to PTAA are from 0.1% to 0.8%), and vortex mixing for 5 min to make more uniform dispersion. The pristine or BP doped PTAA solution was spin coated on the ITO at 5000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 150 °C for 10 min. The perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 497.8 mg PbI  $  _{2}  $  and 159 mg MAI in the mixed solvent with 750 μL DMF and 85 μL DMSO. The perovskite film was spin coated on PTAA or BP doped PTAA at 4000 rpm for 30 s. During the spin coating process, 150 μL CB was dripped onto the substrates, then the film was immediately annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. The PCBM solution (20 mg/mL in CB) was spin coated on the perovskite layer at 1800 rpm for 40 s, subsequently, and the BCP solution (0.5 mg/mL in IPA) was spin coated at 6000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, 100 nm Ag electrode was deposited by thermal evaporation under a vacuum of  $  5 \times 10^{-4}  $  Pa with the resulting device active area of 0.05 cm  $  ^{-2}  $  (defined by a metal aperture). The J-V curves of the devices was measured by utilizing a Series Source meter (2400, Keithley instruments) under AM 1.5G solar simulator (Enlitech) calibrated using NREL standard Si cell. EQE measurement was performed by a system combined with a xenon lamp, monochromator, chopper lock-in amplifier and calibrated silicon photodetector. EIS plots were obtained from an electrochemical workstation (Autolab, PGSTAT302 N).

# 4.3. Film characterization

XRD patterns were recorded by a Rigaku Smart Lab (Cu Kα radiation). SEM and EDS images were measured by a Hitachi S-4800 field emission scanning electron microscope equipped with an energy dispersive spectrometer. TEM was performed by a FEI Titan Themis. UV–Vis spectra were measured with a Shimadzu 3600 spectrometer. The steady-state PL spectra were measured by Horiba Jobin-Yvon Ihr550. TR-PL spectra were obtained from a time-correlated single photon counting system (PicoHarp 300). UPS and XPS measurements were performed in an ultrahigh vacuum surface analysis system equipped with SCIENTA R3000 spectrometer with a base pressure of   $  10^{-10}  $   mbar. UPS employed the He I 21.22 eV as the excitation source with an energy resolution of 50 meV. XPS was measured using the monochromatic Al Kα 1486.6 eV. The Hall effect measurement was carried out at room temperature with a four-probe sample holder placed between the plates of the electromagnet by Nanometrics HL5500 Hall System. A magnetic field (B) (0–0.5 T) perpendicular to the sample surface and a direct current (2 mA) across the sample were applied to obtain Hall voltage (  $  V_{H}  $  ) signal.
