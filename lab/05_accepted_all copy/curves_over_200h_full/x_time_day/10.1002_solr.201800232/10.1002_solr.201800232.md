# High-Mobility Hydrophobic Conjugated Polymer as Effective Interlayer for Air-Stable Efficient Perovskite Solar Cells

Xiao-Xin Gao, Ding-Jiang Xue, Dong Gao, Qiwei Han, Qian-Qing Ge, Jing-Yuan Ma, Jie Ding, Weifeng Zhang, Bao Zhang, Yaqing Feng,* Gui Yu,* and Jin-Song Hu*

Hybrid organic–inorganic perovskite (HOIP) solar cells have achieved a certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 22.7%, which commonly use doped spiro-OMeTAD as hole transport materials (HTMs). However, the additives in spiro-OMeTAD can absorb moisture and cause the degradation of HOIP layers, leading to severe air-instability of devices. Herein, conjugated polymers of PD-10-DTTE-7 as a new effective interlayer between perovskite and doped spiro-OMeTAD to achieve air-stable efficient perovskite solar cells are reported. Its hydrophobic nature can effectively prevent the penetration of moisture and additives. Its superb hole mobility (9.54 cm $  ^{2}  $  V $  ^{-1}  $  s $  ^{-1}  $ , ≈10 $  ^{5}  $  times higher than spiro-OMeTAD) and suitable highest occupied molecular orbital level (-5.33 eV) are preferable to the hole injection and transport at the interface thus enhancing the device PCE. As a result, the MAPbI $  _{3}  $  solar cells with the PD-10-DTTE-7 interlayer achieve remarkable device air-stability and enhanced PCE, compared with the devices without the interlayer. These results provide a feasible approach to enhance solar cell stability and performance simultaneously.

Unfortunately, Owing to the  $ \mathfrak{sp}^3 $  hybridization of nitrogen atom in spiro-OMeTAD, the inherent triangular pyramid configuration results in large intermolecular distances so that they suffer from poor

conductivity (  $  \approx10^{-5}  $   S cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) arising from low hole mobility (  $  \approx10^{-4}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $   v  $  ^{-1}  $   s  $  ^{-1}  $  ) in the pristine state.  $  ^{[21-24]}  $   For that reason, to optimize the charge transport of the spiro-OMeTAD, it must be oxidized with chemical dopants/additives (e.g., lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)-imide (Li-TFSI) and 4-tertbutyl pyridine (tBP)) to improve the mobility and conductivity.  $  ^{[25-28]}  $   However, the use of additive in the spiro-OMeTAD can disadvantageously accelerate the degradation of perovskite layer while the device was being exposed in air due to the following reasons: i) Li-TFSI absorbed moisture in the air, then the H  $  _{2}  $  O directly degraded the perovskite.  $  ^{[29,30]}  $   ii) tBP slowly evaporated during storage, leading to lithium salt aggregation and moisture absorption; it also directly degrades the perovskite by forming a [PbI  $  _{2}  $   · tBP] coordinated complex.  $  ^{[28,31,32]}  $

To tackle this issue, several approaches have been developed. One method is to use the additive-free HTMs with moisture resistance to replace the spiro-OMeTAD, aiming to prevent moisture absorption from HTMs. For instance, Park reported a dopant-free polymeric hole transport material (random copolymer (RCP)) which resulted in higher efficiency (17.3%) for dopant-free HTMs in conventional planar PSCs and excellent stability.  $  ^{[33]}  $   Recently, they used a greensolvent-processable HTM (asy-PBTBDT) without dopants that exhibited long-term stability

Hybrid organic–inorganic lead halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted much attention due to the rapid development of their power conversion efficiency (PCE) from less than 3.8% to above 22% in the past few years.  $  ^{[1-19]}  $   In the architecture of perovskite solar cells, the hole transport materials (HTMs) are usually required to sufficiently separate/transport photo-generated charge carriers for high device PCEs.  $  ^{[20]}  $   Among various promising HTMs, triarylamine-based spiro-OMeTAD has been the most common HTMs in the PSCs, pushing the PCE to the record of 23.3% which has been certified by National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) in 2018 through various material and structure optimizations.

Zhao achieved uniform perovskite film based on novel polymer-scaffold architecture by adding PEG or PCBM/PEG to perovskite exhibiting PCE up to   $  \approx  $  16 and   $  \approx  $  17%, respectively. The unencapsulated devices exhibited strong humidity resistant and self-healing behavior, recovering rapidly after removing from water vapor.  $  ^{[35,36]}  $   Furthermore, they also insert a moisture-resistant interlayer (CuSCN) between perovskite absorber layer and doped spiro-OMeTAD to prevent the moisture penetration, which act as double-layer hole transport material into perovskite solar cells and significantly enhanced the long-term stability of the solar cells.  $  ^{[37]}  $   Thus, in order to achieve prominent PCE, choosing the high mobility/conductivity interlayer with moisture resistance is highly desired for high performance PSCs.

In this work, we demonstrate that using the conjugated polymers (PD-10-DTTE-7) with both the donor and acceptor alkylated as interlayer between  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  ( $ \mathrm{MA} = \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3 $ ) and doped spiro-OMeTAD can significantly enhance the stability and performance of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  solar cells. The PD-10-DTTE-7 exhibits the hole mobility as high as  $ 9.54~\mathrm{cm}^2\mathrm{V}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1} $  which is remarkably higher than that of doped Spiro-OMeTAD and other dopant-free HTMs. It is obvious that the HOMO level  $ (-5.33\mathrm{eV}) $  values of PD-10-DTTE-7 is between the analog values of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $   $ (-5.43\mathrm{eV}) $  and spiro-OMeTAD  $ (-5.22\mathrm{eV}) $ , leading to a good band alignment for holes injection at the interface. These excellent electronic properties of PD-10-DTTE-7 ensure the hole transport/injection in the interlayer. With tailored interface, the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  solar cells with PD-10-DTTE-7 interlayer exhibits the best PCE of  $ 18.83\% $ . In addition to device performance, the hydrophobic properties of surface and long-term stability of devices are also measured. Our results indicate that the PD-10-DTTE-7 layer can effectively protect the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ , thus significantly reducing the degradation of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  layer/device.

Figure 1a showed the chemical structure of poly [2,5-bis(2-decyltetradecyl)pyrrolo[3,4-c]pyrrole-1,4(2H,5H)-dione-alt-5,5'-di(thiophen-2-yl)-2,2'-(E)-1,2-Bis(3-heptylthieno[3,2-b]thiophen-2-yl)ethene] (PD-10-DTTE-7). This conjugated polymer consists of alkylated di (thienothienyl) ethylene (DTTE) units and diketopyrrolopyrole (DPP) moieties with alternating branched side chains. The nonbonding interactions between oxygen in DPP unit and the β-hydrogen of the adjacent thiophene could readily strengthen coplanarity of the polymer backbone, thus benefiting intermolecular π-π stacking. Introducing thieno [3,2-b] thiophene into the polymer main chains has also been theorized to increase the coplanarity and rigidity thus offering better π-orbital overlap, which could facilitate the intra- and intermolecular charge transport. Moreover, alkyl chains are substituted at the positions adjacent to the vinylene linkage, which could maintain the coplanarity of the backbone  $  ^{[38]}  $  as well

as the intermolecular packing between the polymer backbones. The hole drift mobility of PD-10-DTTE-7 was measured on organic field-effect transistor (OFET) substrates and can be found in our previous work.[39] The PD-10-DTTE-7 thin film exhibited a high hole mobility of up to  $ 9.54\mathrm{cm}^2\mathrm{V}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1} $ , which is three orders of magnitude higher than that of doped spiro-OMeTAD  $ (\mu \approx 10^{-3}\mathrm{cm}^2\mathrm{V}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1}) $  and has outclassed most of the known HTMs for PSCs. In addition, the high side chain density of PD-10-DTTE-7 leads to stronger hydrophobic effect around the backbone, which could readily strengthen the side chain-solvent interactions and protect the backbone from water and oxygen, thus affording the polymers with good solution processability, thermal stability, and air stability. # Experimental Section

Synthesis of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $  :   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $   (MAI) was firstly synthesized through the reaction of 24 mL   $  CH_{3}NH_{2}  $   (40% in methanol, Sigma–Aldrich) and 10 mL HI (57 wt% in water, Sigma–Aldrich) in a 100 mL round-bottom flask, and keep it in dark place at 0°C for 2 h with continuously stirring. The precipitate was collected using a rotary evaporator through carefully removing the solvents at 60°C for 1 h. The white product was formed, which was recrystallized with ethanol. After filtration, the precipitate was washed with diethyl ether for three times at least and finally collected and dried at 60°C in a vacuum oven for 24 h.

Fabrication of Perovskite Solar Cell: All starting materials were used directly without further purification. Fluorine-doped tin oxide coated glass substrates (FTO, Pilkington, TEC8) were patterned by laser cutting and cleaned with detergent, deionized water, acetone, and ethanol in the ultrasonic bath for 30 min, respectively. The FTO substrates were then treated with an UV/Ozone cleaner for 15 min before being used. The TiO₂ compact layers (c-TiO₂) were deposited onto the FTO by spray pyrolysis using a titanium diisopropoxide bis-(acetylacetonate) solution (75% in 2-propanol, Sigma-Aldrich), which was diluted in ethanol (99.8%, Sigma-Aldrich) at 1:39 volume ratio on 450 °C and followed by in situ annealing for 30 min. As for the mesoporous TiO₂ (meso-TiO₂) solution, which was composed of 2 g TiO₂ paste (OPV-TP20, OPV) diluted in 10 mL anhydrous ethanol solution, then the meso-TiO₂ layer was deposited onto the c-TiO₂/FTO substrate by two-step spin-coating process at 500 rpm and 4500 rpm for 5 and 30 s, respectively. After annealing at 125 °C for 30 min, the as-deposited TiO₂ films were gradually heated to 525 °C in air, and then they were baked at the same temperature for 30 min to remove organic components. Before using, the FTO/c-TiO₂/meso-TiO₂ substrates were treated with UV/Ozone for 10 min. The PbI₂ solution was prepared by dissolving 708 mg PbI₂ into the mixture of 1 mL DMF and 0.167 mL DMSO under vigorous stirring for 3 h at room temperature, after that, it was spin-coated on the top of FTO/c-TiO₂/meso-TiO₂ substrate at 3000 r.p.m. for 30 s. Then the CH₃NH₃I solution (50 mg mL⁻¹ in isopropyl alcohol) was dropped on the wet PbI₂ layer and awaited it for ≈30 s before another spinning at 3000 r.p.m. for 30 s. Afterwards, the as-prepared films were annealed on the hotplate at 150 °C for 20 min. The PD-10-DTTE-7 was dissolved in the solvent (3 mg mL⁻¹ in 1-Chloronaphthalene), before deposition of the polymers, the solvent was heated and refluxed overnight for complete dissolution of the polymers. As for the PD-10-DTTE-7-based devices, rather than being cooled to room temperature, 60 μL PD-10-DTTE-7 solution was dropped on the perovskite layer promptly and spin-coated at 3000 r.p.m. for 30 s, then, the films were annealed on the

hotplate at 140°C for 3 min, which was used for the interlayer for the devices. As shown in Figure S4, Supporting Information, the annealing temperature was determined by our OFET test. After cooling to room temperature, 60 μL spiro-OMeTAD solution was deposited by spin-coating on the perovskite layer as the hole transport layer. The spiro-OMeTAD solution was prepared by mixing 72.3 mg spiro-OMeTAD (99.1%, Luminescence Technology Corp.) with 1 mL chlorobenzene. After 25-min stirring at room temperature, 28.8 μL 4-tert-butylpyridine (96%, Sigma-Aldrich), 17.5 μL stock solution of 520 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  lithium bis(trifluoromethylsulphonyl) imide (99.95%, Sigma-Aldrich) in acetonitrile (99.8%, Sigma-Aldrich) were added and stirred the solution continuously for 2.5 h. Finally, 80 nm of Au was thermally evaporated as a top electrode to finish the device fabrication.

Device Characterization: The morphology and particle size of obtained samples were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi SU-8020) operating at 10 kV. X-ray diffraction (XRD) experiments were performed on a Rigaku D/max-2500 diffractometer (Rigaku Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) using Cu Ka radiation (  $  \lambda=1.54056\AA  $  ) by measuring the diffraction angle (2  $  \theta  $  ) between 10° and 70° with a scanning rate of 2° min  $  ^{-1}  $   and a step size of 0.02°. Atomic-force microscopy (AFM) experiments were carried out in a glovebox at an argon atmosphere at room temperature and pressure (Multimode 8, Bruker). The steady-state PL and time-resolved PL spectra were measured by an Edinburgh Instrument FLS 980 spectrophotometer at room temperature. For PL measurements, the perovskite thin films were deposited with the exactly same procedures using ultra-clean quartz glass as substrates. TRPL measurements were conducted by a time-correlated single photon counting method. Samples were pulsed by using the supercontinuum laser source with an excitation wavelength of 485 nm. The lifetime was obtained by fitting the TRPL results with a bi-exponential decay function:

$$
R (t) = B _ {1} \exp \left(- \frac {t}{\tau_ {1}}\right) + B _ {2} \exp \left(- \frac {t}{\tau_ {1}}\right)
$$

Contact angles were captured by OCA20 instrument (Dataphysics, Germany) at 25°C. Electron impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was carried out on an Autolab PGSTAT302N (Metrohm, Netherlands) electrochemical workstation in the frequency ranging from 100 mHz to 1 MHz at 10 mV AC amplitude under the LED simulated light. Current–voltage characteristics were measured with a solar simulator (Newport, USA) equipped with 450 W Xenon lamp (OSRAM) and a Keithley 2420 source meter at room temperature. The J–V curves were measured under AM 1.5 G by reverse (forward bias (1.2 V) → short circuit (0 V)) scan. The step voltage and the delay time were fixed at 20 mV and 50 ms, respectively. Light intensity was adjusted using a NREL-certified Si solar cell with a KG  $  ^{-2}  $  filter for approximate AM 1.5G light (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ). The active areas of solar cells were defined by a 0.09 cm  $  ^{2}  $  non-reflective home-made black metal mask. The mask was measured using a microscope (ECLIPSE LV150N, Nicon). The evaluation of the polymer FET devices was accomplished using a Keithley 4200 SCS semiconductor parameter analyzer on a probe stage.
