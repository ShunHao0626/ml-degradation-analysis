# Additive Enhanced Crystallization of Solution-Process for Planar Perovskite Solar Cells with Efficiency Exceeding 19%

By Shuai You,  $  ^{[a]}  $   Shiqing Bi,  $  ^{[b]}  $   Jing Huang,  $  ^{[a]}  $   Qiushuang Jia,  $  ^{[a]}  $   Yuhe Yuan,  $  ^{[a]}  $   Yanjie Xia,  $  ^{[a]}  $   Zhenyu Xiao,  $  ^{[a]}  $   Zhuzhu Sun,  $  ^{[a]}  $   Jiangfeng Liu,  $  ^{[a]}  $   Shujie Sun  $  ^{[a]}  $   and Zhiqiang Zhao  $  ^{*[a]}  $

Shuai You and Shiqing Bi contributed equally to this work.

Abstract: To reduce intrinsic defect density in perovskite films, various additives are often added into the precursor solution. In this paper, a novel solution approach where isopropanol (IPA) was added into the precursor solution was developed for the preparation of normal planar perovskite solar cells (PSCs). A champion power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 19.70\% $  was achieved with this approach. By adding IPA into the precursor solution, CH3NH3PbI3 perovskite showed better crystallization and stability, indicating improved film quality. Because of the improved morphology of perovskite crystal, the PCE of PSCs increased. In addition, the unencapsulated PSCs retained high output for up to 40 days in air at room temperature, indicating the stability of PSCs. These results might provide a new avenue for manufacturing high efficiency and high stability PSCs.

Hybrid organic/inorganic organometal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been attracting enormous research interest. This is due to their simple fabrication, tunable bandgaps, large absorption coefficients, high carrier mobility, especially long charge carrier diffusion lengths and high power conversion efficiency (PCE).  $  ^{[1]}  $   The rapid progress in materials engineering and device optimization have boosted the PCE of PSCs, which now exceeds 20%.  $  ^{[2]}  $   A highly crystallized perovskite thin film without defect may be the main segment that allows PSCs to have favorable photovoltaic performance. However, the production of such thin film requires a careful control of the process.  $  ^{[3]}  $   The perovskite layers with a well-defined grain structure, full surface coverage and small surface roughness are believed to be an essential prerequisite for achieving high PCE.

Focusing on the composition of perovskite film, various strategies have been developed to enhance the nucleation and uniform growth of crystalline layers. These strategies include two-step sequential deposition,  $  ^{[4]}  $  a vacuum flash-assisted perovskite formation,  $  ^{[2a]}  $  solvent engineering,  $  ^{[5]}  $  additive-assisted deposition etc.  $  ^{[6]}  $  Among them, solution coating is one of the most widely used methods. The reason is that it uses inexpensive deposition equipment and allows flexible control of deposition condition as well as the composition of perovskite materials.  $  ^{[7]}  $  However, the fabrication of perovskite thin films is

greatly influenced by the composition of precursor solution. Moreover, perovskite thin films produced by the traditional solution method often have dendritic structure and large uncovered areas, which will directly influence their final photovoltaic performance.

To overcome this problem, a modified solution method, where various additives are added into the precursor solution, has been developed to fabricate perovskite layers with a uniform, continuous and compact morphology. The frequently used additives are chloride additives, hypophosphorous acid (HPA), lithium chloride (LiCl) and 1,8-diiodooctane (DIO)  $  ^{[3b, 8]}  $  . However, colloidal intermediates composed of polyiodide complexes are found to exist in the precursor solutions and they can interfere with the crystallization kinetics of perovskite films and thus influence their morphology. These polyiodide-based colloids could easily induce defects due to a labile Pb/I ratio. Moreover, accidental   $  I_{2}  $   is generated during the process due to the oxidation of iodine ions. This will affect defect density and thus device performance. Clearly, it is one of the challenges for the researchers to reduce the intrinsic defect density in hybrid perovskite films during crystallization process.

Recently, a study about two-step sequential deposition technique indicated that the introduction of a small amount of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) into dimethylformamide (DMF) can improve the quality of   $  PbI_{2}  $   and perovskite films can have better morphology by dipping in isopropanol (IPA) solution.  $  ^{[7,9]}  $   In fact, IPA is the most commonly used solvent for methylammonium halide and even reduced iodine to I' in a heating progress.  $  ^{[11]}  $

In this paper, a simple and effective solution coating process was developed for the preparation of planar PSCs. IPA was added into a mixed solvent of DMF/DMSO containing   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I/PbI_{2}  $  , which was as the precursor solution for perovskite films. Results indicated that the obtained   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   perovskite films had low defect density and high uniformity and

Figure 3a and b shows the scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of surface morphology of perovskite films prepared without and with IPA additives. To the sample without IPA additive, the grain size varied a lot and there were obvious pin holes and grain boundaries (Figure 3a). These defects could introduce strong energetic disorder, which impeded charge transport, induced recombination and thus led to poor photovoltaic performance.[13] Note that the film prepared with IPA (Figure 3b) presented larger grain size, fewer lateral grain boundaries and flatten surface, than the W/O IPA film. This implied that the charge carriers can efficiently transport across the perovskite film and reach the corresponding electrode interfaces before recombination.[7,14] These results indicated that adding IPA in the perovskite precursor solution can improve both the film uniformity and the perovskite crystallinity, which were the two key requirements for high-performance PSCs.

Fitting the data with bi-exponential decay model could yield two time constants: faster decay (  $  \tau_{1}  $  ) and slower decay (  $  \tau_{2}  $  ).  $  ^{[18]}  $     $  \tau_{1}  $   came from the bulk recombination in perovskite bulk films at a relatively short time scale (several tens of nanoseconds), and   $  \tau_{2}  $   came from the delayed recombination of trapped charges at a relatively long time scale (hundreds of nanoseconds). For the perovskite film without IPA additive,   $  \tau_{1}  $   was 3.37 ns and the   $  \tau_{2}  $   was 131.17 ns. This suggested that the depopulation of photogenerated charges was dominated by charge collection through the   $  TiO_{2}/Perovskite  $   interface, accompanied with weak delayed radiative recombination. For the perovskite film with IPA additive,   $  \tau_{1}  $   and   $  \tau_{2}  $   were shortened to 3.36 ns and 63.36 ns, respectively. This was not only because IPA induced fast electron transfer from perovskite film but also because high quality perovskite films reduced the interface trap state density.  $  ^{[18b, 19]}  $   At the same time, to investigate radioactive and nonradioactive recombination channels within perovskite films, we carried out steady-state photoluminescence and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements of glass/perovskite (Figure S2, Supporting Information). However, in the forward bias region of 0.95–1.5 V, the injected current density of PSC with IPA was much higher than that of PSC without IPA, suggesting a smaller injection barrier between Perovskite and the TiO  $  _{2}  $  /FTO cathode of PSC with IPA and consequently the improved electron transport. In conclusion, IPA was used as an additive for the preparation of perovskite films by a modified solution process. Adding a proper amount of IPA in the perovskite precursor solution could not only improve the film morphology and crystallinity but also modify the optical and electrical properties of perovskite photoactive layer. Charge carrier extraction at the interface between perovskite layer and charge collection layer was also enhanced. Moreover, A champion PCE of 19.70% was achieved for the planar PSCs fabricated with IPA additive, which is much higher than that of reference devices based on films without IPA. The optimized device also showed nearly negligible J-V hysteresis. More importantly, the high quality perovskite films allowed PSCs to have significantly improved stability against moisture. These results might provide a new avenue for fabricating efficient and low-cost PSCs.

# Experimental section

# Materials

Isopropanol (IPA), dimethylformamide (DMF), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), chlorobenzene (CB), acetonitrile and   $  PbI_{2}  $   were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification.   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}I  $   was purchased from Dyesol and used as received. Spiro-OMeTAD (2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-di-p-methoxyphenylamino)-9,9'-spirobifluorene) was purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corporation (Xi'an, China).

# Device fabrication

For solar cells, the precursor solutions for perovskites were prepared by dissolving the mixture of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{I} $  (1:1 in molar ratio) in a mixed solvent of DMF and DMSO(4:1 in volume ratio) with addition of IPA. FTO substrates were cleaned by soap-scrubbing and sonication sequentially in deionized water, acetone and isopropanol. Then, they were dried overnight in a vacuum drying box before use and subjected to an  $ \mathrm{O}_3/ $  ultraviolet treatment for  $ 30\mathrm{min} $ .

The perovskite precursor solution was then spin-coated onto FTO/TiO $ _2 $  substrate at 1000 rpm for 10 s and 5000 rpm for 40 s. During the second spin-coating step, the substrate was treated with CB drop-casting. Then the films were annealed at 110 °C for 5 min. The schematic illustration of deposition process was presented in Scheme 1. The thickness of CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ PbI $ _3 $  layer was about 320 nm, determined by a profilometer (KLA-Tencor D-120). # Characterization

The morphologies of perovskite films were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, S4800, Hitachi, Japan). The X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra of the prepared films were obtained using a D8/Advance (Bruker) equipped with a push plug gobel mirror and a monochromatic CuKα (  $  \lambda = 1.5405 \AA  $  ) radiation source. The reflection and transmission spectra were obtained with a UV–vis spectrometer (UV-5800 (PC) UV–vis Spectrophotometer (Metash Instruments Co., Ltd)) in the 200–850 nm wavelength range at room temperature. The excitation and emission spectra were obtained with a HORIBA Fluorolog-III spectrofluoroemometer (Lake Shore Cryotronics as the cryogenic refrigeration equipment). Luminescent lifetime was determined on a HORIBA Jobin-Yvon Inc Fluorolog-III and a 467-nm diode laser was used as excitation source. The fluorescence decay was acquired with a preset peak of 3000 counts. The decay data was analyzed using Data Analysis Software. The incident photon-to-electron conversion efficiency (IPCE or EQE) was measured by a Newport external quantum measurement system. Current-density versus voltage (J-V) curves of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   solar cells were obtained using a solar simulator with a source meter (Keithley 2400) at   $  100 mW\cdot cm^{-2}  $  , AM 1.5 G illumination. The J-V curves were scanned by reverse (from 1.2 V to 0 V) and forward (from 0 V to 1.2 V) scans. The step voltage was fixed at 10 mV and the dwell time at 100 ms for each step, which is a set delay at each voltage step before measuring each current. Impedance spectroscopy of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   solar cells was measured by a potentiostat under nitrogen environment and the data were analyzed using the ZView 2 program.
