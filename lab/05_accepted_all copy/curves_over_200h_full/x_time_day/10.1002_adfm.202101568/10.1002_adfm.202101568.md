# In-Situ Hot Oxygen Cleansing and Passivation for All-Inorganic Perovskite Solar Cells Deposited in Ambient to Breakthrough 19% Efficiency

Kang Wang, Changji Gao, Zhuo Xu, Qingwen Tian,* Xiaojing Gu, Lu Zhang, Shiang Zhang, Kui Zhao,* and Shengzhong (Frank) Liu*

All-inorganic perovskite   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   has attracted extensive attention recently because of its excellent thermal and chemical stability. However, its photovoltaic performance is hindered by large energy losses (  $  E_{loss}  $  ) due to the presence of point defects. In addition, hydroiodic acid (HI) is currently employed as a hydrolysis-derived precursor of intermediate compounds, which often leads to a small amount of organic residue, thus undermining its chemical stability. Herein, an in-situ hot oxygen cleansing with superior passivation (HOCP) for the triple halide-mixed   $  CsPb(I_{2.85}Br_{0.149}Cl_{0.001})  $   perovskite solar cells (abbreviated as   $  CsPbTh_{3}  $  ) deposited in an ambient atmosphere to reduce the   $  E_{loss}  $   to as low as 0.48 eV for the power conversion efficiency (PCE) to reach 19.65% is demonstrated. It is found that the hot oxygen treatment effectively removes the organic residues. Meanwhile, it passivates halide vacancies, hence reduces the trap states and nonradiative recombination losses within the perovskite layer. As a result, the PCE is increased significantly from 17.15% to 19.65% under 1 sun illumination with an open-circuit voltage enlarged to 1.23 from 1.14 V, which corresponds to an   $  E_{loss}  $   reduction from 0.57 to 0.48 eV. Also, the HOCP-treated devices exhibit better long-term stability. This insight should pave a way for decreasing nonradiative charge recombination losses for high-performance inorganic perovskite photoelectronics.

# 1. Introduction

To meet the growing demand for energy, energy harvesting through solar cells has been recognized as a next-generation clean sustainable energy contributor.  $  ^{[1]}  $   For decades, researchers have developed various thin-film solar cells with alternative light-harvesting materials including inorganic, organic, and perovskite semiconductors.  $  ^{[2-9]}  $   Among thin-film photovoltaic technologies, all-inorganic cesium lead halide perovskite (CsPbI  $  _{3}  $  ) has been attracting ever-increasing attention in the last few years due to its excellent thermal stability and chemical resistance.  $  ^{[10-12]}  $   For instance, the CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite exhibits a suitable optical band gap (  $  E_{g}  $  : ≈1.7 eV) and high absorbance. In addition, it exhibits high temperature tolerance up to 400 °C, which is significantly improved compared to those organic–inorganic hybrid perovskites composed of volatile organic components that rapidly degrade over 150 °C.  $  ^{[13-15]}  $   However, the

CsPbI $ _3 $ -based solar cells suffer from a large  $ E_{\text{loss}} $  (described by the equation:  $ E_{\text{loss}} = E_{\text{g}} - \text{q} V_{\text{OC}} $ ), $ ^{[16-19]} $  which leads to their device performance remaining inferior to that of the hybrid perovskites. $ ^{[20-23]} $

According to the statistical results of Wang et al.,  $  ^{[24]}  $  the  $  E_{loss}  $  of most  $  CsPbX_{3}  $  (X=Br, I) solar cells varies from 0.60 to 1.00 eV, much higher than that of the hybrid ones (0.40–0.50 eV).  $  ^{[25-27]}  $  In general, under the Shockley–Queisser (S–Q) limit, a low  $  E_{loss}  $  for attractive photovoltaic materials is equivalent in importance to an appropriate  $  E_{g}  $ .  $  ^{[28-30]}  $  Much of this large  $  E_{loss}  $  has been attributed largely to the presence of point defects (  $  V_{I}  $ ,  $  V_{Pb}  $ ,  $  Pb_{I}  $ , and  $  I_{Pb}  $ ) at the interfaces and in the bulk of the  $  CsPbI_{3}  $  films, which cause some undesirable shallow or deep trap states, leading to local fluctuations of the bandgap and/or the band edges due to locally varying electrostatic potential.  $  ^{[31-33]}  $  To address this issue, some organic molecules such as PEAI,  $  ^{[34]}  $  ADMA,  $  ^{[35]}  $  ABA,  $  ^{[36]}  $  PVP,  $  ^{[37]}  $  and sulfobetaine zwitterion  $  ^{[38]}  $  with functional groups were introduced in  $  CsPbI_{3}  $  film for defect passivation, all of which play a positive role in passivating those point defects and reducing  $  E_{loss}  $ .

In addition, the introduction of hydrolysis-derived intermediate compounds   $  [DMAPbI_{3}, DMA = (CH_{3})_{2}NH_{2}^{+}]  $   has become an increasingly popular method for the preparation of high quality and stable   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   perovskite solar cells (PSCs). However, the incorporation of the organic   $  DMA^{+}  $  A-site cations appear to be another important factor that deteriorates the chemical stability.  $  ^{[39]}  $   Recently, we designed a beach-chair-shaped band structure (CSB) to steer an optimal energy-band alignment and heterojunction interface passivation in   $  \beta-CsPbI_{3}  $   PSCs, the champion solar cell efficiency is increased to 17.12%, with an   $  E_{loss}  $   of 0.55 eV.  $  ^{[40]}  $   More recently, we successfully demonstrated ambient fabrication of high-performance inorganic PSCs via blade-coating.  $  ^{[41]}  $   Based on our preliminary annealing process, it appears that there are still organic residues in the final films produced. When the fabricated devices are stored under ambient conditions, it is believed that the reaction of superoxide with protonated organic cation such as   $  DMA^{+}  $  can seriously undermine the chemical stability.  $  ^{[42]}  $   Therefore, both the removal of organic residues and the passivation of point defects in the process of preparing all-inorganic perovskite films are very important for the improvement of device performance.

In this work, we attempt to specifically further address the  $  E_{\mathrm{loss}}  $  issue and stability in the  $  \mathrm{CsPbI_3}  $  film via an in-situ hot oxygen cleansing and passivation (HOCP) method to synergistically decrease  $  E_{\mathrm{loss}}  $  and improve stability for the  $  \mathrm{CsPb(I_{2.85}Br_{0.149}Cl_{0.001})}  $  (abbreviated as  $  \mathrm{CsPbTh_3}  $ )-based solar cells. Continuous oxygen flow was introduced during thermal annealing of the dry ambient-fabricated perovskite films. It is found that this HOCP treatment can effectively remove the organic residues in the resultant films. Halide alloying via introducing a small amount of Br and Cl was further demonstrated for improving the crystallinity of  $  \mathrm{CsPbI_3}  $  film. The oxygen state within the perovskite films and its influence on crystal structure, optical properties and optoelectronic properties were investigated in detail by performing density functional theory (DFT) calculation and experimental analyses. Based on the above optimization measures, we showed high-performance solar cells with power conversion efficiency (PCE) as high as 19.65%, which resulted from an extremely low  $  E_{\mathrm{loss}}  $  of 0.48 eV.

# 2. Results and Discussion

# 2.1. Effects of Halide Alloying

We began evaluation of the effects of halide alloying in the mixed-halide perovskites. Thin films of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   and alloyed mixed-halide perovskites   $  CsPb(I_{2.85}Br_{0.15})  $   and   $  [CsPb(I_{2.85}Br_{0.149}Cl_{0.001})  $  , abbreviated as   $  CsPbTh_{3}]  $   were fabricated in inert atmosphere using one-step spin-coating. It was anticipated from the DFT analysis that partial substitution of I by Br or Cl in   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film is beneficial for a deeper valence band (VB) because Cl and Br have much higher electronegativity and ionization potential than I. This was expected to help holes easily spread out away from their preferred spatial confinement around   $  V_{Pb}  $   and provide a significant increase in charge transport properties.  $  ^{[43,44]}  $   We therefore sought to identify the effects of halide engineering experimentally, as shown below.

Figure 1a shows X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns for the  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_3 $ ,  $ \mathrm{CsPb(I_{2.85}Br_{0.15})} $ , and  $ \mathrm{CsPbTh}_3 $  perovskite films. All three films exhibited the tetragonal  $ \beta $ -CsPbI $ _3 $  phase, which is consistent with previous reports and means there is negligible influence of Br and Cl alloying on the crystal packing behavior.[41,45,46] Lattice shrinkage can be observed after Br incorporation, as evidenced by a shift of the diffraction peaks to higher values (Figure S1, Supporting Information), which, however, is negligible when further alloying a tiny amount of Cl. Note that the Br and Cl alloying led to stronger diffraction intensity of the  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_3 $  film, suggesting a higher crystallinity. Top-view scanning electronic microscopy (SEM) images showed a gradual increase in the average grain size from 275 to  $ 515~\mathrm{nm} $  with the Br and Cl alloying (Figure 1b). The enlarged grain size is suspected to benefit from decreased nucleus density but promoted grain growth, which is confirmed from optical observations that the Br and Cl alloying significantly prolonged crystallization of the perovskite layer during thermal annealing (Figure S2, Supporting Information).[47] To ascertain whether chlorine can be detected in the final  $ \mathrm{CsPb(I_{2.85}Br_{0.149}Cl_{0.001})} $  films, we performed X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) measurements, as shown in Figure S3, Supporting Information. From the XPS and SIMS results, it is obvious that the amount of chlorine is too tiny to be detected by these conventional characterization methods.

In addition, we observed distinct photophysics when alloying with Br and Cl. The optical absorption spectra exhibited an approximately 7-nm blue-shift of the band edge from 732 to 725 nm for the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film after alloying with Br and Cl (Figure 1c), with bandgaps increasing from 1.69 to 1.71 eV. The halide-induced shift of exciton emissions was also observed in the photoluminescence (PL) spectra (Figure 1d). Moreover, time-resolved PL (TRPL) spectroscopy was carried out to evaluate the dynamics of charge carrier recombination in three films. The carrier lifetime increased from 8.63 to 13.06 and 16.64 ns for the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film when sequentially alloying with Br and Cl, indicating the least nonradiative recombination loss for the   $  CsPbTh_{3}  $   film (Figure 1e and Table S1, Supporting Information).

# 2.2. In-Situ Hot Oxygen Cleansing and Trap Passivation

Interestingly, we found that devices prepared in dry ambient air (RH: 10–20%) exhibited higher PCE than those prepared in inert atmosphere (17.16% versus 17.96%), especially in   $  E_{loss}  $   (0.56 eV versus 0.53 eV) (Figure S4, Supporting Information). This hints at a role of oxygen in photovoltaic outcome when fabricating perovskite films under ambient conditions. Therefore, we enlarged oxygen involvement via introducing continuous oxygen flow treatment during thermal annealing of the ambient-fabricated films (denoted as HOCP-CsPbTh  $  _{3}  $  , see Figure S5, Supporting Information, and the Experimental Section for more details) to better understand the underlying mechanisms. It is found that this HOCP treatment is effective in the humidity range of 10–20%, and would not work under higher humidity conditions (e.g., above 30%).

XPS measurements were further conducted to probe the presence of organic residue within the perovskite films. The organic residues were eliminated through this straightforward HOCP process, especially in the bulk of films, as confirmed by the XPS analyses (Figure 2a–d). A noticeable N1s peak located at 402.2 eV and another obvious C 1s peak at 285.9 eV were observed in the pristine film before and after etching, which could be attributed to the C–N bond, indicating that there is still some organic DMA  $  ^{+}  $  residues  $  [DMA = (CH_{3})_{2}NH_{2}^{+}]  $  both in the surface and bulk of films.  $  ^{[48-50]}  $  Surprisingly, after this HOCP treatment, both the N1s and C1s peaks resulted from the C–N bond disappeared, suggesting that the undesirable organic DMA  $  ^{+}  $  residue was eliminated. The C 1s peak located at 284.8 eV assigned to C–C bond was observed, which may be due to the inevitable carbon adsorption and unintentional contamination during the sample transfer and testing. Thus, with proper annealing and preparation atmosphere, the organic residues, especially in the bulk of film, can be effectively removed.

To further confirm whether there were organic residues or not in the HOCP-treated perovskite thin film, we conducted the   $  {}^{1}  $  H nuclear magnetic resonance (  $  {}^{1}  $  H-NMR) and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) measurements of the two types of films, as shown in Figure 2e,f. The   $  {}^{1}  $  H NMR spectrum of the in untreated sample shows the organic DMA  $  ^{+}  $  signals at   $  \delta = 8.11  $   ppm and   $  \delta = 2.52  $   ppm, corresponding to protons in   $  -NH_{2}^{+}-  $  and   $  -CH_{3}  $ , respectively. After HOCP treatment, the DMA  $  ^{+}  $  signal becomes so weak that it is difficult to distinguish it in the HOCP sample. The   $  {}^{1}  $  H NMR results showed that there were organic residues in the untreated film, and these organic residues disappeared after HOCP treatment.

XPS measurements were also conducted to probe the oxygen state within the perovskite films. The two films exhibited an O 1s peak at 532.5 eV corresponding to oxygen molecules on the perovskite film surfaces (Figure 3a). It is important to note that the HOCP treatment led to an appearance of the O 1s peak at 529.4 eV, which corresponds to atomic oxygen. This observation is consistent with previous reports and suggests the formation of a metal-oxide bond such as Pb-O after HOCP treatment.  $  ^{[51,52]}  $   It is worth noting that the lattice oxygen peak was absent after etching by a gas cluster ion beam for 60 s for the perovskite film without HOCP treatment, while it remained for the HOCP-film (Figure 3b). These distinct observations are ascribed to the fact that the metal-oxygen bond now existed both on the surface and in the bulk of the perovskite film after HOCP treatment. The formation of the metal-oxygen bond suggests a decreased concentration of halide vacancies, which is beneficial for suppressing defects and decreasing charge recombination. For the HOCP-treated film, the formation of the metal-oxygen bond is also confirmed by the shift of Pb   $  4f_{5/2}  $   and Pb   $  4f_{7/2}  $   peaks to lower binding energies for about 0.21 eV (see Figure 3c and Figure S6, Supporting Information). The result suggests a decreased concentration of halide vacancies, which is beneficial for suppressing defects and decreasing charge recombination.

The spatial distributions of oxygen within the perovskite films were also investigated using secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) analysis. Figure 3d shows the depth profiles of the oxygen species throughout the perovskite films without and with HOCP treatment on the FTO/glass substrate. We observed uniform distribution of oxygen throughout the entire perovskite films for both cases. Clearly, the HOCP treatment resulted in a stronger oxygen signal, possibly suggesting higher oxygen concentration in the perovskite layer.

The influences of the HOCP treatment on the optical and optoelectronic properties of the perovskite films were further examined. The HOCP treatment did not change the absorption intensity or band edge (  $  E_{g} \approx 1.71  $   eV) (Figure S7, Supporting Information), but significantly promoted the steady-state photoluminescence (PL) intensity (Figure S8, Supporting Information). This observation suggests decreased nonradiative recombination for the perovskite films.  $  ^{[53]}  $

The uniformity remained unchanged after HOCP treatment, but there was a longer decay time. # 2.3. Electronic Properties

# 3. Conclusions

In summary, we demonstrate an in-situ HOCP for   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   films during dry ambient fabrication of solar cells with   $  E_{loss}  $   as low as 0.48 eV and PCE approaching 19.65%. The results demonstrate that the organic residues can be removed by this straightforward technique. Halide alloying was first demonstrated via introducing a small amount of Br and Cl to improve the crystallinity of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film. The comprehensive experimental and theoretical results further revealed that the density of halide vacancies can be effectively decreased by single or double dissociated O atoms after in-situ passivation by oxygen during film fabrication, which resulted in decreased nonradiative charge recombination losses. As a result, the mixed-halide   $  CsPbTh_{3}  $  -based solar cells achieved an extremely low   $  E_{loss}  $   of only 0.48 eV and a high   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.23 V. The in-situ HOCP enabled us to demonstrate   $  CsPbTh_{3}  $  -based solar cells with a PCE up to 19.65% and good long-term stability. We anticipate the in-situ cleansing and passivation via this simple and promising strategy would help decrease energy losses for future high-performance perovskite optoelectronics.

Materials: Cesium iodide (CsI, 99.999%), lead bromide (PbBr₂, ≥98%), lead chloride (PbCl₂), and 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-di-p-

N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, ≥ 99.9%), Li-bis-(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (Li-TFSI), and 4-tert-butylpyridine (TBP, 96%) were purchased from Alfa Aesar. All chemicals were used as received.

Synthesis of DMAPbI $ _3 $ : DMAPbI $ _3 $  [DMA = (CH $ _3 $ ) $ _2 $ NH $ _2^+ $ ] was synthesized using the methods described by the authors' previous works. $ ^{[19,34,40]} $

Synthesis of the Perovskite Solution: The mole concentration of all perovskite solutions was kept at 0.6 M. The single-halide   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving stoichiometric   $  CsI  $ ,   $  DMAPbI_{3}  $   with 1:1 molar ratio. The double-halide perovskite   $  CsPbI_{2.85}Br_{0.15}  $   was formulated by mixing   $  CsI  $ ,   $  DMAPbI_{3}  $ , and   $  PbBr_{2}  $   with 3:2.85:0.15 molar ratio. The triple-halide perovskite   $  CsPbI_{2.85}Br_{0.149}Cl_{0.001}  $   (denoted as   $  CsPbTh_{3}  $ ) was fabricated via mixing   $  CsI  $ ,   $  DMAPbI_{3}  $ ,   $  PbBr_{2}  $ , and   $  PbCl_{2}  $   with 3:2.85:0.149:0.001 molar ratio. Solvent mixture concentration of DMF:DMSO is 9:1 (v/v). Notably,   $  DMAPbI_{3}  $ ,   $  PbBr_{2}  $ , and   $  PbCl_{2}  $   were first dissolved in solvent to ensure good solubility, followed by CsI precursor added in the mixed solution. All three types of perovskite precursor solutions were stirred at room temperature for 2 h and filtered through 0.2 μm syringe filter.

Device Fabrication: Pre-cleaned FTO glasses were treated with UV for 10 min. The 50 nm thick compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   film was fabricated on FTO glass via a common sol–gel approach. The inorganic single-halide, double-halide, and triple-halide perovskite precursor solutions were prepared by one-step spin coating at 1000 rpm for 10 s and 4000 rpm for 40 s, followed by annealing at 210 °C for 5 min. For HOCP-  $  CsPbTh_{3}  $   film, a continuous flow of hot oxygen gas was passed through   $  CsPbTh_{3}  $   surface during annealing process in dry ambient air (RH: ≈10–20% at the room temperature). Conversely, the   $  CsPbTh_{3}  $   film without HOCP process was fabricated in a nitrogen-filled glovebox as the reference sample. Then, 30 μL of Spiro-OMeTAD solution (90 mg of Spiro-OMeTAD, 36 μL TBP, and 22 μL of Li-TFSI [1 mL acetonitrile contains 520 mg Li-TFSI]) were dissolved in 1 mL of CB, and it was spin-coated on perovskite layer at 5000 rpm for 30 s in dry ambient environment with low humidity. Finally, ≈80 nm-thick Au contacts were deposited on the top of the hole transport layer (HTL) layer via thermal evaporation methods.
