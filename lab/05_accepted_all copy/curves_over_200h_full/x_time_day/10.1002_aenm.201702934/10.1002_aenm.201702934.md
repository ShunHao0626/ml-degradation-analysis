# Simultaneous Improvement in Efficiency and Stability of Low-Temperature-Processed Perovskite Solar Cells by Interfacial Control

Randi Azmi, Chang-Lyoul Lee, In Hwan Jung, and Sung-Yeon Jang*

In most current state-of-the-art perovskite solar cells (PSCs), high-temperature (  $  \approx500\ ^{\circ}C  $  )-sintered metal oxides are employed as electron-transporting layers (ETLs). To lower the device processing temperature, the development of low-temperature-processable ETL materials (such as solution-processed ZnO) has received growing attention. However, thus far, the use of solution-processed ZnO is limited because the reverse decomposition reaction that occurs at ZnO/perovskite interfaces significantly degrades the charge collection and stability of PSCs. In this work, the reverse decomposition reaction is successfully retarded by sulfur passivation of solution-processed ZnO. The sulfur passivation of ZnO by a simple chemical means, efficiently reduces the oxygen-deficient defects and surface oxygen-containing groups, thus effectively preventing reverse decomposition reactions during and after formation of the perovskite active layers. Using the low-temperature-processed sulfur-passivated ZnO (ZnO–S), perovskite layers with higher crystallinity and larger grain size are obtained, while the charge extraction at the ZnO/perovskite interface is significantly improved. As a result, the ZnO–S-based PSCs achieve substantially improved power-conversion-efficiency (PCE) (19.65%) and long-term air-storage stability (90% retention after 40 d) compared with pristine ZnO-based PSCs (16.51% and 1% retention after 40 d). Notably, the PCE achieved is the highest recorded (19.65%) for low-temperature ZnO-based PSCs.

# 1. Introduction

Surface modification of ZnO using materials with induced dipoles (e.g., polyelectrolytes, self-assembled monolayers, and polar chemicals) has effectively improved the charge collection efficiency due to enhanced internal electric fields. Reduction of surface defects of ZnO has also been an important strategy.  $  ^{[25,30,33-35]}  $  Successful defect passivation using oxygen-containing polymers or sulfur or metal ion-containing chemicals has been demonstrated.  $  ^{[26,30,31,33,35-37]}  $

In order to employ ZnO as ETLs for PSCs, however, there is an additional obstacle: the reverse decomposition reaction at the ZnO/perovskite interfaces.  $  ^{[38-41]}  $   The basic nature of the ZnO surface leads to proton-transfer reactions at the ZnO/perovskite interface, which results in decomposition of the perovskite to   $  PbI_{2}  $  .  $  ^{[36,37,40-43]}  $   The decomposition process is accelerated by the presence of surface hydroxyl groups and/or residual organic acetate ligands.  $  ^{[40]}  $   Intensive efforts have been made

Use of preheated ZnO at high temperature (  $  >400\ ^{\circ}C  $  ),  $  ^{[37,40]}  $   doping with metal atoms such as Al or Mg,  $  ^{[36,37]}  $   ultraviolet (UV)-ozone treatment,  $  ^{[42]}  $   modification with polyelectrolytes,  $  ^{[43]}  $   and a restricted-volume solvent annealing technique,  $  ^{[41]}  $   have all been reported. Although those techniques partially reduced the reverse reaction during perovskite formation on ZnO ETLs, the PCE of PSCs was limited only to 14–16%, and the long-term stability was poor.

In this work, we report an efficient strategy to improve PCE and long-term stability of PSCs simultaneously through effective suppression of reverse decomposition reaction at ZnO/perovskite interfaces using simple chemical modification. Sulfur passivation of ZnO surfaces by chemical means effectively prevented the reverse decomposition reaction during and after formation of perovskite active layers. The resulting perovskite layers showed higher crystallinity with larger grain size than those prepared on pristine ZnO ETLs. Furthermore, the interfacial charge extraction at ZnO/perovskite junctions was significantly improved because of the reduced surface defects that act as charge recombination sites. As a result, the PSCs using sulfur-passivated ZnO (ZnO-S) achieved the PCE of 19.65% (  $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.10 V,   $  J_{SC}  $   of 23.15 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and FF of 0.79) with negligible photocurrent hysteresis, whereas the PSCs using pristine ZnO had PCE of 16.51% (  $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.07 V,   $  J_{SC}  $   of 21.18 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and FF of 0.72) with considerable hysteresis. Notably, the PCE of 19.65% was the highest reported among low-temperature ZnO-based PSCs. Prevention of interfacial reverse decomposition reactions by sulfur passivation also influenced remarkably the long-term air stability of PSCs. The device using ZnO-S retained 90% of its initial PCE after 40 d of air storage, during which time the device using pristine ZnO completely lost its photovoltaic activity.

# 2. Results and Discussion

The ZnO ETLs were prepared by in situ sol-gel conversion at 130 °C (details in the Experimental Section in the Supporting Information).  $  ^{[26,28,32]}  $   The sulfur passivation was performed on ZnO ETLs using 1,2-ethanedithiol (EDT) following a previous report with slight modification.  $  ^{[25]}  $   The sulfur passivation of ZnO was confirmed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis. As shown in the wide-scan XPS spectra in Figure S1a,b in the Supporting Information, the S 2p and S 2s peaks located at 163 and 227 eV appeared after thiol modification, confirming the formation of Zn-S bonds. The shifting of the Zn 2p  $  _{3/2}  $   peak to lower binding energy (  $  \approx  $  0.21 eV) in ZnO–S films (Figure S1c, Supporting Information) confirmed the replacement of surface oxygen-containing groups with Zn-S bonds.  $  ^{[25,30,44]}  $   The reduction of surface oxygen-containing groups by thiol modification was also indicated in O1s core-level spectra (Figure S1d, Supporting Information). The higher binding-energy peak (  $  \approx  $  532.20 eV), which is attributable to oxygen-deficient defects such as oxygen vacancies and hydroxyl groups of ZnO,  $  ^{[25,30,44]}  $   was considerably reduced by sulfur passivation. The photoluminescence (PL) spectra in Figure S1e in the Supporting Information further confirmed the decrease of defect-related PL emission at   $  \approx  $  550 nm by

sulfur passivation.  $  ^{[25,26,30,44]}  $   Figure 1b shows the penetration depth into ZnO–S films indicated by S 2p XPS spectra. While sulfur passivation atoms were more concentrated at the surface (  $  \approx0.6  $   at% until the depth of 20 nm), sulfur atoms were still present entirely throughout the ZnO–S films. The inset shows the atomic percent with respect to etching depth (7 nm for each step). The suppression of trap sites influenced the conductivity under UV light illumination (Figure 1c). The UV illumination on ZnO can fill trap states and lead to increased conductivity; thus, the increment in conductivity is related to the total trap density in ZnO.  $  ^{[45,46]}  $   The ZnO–S showed less increment than did pristine ZnO, indicating that sulfur passivation reduced the trap states in the ZnO films.

The effects of sulfur passivation on the reverse decomposition of perovskite layers were investigated. Methylammonium lead iodide perovskite layers were prepared on ZnO ETLs via the two-step sequential deposition method.  $  ^{[47-49]}  $   Two-step sequential deposition is known as an efficient method for forming perovskite layers on ZnO layers with reduced reverse reactions.  $  ^{[21,50]}  $   We first deposited a   $  PbI_{2}  $   layer on ZnO ETLs by spin-coating   $  PbI_{2}  $   solution in dimethylformamide (DMF)/dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), followed by heating at 100 °C for 3 min. The final perovskite layers were prepared by immersing   $  PbI_{2}  $   layers in methylammonium iodide (MAI) in 2-propanol/  $  H_{2}O  $   for 1 min. To remove excess MAI, the film was immersed in 2-propanol for 20 s and then annealed at 100 °C for 10–90 min. The photo images of the resulting perovskite films on the ZnO ETLs annealed under various conditions are shown in Figure S2 in the Supporting Information. The perovskite layers formed on pristine ZnO showed typical decomposition during prolonged annealing at 100 °C. After 30 min of annealing at 100 °C, significant yellowing occurred due to reverse reaction to form   $  PbI_{2}  $ . This result is in good agreement with previous literature regarding perovskite film formation on solution-processed ZnO.  $  ^{[36,37,40]}  $   In contrast, the reverse decomposition reaction was effectively suppressed in the perovskite layers on ZnO–S. No discernable yellowing was observed even after 60 min of annealing. The thickness of the perovskite films on both types of ZnO layers (pristine ZnO and ZnO–S) was approximately the same (  $  \approx  $  500 nm). Therefore, this result is attributable to the effective passivation of surface oxygen-containing groups in ZnO–S, because the reverse decomposition process is facilitated by the presence of surface hydroxyl groups and/or residual organic acetate ligands.  $  ^{[36,37,40,41]}  $

The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images in Figure 1d,e exhibited the microstructure of perovskite layers on ZnO ETLs. The perovskite layer on ZnO–S contained larger crystal grains (average ≈385 nm) compared with that on pristine ZnO (average ≈265 nm). As shown in the XRD spectra in Figure 1f, perovskite layers deposited on ZnO–S showed better developed perovskite crystalline peaks with a lower (001) PbI $ _{2} $  peak, compared with the perovskite layers prepared on pristine ZnO. This result revealed that the reverse decomposition of perovskite to PbI $ _{2} $  was effectively reduced on ZnO–S. The effects of sulfur passivation on the surface wetting property of ZnO films were evaluated using water contact angle measurement. As shown in the inset of Figure 1d,e, the water contact angle of pristine ZnO (34.30°) was increased by sulfur modification (to 46.10°), indicating that the ZnO surface became more hydrophobic due to the removal of hydrophilic oxygen-containing species. # 3. Conclusions

Simultaneous improvement in efficiency and stability of L-PSCs was achieved by the development of defect-controlled solution-processed ZnO ETLs. The sulfur passivation of ZnO by simple chemical means efficiently reduced the oxygen-deficient defects and surface oxygen-containing groups, which can cause reverse decomposition reactions at ZnO/perovskite interfaces. There were three noteworthy effects from using our ZnO-S. First, the reverse decomposition during perovskite formation was substantially reduced, and perovskite layers with greater crystallinity and larger grain size were obtained. Second, surface defects that can cause interfacial charge recombination were well passivated; thus, the charge collection efficiency in the devices was enhanced. Third, the long-term stability in air (with and without illumination) was also significantly improved. As a result, the ZnO-S-based L-PSCs showed the highest PCE (19.65%) reported for ZnO-based PSCs, while the pristine ZnO-based L-PSCs showed only 16.51%, which is consistent with other reported performance. # 4. Experimental Section

Device Fabrication: Each ITO substrate was cleaned with acetone and isopropyl alcohol for 15 min. After cleaning, the ITO was put in the UV–ozone chamber for 20 min. Zinc oxide (ZnO) in situ sol–gel conversion was employed by adopting the previous method.  $  ^{[26,32,61]}  $   The ZnO sol–gel solution was spin cast onto ITO/glass substrates at 2000 rpm for 15 s; then annealed at 130 °C for 10 min (  $  \approx  $  40 nm thickness). The sulfur passivation was performed on ZnO ETLs using EDT with optimum concentration of   $  10 \times 10^{-3}  $   M in acetonitrile.  $  ^{[25]}  $   The perovskite active layers were fabricated using the sequential deposition method with modification.  $  ^{[47,62]}  $   A mixture solution of   $  1.3 \times 10^{-3}  $   M PbI  $  _{2}  $   in DMF/DMSO (90/10 vol%) was heated at 100 °C for 2 h; then, 50 μL of PbI  $  _{2}  $   solution was spin cast at 2500 rpm for 20 s followed by thermal annealing at 100 °C for 3 min. After cooling for several minutes, the PbI  $  _{2}  $   layers were dipped in   $  0.25 \times 10^{-3}  $   M MAI solution in 2-propanol/H  $  _{2}  $  O (95/5%) for 1 min. To remove excess MAI, the film was immersed in 2-propanol solution for 20 s, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 10 min in air. The HTL solution was spin coated (4000 rpm for 30 s) with   $  61 \times 10^{-3}  $   M of Spiro-OMeTAD in 1 mL chlorobenzene with additive (  $  31 \times 10^{-3}  $   M of lithium-bis-trifluoromethanesulfonyl-imide (Li-TFSI);   $  190 \times 10^{-3}  $   M of tert-butylpyridine (TBP)). All fabrication processes were done in ambient air. Finally, a 100 nm Au electrode was evaporated at low pressure (  $  <10^{-6}  $   Torr). The active area was designated using a metal mask with area 7.07 mm  $  ^{2}  $  .

Device Analysis: J-V measurements were performed using a Keithley 2401 source unit under the light intensity AM 1.5G (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) illumination (Newport). The spectral mismatch was calibrated using a KG-5 filter-covered mono-silicon detector. All devices were measured under the conditions of one sun illumination and ambient air for reverse and forward scans with scanning rate of 50 mV s  $  ^{-1}  $  . EQE spectra were obtained by passing the output of a 400 W Xenon lamp through a monochromator and using the appropriate order-sorting filters. The collimated output of the monochromator was measured through a 1 mm aperture (McScience, K3100 IQX). The calibration was performed by using a silicon photodiode standard. The wavelength values were scanned at chopping frequency of 4 Hz from 300 to 850 nm. Transient photovoltage measurements (McScience, K3400) were performed at steady state under continuous illumination. The system was perturbed using a green (  $  \lambda = 535  $   nm) pulsed laser at 1 Hz. The resulting voltage transient was acquired using a DPO 3052 Tektronix Digital Phosphor Oscilloscope (impedance 1 M  $  \Omega  $  ). The recombination lifetime was extracted from the decay curves using mono-exponential fitting. TCSPC measurements were performed with a pulsed diode-laser head (LDH-P-C-405, PicoQuant) coupled with a laser-diode driver (PDL 800-B, PicoQuant) as an excitation source (the excitation wavelength was 400 nm) with a repetition rate of 5 MHz. The PL emission was spectrally resolved using collection optics and a monochromator (SP-2150i, Acton). A TCSPC module (PicoHarp 300, PicoQuant) with MCP-PMT (R3809U-59, Hamamatsu) was used for ultrafast detection. Deconvolution of the PL decay curve, which separates the IRF and actual PL decay signals, was performed using fitting software (FluoFit, PicoQuant) to determine the time constant associated with each exponential decay curve. IMPS/IMVS methods were performed using an impedance analyzer (Ivium Stat.) under illumination (green-emitting LED,   $  \lambda = 535  $   nm). The mean transport (  $  \tau_{ct}  $  ) and recombination times (  $  \tau_{r}  $  ) of the photogenerated charges were obtained from the IMVS/IMPS curves by setting   $  \tau_{ct,r} = 1/2\pi f_{min}  $   (IMPS, IMVS), respectively.

Sample Characterization: Steady-state photoluminescence measurements were performed using an SPEX Nanolog 3-211 (Horiba). The signal was analyzed using an near infrared (NIR) spectrometer equipped with an FL-1073 (R928 UV-Vis PMT detector). The water contact angle was analyzed using a contact angle analyzer (Phoenix 300).

The SEM images were taken using a JSM-7610F (JEOL) field-emission scanning electron microscope. The average grain size was determined using image analysis software (ImageJ).
