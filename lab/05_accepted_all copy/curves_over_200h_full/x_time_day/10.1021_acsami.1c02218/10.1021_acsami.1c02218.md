# Efficient and Stable Carbon-Based Perovskite Solar Cells via Passivation by a Multifunctional Hydrophobic Molecule with Bidentate Anchors

Tingting Xu,  $  ^{*}  $  ,  $  ^{\perp}  $   Kai Zou,  $  ^{\perp}  $   Shaoshen Lv, Hebing Tang, Yingxiang Zhang, Yonghua Chen, Lixin Chen, Zhen Li,  $  ^{*}  $   and Wei Huang  $  ^{*}  $

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Surface passivation has demonstrated to be an effective strategy to improve the power conversion efficiency (PCE) and long-term stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Passivation treatment can effectively reduce the density of defect states at the surface and grain boundaries of perovskite films. Herein, a passivation agent of 2-amino-5-(trifluoromethyl)pyridine (5-TFMAP) with bidentate groups is applied to passivate perovskite  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  films for the first time. Two types of electron-rich nitrogen atoms from both the pyridine ring and the amino group provide strong interaction with the under-coordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $ . Additionally, the trifluoromethyl group offers a hydrophobic property and improves moisture stability of the as-fabricated PSCs. It is found that the 5-TFMAP passivation layer can effectively reduce the defect states, promote better carrier transport, and suppress non-radiation recombination of the perovskite films. The best PCE of carbon-based PSCs passivated by the 5-TFMAP agent achieves a high efficiency of  $ 14.96\% $  compared with that of  $ 11.90\% $  for the control PSCs. Moreover, the long-term stability of PSCs with the 5

TFMAP passivation treatment is greatly improved, and its PCE can maintain  $ 80\% $  of its original PCE after being stored for  $ 1200\mathrm{h} $  with a relative humidity of around  $ 35\% $  at room temperature.

KEYWORDS: carbon-based perovskite solar cell, passivation, bidentate anchors, interface, long-term stability

# 1. INTRODUCTION

Organic–inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been demonstrated as a promising alternative energy conversion technique to generate electricity from the abundant sunlight. The efficiency of PSCs has gained much faster growth for the past 10 years, as compared with that of dye-sensitized solar cells.  $  ^{1,2}  $   Perovskite light harvesting materials have showed outstanding advantages in photovoltaic applications, for example, suitable band gap matching to the broad range of the solar spectrum, high absorption coefficient, long carrier transport lifetime, and unique properties from large-sized crystal dimension and so forth.  $  ^{3-6}  $   Moreover, cost-effective PSCs can be made by the promising feasible solution-processing property of perovskites. Thus, PSCs have drawn a huge research interest worldwide, and the state-of-the-art power conversion efficiency (PCE) of single-junction PSCs has reached more than 25%.  $  ^{7,8}  $   Several key factors determining the performance of PSCs include good quality of perovskite films, effective functional layers of electron transport layer (ETL) and hole transport layer (HTL), as well as proper interfacial layers.  $  ^{9-13}  $   Uniformly covered perovskite films with few defects are highly desired for gaining high PCE.  $  ^{14,15}  $   A solution-processing procedure typically produces polycrystalline perov

At the same time, trifluoromethyl groups on the pyridine ring can exhibit hydrophobic property and improve the moisture stability of the as-fabricated PSCs. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) characterizations of the perovskite films treated with the passivation agent 5-TFMAP confirmed the interaction. # 2. MATERIALS AND METHODS

2.1. Preparation of Perovskite Precursor Solution. The perovskite precursor solution was prepared according to our previous method.  $  ^{35}  $   In detail, 159 mg of methyl ammonium iodine (  $  >99.5\%  $  , Xi'an p-OLED Corp.) and 461 mg of PbI  $  _{2}  $   (  $  >99.99\%  $  , Xi'an p-OLED Corp.) were dissolved in mixed solvents of 78 mg of N, N-dimethyl sulfoxide (99.7%, Macklin) and 600 mg of N, N-dimethylformamide (99.8%, Aladdin). The mixture was stirred at 35 °C overnight before further use.

2.2. Fabrication of PSCs. The HTL-free carbon-based PSCs with a structure of FTO/TiO $ _{2} $ /CH $ _{3} $ NH $ _{3} $ PbI $ _{3} $ /carbon were fabricated. $ ^{35} $  First, fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass substrates were cleaned in detergent, acetone, isopropanol (IPA), and anhydrous ethanol for 15 min sequentially. Then, 0.15 M titanium diisopropoxide dis-(acetylacetonate) (Sigma-Aldrich, 75 wt % in IPA) in 1-butanol

(Sigma-Aldrich, 99.8%) was spun-coated on the FTO substrate at 2000 rpm for 30 s to form the   $  TiO_{2}  $   compact layer (c-  $  TiO_{2}  $  ), and the films were annealed at 125 °C for 10 min. The mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer was prepared by spin-coating diluted   $  TiO_{2}  $   (Dyesol, 18NR-T) suspension in anhydrous ethanol (1:3.5, wt/wt) at 5000 rpm for 30 s and then annealed up to 500 °C for 30 min. The perovskite film was prepared via one-step processing assisted by the anti-solvent extraction, that is, filtrated perovskite precursor solution was spin-coated on top of the mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   films at 4000 rpm for 20 s. During the spin-coating process, anti-solvent diethyl ether dripped quickly onto the perovskite to dry the film, followed by annealing at 95 °C for 10 min. A commercial carbon paste (Guangzhou Seaside Technology Development Co., Ltd.) was doctor-bladed and used as the electrode. For the perovskite/carbon interfacial passivated solar cells, a passivate agent of 5-TFMAP (Aladdin) in IPA solution with different concentrations was spin-coated onto the perovskite film at 4000 rpm for 30 s, respectively. The remaining fabrication procedures were the same as that described above.

2.3. Characterization. The X-ray photoelectron spectra were recorded by Shimadzu 7000 in the   $  2\theta  $   range from 5 to   $  50^{\circ}  $   using Cu Kα as the radiation source (  $  \lambda = 1.5418 \AA  $  ). The surface morphology of perovskite films was obtained by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, FEI, Verios G4). Atomic force microscopy (AFM, Bruker, Dimension Fast Scan and Dimension Icon) was used to obtain the top view images and surface roughness of perovskite films. XPS was performed by the X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (Axis Supra). The absorption spectra of perovskite thin films were measured by the Hitachi spectrophotometer (U-3900h). Steady-state PL spectra and time-resolved PL spectra were measured by a combined spectrometer with steady-state and fluorescence lifetime functions (PICQUANT, FluoTime 300). # 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Figure 1a shows the schematic graph of the passivation agent 5-TFMAP interacted with perovskite  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  films. The nitrogen atoms on the 5-TFMAP are expected to coordinate with the  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $ , and the passivation layer would prevent the moisture penetration due to the hydrophobic fluorine element. Figure 1b shows the solar cell structure with the mesoporous  $ \mathrm{TiO}_2 $  films coated by the perovskite  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $ , and then the carbon electrode was directly covered on top of the perovskite films without using any hole transport materials. The passivation layer was inserted as the interface layer

between the perovskite film and the carbon electrode. Figure 1c–h shows the top view SEM images of the perovskite films treated with different concentrations of 5-TFMAP solution ranging from 0 to 5 mg/mL. Figure S1 shows the statistical distribution histogram of grain size for perovskite/5-TFMAP films. The pristine perovskite film showed clear grain boundaries (GBs) and uniform crystal distribution with the average grain size of 163 nm. With increasing the concentration of 5-TFMAP solution, the grain size of the perovskite/5-TFMA films increased from 190 nm at 1 mg/mL, then reached to the maximum value of 224 nm at 2 mg/mL, and finally decreased to 154 nm at 5 mg/mL. Bright particles can be seen on the GBs of the surface-passivated perovskite films with increasing the concentration of 5-TFMAP to 2 mg/mL, and more bright particles are covered on the film surface when further increasing the concentration of 5-TFMAP to 5 mg/mL. A similar phenomenon was reported by Li et al. using a 2-aminoterephthalic acid cross-linking agent to purposely passivate the GBs of the perovskite.  $  ^{36}  $   They revealed that the passivation on the GBs of perovskite films can have higher current flow, and it would be more efficient for charge transfer than that without surface passivation. It can be concluded from the SEM images that 2 mg/mL concentration of the 5-TFMAP can promote largest grain size of the perovskite films, and it could properly passivate the GBs of the perovskite films.

Figure 2a shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the pristine perovskite   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   film and these films passivated with different concentrations of 5-TFMAP solution. The major diffraction peaks originated from the perovskite films at the planes of (110) and (220) can be clearly observed for all the tested films. A tiny   $  PbI_{2}  $   peak can be observed for the perovskite films treated with the 5-TFMAP since IPA can dissolve a small amount of perovskite, resulting in the appearance of the   $  PbI_{2}  $   diffraction peak.  $  ^{16}  $   The position of the main peak corresponding to the (110) crystal plane did not change, indicating that 5-TFMAP did not enter the perovskite lattice. The effect of 5-TFMAP on the crystallinity of perovskite films was further analyzed by comparing the diffraction intensity of the (110) crystal plane and their full width at half-maximum (fwhm) at different concentrations of 5-TFMAP, as shown in Figure 2b. According to Scherrer formula, the fwhm values for the respective perovskite films are 0.343, 0.328, 0.324, 0.326, 0.329, and 0.332 for the concentration of the passivation agent changing from 0 to 5 mg/mL, respectively. The perovskite treated with the 2 mg/mL 5-TFMAP gives the highest diffraction intensity and the smallest fwhm value at the (110) crystal face. The lower fwhm value generally relates to the improved crystallinity of the perovskite film and preferred growth orientation of the perovskite crystals.  $  ^{35}  $   The XRD results indicate that the crystallinity of the perovskite/5-TFMAP (2 mg/mL) film is increased, which is also consistent with the previous SEM results, as shown in Figure 1.

Figure 2c,d shows the UV–vis absorption spectra of perovskite films passivated by different concentrations of the 5-TFMAP agent in the wavelength range of 450–850 nm. Compared with the control perovskite film, perovskite films treated with the moderated concentration of 5-TFMAP below 2 mg/mL gave increased absorption over the entire test range. However, further increasing the concertation of 5-TFMAP beyond 3 mg/mL caused the prominent decrease in the absorption, as shown in the partially enlarged spectrum of Figure 2d. The perovskite films treated with the 2 mg/mL 5-TFMAP passivation agent gained the highest optical absorption intensity among all the films.

Carbon-based PSCs without HTL were fabricated to evaluate the passivation effects. The current density–voltage   $  (J-V)  $   curves of the champion PSCs treated with different concentrations of S-TFMAP agent were plotted and are shown in Figure 3a. Table 1 summarizes the major photovoltaic parameters extracted from the J-V curves under reverse scan. The control PSC showed the average PCE of 9.91%, and the best PCE is 11.90%. The PCE of passivated PSCs followed a trend of initial increase and then decrease with increasing the concentration of the S-TFMAP. The PSCs passivated by the 2 mg/mL of S-TFMAP achieved the highest PCE among all the other conditions, reaching the maximum PCE of 14.96% with

$  V_{oc}  $  of 1.074 V,  $  J_{sc}  $  of 21.703 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $ , and fill factor (FF) of 64.19%. The average PCE of 13.93% for the PSCs made by the 2 mg/mL 5-TFMAP was also much higher than that of the control PSCs. When continuously increasing the concentration of the passivation agent from 4 to 5 mg/mL, the PCE of these PSCs was further reduced to the value lower than that of the control cell. The enhancement of PCE for the PSCs treated with the proper amount of 5-TFMAP can be ascribed to the distinct improvement of  $  J_{sc}  $  and FF, and all the PSCs after passivation showed an increased  $  J_{sc}  $ . Moreover, the PSCs made by the 2 mg/mL 5-TFMAP-passivated perovskite film showed significant improvement of the 6%  $  J_{sc}  $  and 16% FF compared with the control cell. The significant improvement of PCE for the appropriate concentration of the 5-TFMAP can be attributed to the passivation of the perovskite films. In order to compare the bidentate effect for the passivation agent, a 4-aminobenzotrifluoride (4-ABTF) passivation agent with only one amine group was applied to fabricate the solar cell with the same procedure as that of the 5-TFMAP molecule. The photovoltaic performance of PSCs with the 4-ABTF passivation agent is illustrated in Figure S2 and Table S1. The highest PSCs based on the 4-ABTF treatment gained 12.98% efficiency under the same fabrication condition, and it is much lower than that of the 5-TFMAP agent. It suggests the effectiveness of using 5-TFMAP with its unique molecular structure to interact with the perovskite films. Figure S3 shows the EQE of the pristine PSCs and the 2 mg/mL 5-TFMAP-treated PSCs. It confirmed that the photocurrent of the PSCs treated with the passivation agent is higher than that of the control PSCs.

Figure 3b shows that the PSCs treated with the proper passivation agent had a lower dark current density (  $  J_{sc}  $  ) than the control PSCs, revealing that the 5-TFMAP can inhibit leaking current and promote a higher   $  J_{sc}  $  .  $  ^{37}  $   Figure 3c,d shows the statistical information of photovoltaic parameters of devices passivated with different concentrations of 5-TFMAP. After passivation with 2 mg/mL 5-TFMAP, the average values of each parameter were distinctly increased compared with the control PSCs, and the PCE distribution, as shown in Figure 3d, was narrower with good repeatability. It clearly confirmed the positive passivation effect of the 5-TFMAP molecule on the photovoltaic performance of the PSCs. The hysteresis effect before and after 5-TFMAP passivation (2 mg/mL) were also investigated and are shown in Figure S4, and the corresponding photovoltaic parameters are listed in Table S2. The hysteresis index (HI) of the PSCs after passivated by a proper amount of 5-TFMAP decreased to 13.7% with the original value of 21.3% for the control cell. The defect passivation and enlarged grain size of the perovskite/5-TFMAP films can reduce the HI, and it proved that the perovskite films after proper passivation is beneficial for charge transfer and collection.  $  ^{38}  $

# 4. CONCLUSIONS

In summary, a pyridine derivative 5-TFMAP with multifunctional groups of trifluoromethyl and amino groups was applied as the passivation agent to modify the interface of perovskite  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  and the carbon electrode. The N atoms with high electronegativity in the pyridine ring and lone pair electrons in the amino group interacted with the uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  in perovskite films. Thus, the 5-TFMAP passivation agent successfully reduced the defect states and promoted better charge transport in the modified perovskite films compared with that without treatment. The 5-TFMAP-passivated carbon-based PSCs achieved an improved champion PCE of  $ 14.95\% $  compared with that of  $ 11.90\% $  gained by the PSCs without passivation. Moreover, the enhanced long-term stability of the 5-TFMAP-treated PSCs remained  $ 80\% $  of the initial PCE after 50 days storage at room temperature with a relative humidity of  $ 35\% $ . This work provides a meaningful insight for applying bidentate passivation agents with multifunctional groups to treat perovskite films. It could be extended to a broad application for a variety of PSCs for mixed cations or halide-based perovskites for high performance.
