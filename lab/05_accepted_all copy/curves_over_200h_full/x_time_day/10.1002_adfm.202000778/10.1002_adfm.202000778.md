# Phenylhydrazinium Iodide for Surface Passivation and Defects Suppression in Perovskite Solar Cell

Md Ashiqur Rahman Laskar, Wenqin Luo, Nabin Ghimire, Ashraful Haider Chowdhury, Behzad Bahrami, Ashim Gurung, Khan Mamun Reza, Rajesh Pathak, Raja Sekhar Bobba, Buddhi Sagar Lamsal, Ke Chen, Md Tawabur Rahman, Sheikh Ifatur Rahman, Khalid Emshadi, Tingting Xu,* Mao Liang,* Wen-Hua Zhang,* and Qiquan Qiao*

In recent years, hybrid perovskite solar cells (HPSCs) have received considerable research attention due to their impressive photovoltaic performance and low-temperature solution processing capability. However, there remain challenges related to defect passivation and enhancing the charge carrier dynamics of the perovskites, to further increase the power conversion efficiency of HPSCs. In this work, the use of a novel material, phenylhydrazinium iodide (PHAI), as an additive in  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite for defect minimization and enhancement of the charge carrier dynamics of inverted HPSCs is reported. Incorporation of the PHAI in perovskite precursor solution facilitates controlled crystallization, higher carrier lifetime, as well as less recombination. In addition, PHAI additive treated HPSCs exhibit lower density of filled trap states ( $ 10^{10}~\mathrm{cm}^{-2} $ ) in perovskite grain boundaries, higher charge carrier mobility ( $ \approx 11\times 10^{-4}\mathrm{cm}^2\mathrm{V}^{-1}\mathrm{s} $ ), and enhanced power conversion efficiency ( $ \approx 18\% $ ) that corresponds to a  $ \approx 20\% $  improvement in comparison to the pristine devices.

It is believed that the uncoordinated ions like   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and corresponding dangling bonds at the grain boundaries and surfaces are the main sources of charge traps.  $  ^{[8]}  $   Different types of passivating agents have been attempted to solve this structural defect issue by utilizing the lone pair electrons of the passivating agents.  $  ^{[9]}  $   Besides that, other issues such as volatility of the organic cations and ionic defects induced from weak interaction energy between metal cations and halide anions also appear in HPSCs.  $  ^{[1b,10]}  $   At the same time, controlled crystallization and uniform morphology of perovskite films are noteworthy to develop high-performance and stable devices as they influence the electrical shunt and bulk traps during solar cell operation.  $  ^{[11]}  $   Considering all these factors, additive engineering has been introduced to control crystal growth, grain size, passivate defects, enhance charge transfer and establish a moisture protection aimed at achieving improved PCE and stability. To date, researchers have proposed various kinds of additives, for example, ionic liquids (BMIMBF4, MAF, ACN, etc.), different salts (PEAI, KCl, OTAB, etc.), long-chain polymer (PVP, P123, PMMA) and small organic molecules (DIO, TSI, etc.).  $  ^{[12]}  $

Here, we developed a new organic halide salt, Phenylhydrazinium iodide (  $  C_{6}H_{5}NHNH_{3}I  $  ) (PHAI) to perform additive engineering of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   perovskite. The Phenylhydrazinium iodide (PHAI) when used as an additive in   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   precursor solution not only helps to prevent amateur crystal growth but also minimizes defects in the perovskite films. Furthermore, the PHAI accelerates the charge carrier transfer providing higher conductivity of the perovskite films and offers better stability of the perovskite film by deactivating the uncoordinated

$ \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} $  ions.  $ 2\mathrm{mg} $  PHAI treated (MAPbI-PHAI) HPSCs exhibit comparatively higher short circuit current density,  $ J_{sc} $  ( $ \approx 20 $  to  $ \approx 23\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $ ), higher open circuit voltage,  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  ( $ \approx 0.97 $  to  $ \approx 1.03\mathrm{V} $ ) and significantly enhanced power conversion efficiency ( $ \approx 15 $  to  $ \approx 18\% $ ) than the untreated (MAPbI₃) solar cells.

# 2. Result and Discussion

Figure 1a depicts the device structure as FTO/PEDOT:PSS/Perovskite/PCBM/Rhodamine/Ag. Figure 1b represents Phenylhydrazinium iodide (PHAI) which is an organic compound synthesized from phenylhydrazine and hydriodic acid. Detail procedures of PHAI (Figure S1, Supporting Information) preparation is available in the experimental section. PHAI containing two nitrogen (N) atoms can be dissolved in most polar solvents, especially in the perovskite solvents. The reason behind choosing this new additive compound is its N elements. N performs  $ \mathfrak{sp}^3 $ -hybridization with its  $ 1s^2 2s^2 2p^3 $  electronic configuration in the chemical compound. Among four of the hybridized orbitals, three form covalent bonds accommodating the three electrons. Interestingly, the lone pair electrons available in another hybridized orbital have the capability of forming coordination bond where required. The coordination phenomenon facilitated by the PHAI treatment can induce this slow crystallization. UV–Vis absorption spectra were evaluated to get more intuitive understanding of the absorption of PHAI treated MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  films. As shown in Figure 4b, light absorption is higher for the PHAI treated perovskite film over the entire spectrum region. Moreover, overlapping spectra at larger wavelength region (near 780 nm) indicate that the bandgap remains same. There are few possible reasons for the enhanced light absorption in the doped perovskite films: a) improved crystallinity, b) FÖrster resonance energy transfer (FRET), and c) change in transition dipole moment of the perovskite.  $  ^{[26]}  $  The improved crystallinity is supported by our XRD measurements. FRET is invalid here because PHAI, by itself, does not have light absorption capability (Figure S8, Supporting Information). However, we hypothesize that PHAI molecules can cause a slight change in transition dipole moment into the perovskite crystals. It was reported that the size and rotation of A cations in ABX  $  _{3}  $  structure can affect the dipole moment.  $  ^{[27]}  $  Since  $  -NH_{2}  $  tail has the ability to occupy A-site vacancies of the perovskite crystals,  $  ^{[28]}  $  we imply that the  $  -NH_{2}  $  tails of PHAI additive fill some A-site vacancies resulting slightly change in transition dipole moment. UV–vis absorption spectra for different amounts of PHAI doped perovskite films are also available in the supporting section (Figure S9, Supporting Information).

We further examined the devices using Mott–Schottky characterization to elucidate the effect of the PHAI on built in potential   $  (V_{\mathrm{bi}})  $   of the device's p-n junction. Figure 5a shows that PHAI treated PSCs has a higher   $  V_{bi}  $   of 0.81 V versus the control PSCs with 0.71 V. Higher   $  V_{bi}  $   can be correlated to   $  V_{oc}  $   improvement  $  ^{[31]}  $   and can facilitate charge carrier extraction.  $  ^{[32]}  $   This statement is also supported by carrier mobility values estimated from the Photo-CELIV curves (Figure 5b). The Photo-CELIV is widely employed to investigate the charge extraction qualitatively. We calculated mobility   $  (\mu)  $   using this equation,   $  \mu = 2d^{2}/(3At_{\max}^{2})^{[33]}  $   where d is active layer thickness, A is the slope of applied ramp voltage and   $  t_{max}  $   is elapsed time for rising up to maximum voltage. PHAI treated PSC showed notable change with an increase in carrier mobility from   $  6.67 \times 10^{-4}  $   to   $  11.33 \times 10^{-4}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $   V  $  ^{-1}  $   s  $  ^{-1}  $  .

Several reports have confirmed that defects available at grain boundaries and interfaces are the most vulnerable sites to initiate the degradation of perovskite films.  $  ^{[39]}  $   As these sites are susceptible to moisture and oxygen, they facilitate diffusion and reaction causing irreversible decomposition of perovskite.  $  ^{[37a,39a,40]}  $   Besides, particular surface defects such as MA and I vacancies can speed up the degradation by vacancy-assisted decomposition mechanism.  $  ^{[41]}  $   We speculate that the PHAI molecules can passivate the major defects by deactivating the under-coordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   through the coordination bond (Pb-N) formation. In the meantime,   $  -NH_{2}  $   tails of PHAI can occupy the MA vacancies.  $  ^{[28,42]}  $   Some of the iodine vacancies may be filled at the same time.  $  ^{[42a,43]}  $   Figure 6a illustrates the possible defect passivation mechanism in detail. We propose that PHAI exposes its phenyl ring in outward direction while attaching with perovskite. Hydrophobic phenyl rings form a moisture-repelling barrier around the perovskite and thus can improve stability. To further elucidate, we performed water contact angle measurement of the perovskite films.  $  ^{[28,44]}  $   As observed in Figure 6b, bare MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   films exhibit smaller contact angles of 41°–43° whereas PHAI passivated films show higher contact angles of 67–69°, which indicates the superior hydrophobicity of the treated perovskite. Further, in order to evaluate the final device stability, we stored unencapsulated PSCs in N  $  _{2}  $   glove box for 60 days at 26 °C. The devices were taken out from glove box at a particular time interval and exposed to room environment for 1–2 h to carry out the I-V measurement in air (30 ± 5%

RH). Long-term stability data (Figure 6c) indicates that PHAI treated PSCs can retain  $ \approx 88\% $  of their initial efficiency even after 60 days. In contrast, untreated PSCs keep hold of only  $ \approx 53\% $  of the initial efficiency in the same environment. We also tested our devices (unencapsulated) keeping in ambient room environment (dark) at  $ 30 \pm 5\% $  RH and  $ 24 \pm 2^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . As shown in Figure 6d, in the ambient environment, the pristine  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  devices almost died in 16 days, while the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -PHAI based devices encounter only  $ 17\% $  PCE loss in 20 days. This suggests that the PHAI treatment of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskites can lead to better ambient stability of the PSCs.

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrated a novel additive material, PHAI that can passivate the perovskite surface and minimize the defects as well. Notable enhancements of current density and open circuit voltage are observed in the treated PSCs. Furthermore, trap-assisted non-radiative recombination is significantly suppressed in the treated devices due to excellent perovskite grain orientation and less filled trap states in the grain boundaries. The additive treatment not only enhances the light absorption efficiency of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  active layer but also reduces the charge transport time. Our additive has also played multifunctionalities for controlled crystallization, higher conductivity, stabilizing under-coordinated ions and self-supported moisture

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials: PHAI was synthesized from the reaction of phenylhydrazine   $  \left(\mathrm{C}_{6}\mathrm{H}_{5}\mathrm{NHNH}_{2}\right)  $   with hydriodic acid (HI) (57 wt% in water) at 0 °C. 10 mL   $  C_{6}H_{5}NHNH_{2}  $   was added into 25 mL methanol. 10 mL HI (57 wt%) was dropwise added into the   $  C_{6}H_{5}NHNH_{2}  $   solution under vigorous stirring. The ice bath was removed, and the mixture was continuously stirred at room temperature for 2 h. Then the solvent was removed by a rotary evaporator at 60 °C. The crude powder was washed with diethyl ether for three times and finally recrystallized from a mixed solvent of ethanol and diethyl ether. After filtration, the white solid was dried in vacuum at 60 °C for 12 h.

$ 1.5 \times 1.5 \text{ cm}^2 $  FTO substrates were purchased from Hartford Glass Co, USA. PEDOT:PSS (Clevios P VP AI 4083) was acquired from Heraeus. Lead iodide (PbI $ _2 $ ) (99%) and Rhodamine were bought from Sigma-Aldrich. Methylammonium iodide (MAI) and PC $ _{60} $ BM (PCBM) were of Dyesol and Nano-C respectively. Solvents like anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (>99.9%), γ-butyrolactone (GBL) (>99%) and chlorobenzene (CB) (>99%) were obtained from Acros Organics. Anhydrous isopropanol alcohol (IPA) was collected from Fisher Scientific.

Device Fabrication: First, FTO-coated glass substrates were etched with Zn powder and 0.1 M HCl before cleaning. Later, etched substrates were cleaned with detergent water, de-ionized water, acetone and isopropanol sequentially. Each step took about 20 min by ultra-sonication. The substrates were dried with dry   $  N_{2}  $   and then cleaned by UV–  $  O_{3}  $   for 20 min. PEDOT:PSS solution at room temperature was filtered by 0.45   $  \mu  $  m filter, followed by spin coating at 4500 rpm for 45 s and annealing at 145 °C for 10 min. Substrates with PEDOT:PSS film were then transferred to a   $  N_{2}  $   filled glove box. 70   $  \mu  $  L of filtered perovskite solution was spin coated on top of PEDOT:PSS layer at 750 rpm for first 20 s, followed by 4000 rpm for an additional 60 s. 165   $  \mu  $  L of toluene as anti-solvent was dripped quickly onto the perovskite film during spin coating at 40th s. The MAPBl  $  _{3}  $   precursor was prepared from 581 mg of PbI  $  _{2}  $   and 209 mg of MAI mixture dissolved in a solvent mixture of GBL and DMSO with 7:3 (v/v) ratio, stirred for 12–15 hours at 70 °C inside the   $  N_{2}  $   glovebox. PHAI additive was mixed with the precursor 2–3 h before of the spin coating. The spin coated perovskite films were annealed for 10 min at 80 °C. After the substrates were completely cooled down, 60   $  \mu  $  L of filtered hot solution of PCBM (dissolved in chlorobenzene (20 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) and kept at 70 °C) was spin-coated at 2000 rpm for 40 s and subsequently annealed for 5 min at 80 °C. After cooling down, 0.5 mg Rhodamine in per mL dry IPA was spin-coated at 5000 rpm for 20 s. Finally, 85 nm thick silver electrodes were deposited by thermal evaporation.

Characterization: Raman spectroscopy was done using Horiba LabRam HR 800 Raman spectrometer having a green laser beam of  $ 532\mathrm{nm} $  wavelength. FTIR was done by Nicolet 6700 FTIR with Smart Orbit ATR Unit. SEM was carried out by Hitachi-S4700N SEM. XRD was measured on Rigaku SmartLab diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation  $ (\lambda = 1.54178\AA) $ . Agilent 5500 scanning probe microscopy was utilized for AFM topography and KPFM in tapping mode whereas CS-AFM in contact mode with  $ 1\mathrm{V} $  applied bias. EIS measurement was performed by Ametek VERSASTAT3-200 potentiostat from  $ 1\mathrm{Hz - }1\mathrm{MHz} $  frequency. TPC and TPV were measured using an oscilloscope and OBB's OL-4300 nitrogen laser coupled with dye laser producing  $ < 1 $  ns pulse at  $ 532~\mathrm{nm} $  wavelength.  $ 50\Omega $  input oscilloscope impedance was considered for short circuit conditions. During TPV, 1 sun illumination was applied to the PSCs and considered  $ 1\mathrm{M}\Omega $  input oscilloscope impedance for open circuit conditions. To obtain photo-CELIV curves, ns pulse width laser was used by varying delay time between ramp and the laser. Agilent 8453 UV-vis Spectroscopy System was used for absorption measurement of

the films. J-V data were obtained from Agilent 4155C semiconductor parameter analyzer including a Xenon arc lamp (Newport 67 005) having AM1.5 filter with 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  light intensity. NREL S1133-14 silicon reference solar cell was used for calibration. The PSCs having an active area of 0.13 cm  $  ^{-2}  $  were swept from 0 to 1.2 V with a scan speed of 1 V  $  ^{-1}  $  s  $  ^{-1}  $  in forward and reverse direction. For IPCE measurement, Newport 74 125 monochromator was used. Mott–Schottky measurement was done using the same instrument as used in EIS but at a fixed frequency of 1 kHz for 0–1 V. Edinburgh FLS 920 fluorescence spectrophotometer was used for steady-state PL. Contact angle was measured by using the instrument of VCA 2000 video contact angle system.
