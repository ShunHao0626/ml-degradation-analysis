# 1. Introduction

The power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of metal hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have improved dramatically in the past decade with a certified value of 25.5% [1]. Compositional engineering [2–4], interfacial engineering [5–7], additive doping [8,9], film crystallization [10,11], and surface passivation [12,13] have been used widely to improve the device performance of PSCs. It is well known that the presence of surface recombination is inherent to a perovskite material due to a lack of stoichiometric compositions and the sublimation of organic molecules during the thermal annealing process [14]. Thus, surface passivation is an essential approach to enhance the performance of devices, including device efficiency, the current-voltage (I–V) hysteresis, ions immigration, as well as operating stability under real working conditions [15,16].

# ABSTRACT

Surface passivation has played a critical role for efficient and stable perovskite solar cells by reducing surface defects, promoting charge transport, and preventing the penetration of degrading agents. State-of-the-art passivation approaches mainly rely on the formation of a two-dimensional (2D) perovskite layer or the deposition of an ultrathin layer based on the molecular design. Here, we demonstrated a novel nanocrystal-pinning passivation by dripping 2-bromoethyltrimethylammonium bromide (BETAB) colloidal solution onto perovskite films. Theoretical simulation and kinds of experimental results confirm that BETAB nanocrystals can effectively reduce the defect density of perovskite films. Impressively, the resulting  $ \mathrm{FA}_{1 - x}\mathrm{MA}_x\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  based planar devices exhibit a champion power conversion efficiency (PCE) of  $ 23.04\% $  (certified:  $ 22.10\% $ ) with a voltage loss of only  $ 390~\mathrm{mV} $ . Besides, the BETAB nanocrystals could simultaneously increase the hydrophobic property of perovskite films and prevent the reaction and formation of 2D perovskites during device operation. Correspondingly, the resulted devices exhibit excellent stability under moisture, heating, and operational tracking conditions.

In this case, the primary ammonium ions with the formula   $  RNH_{3}^{+}  $   (R is an alkyl or aryl group) have been mostly tailored by either varying the organic chain length or geometrical patterns [18–23]. One challenge for this approach is to control the thickness and uniformity of the 2D perovskite layer to achieve excellent charge transfer, as the 2D perovskite intrinsically confers large exciton binding energies, posing poor optoelectronic behaviors and low device performance [21,24]. Furthermore, the optoelectronic properties of a 2D perovskite can be affected by many factors, including molecular aggregation [13], crystalline orientation [25,26], length of the alkyl chain [20], and inappropriate solvents [27]. To the best of our knowledge, it remains challenging to deliver optimal 2D layer onto 3D perovskite film to gain higher device efficiency while ensuring a stabilized 2D–3D perovskite interfaces.

Considering the complexity and diversity of surface defects, multifunctional molecules with different functional groups were designed for different types of defects [28]. For example, Huang et.al employed the quaternary ammonium salts to passivate both anionic Pb-I antisites and cationic Pb clusters [29]. An issue with this approach is that most treated perovskite interfaces are probably converted into 2D perovskites under external conditions, especially under heating. Taken phenethylammonium iodide (PEAI) as an example, the reaction of the PEAI layer on perovskite surface during device operation would reduce the passivation effect of PEAI and affect the long-term stability of devices [12].

In this work, we develop a novel nanocrystal-pinning approach for defects passivation by introducing 2-bromoethyltrimethylammonium bromide (BETAB) nanocrystals onto the surface of perovskite films. The BETAB colloidal solution is prepared by self-assembling of BETAB molecules in the isopropanol solvent. By comparing different organic molecules, we find that the bromo group  $ (\mathrm{C}_2\mathrm{H}_4\mathrm{Br}) $  in BETAB is critical for the self-assembling process. Theoretical simulation and experimental results indicate BETAB suppresses the generation of metallic  $ \mathrm{Pb}(0) $ , passivating iodine vacancies, and the Pb-I antisites defects on perovskite films. Correspondingly, BETAB treatment results in prolonged charge carrier lifetimes and improved open-circuit voltages  $ (V_{\mathrm{OC}}) $  with a high value of  $ 1.14\mathrm{V} $  with the voltage loss of only  $ 390\mathrm{mV} $ , much lower than

that of control devices without modification (450 mV). We achieve a champion high efficiency of 23.04% (with a certificated efficiency of 22.10%) for planar PSCs, which is among the highest level on record for planar-structure PSCs. We also demonstrate an efficiency of 20.84% for large-area devices (with an active area of   $  1 \, cm^2  $  ). Moreover, the nanocrystals exhibit excellent thermal stability without the conversion into a 2D perovskite phase. Impressively, the nanocrystal-treated devices show significantly improved moisture and thermal stability, which also retains over 90% of the initial efficiency after 500 h of operational tracking.

We used scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to characterize the surface morphologies of BETAB nanocrystals modified  $ \mathrm{FA}_{1 - x}\mathrm{MA}_x\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  films (Fig. 1c-d). The pristine perovskite film was highly crystallized with crystal grain sizes on the sub-micron scale. For the BETAB treated perovskite films (Fig. 1d), nanoparticles are clearly observed on the perovskite surfaces (Fig. S2). The Br mapping by Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) indicates the uniformly capping of BETAB nanoparticles (Fig. S3). The surface roughness of the perovskite film before and after BETAB coating are about  $ 27.0\mathrm{nm} $  and  $ 24.8\mathrm{nm} $ , respectively, suggesting almost no morphology change of perovskite films as confirmed by atomic force microscopy (AFM) images (Fig. S4). It is noted that these BETAB nanocrystals cannot be washed away by IPA (Fig. 1e), implying a strong binding with the perovskite surface [32].

To understand the crystal feature of BETAB nanoparticles, we employed grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS). Fig. 1f-g presents the 2D GIWAXS patterns of the control and BETAB-treated perovskite films. A distinct signal at a value of  $  q_{xy}  $  of  $  0.73\AA^{-1}  $  along the direction parallel to the substrate was detected for the BETAB-treated film (Fig. 1h); another signal was observed at a value of  $  q_z  $  of  $  0.67\AA^{-1}  $  along the perpendicular direction to the substrate, consistent with the adhesion of BETAB (Fig. S5). These features suggested that BETAB nanocrystals are oriented to the perovskite surface.

To further understand the binding of BETAB nanocrystals with perovskite films, we studied the effect of crystal distribution with different incubation times. As expected, the size of the nanocrystals was

not affected by the incubation time (Figs. S6 and S7). While the quantity of BETAB nanocrystals increased slightly with increasing the incubation time and reached a maximum coverage after 2 min loading. Increasing the incubation time thereafter did not further increase the amount of BETAB nanocrystals on perovskite surface, suggesting a correlation between the nanocrystal adhesion and the charge sites on perovskite surface [33]. Therefore, 2 min loading time is viewed as the optimal time for our BETAB nanocrystals.

To understand the formation mechanism of BETAB nanocrystals, we examined the surface morphology of a similar passivating molecule  $ n $ -hexyltrimethylammonium bromide (HTAB), which replaces the  $ \mathrm{C_2H_4Br} $  moiety of BETAB with the  $ \mathrm{C_6H_{13}} $  alkyl chain [34]. Interestingly, HTAB showed a full surface coverage without nanocrystal formation (Fig. S8). It is worth noting that solubilities of HTAB and BETAB vary greatly in IPA, where  $ 2\mathrm{mg / ml} $  for BETAB and  $ 600~\mathrm{mg / ml} $  for HTAB. We further dissolved the molecule BETAB in other two different solvents (MeOH and  $ \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} $ ), the absence of typical Tyndall effects in these two solutions indicated that the molecule BETAB did not form into nanocrystals in their precursor solutions (Fig. S9). These result suggested that the formation of the self-assembled nanocrystals related with the decreased solubility of molecules induced by the Br group.

Besides the formation of nanocrystals, another reason for choosing the BETAB molecule is because its quaternary ammonium cation and bromine anion are expected to passivate both positively and negatively charged defects [9]. To elucidate the passivation effect of BETAB on the improvement of device performance, the chemical nature of the BETAB on the perovskite surface was confirmed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). As expected, the steady-state PL intensity of the BETAB-treated sample was much stronger than

that of the control sample (Fig. S11). Meanwhile, the BETAB modification increased the carrier lifetime of perovskites from  $ 0.52\mu \mathrm{s} $  to  $ 2.13\mu \mathrm{s} $  (Fig. 2c and Table S1). The PL and PL decay measurements indicate decreased defects and hence enhanced radiative recombination in the BETAB-treated perovskite film [20].

To understand the mechanism of BETAB passivation on perovskite surface, we employ first-principles density functional theory (DFT) computational analyses through the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) with a slab model of both materials [43]. We firstly simulated the interaction energetics for two surface terminations with the BETAB molecule: MAI- and  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ -terminated perovskite (100) surfaces. The corresponding interfacial binding energies  $ (E_{\mathrm{int}}) $  were 0.17 and  $ 0.32\mathrm{eV} $ , respectively (Fig. 3a). The higher binding energy of the latter suggests that the  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ -terminated perovskite interface was favorable to the combination with BETAB, which are consistent with the XPS results. Then, we also applied DFT to examine the effective defect passivation of BETAB. For vacancy defects, one  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  or MAI unit was removed randomly from the perovskite-BETAB interface; for the Pb-I antisites, the positions of Pb and I atoms were flipped [5]. We found that BETAB at the top of the perovskite surface had a higher defect formation energy (Table S3), indicating that it would be less favorable to form trap states on a BETAB-treated perovskite surface. Previous studies have suggested that antisites are the most detrimental defects (deep-level defects), and

that Pb-I antisite defects form more readily and predominate on the top perovskite surface, due to a lower formation energy relative to that in the bulk [44]. Fig. 3b plots the calculated density of states (DOS) of Pb-I antisite for the BETAB-passivated and unpassivated (control) perovskite surfaces. In the absence of BETAB treatment, an emerging peak appeared at  $ 1.38\mathrm{eV} $  in the middle of the band gap, indicating that a deep level defect was present for the unpassivated sample; this defect could capture holes and become a recombination center. Thus, the Pb-I antisite defect became shallower after treating the perovskite surface with BETAB. We further visualized the electron distribution in the structure model. The Pb-I antisite led to localized charge states for the unpassivated sample (Fig. 3c). In contrast, the BETAB-treated perovskite interface featured a delocalized electronic distribution (Fig. 3d), indicating that BETAB passivated the Pb-I antisite defects [5]. Overall, the incorporation of BETAB at the perovskite-HTL interface could result in an energetically lower density of interfacial trap states.

The thickness of the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  layer, the perovskite active film, the HTL (Spiro-OMeTAD), and the Au electrode were approximately 30, 800, 150, and  $ 150\mathrm{nm} $ , respectively. BETAB modification resulted in an increased PCE from  $ 20.24\% $  to  $ 22.70\% $  with the champion efficiency of  $ 23.04\% $  (Fig. 4c). Particularly, the  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  was increased from  $ 1.08\mathrm{V} $  to  $ 1.14\mathrm{V} $  after BETAB passivation (Table S4). The BETAB-passivated devices also demonstrated better reproducibility than that of the control devices, as indicated by statistical distributions of the PCEs (Fig. 4f, Fig. S14, and Table S5). Moreover, the device with BETAB modification exhibited less hysteresis with the hysteresis index of  $ 3.4\% $ , much lower than that for the control device ( $ 10.2\% $ ). The  $ J_{\mathrm{SC}} $  values from the  $ J - V $  scans were further confirmed from external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra (Fig. 4e), which gave integrated current densities of 23.77 and  $ 24.31\mathrm{mA/cm}^2 $  for the control and BETAB-modified devices, respectively. We also measured the stabilized power outputs of the control and BETAB-modified solar cells by operating these devices at the maximum power point (MPP) under full sunlight illumination at AM  $ 1.5\mathrm{G} $  conditions. The best-performing BETAB-capped device (Fig. 4d) delivered a stabilized steady-state efficiency of  $ 22.25\% $  with the photocurrent density at  $ 23.19\mathrm{mA/cm}^2 $  under a constant bias voltage of  $ 0.96\mathrm{V} $  for over  $ 30\mathrm{min} $ . For the control device prepared without BETAB in comparison, the output was just  $ 19.74\% $  (Fig. S15). The

best-performing BETAB-treated device was also certified with a PCE of 22.10% by the accredited PV Metrology Laboratory of the Institute of Metrology (NIM) in China. This represents one of the best performing planar PSCs ever reported (Fig. S16).

We also find the larger-area BETAB-treated devices (1 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ) can retain the high efficiency (20.84%), with only a slight drop in the FF, when compared with the small-size (0.1 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ) devices (Fig. 5a and Table S6). The high efficiency suggests the relatively uniform distribution of nanocrystals, which can be indicated by time-resolved confocal fluorescence imaging (TCFI) [45]. As shown in Fig. S17, the BETAB-treated film at a large scale of 200   $  \mu  $  m x 200   $  \mu  $  m showed a prolonged carrier lifetime with a uniform orange color, in contrast to the green color of the control film. These changes were also evident in the phasor plots of the corresponding raw lifetime data extracted from the TCFI images (Fig. S18). The control film exhibited a narrow distribution between 404 and 446 ns with a peak at 440 ns. In contrast, an overall higher average lifetime at 1000 ns was recorded for the BETAB-treated sample.

Besides high efficiency, another advantage of nanocrystal passivation is to simultaneously increase hydrophobic property and thermal stability of perovskite devices. Specifically, as shown in Fig. 5b, the enlarged contact angle of a water droplet from   $  67^{\circ}  $   for the control film to   $  82^{\circ}  $   after BETAB-passivation. Meanwhile, unlike many organic molecules, BETAB nanocrystals can prevent the reaction between passivating molecules and perovskite films with the formation of 2D perovskites during the device operation, which can be confirmed by XRD and PL results (Figs. Correspondingly, the BETAB-passivated device maintained 90% of its initial PCE after 80 days under a humidity of 30–35% at room temperature, while the control device decreased dramatically to 50% of its initial value (Fig. 5b). The BETAB-passivated PSC also displayed superior thermal stability (maintaining over 85% of its initial PCE) when compared with the control device (losing 40% of the initial efficiency) after annealing at 60 °C for 200 h in the dark (Fig. 5c). # 3. Conclusion

We have developed a novel nanocrystal-pinning approach for highly efficient and stable planar PSCs. The modification can be controlled easily by dripping BETAB colloidal solution onto perovskite films. We also reveal the critical function of bromo group (  $  C_{2}H_{4}Br  $  ) for the nanocrystal formation possibly due to the decreased solubility in IPA solvent. Experimental studies and theoretical calculations confirmed that BETAB can effectively retard the generation of metallic Pb(0) on perovskite surfaces, decreasing multiple types of trap density and suppressing nonradiative recombination. Correspondingly, the champion planar PSCs showed an efficiency as high as 23.04% with a certified value of 22.10%. More attractively, the BETAB passivation significantly improved the stability of devices against moisture and heat. The passivated PSC retained over 90% of its pristine PCE after exposure in air (around 30% RH) 80 days, and maintain 85% of its initial efficiency after thermal aging at 60 °C for 200 h. Besides, the BETAB-passivated PSCs also displayed improved operating stability with retaining over 90% of the initial efficiency after 500 h of operational tracking. We believe that this novel passivation process can stimulate significant interest in the efforts to boost effective passivation and large-scale fabrication of PSCs for future commercialization.
