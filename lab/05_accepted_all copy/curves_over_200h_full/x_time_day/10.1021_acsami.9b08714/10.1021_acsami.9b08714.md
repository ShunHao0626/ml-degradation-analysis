# Optimization of   $  CuIn_{1-x}Ga_{x}S_{2}  $   Nanoparticles and Their Application in the Hole-Transporting Layer of Highly Efficient and Stable Mixed-Halide Perovskite Solar Cells

ABSTRACT: Inorganic hole-transport materials (HTMs) have been frequently applied in perovskite solar cells (PSCs) and are a promising solution to improve the poor stability of PSCs. In this study, we investigate solution-processed copper indium gallium disulfide (CIGS) nanocrystals (NCs) as a dopant-free inorganic HTM in n-i-p type PSCs. Moreover,   $  Cs_{0.05}(MA_{0.17}-FA_{0.83})_{0.95}Pb(I_{0.83}Br_{0.17})_{3}  $   mixed-halide perovskite with proper crystalline quality and long-time stability was utilized as the light-absorbing layer under ambient conditions. To optimize the cell performance and better charge extraction from the perovskite layer, the Ga concentration in the   $  \mathrm{Cu(In_{1-x}Ga_{x})S_{2}}  $   composition was changed, and the X value was altered between 0.0 and 0.75. It was shown that the CIGS band gap enhances with increasing Ga content; thus, with tunable band gaps and engineering of the energy level alignment, a better

collection of photogenerated holes and a reduced electron–hole recombination rate could be achieved. The maximum power conversion efficiency of 15.6% was obtained for the PSC with   $  \mathrm{Cu(In_{0.5}Ga_{0.5})S_{2}}  $   hole-transport layer composition, which is the highest efficiency reported so far for CIGS-based dopant-free PSCs. This value is very close to the efficiency of devices fabricated with doped spiro-OMeTAD as an organic HTM. Additionally, the stability of nonencapsulated PSCs was studied, and CIGS-based devices demonstrated 70% retention after 90 days of aging in the dark and in 50% relative humidity conditions. This result is quite better than the similar measurements for the doped spiro-OMeTAD-based devices.

KEYWORDS: perovskite solar cell, hole transport layer, CIGS, energy conversion efficiency, longtime stability

# 1. INTRODUCTION

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted considerable attention owing to their high power conversion efficiency (PCE) reaching 22.7% with relatively low cost of fabrication compared with conventional silicon-based solar cells.  $  ^{1,2}  $   The excellent optoelectronic properties of organic–inorganic perovskites such as the high absorption coefficient, broad band light absorption, direct and tunable band gap energy, low exciton binding energy, ambipolar charge transport, and long charge-carrier diffusion length lead to extremely high performance in photovoltaic devices.  $  ^{3-9}  $

In spite of the high efficiency and relatively low-cost fabrication methods of PSCs, the stability of perovskites still remains the main challenge.  $  ^{10,11}  $   The degradation of PSCs occurs under moisture,  $  ^{11,12}  $   light,  $  ^{13,14}  $   oxygen,  $  ^{15}  $   and temperature.  $  ^{11-15}  $   However, part of the instability of PSCs is related to the organic hole-transport materials (HTMs) and the dopants that are usually used to improve its conductivity. 2,2',7,7'-Tetrakis(N,N-di-p-methoxyphenylamine)-9,9'-spiro-bifluorene (spiro-OMeTAD) is the conventional HTM used in high performance n-i-p type PSCs. To improve the hole conductivity of spiro-OMeTAD, lithium bis-

(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide is usually used to enhance the oxygen uptake and oxidation of spiro-OMeTAD.  $  ^{16}  $   However, it is well-known that this dopant exhibits a tendency to absorb moisture,  $  ^{17-19}  $   leading to degradation of the perovskite film.  $  ^{20,21}  $   Besides, photo-oxidation and deformation of spiro-OMeTAD under illumination and temperature are other main factors in the performance deterioration of the cells.  $  ^{16,22}  $   In addition to stability problems of spiro-OMeTAD, it is expensive and hard to synthesize and hence not ideal for cost-effective PSCs. For a suitable HTM, proper energy-level alignment, high hole mobility, being dopant-free, good stability, and simple deposition are significant factors.  $  ^{23}  $   Inorganic HTMs can intrinsically demonstrate higher hole mobility, better stability, and lower cost of synthesis compared to organic materials.  $  ^{24,25}  $   Among them, inorganic copper-based materials such as CuI,   $  CuO_{x}  $  ,   $  Cu_{2}O  $  , CuSCN, and copper indium gallium disulfide (CIGS) compounds have been introduced as spiro-OMeTAD replacements in PSCs.  $  ^{24-28}  $

CIG(S,Se) is known as a light absorber in thin-film solar cells.  $  ^{29,30}  $   However, it has been recently used as an HTM layer in PSCs (Table S1).  $  ^{31-34}  $   CIGS is usually deposited using vacuum-based techniques at high deposition temperatures. These methods are not compatible with the PSC fabrication process, therefore, the solution-based techniques have been applied under ambient conditions and low temperature.  $  ^{35}  $   The p-type CIGS compounds synthesized based on solution-processing are promising materials as HTMs in terms of cost and being dopant-free.  $  ^{36}  $   There are a few reports on the application of CIGS or CIS compounds as a hole-transport layer (HTL) in PSCs. For instance, Dai et al. reported 6.57 and 9.15% efficiencies with CIS and CIGSe, respectively.  $  ^{31,32}  $   These efficiencies are still far from the best record efficiencies of PSCs (23%), which utilize organic HTM. The energy band gap of   $  \mathrm{Cu(In_{1-X}Ga_{X})(S_{Y}Se_{1-Y})_{2}}  $   semiconductor is tunable by changing X (Ga/Ga + In) and Y (S/S + Se) ratios.  $  ^{37,38}  $   Consequently, by changing X and Y, the positions of the conduction band and valence band change. The corresponding energy band gap of   $  \mathrm{Cu(In_{1-X}Ga_{X})(S_{Y}Se_{1-Y})_{2}}  $   could be changed in the range of 1.0–2.4 eV based on the literature.

Here, we report the application of   $  \mathrm{Cu(In_{1-x}Ga_{x})S_{2}}  $   nanocrystalline layer as an inorganic HTM in triple cations mixed-halide PSCs. The stable CIGS ink was prepared by the synthesis of nanoparticles in oleylamine solvent followed by dispersion in chloroform and deposited on the substrates by the spin-coating method. The percentage of Ga in   $  \mathrm{Cu(In_{1-x}Ga_{x})S_{2}}  $   nanocrystals (NCs) was changed, the X value was altered in the range of 0.0–0.75, and the photovoltaic performance was measured. The change in Ga content created the possibility of tuning the band gap energy of the CIGS NCs from 1.5 to 1.8 eV. This was utilized for better optimization of the energy-level alignment in fabricated PSCs and resulted in better collection of the photogenerated holes and lower rate of electron–hole recombinations. Finally, the best efficiency of about 15.6% was obtained for the PSC with the optimized   $  \mathrm{Cu(In_{0.5}Ga_{0.5})S_{2}}  $   HTL composition. Furthermore, the PSCs which were fabricated with   $  \mathrm{Cu(In_{0.5}Ga_{0.5})S_{2}}  $   HTL demonstrated better stability and close PCE compared to those of the spiro-OMeTAD-based PSC.

# 2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

2.1. Synthesis of   $  \mathrm{Cu(In_{1-x}Ga_{x})S_{2}}  $  . Chalcopyrite CIGS nanoparticles were synthesized using a simple method as below: 1 mmol CuCl, x mmol InCl  $  _{3}  $  , y mmol Ga(acac)  $  _{3}  $   (x and y selected according to the final CIGS stoichiometry between 0 and 1 so that   $  x + y = 1  $  ), and 2 mmol S element were added to 20 mL of oleylamine in a three-necked balloon at room temperature. After adding them, the temperature rose to 120 °C, and the balloon was held for 1 h under a N  $  _{2}  $   atmosphere at this temperature by stirring. Then, the temperature was increased to 200 °C at 8 min, and the mixture was held for 1 h at this temperature. Finally, for completing the synthesis, the temperature was increased to 240 °C at 3 min, and the mixture was held for 10 min at this temperature. Finally, the balloon was exited from the heater and cooled in air to room temperature. Then, 10 mL of chloroform and immediately 5 mL of ethanol were added to the mixture. Finally the nanoparticles were centrifuged and washed with chloroform and ethanol as the solvent and anti-solvent materials for more purification.

2.2. Fabrication of PSC. We applied the conventional structure of   $  TiO_{2}  $  /mixed-halide perovskite/HTL/Au, as reported in our previous work for fabrication of PSCs.  $  ^{8,25}  $   After deposition of   $  Cs_{0.05}(MA_{0.17}-FA_{0.83})_{0.95}Pb(I_{0.83}Br_{0.17})_{3}  $   mixed-halide perovskites using the anti-solvent method on the substrate, organic and inorganic HTLs were deposited as below. The prepared spiro-OMeTAD solution was spin-coated at 5000 rpm for 30 s. The inorganic HTL was also deposited over the perovskite film by solution processing and the prepared CIGS ink. The CIGS layers were spin-coated from its 20 mg/mL solution in chloroform at 3000 rpm for 60 s and then heated at 100 °C for 5 min. Finally, the metal contact (Au) was evaporated onto the HTL.

2.3. Characterization. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images was observed using a TESCAN-Mira 3 field-emission scanning electron microscope. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were measured by a PANalytical, X'Pert Pro MPD X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation. Ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption spectroscopy was performed by a PerkinElmer Lambda 25 equipment. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were recorded on a PGE-16 potentiostat/galvanostat at 50 mV s  $  ^{-1}  $  scan rate. CV measurements were performed in a three-electrode cell consisting of a platinum wire as the counter electrode, Ag/Ag  $  ^{+}  $  (Ag wires with 0.01 M AgNO  $  _{3}  $  in acetonitrile) as the reference electrode, glassy carbon discs as the working electrode (0.25 cm  $  ^{2}  $ ), and a Pt wire as the counter electrode, and Ag/Ag (Ag wires with 0.01 M AgNO  $  _{3}  $ ) in acetonitrile and 0.1 M tetrabutylammoniumpercholorat (TBAPF6) dissolved in acetonitrile were used as the supporting electrolyte.

# 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

CIGS NCs with different Ga contents were synthesized, dispersed in chloroform, and deposited by the spin-coating method. Figure 1a demonstrates the XRD patterns of  $ \mathrm{Cu(In_{1 - X}Ga_X)S_2} $  compounds with  $ X = 0 $ , 0.25, 0.5, and 0.75. A strong typical peak at  $ 2\theta $  angle of  $ 27.9^{\circ} $  with several weak peaks at  $ 2\theta $  angles of  $ 46.48^{\circ} $  and  $ 55.1^{\circ} $  could be seen for  $ X = 0 $  in the XRD patterns. The position of the strong peaks shifts to higher diffraction angle of  $ 2\theta $  with increasing  $ X $ , which was previously observed.[39,40] Furthermore, strong peaks at  $ 28.2^{\circ} $ ,  $ 28.48^{\circ} $ , and  $ 28.68^{\circ} $  have been observed for  $ X = 0.25 $ , 0.5, and 0.75, respectively. The revealed peaks in accordance with JCPDS 85-1575 or 38-0777 indicate the formation of the tetragonal chalcopyrite structure of CIGS for all  $ X $  values.[41,42] No other impurities were observed in the XRD patterns. The optical transmittance and optical band gap of CIGS films are displayed in Figure 1b,c. Transmittance curves show an absorption edge corresponding to the band gap of the film, which shows a clear blue shift with increasing Ga content. As expected, addition and replacement of the Ga element results in larger band gap energy.[37,43,44] Furthermore, this blue shift is measured about  $ 0.3\mathrm{eV} $  and demonstrates a change from 1.5 to  $ 1.8\mathrm{eV} $  in the energy band gap of CIGS NCs. The inset in Figure 1c shows CIGS NC inks with different  $ X $  values. It could be seen that the color of CIGS solution changes and turns light brown for higher  $ X $  values. The increase of transparency implies the higher energy band gap of CIGS NCs. In order to determine the position of the conduction band minimum (CBM) and valence band maximum (VBM) energy levels of the CIGS nanocrystalline film, the cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements have been used.[45,46] Figure 1d displays the CV measurements of CIGS NCs for different  $ X $  values. As can be seen, variation of the chemical composition of CIGS NCs according to the optical measurement in Figure 1b and other literature[41,47-49] leads to alter the energy band gap as well as the position of CBM and VBM in these semiconductors. The oxidation and reduction potential for  $ X = 0 $  occurred ranging between  $ 0.8\mathrm{V} $  and  $ -0.7\mathrm{V} $  (vs  $ \mathrm{Ag / Ag^{+}} $ ),

respectively. The reduction potential moved to the negative value and the oxidation potential nearly shifts to the positive value with increasing Ga/In ratio. Therefore, the UV–vis absorption spectra (Figure 1b) and CV measurement (Figure 1d) were applied to construct energy band diagrams as shown in Figure 1e.  $  ^{50}  $   The calculated energy band diagram is compatible with the literature.  $  ^{51}  $   Varying the content of Ga element mainly contributes to the conduction band edge energies (  $  E_{CB}  $  ) compared to the valence band edge energies (  $  E_{VB}  $  ).  $  ^{52,53}  $

Figure 2a demonstrates the UV–vis absorption spectra of the perovskite layer and the perovskite/CIGS double layer with different Ga percentages. As can be seen in Figure 2a, absorption of the perovskite/CIGS double layer slightly increased compared to that of the perovskite layer, which seems to be related to light absorption of CIGS. To further study the mechanism of   $  \mathrm{Cu(In_{1-x}Ga_{x})S_{2}}  $   as a HTL over the perovskite film, we measured the steady-state PL spectra and compared with the perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD double layers. The highest reduction of perovskite PL intensity belongs to 50% Ga, which is approximately similar to quenching of spiro-OMeTAD. Fewer carrier recombination displays better charge extraction from the perovskite layer to the HTL.

Figure 3a,b shows the top-view SEM images of the perovskite   $  \left[\mathrm{Cs}_{0.05}\left(\mathrm{MA}_{0.17}\mathrm{FA}_{0.83}\right)_{0.95}\mathrm{Pb}\left(\mathrm{I}_{0.83}\mathrm{Br}_{0.17}\right)_{3}\right]  $   layer and the perovskite/CIGS double layer, respectively. According to Figure 3a, the mixed-halide perovskite layer with the antisolvent method is well-formed with grain sizes in the range of   $  0.3-1\mu m  $  . As can be seen in Figure 3b,c the CIGS layer has been coated on the perovskite layer uniformly, without cracks and pinholes. The cross-sectional image of the completed device of fluorinated tin oxide (FTO)/mp-  $  TiO_{2}  $  /perovskite/CIGS/Au is shown in Figure 3d. This figure indicates and confirms the high quality and uniform coverage of the CIGS layer. The cross-sectional image of the completed device with spiro-OMeTAD is shown in Figure S1. It could be observed that the thickness of the CIGS layer with excellent performance is less than that of the spiro-OMeTAD layer. As mentioned above, the reference and CIGS-based PSCs with FTO/  $  TiO_{2}  $  /perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD/Au and FTO/  $  TiO_{2}  $  /perovskite/Cu(  $  In_{1-x}Ga_{x}  $  )  $  S_{2}  $  /Au structures (X=0, 0.25, 0.5, and 0.75) were fabricated, and photovoltaic parameters were measured.

# 4. CONCLUSIONS

We have studied  $ \mathrm{Cu(In_{1 - x}Ga_x)S_2} $  nanocrystalline films as HTM in PSCs. We demonstrate that by adding Ga in CIGS compounds, the PCE of PSCs is improved to about  $ 44\% $  for  $ \mathrm{X} = 0.5 $  compared to  $ \mathrm{X} = 0 $ . In other words, a good PCE of  $ 15.6\% $  has been achieved for  $ \mathrm{X} = 0.5 $  while this improvement was due to the controlled band gap changes of the CIGS NCs and engineering of the bands alignment in the optimized cell. This could create a better blocking for the photo-generated electrons in perovskite layer to move toward the Au metallic contact as well as an efficient hole extraction. Consequently, the reduction recombination's rate and increment of the filling factor of the device have been obtained. Furthermore, the stability of devices fabricated with dopant-free CIGS NCs was increased acceptably. Ultimately, PCSs were produced using a low-cost inorganic HTL and an easy deposition method.
