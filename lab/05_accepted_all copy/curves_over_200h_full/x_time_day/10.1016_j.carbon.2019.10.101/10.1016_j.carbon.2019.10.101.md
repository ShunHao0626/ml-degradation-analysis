# Abstract

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have rapidly achieved a remarkable power conversion efficiency (PCE). However, the inherent instability of components has impeded industrialization. Here we employed atomically-thin impermeable graphene (3 layers) as an interfacial barrier for moisture,  $ \Gamma^{-} $ ion, and Au diffusion. A new graphene transfer method was developed, and the position was carefully optimized between the CuSCN and Au electrode in a full cell considering the band alignment of cell components. There was a negligible change in maximum PCE (15.2-15.8%) with the incorporation of graphene due to the high hole mobility of graphene. The moisture intrusion was significantly reduced under  $ 85\% $  relative humidity (RH) for 3 weeks, suppressing  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  formation. The graphene barrier maintained  $ >94\% $  of initial PCE under  $ 50\% $  RH for 30 days. It mostly inhibited  $ \Gamma^{-} $ ion migration and perfectly blocked Au diffusion between the perovskite and Au electrode, allowing reversible recovery of electrical power during 3 continuous illumination/dark cycles (12 h each) with a positive bias. A further improvement in the graphene transfer method may enable a perfect single-layer graphene barrier, without compromising the average PCE of multiple devices.

# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) based on organic-inorganic metal halide have rapidly attained the remarkable power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 25.2% [1] from the firstly reported value of 9.7% in 2012 [2]. This enhancement was achieved by morphological and compositional engineering for perovskite film crystallinity [3, 4], interface optimization [5, 6], and selective charge transport layers [7, 8]. Most of the highly efficient PSCs employed 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-di-p-methoxyphenyl-amine)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (spiro-OMeTAD) as an organic hole transport material (HTM) with a glass/FTO/TiO  $  _{2}  $  /perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD/Au (or Ag) structure [3-8].

However, the inherent instability of PSC elements is one of hurdles for practical applications [9, 10]. Firstly, the organic halide in perovskite is moisture sensitive, limiting long-term stability under a humid condition [9, 11]. The external encapsulation was proposed to prevent intrusion of ambient moisture into the perovskite layer [11]. However, the degradation of PCE was still observed even after the encapsulation or in an inert environment without moisture [11-13]. Secondly, the undesired reaction between  $ \Gamma^{-} $ ion and Au degraded the Au electrode and metal/HTM interface [12, 13]. The  $ \Gamma^{-} $ ion migration from perovskite to the metal electrode through a hole transport layer (HTL) was accelerated due to the positive bias, under constant light illumination, resulting in a severe irreversible PCE drop [12]. The reacted Au also diffused to perovskite through the HTL [12, 13]. Thirdly, the organic spiro-OMeTAD was unstable since it employed moisture-sensitive Li-bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (Li-TFSI) as a p-type dopant [14]. The thermally induced pinholes on the spiro-OMeTAD layer also degraded PCE [10, 11]. The inorganic CuSCN has been investigated as an alternative HTM due to the thermal and moisture stability [10, 15]. The CuSCN provided a high PCE ( $ >20\% $ ) [9, 10]. The low cost of CuSCN is also beneficial for large-scale industrialization [9, 10]. However, the permeable CuSCN could not prevent the moisture

diffusion and I  $  ^{-}  $  ion migration, limiting PCE stability [9, 10].

A  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  or Al-doped ZnO (AZO) interfacial barrier was constructed below the metal electrode, by the atomic layer deposition (ALD) method, to prevent moisture intrusion and  $ \Gamma $  ion migration [16, 17]. The egression of volatile components was also suppressed. However, the deposition method was complicated, and there was a trade-off relationship between PCE and impermeability due to the low electrical conductivity of metal oxide [16, 17]. Recently, a passivation layer was constructed by spin coating reduced graphene oxide (rGO) flakes on CuSCN [10]. The rGO interfacial layer (thickness:  $ \sim 10\mathrm{nm} $ ) between the CuSCN and Au electrode maintained stable PCE ( $ >95\% $  of initial value) under continuous illumination for  $ 500\mathrm{h} $  (1-sun light illumination, electrical bias:  $ 0.8\mathrm{V} $ ) in an inert  $ \mathbf{N}_2 $  environment [10]. The rGO interfacial layer successfully suppressed unfavorable reaction between the  $ \Gamma $  ion and Au electrode. However, it is difficult to completely reduce graphene oxide flakes, limiting charge mobility of the passivation layer [18]. Moreover, the film thickness cannot be significantly decreased while preserving impermeability. It is challenging to achieve a highly conductive and atomically thin rGO passivation layer by the spin coating process.

The impermeability, high hole mobility, and small thickness are required for an interfacial passivation layer. Graphene is a monolayer of carbon atoms covalently bonded in a hexagonal lattice [19]. It has high mechanical strength (130 GPa) [19], chemical stability [20], and impermeability to molecules and ions [21]. The hole mobility of graphene is as high as   $  24,000 \, cm^2 \, V^{-1} \, s^{-1}  $   [22] which is orders of magnitude higher than that of CuSCN (  $  0.01 \sim 0.1 \, cm^2 \, V^{-1} \, s^{-1}  $  ) [10]. It can also be synthesized in large scale (30 inch) by the chemical vapor deposition (CVD) method [23]. Graphene has been investigated as a conductive electrode for PSCs [24]. A perovskite film was also simply covered by graphene, and impermeability was investigated [25]. The graphene-

covered perovskite film maintained initial composition even after 3 months under a relative humidity (RH) of  $ 58\% $  [25]. However, a complete cell was not fabricated, and photovoltaic performance was not investigated [25].

Here we employed CVD-synthesized graphene (1.4 × 1.4 cm $  ^{2}  $ ) as an interfacial barrier for moisture,  $  \Gamma^{-}  $ ion, and Au diffusion. A new graphene transfer method was developed utilizing the meniscus of CuSCN precursor solution. Three layers of graphene (GRP(3)) were used since single layer could not provide perfect barrier performance due to the defects and holes generated during the transfer process. The position of GRP(3) in the full cell was carefully optimized between the CuSCN and Au electrode considering a band alignment of cell components. GRP(3) significantly reduced moisture diffusion to perovskite even under 85% RH for 3 weeks and suppressed the formation of PbI $  _{2}  $ . There was a negligible change in maximum PCE (15.2-15.8%) with the incorporation of GRP(3) due to the high hole mobility of graphene. However, the average PCE from 25 devices decreased from 14.9% to 13.9% due to the imperfections during the transfer process which need to be improved further in the future. The PSC with GRP(3) maintained >94% of initial PCE under a controlled humidity condition (50% RH) for 30 days. The graphene mostly inhibited  $  \Gamma^{-}  $ ion migration from the perovskite to Au electrode after 3 cycles of continuous light illumination (12 h, positive bias = 0.78 V) and dark state (12 h). It perfectly inhibited the diffusion of reacted Au to perovskite which was investigated by cross-sectional scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) and energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis. This allowed reversible recovery of the electrical power at a maximum power point during the 3 cycles. The graphene was an excellent barrier for moisture,  $  \Gamma^{-}  $ ion, and Au diffusion while maintaining the maximum PCE due to the high hole mobility. # 2. Experimental section

# 2.1. The synthesis of graphene:

Monolayer graphene was synthesized on an electropolished copper substrate (thickness: 0.25  $ \mu $ m, Nilaco) by the low pressure chemical vapor deposition method [26]. The raw copper substrate was firstly annealed at 1050  $ ^{\circ} $ C for 30 min. It was then electropolished in a H $ _{3} $ PO $ _{4} $  electrolyte (2.17 M) with an ethylene glycol additive for 5 min (current density: 380 mA/cm $ ^{2} $ , 65  $ ^{\circ} $ C) to decrease surface roughness [26]. The electropolished copper substrate was loaded in a 2 inch quartz tube to synthesize fully covered graphene [26]. The temperature was ramped to 1000  $ ^{\circ} $ C under H $ _{2} $  flow (30 sccm) and maintained constant at 1000  $ ^{\circ} $ C for 30 minutes [26]. In the next step, H $ _{2} $  (2 sccm) and CH $ _{4} $  (30 sccm) were introduced for 10 min. Finally, the substrate was rapid cooled by moving the furnace without any gas flow [26]. The PMMA dissolved in chlorobenzene (Aldrich, 46 mg/mL) was spin-coated on top of the graphene (Cu/GRP(1)/PMMA), and the Cu foil was etched using an FeCl $ _{3} $ -based Cu etchant (Transene, CE-100) to obtain the GRP(1)/PMMA.

# 2.2. The construction of Glass/FTO/TiO  $  _{2}  $  /Perovskite Structure:

All chemicals were of analytical grade and used as received without further purification. The chemical bath deposition method was used to construct a planar  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  layer on a fluorine doped  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $  (FTO)-coated glass (Pilkington, TEC 8) following a previously reported method [4, 7]. The FTO-coated glass was immersed in the  $ \mathrm{TiCl_4} $  solution (0.2 M in deionized water) in an oven (60 °C, 65 min) for the chemical bath deposition process. The CsFAMAPbI $ _{3-x} $ Br $ _x $  precursor solution was prepared by mixing FAI (GreatCell Solar, 1.17 M), PbI $ _2 $  (Alpha Aesar, 1.23 M), MABr (GreatCell Solar, 0.2 M), PbBr $ _2 $  (Alpha Aesar, 0.2 M), and CsI (Alpha Aesar, 0.03 M) in an anhydrous solvent (DMF:DMSO = 80:20 vol %). This was a lead excess composition [3]. The

# 2.3. Characterization:

The morphology was investigated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL, JSM7600F) and STEM equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray spectrometer (JEOL, JEM ARM 200F). The specimens for STEM measurements were prepared by a focused ion beam instrument (SII, SMI3050TB). The crystallinity was investigated by grazing incidence X-ray diffraction (GIXRD, Rigaku, D/MAX-2500/PC, Cu,  $ \mathrm{K_a} $  radiation,  $ \lambda = 1.5418\AA $ , incident X-ray angle:  $ 0.5^{\circ} $ ). The current density-voltage ( $ J-V $ ) characteristics were measured using a digital source meter (Keithley 2400) under the AM 1.5 one sun illumination (Oriel, Sol3ATM, 100 mW/cm²). The light intensity was calibrated by a reference silicon cell (Oriel, SRC-1000-TC-KG5-N). The  $ J-V $  curves were measured by reverse scan (scan rate: 0.3 V/s) unless otherwise specified. The active area was maintained constant ( $ 0.15~\mathrm{cm}^2 $ ) using a photo mask. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectrum was also measured (Oriel, Sol3ATM). The current at an electrical bias of 0.78 V was measured by a potentiostat/galvanostat (Biologic, SP-240). The water droplet contact angle was measured by Smartdrop (Femtofab, SDL200TEZD). The relative humidity was measured using a hygro-thermometer (CENTER Technology, CENTER-314). Time-resolved photoluminescence was measured using a confocal microscope system (NTEGRA SPECTRA, NT-MDT).

A schematic of the graphene interfacial barrier construction process in a PSC is shown in Fig. 1A. A monolayer graphene (  $  1.4 \times 1.4 \, cm^2  $  ) was synthesized on an electro-polished copper substrate (Cu/GRP(1)) by the low pressure CVD method (Fig. 1A-i, see Experimental Section for details) [26]. The electro-polishing process decreased surface roughness of the Cu substrate (  $  \sim 13 \, nm  $  ) which decreased defect and increased grain size of graphene (  $  > 3 \, \mum  $  ) [26]. The successful synthesis of high quality monolayer graphene was confirmed by Raman spectroscopy (see Supplementary Fig. S1a). The intensity ratio of G/2D mode was less than 0.5 with a negligible D band. The polymethyl methacrylate was spin-coated on top of the graphene (Cu/GRP(1)/PMMA), and the Cu foil was detached using an FeCl₃-based Cu etchant (Fig. 1A-ii). The GRP(1)/PMMA was thoroughly rinsed by floating it in deionized water for 3 h. The GRP(1)/PMMA was transferred on another Cu/GRP(1) to construct a double layer graphene (Cu/GRP(2)/PMMA). The process was repeated to further increase the graphene layer (Cu/GRP(3)/PMMA). The multi-stacked graphene layer (GRP(n),   $  1 \leq n \leq 3  $  ) was required due to the imperfections in graphene transfer process as will be discussed later [27]. The conventional aqueous graphene transfer method could not be directly used on a moisture-sensitive perovskite layer. Here we developed a new transfer method by controlling the meniscus of CuSCN precursor solution. The Cu substrate was chemically etched, and GRP(n)/PMMA was transferred on a polyethylene terephthalate (PET) frame (thickness:   $  100 \, \mum  $  ) with a square hole (area =   $  1 \times 1 \, cm^2  $  ) (Fig. 1A-iii). The edge of GRP(n)/PMMA was attached on the PET frame, and the central region was suspended.

The CuSCN specimen exhibited a decrease in perovskite peak (14.1°) and an increase in   $  PbI_{2}  $   peak at 30% RH (Fig. 2B). This indicated that external moisture penetrated through the CuSCN layer decomposing the underlying perovskite. In contrast, CuSCN/GRP(1) and CuSCN/GRP(3) specimens did not show any indication of perovskite degradation. The top graphene layer effectively blocked moisture intrusion. The decomposition of perovskite in the CuSCN specimen was accelerated at an increased RH of 50% (Fig. 2C). There was a large increase in   $  PbI_{2}  $   peak. However, the   $  PbI_{2}  $   peak was negligible for the CuSCN/GRP(1) and CuSCN/GRP(3) specimens. Under the highest RH condition (85%), the perovskite decomposition was severe for the CuSCN specimen. The intensity of   $  PbI_{2}  $   peak also increased for the CuSCN/GRP(1) specimen. The CuSCN/GRP(3) specimen demonstrated excellent moisture impermeability. There were only tiny   $  PbI_{2}  $   peak at 12.6° and hydrated (FA)  $  _{4}  $  PbI  $  _{6}  $  ·H  $  _{2}  $  O peak at 11.4° [31]. This could be due to the trace amount of water penetrated through the small defected region of the multilayer graphene generated during the transfer process. A further improvement in the graphene transfer technology may provide perfect moisture barrier property in the future. There was negligible change in CuSCN peaks for all specimens demonstrating outstanding moisture stability of CuSCN [10, 15].

The position of graphene in the full cell was optimized considering the energy band alignment of cell components. Fig. 3A shows the band alignment where the graphene is located between the perovskite and CuSCN. The graphene was directly transferred on  $ \mathrm{CsFAMAPbI}_{3 - x}\mathrm{Br}_x $  by the MGT method using a diethyl sulfide droplet, constructing a  $ \mathrm{GRP}(n) / \mathrm{CuSCN} $  specimen ( $ 1\leq n\leq 3 $ ). The  $ \mathrm{GRP}(n) / \mathrm{CuSCN} $  structure also blocked moisture intrusion to perovskite as shown in Supplementary Fig. S4. However, the direct contact between  $ \mathrm{GRP}(n) $  and  $ \mathrm{CsFAMAPbI}_{3 - x}\mathrm{Br}_x $  induced undesirable back-electron transfer. The Dirac point of graphene is  $ -4.4\mathrm{eV} $  which does not significantly vary even for multiple layers [32]. The Dirac point of graphene was higher than the

highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) level of   $  CsFAMAPbI_{3-x}Br_{x}  $   (-5.4 eV) providing a hole extraction path [7, 33]. However, the Dirac point was lower than the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) level (-3.8 eV) of   $  CsFAMAPbI_{3-x}Br_{x}  $   simultaneously allowing a path for back-electron transfer [7, 33]. The conduction band edge of CuSCN (-1.8V) was higher than that of   $  CsFAMAPbI_{3-x}Br_{x}  $   blocking the back-electron transfer. The progressive increase in valence band edge (-5.4, -5.3, and -4.4 eV) of   $  CsFAMAPbI_{3-x}Br_{x}  $ , CuSCN, and graphene constructed a continuous hole extraction path to the Au electrode. The time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurement was conducted to compare charge separation behavior of the   $  CsFAMAPbI_{3-x}Br_{x}/CuSCN  $   and   $  CsFAMAPbI_{3-x}Br_{x}/CuSCN/GRP(3)  $   as shown in Supplementary Fig. S5 [4]. The average carrier life time (2.63 ns) of the   $  CsFAMAPbI_{3-x}Br_{x}/CuSCN/GRP(3)  $   was similar with that (2.60 ns) of the   $  CsFAMAPbI_{3-x}Br_{x}/CuSCN  $ . This indicated that the graphene layer did not degrade the charge separation behavior. The solar cell performance was consistent with the energy band analysis (Fig. 3C). The PCE of the GRP(1)/CuSCN/Au PSC was only 9.1% due to the back-electron transfer. It further decreased as the number of graphene layers increased. In contrast, the PCE of the CuSCN/GRP(1)/Au PSC was 13.7%. The PCE slightly increased with increasing graphene layers reaching 14.3 % for the CuSCN/GRP(3)/Au PSC.

The CuSCN/GRP(3) enabled selective hole transport by the carefully aligned energy band structure. The high hole mobility of graphene [22, 26] did not degrade the best PCE, compared with that of the CuSCN/Au PSC, unlike metal oxide-based interfacial barriers [16, 17].

# 4. Conclusion

Graphene (1-3 layers) was employed as a conductive diffusion barrier between the CuSCN and Au electrodes by carefully optimizing the energy band alignment. The incorporation of graphene (CuSCN/GRP(3)/Au PSC) did not compromise the best PCE of 15.2 % ( $ V_{\text{OC}} = 1.013 $  V,  $ J_{\text{SC}} = 21.3 $  mA/cm $ ^2 $ , and  $ FF = 70.9\% $ ), compared with the cell without graphene, due to the high hole mobility. The average photovoltaic performance from 25 cells decreased due to the imperfections in the graphene transfer process. The 3 graphene layers significantly decreased moisture intrusion to the underlying perovskite at 85% RH for 3 weeks. It also blocked the interdiffusion of  $ \Gamma^{-} $ ion and Au across CuSCN, enabling long term operation. There was a partially reversible recovery of the CuSCN/GRP(3)/Au PSC during the 3 cycles of continuous light illumination (12 h) and dark state (12 h). The CuSCN/GRP(3)/Au PSC maintained >94% of the initial PCE after 30 days at 50% RH (dark state except for the 5 min measurement duration). The graphene with a high hole mobility was an excellent conductive barrier for moisture,  $ \Gamma^{-} $ ion, and Au diffusion. A further improvement in the transfer technology may enable perfect monolayer graphene barrier for long term stability of PSCs.
