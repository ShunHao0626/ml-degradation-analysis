# A Two-Dimensional Hole-Transporting Material for High-Performance Perovskite Solar Cells with 20% Average Efficiency

Qian-Qing Ge,  $  ^{\dagger}  $   Jiang-Yang Shao,  $  ^{\dagger,*}  $   Jie Ding, Li-Ye Deng, Wen-Ke Zhou, Yao-Xuan Chen, Jing-Yuan Ma, Li-Jun Wan, Jiannian Yao, Jin-Song Hu,  $  ^{*}  $   and Yu-Wu Zhong  $  ^{*}  $

Abstract: A readily-available small molecular hole-transporting material (HTM), OMe-TATPyr, is synthesized and tested in perovskite solar cells (PSCs). OMe-TATPyr is a two-dimensional π-conjugated molecule with a pyrene core and four phenyl-thiophene bridged triarylamine groups. It can be readily synthesized in gram scale with a low lab cost of around 50 US\$/g. The incorporation of the phenyl-thiophene units in OMe-TATPyr are beneficial for not only carrier transportation through improved charge delocalization and intermolecular stacking, but also potential trap passivation via Pb-S interaction as supported by depth-profiling XPS, photoluminescence, and electrochemical impedance analysis. As a result, an impressive best power conversion efficiency (PCE) of up to 20.6% and an average PCE of 20.0% with good stability has been achieved for mixed-cation PSCs with OMe-TATPyr with an area of 0.09 cm $  ^{2}  $ . A device with an area of 1.08 cm $  ^{2}  $  based on OMe-TATPyr demonstrates a PCE of 17.3%. Together with the scalable synthesis and low cost of OMe-TATPyr, such an impressive performance makes it a new promising HTM for efficient and stable PSCs.

Hybrid organic-inorganic halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have recently attracted intense interest as the next-generation solution-processed photovoltaic devices.  $  ^{[1]}  $   Within less than ten years, the power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of PSCs have been rapidly boosted from the initial 3.8% in 2009 to over 22% today.  $  ^{[2]}  $   Currently, intensive research efforts have been focusing on the improvement of the long-term operation stability of PSCs, the optimization of the PCEs of large-area devices, and the development of cheap and readily available hole transporting materials (HTMs).  $  ^{[3]}  $   These three aspects are considered crucial for the future practical applications of PSCs.

To date, a great number of organic small molecules have been developed as HTMs in PSCs.  $  ^{[4]}  $   The most commonly used and effective small molecular HTM is 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis [N,N-di(p-

methoxyphenyl)amino]-9,9'-spirobifluorene (spiro-OMeTAD) and it has been acknowledged as the standard HTM since its debut in solid-state dye-sensitized solar cells.  $  ^{[5]}  $   The main issue of spiro-OMeTAD is its lengthy and expensive synthesis. In this sense, tremendous efforts have been undertaken to the development of cheap and readily available small molecular HTMs with the hope to replace spiro-OMeTAD.  $  ^{[4,5]}  $   However, only few of them can achieve comparable photovoltaic performance as spiro-OMeTAD with PCEs around 20%.  $  ^{[6-9]}  $   The development of cheap and readily available HTMs with highly efficient and stable photovoltaic performance is still in urgent demand.

Herein, we report a readily available small organic molecule, 1,3,6,8-tetrakis[5-(N,N-di(p-methoxyphenyl)amino-p-phenyl)-thiophen-2-yl] pyrene (abbreviated as OMe-TATPyr, Figure 1), as a highly-efficient HTM for PSCs. It has a star-shaped molecular structure with a pyrene core, to which four triarylamine arms are connected with a phenyl-thiophene bridge. Seok and co-workers previously reported a pyrene-based HTM, 1,3,6,8-tetrakis[(N,N-di(p-methoxyphenyl)aminophen-4-yl)]-pyrene (coined as Py-C; Figure 1) with a best PCE of  $ 12.4\% $  being achieved in  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $ -based devices,[10] suggesting pyrene is a promising molecular framework for HTMs. Compared to Py-C, OMe-TATPyr contains much longer side arms, which makes the molecule take a star shape with a two-dimensionally expanded system. This geometry is believed advantageous for enhancing the hole-carrier mobility of HTMs by effective delocalization and intermolecular stacking.[11] The incorporation of the phenylthiophene unit in HTM is considered beneficial for the defect passivation at the HTM/perovskite interface via the Pb-S interaction.[9,12] In addition, the use of the phenyl-thiophene unit is able to promote the degree of charge delocalization and mobility of the HTM molecule.[13] With these modifications, OMe-TATPyr exhibits superior photovoltaic performance (the best PCE of  $ 20.6\% $  and an average PCE of  $ 20.0\% $ ) and stability. The synthetic pathway toward the preparation of OMe-TATPyr is shown in Figure S1 in the Supporting Information. OMe-TATPyr was readily obtained in gram scale in a total yield of 26.4% via a four-step synthetic procedure from commercially available starting materials. The key step features the four-fold Suzuki coupling reactions of 1,3,6,8-tetrabromopyrene with 5-(N,N-di(p-methoxyphenyl)amino-p-phenyl)thiophene-2-boronic acid in 76% yield. Full structural characterizations of OMe-TATPyr were undertaken using analytical and spectroscopic techniques including nuclear magnetic resonance, mass spectrometry, and microanalysis. The lab synthesis of OMe-TATPyr is considerably shorter and cheaper with respect to that of Spiro-OMeTAD. By using the cost model described by Dingemans, Docampo, and Osedach, the total estimated materials cost for the lab synthesis of 1 gram of OMe-TATPyr is estimated to be ~50.5 US\$ (Table S1), which is about a half of that of spiro-OMeTAD (91.67 US\$/g).  $  ^{[9,14]}  $

The density functional theory (DFT)-optimized structure of OMe-TATPyr is shown in Figure 2a. Big torsional angles  $ (>40^{\circ}) $  are predicted between the phenyl-thiophene bridges and the pyrene core. This non-planar geometry would be beneficial for the formation of thin films with a low degree of crystallinity. X-ray diffraction (XRD) data of the spin-coated film of OMe-TATPyr is consistent with an amorphous morphology (Figure S2). DFT calculations show that the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of OMe-TATPyr is delocalized over the whole molecular system (Figure 2b), which will facilitate the hole transport when it used as the HTM. The mixed-cation perovskite we used in this work is   $  Cs_{0.05}FA_{0.81}MA_{0.14}PbI_{2.55}Br_{0.45}  $   (CsMAFA),  $  ^{[16]}  $   which has a valence band of -5.5 eV.  $  ^{[16c]}  $   This suggests that OMe-TATPyr would be favourable for the hole transfer from perovskite. OMe-TATPyr displays an intense absorption peak at 458 nm and an emission band at 581 nm in   $  CH_{2}Cl_{2}  $   (Figure 2e). The energy band gap (  $  E_{g}  $  ) of OMe-TATPyr was calculated to be 2.3 eV from the absorption onset wavelength. The LUMO level of OMe-TATPyr is thus determined to be -3.1 eV based on the electrochemical and absorption data. The significantly higher LUMO level of OMe-TATPyr relative to the conduction band of perovskite (-3.8 eV) can efficiently block the electron transfer from perovskite to the Au counter electrode. The HOMO and LUMO levels of OMe-TATPyr are both distinctly destabilized with respect to those of the previously reported HTM Py-C. The absorption and emission spectra of the spin-coated film of OMe-TATPyr are slightly red-shifted with respect to those in   $  CH_{2}Cl_{2}  $   solution (Figure 2e). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) indicates that OMe-TATPyr and Py-C begin to decompose at around 450 and 400 °C, respectively (Figure 2f), indicating the excellent thermal stability of pyrene derivatives.

To investigate the photovoltaic performance of OMe-TATPyr as HTM in PSCs, we fabricated the planar heterojunction devices with the state-of-the-art cesium-containing mixed-cation perovskite CsMAFA.  $  ^{[16]}  $   The architecture and cross-section view of the device are shown in Figure 3a, with a device configuration of ITO/cp-SnO  $  _{2}  $  /CsMAFA/HTM/Au (cp-SnO  $  _{2}  $  : compact SnO  $  _{2}  $   electron transport layer). For the purpose of comparison, devices were made with OMe-TATPyr, Py-C, or spiro-OMeTAD as the HTM with the same solution concentration and identical fabrication processes. The current density-voltage (J-V) curves of the PCSs were measured under AM 1.5 G (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) (Figure 3 and Table 1).

short-circuit current density (  $  J_{sc}  $  ) of the devices with OMe-TATPyr is 23.42 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  , which is higher relative to those with Py-C or spiro-OMeTAD (20.1 and 22.0 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  , respectively; Figure 3d). In addition, the average open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ) and fill factor (FF) of the OMe-TATPyr-based devices (1.092 V and 78.11%) are also higher with respect to those with Py-C or spiro-OMeTAD (Figure 3e and 3f; Py-C:   $  V_{oc} = 0.82  $   V, FF = 53.2%; spiro-OMeTAD:   $  V_{oc} = 1.03  $   V, FF = 75.1%). As a result, an average PCE of 20.0% was achieved from the OMe-TATPyr-based devices, which are considerably higher with respect to that with Py-C (8.8%) and spiro-OMeTAD (17.3%). As can be seen from the histogram of the statistical efficiency distribution, the devices with OMe-TATPyr show excellent performance with a narrow PCE distribution between 18.5% and 20.6% (Figure 3g). In contrast, the best PCE value achieved for the devices with Py-C and spiro-OMeTAD is 9.3% and 18.4%, respectively (Table 1). Moreover, the J-V curves of a device based on OMe-TATPyr at forward and reverse scanning exhibits small hysteresis (Figure S3). Figure 3c shows the spectral dependence of the external quantum efficiencies (EQEs) and the corresponding integrated currents of PSCs with different HTMs evaluated. The devices with OMe-TATPyr or spiro-OMeTAD show similar spectral responses between 350 and 700 nm, with much higher EQEs (80%~96%) being observed relative to those with Py-C. The EQE value of the device with OMe-TATPyr is slightly higher than that with Spiro-OMeTAD, suggesting the superior hole transport ability and charge extraction efficiency of OMe-TATPyr. This is further supported by the following photoluminescence results. The trend and values of the integrated current of different devices are consistent with those obtained from the experimental J-V measurements. The device with OMe-TATPyr showed a highest integrated   $  J_{sc}  $   of 20.7 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , which is slightly smaller than the current with the same HTM in the J-V measurements (23.3 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ). This is mostly related to the fact that the EQE measurements were measured at a single wavelength with much lower intensity than one sun irradiation.  $  ^{[17]}  $

We carried out the stability test of the devices with three different HTMs without encapsulation under ambient conditions with a humidity of  $ 30\% $  at room temperature. The  $ J - V $  curves were measured at different time intervals (Figure 3h). The device with OMe-TATPyr show comparable efficiency stability as that with spiro-OMeTAD. In contrast, the Py-C-based cell lost around half of the original performance after 20 days. Although the PCE of the spiro-OMeTAD based device did not significantly decreased after 60 days, the presence of some pinholes was clearly observed from the device surface, suggesting the partial decomposition of the perovskite layer (Figure S5). In contrast, a larger amount of pinholes were observed from the device with Py-C. In stark contrast, the surface of the device with OMe-TATPyr did not show distinct morphology changes after 60 days (Figure S5), which will be beneficial for the fabrication of PSCs

with long-term stability. Furthermore, the steady-state photocurrent density and PCE as a function of time at the maximum power output of 0.93 V were measured with the device with OMe-TATPyr (Figure 3i). It shows reliable high performance with a steady Jsc around 23 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  and PCE of 19% after 500 s of consecutive operation (the current and efficiency loss is less than 2%).

Encouraged by the above promising performance of small-area devices (0.09 cm $  ^{2}  $ ), we fabricated a OMe-TATPyr-based cell with a larger area of 1.08 cm $  ^{2}  $  (Figure S6). Under the standard AM 1.5 G conditions, this device exhibits a high PCE of 17.3% with  $  J_{sc}  $  of 22.3 mA/cm $  ^{2}  $ ,  $  V_{oc}  $  of 1.12 V, and  $  FF  $  of 69%. Compared to the smaller cells, the 1.08 cm $  ^{2}  $  cell shows a somewhat decreased  $  FF  $  and PCE, which is possibly caused by the defect formation in large-area thin films and the unoptimized electrode configuration. $ ^{[18]} $  Very similar EQE spectra were recorded from the five different positions of the 1.08 cm $  ^{2}  $  device as shown in Figure S6c, indicating the good homogeneity of the film and interface.

To gain insight into the hole extraction and transport of OMe-TATPyr, the properties of the perovskite/HTM interfaces were further examined. The surface morphology of the perovskite/OMe-TATPyr thin film prepared by spin-coating of OMe-TATPyr on perovskite was investigated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM). It shows a smooth and pinhole-free surface (Figure S7) with high uniformity and low root mean square (rms) roughness of 6.4 nm over 5   $  \mu  $  m × 5   $  \mu  $  m size (Figure S8). This smooth and uniform surface is believed critical for the superior photovoltaic performance of the device.

We further conducted the XPS measurements combined with the low-energy  $ \mathrm{Ar^{+}} $  cluster ion sputter depth profiling to probe the interactions on the OMe-TATPyr/perovskite interface. Results of the depth-profiling XPS analysis for OMe-TATPyr/perovskite are showed in Figure S9. Interestingly, we found that efficient Pb-S interaction was present on the interface. The intensity of the Pb 4f and S 2p XPS spectra were monitored in steps of the depth-profiling. In the beginning, the XPS spectra was dominated by the OMe-TATPyr upper layer, which can only detect the S 2p signals, including 165.3 and  $ 169.6\mathrm{eV} $  for S on thiophene and  $ 164.1\mathrm{eV} $  for S of lithium salt. The intensity of S 2p decreased with etching depth while simultaneously the intensity of the Pb 4f signals on perovskite increased. The observed peaks at 138.2 and  $ 143.1\mathrm{eV} $  are both attributed to the pristine perovskite  $ \mathrm{Pb(II)} $  state. We detected that near the level 9, peaks of the S 2p signals broadened slightly toward the high binding energy region; meanwhile, two additional weak peaks at the lower binding energy region (136.4 and  $ 142.1\mathrm{eV} $ ) appeared. These two additional peaks are attributed to the Pb sites binding with the electron-donating thiophene units of OMe-TATPyr, which could be taken as a strong evidence for the efficient Pb-S interaction. In this sense, the thiophene units of OMe-TATPyr can lead to the defect passivation between perovskite and HTM layers. To exclude the hypothesis that the shift of the above peaks were not induced by the reduction of  $ \mathrm{Ar^{+}} $  cluster ion beam for the sputtering process, we further used argon sputtering method to etch the perovskite-only and OMe-TATPyr-only film. Steady photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements were carried out to investigate the charge separation and collection processes at the perovskite/HTM interface. Compared to the bare perovskite film, the emission intensities of those coated with each HTM were significantly reduced (Figure 4a). About  $ 97\% $  emission was quenched on the perovskite film coated with OMe-TATPyr, which is higher with respect to that with Py-C  $ (68\%) $  and spiro-OMeTAD  $ (92\%) $ . This suggests that OMe-TATPyr has an excellent hole-extraction capability,[19] which is further supported by the TRPL measurements (Figure 4b). By fitting the TRPL spectra with a bi-exponential mode, decay time of different films was estimated, with  $ \tau_{1} = 4.91 $  ns and  $ \tau_{2} = 87.10 $  ns for the bare perovskite,  $ \tau_{1} = 2.86 $  ns and  $ \tau_{2} = 23.64 $  ns for the perovskite/Py-C film,  $ \tau_{1} = 0.94 $  ns and  $ \tau_{2} = 13.1 $  ns for the perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD film, and  $ \tau_{1} = 0.83 $  ns and  $ \tau_{2} = 5.51 $  ns for the perovskite/OMe-TATPyr film, respectively (Table S3). Clearly, the perovskite film coated with OMe-TATPyr shows the shortest lifetime with respect to other films, indicating that the OMe-TATPyr have efficient hole extraction capability and significantly inhibit trap-induced non-radiative carrier recombination and thus the photocurrent loss in solar cells.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analysis were performed on PSCs with different HTM to probe the charge transport at the interface between the active layer and electrode.  $  ^{[20]}  $   Figure 4c presents the Nyquist plots of the devices with different HTM at a potential bias of 0.8 V under 10 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  . The internal resistance in all devices is small (< 30 Ω, Table S4), indicating the charge transfer in the bulk is favorable because of the excellent crystallinity and low density of bulk defects of the perovskite films. The recombination resistance (  $  R_{rec}  $  ) and capacitance (  $  C_{rec}  $  ) can be obtained by fitting the data using the

equivalent circuit model shown in Figure 4d (Table S4). The interface charge recombination lifetime  $ \tau_{\mathrm{rec}} $  could be determined by  $ \tau_{\mathrm{rec}} = R_{\mathrm{rec}} \times C_{\mathrm{rec}} $ .[20] Among three devices, the  $ \tau_{\mathrm{rec}} $  of the device with OMe-TATPyr (426 μs) is relatively longer than that with Spiro-OMeTAD (311 μs) while both are much longer than the device based on Py-C (0.866 μs), indicating that the major recombination in these devices was caused by the interface charge recombination which in turn was highly dependent on the interface trap density. This result suggests that the interface with OMe-TATPyr can remarkably restrain charge recombination and thus promote cell performance, which agrees well with the TRPL results. We believe that the presence of multiple phenylthiophene groups in OMe-TATPyr may lead to efficient Pb-S[21] interactions between the perovskite layer and HTM, which can effectively reduce the formation of deep trap states through surface passivation and thus diminish the charge recombination at the perovskite/HTM interface.

In summary, we present a new small molecule OMe-TATPyr as a cheap and readily available HTM for highly-efficient and stable PSCs. It could be synthesized by a simple method in gram scale at around half the synthetic cost of the standard HTM spiro-OMeTAD. A best PCE of up to 20.6% and an average PCE of 20.0% has been achieved with OMe-TATPyr. This performance is distinctly superior to that of the device with spiro-OMeTAD, which displays a best PCE of 18.4% and an average value of 17.3% under the same measurement conditions. The incorporation of the phenyl-thiophene units in OMe-TATPyr are beneficial for not only carrier transportation through improved charge delocalization and intermolecular stacking, but also potential trap passivation via Pb-S interaction. Considering its simple synthesis and low cost and the superior performance and good long-term stability of the device with OMe-TATPyr, this new HTM offers an appealing alternative for the future industrialization of perovskite-based photovoltaic technology.

A two-dimensional hole-transporting material with a pyrene core and four phenyl-thiophene bridged triarylamine groups, OMe-TATPyr, was readily synthesized in gram scale with a low lab cost. A best power conversion efficiency (PCE) of up to 20.6% and an average PCE of 20.0% has been achieved for mixed-cation perovskite solar cells with OMe-TATPyr, which outperforms the devices with Spiro-OMeTAD under the same conditions.
