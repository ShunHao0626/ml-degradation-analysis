# Polyethylene Glycol-[60]fullerene-based Materials for Perovskite Solar Cells with Improved Moisture Resistance and Reduced Hysteresis

Abstract: A series of [60]fullerenes covalently functionalized with the polymer polyethylene glycol (PEG) is presented. These novel [60]fullerene-based materials have been incorporated as additives in  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  (MAPbI₃), the most common organic-inorganic perovskite used in perovskite solar cells. The extensive photovoltaic study performed using these materials shows beneficial effects on the performance of these cells, reducing hysteresis and increasing stability against moisture, thereby retaining up to  $ 97\% $  of their initial power conversion efficiency at ambient atmosphere.

Despite this remarkable advance in terms of PCE, PSCs have some intrinsic drawbacks which need to be overcome to lead this technology to the market. One main disadvantage is undoubtedly the stability, as the perovskites used in solar cells show instability when exposed to light, oxygen, and moisture.[4] In particular, when exposed to humidity,  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  tends to decompose into methylammonium iodide (MAI) and lead(II) iodide  $ (\mathrm{PbI}_2) $ , which are the reactants used in its formation. Walsh et al.[6] suggested a mechanism to explain this reversible perovskite degradation. They propose that a molecule of water acting as a Lewis base coordinates to the ammonium proton initiating the degradation mechanism. Thus, a plausible solution to overcome this issue would be the use of chemical strategies to prevent the presence of water molecules close to the perovskite or bind potentially present water molecules by terms of coordination to non-perovskite moieties. Following this idea, Zhao, Wei, Li et al.[7] used a simple polymeric framework added to the perovskite solution to improve perovskite stability against moisture. Resorting to polyethylene glycols (PEG) of molecular weights within a range of 12000–10000 Da, the group fabricated a series of solar cells which showed that the optimal molecular weight of PEG was 20000 Da. The PEG solution (20 or  $ 40\mathrm{mgml^{-1}} $ ) was added to the perovskite solution in order to get a molar ratio of 1:1 between perovskite and the ethylene glycol (EG) monomer units. Eventually, the authors demonstrated the feasibility of this strategy, obtaining highly resistant perovskite layers which withstand ambient moisture. The high hygroscopicity of PEG, which can coordinate water molecules, seems to be the origin of this effect, preventing these water molecules from degrading the perovskite structure.

# Stability measurements

One of the goals of this study was to prove the role of PEG-[60]fullerenes in the protection of the PSCs against humidity.

A preliminary test was performed leaving the cells in a drawer for several days with ambient air and with a relative humidity of  $ \sim 50\% $ . The cell performances were measured at the beginning of the experiment and after several days. To avoid the problem of batch-to-batch variations, cells were compared to references belonging to their respective batches (Figure 3).

The MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  :PEG-[60]fullerene cells showed increased stability. In fact, devices containing F1, F2, and F3 maintain 97, 81, and 94% of their initial PCE after 13 days respectively, therefore indicating that these PEG-[60]fullerenes have an unambiguous effect on MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   stability when used as additives as the drop of efficiency was more pronounced for the reference device in all the cases.

In order to exclude the possibility that the beneficial effect could be just due to the [60]fullerene cage, a saturated solution of [60]fullerene in DMSO was prepared (around  $ 0.06\mathrm{mM} $ ) and added to the perovskite solution before processing. The final perovskite solution contained either 5 or  $ 10\% $  v/v of this

saturated solution. The degradation in the drawer was almost the same for pristine [60]fullerene-containing cells and the reference, most probably because the quantity of [60]fullerene was insufficient to have a pronounced effect, even though the difference in concentration was just one order of magnitude lower as in our previous report, where fullerene-containing solar cells showed increased stability.[17]

After these very promising preliminary studies, we planned different experiments to evaluate the effect of these materials under a more controlled environment, while constantly monitoring maximum power point (MPP) in order to have a more realistic view of the cell behaviour.

In the first of these studies,  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3:\mathbf{F1} $  and  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  reference solar cells were left approximately 200 hours under 1 sun, at room temperature, under nitrogen flow, and with constant MPP measurement (Figure 4A). After 170 hours, when both cells showed good stability, the  $ \mathbf{N}_2 $  flow was interrupted, letting ambient air with  $ \sim 60\% $  relative humidity diffusing slowly into the chamber. The efficiency of the reference device dropped immediately, while the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3:\mathbf{F1} $  cell was barely affected. # Hysteresis measurements

The phenomenon of hysteresis is one of the major drawbacks of PSCs. A large hysteresis leads to errors in the evaluation of photovoltaic performance and this could jeopardize industrial applications. Studies involving pristine [60]fullerene  $  ^{8}  $  showed a beneficial effect towards the phenomenon, due to passivation of trap states.

Hysteresis was studied measuring current density-voltage (J-V) curves forwards and backwards under the same conditions (AM 1.5, 10 mV s  $  ^{-1}  $  ).

The hysteresis phenomenon is notably reduced in every MAPbI $ _3 $ :PEG-[60]fullerene device, as can be seen in Figure 5, where the statistics of hysteresis measurements for several MAPbI $ _3 $  references and MAPbI $ _3 $ :PEG-[60]fullerene devices is reported. When hysteresis is considered as the ratio between the backward scan P1 and the forward scan P2, a value as close to 1 as possible is highly desired, meaning the lowest difference between the two registered curves. The degree of reduction of hysteresis is highly comparable to what could be found in above mentioned reports. $ ^{[8d, 9]} $  This is remarkable since in our study the amount of fullerene is notably lower ( $ \sim $ 10 $ ^{-5} $  mol/mol with respect to perovskite). Examples of  $ J-V $  curves showing this behaviour can be found in the SI (Figures S32-S34). We also analyzed the hysteresis of devices containing 5% or 10% of pristine [60]fullerene. In similarity to the moisture stability experiments it seems that the [60]fullerene concentration is not sufficient to show a significant effect on the hysteresis, even though the saturated solution concentration is just one magnitude lower compared to the PEG-[60]fullerene concentrations. # Conclusions

Three novel PEG-[60]fullerenes F1, F2, and F3 were successfully synthesized and characterized. These molecules were incorporated into devices in small amounts (  $  \sim10^{-5}  $   mol/mol with respect to perovskite) without resorting to co-solvents to help solubilising the fullerene into the perovskite solution, which could change the way perovskite crystallizes. MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  :PEG-[60]fullerene devices showed increased stability with respect to reference devices, retaining 97, 81 and 94% of the original performances respectively after 13 days of exposure to ambient atmosphere, outperforming the reference in every case. F1 presents a double amount of PEG chain per mole with respect to F2 and F3, and thus, its ability to improve moisture resistance is the highest reported in these poly-ethylene glycol-[60]fullerene based materials. This is most likely due to the hygroscopicity of PEG chains, which help in retaining water, thus preventing perovskite from degrading. Further studies are required to better understand the mechanism of this protection. In addition, the phenomenon of hysteresis is reduced due to the presence of the fullerene cage.

# Materials

Chemicals and reagents for synthesis were purchased from commercial suppliers and used as received. Air-sensitive reactions were carried out under argon atmosphere. Flash chromatography was performed using silica gel (40-60  $  \mu  $  , Acros Organics).

Analytical thin layer chromatography (TLC) was performed using aluminum coated Macherey Nagel™ Standard SIL G Silica Layers on Alugram™ Aluminum Sheets UV254. The materials used in the photovoltaic study were obtained from commercial suppliers in high purity and used without further purification: glass/FTO (Nippon Sheet Glass), methylammonium iodide (MAI, DYESOL), PbI₂ (99.99%, TCI chemicals), spiro-OMeTAD (Solarpur), lithium bis(trifluoromethane) sulfonimidate (LiTFSI, 99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), tert-butylpyridine (96%, Sigma-Aldrich), tris(2-(1H-pyrazol-1-yl)-4-tert-butylpyridine)cobalt(III)

tri[bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide] (FK 209, Dyenamo), DMSO (extra dry, Acros Organics), acetone (technical grade, Scharlab), chlorobenzene (extra dry, Acros Organics) and acetonitrile (extra dry, Acros Organics).

# Material Characterization

NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Advance 300 (  $  ^{1}  $  H: 300 MHz;   $  ^{13}  $  C: 75 MHz) spectrometer at 298 K using partially deuterated solvents as internal standards. Chemical shifts (  $  \delta  $  ) are denoted in ppm. Multiplicities are denoted as follows: s = singlet, d = doublet, t = triplet, m = multiplet, dd = doublet of doublets.

UV-Vis spectra were recorded in a Varian Cary 50 spectrophotometer. High Resolution Matrix Assisted Laser Desorption Ionization (coupled to a Time-Of-Flight analyzer) experiments (MALDI-TOF) were performed on a Bruker Ultraflex III in the facilities of CIC Biomagune by Dr. Javier Calvo.

Electrochemical measurements were carried out on a Princeton Applied Research Parstat 2273 in a 3-electrode single compartment cell with

platinum working electrode (  $  \varnothing = 0.5  $   mm), a platinum wire counter electrode (  $  \varnothing = 0.5  $   mm) and a Ag/AgNO  $  _{3}  $   wire pseudoreference electrode. Cyclic voltammograms were recorded at a scan rate of 100 mV s  $  ^{-1}  $  . Reduction potentials were measured in c-DCB/MeCN (4:1) solution (Bu4NPF  $  _{6}  $   0.1M as supporting electrolyte) at room temperature. All potentials are reported with reference to the internal standard of the ferrocene/ferrocenium couple (Fc/Fc+ = 0.00 V).

# Device fabrication

FTO/Glass 10  $ \Omega/\mathrm{sq} $  was cleaned by sonication in  $ 2\% $  Hellmanex water solution for 15 minutes. After rinsing with deionised water, the substrates were further sonicated with ethanol for another 15 minutes and, after drying, with acetone for the same amount of time. After a 15-minute UV-ozone treatment, a  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  compact layer was deposited on FTO via spray pyrolysis at  $ 450^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  from a precursor solution of titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetylacetonate) and acetylacetone in anhydrous ethanol. After spraying, the substrates were left at  $ 450^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for 30 minutes and left to cool down to room temperature. Then, mesoporous  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  layer was deposited by spin coating for  $ 10\mathrm{s} $  at  $ 4000~\mathrm{rpm} $  with a ramp of  $ 2000~\mathrm{rpm} $   $ \mathrm{s}^{-1} $ , using  $ 30~\mathrm{nm} $  particle paste (Dyesol 30 NR-D) diluted in ethanol to achieve  $ 150 - 200~\mathrm{nm} $  thick layer. After the spin coating, the substrates were sintered with a heating ramp up to  $ 450^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ , at which they were left for 30 minutes under dry air flow. Li-doping of mesoporous  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  is obtained by spin coating a  $ 10\mathrm{mgml^{-1}} $  solution of Li-TFSI in acetonitrile at  $ 3000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 10\mathrm{s} $ . The substrate with Li-doped mesoporous  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  was completed with a second sintering process, the same as before. After cooling down to  $ 150^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  the substrates were immediately transferred in a nitrogen atmosphere glove box for deposition of the perovskite films.

The perovskite films were deposited from a freshly prepared precursor solution, containing PbI  $  _{2}  $  :MAI, 1:1 mol/mol, 1.2 M in anhydrous DMSO, and either 0 or 10% v/v of each fullerene (0.5 mmol in anhydrous DMSO). The perovskite solution was spin coated in a two step-program at 1000 and 6000 rpm for 10 and 30 s respectively. During the second step, 150   $  \mu  $  l of chlorobenzene were dripped onto the spinning substrate 5 s prior the end of the program. The substrates were then annealed at 100°C for 30 minutes in a nitrogen filled glove box.

After the perovskite annealing the substrates were cooled down for few minutes and 50   $  \mu  $  l spiro-OMeTAD solution (70 mmol in chlorobenzene) were spun at 4000 rpm for 20 s. The HTM solution was doped with tert-butyl pyridine (3.3 mol/mol with respect to Spiro), Bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt (1.8 M in acetonitrile, 0.5 mol/mol with respect to Spiro) and FK209 (0.25 M in acetonitrile, 0.05 mol/mol with respect to Spiro). Finally, a 80 nm gold top electrode was thermally evaporated using a Leica EM MED020 evaporator, under around 2·10-5 Torr, through an appropriate shadow mask (0.25 cm  $  ^{2}  $   pixel).

# Photovoltaic performances

The solar cells performances were measured using a  $ 450\mathrm{W} $  xenon light source (Oriel). The light intensity was calibrated with a Si photodiode equipped with an IR-cutoff filter (KG3, Schott) and it was recorded during each measurement. Current-voltage characteristics of the cells were obtained by applying an external voltage bias while measuring the current response with a digital source meter (Keithley 2400). The voltage scan rate was  $ 10\mathrm{mV}s^{-1} $  and no device preconditioning was applied before starting the measurement, such as light soaking or forward voltage bias applied for long time. The starting voltage was determined as the potential at which the cells furnish  $ 1\mathrm{mA} $  in forward bias, no equilibration time was used. The cells were masked with a black metal mask  $ (0.16~\mathrm{cm}^2) $  to estimate the active area and reduce the influence of the scattered light.

# Maximum power point tracking

Aging under maximum power point tracking was carried out on masked devices which were mounted on a temperature controlled plate. The aging was mainly performed under nitrogen atmosphere, sometimes alternating with dry air or spontaneous flow of ambient air, and 1-Sun equivalent illumination provided by an array of white LEDs. The devices were aged keeping them under maximum load under illumination. The maximum power point was updated every  $ 10\mathrm{s} $  by measuring the current response to a small perturbation in potential. Additionally, a full JV scan was taken every 120 minutes (at a scan rate of  $ 100\mathrm{mV}\mathrm{s}^{-1} $  starting from forward bias) which was used to extract the displayed parameters for the aging data.
