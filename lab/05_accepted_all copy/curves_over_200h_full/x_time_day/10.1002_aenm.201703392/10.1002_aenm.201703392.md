# Mixed 3D–2D Passivation Treatment for Mixed-Cation Lead Mixed-Halide Perovskite Solar Cells for Higher Efficiency and Better Stability

Yongyoon Cho, Arman Mahboubi Soufiani, Jae Sung Yun, Jincheol Kim, Da Seul Lee, Jan Seidel, Xiaofan Deng, Martin A. Green, Shujuan Huang, and Anita W. Y. Ho-Baillie*

Layered low-dimensional perovskite structures employing bulky organic ammonium cations have shown significant improvement on stability but poorer performance generally compared to their 3D counterparts. Here, a mixed passivation (MP) treatment is reported that uses a mixture of bulky organic ammonium iodide (iso-butylammonium iodide, iBAI) and formammitinium iodide (FAI), enhancing both power conversion efficiency and stability. Through a combination of inactivation of the interfacial trap sites, characterized by photoluminescence measurement, and formation of an interfacial energetic barrier by which ionic transport is reduced, demonstrated by Kelvin probe force microscopy, MP treatment of the perovskite/hole transport layer interface significantly suppresses photocurrent hysteresis. Using this MP treatment, the champion mixed-halide perovskite cell achieves a reverse scan and stabilized power conversion efficiency of  $ 21.7\% $ . Without encapsulation, the devices show excellent moisture stability, sustaining over  $ 87\% $  of the original performance after 38 d storage in ambient environment under  $ 75 \pm 20\% $  relative humidity. This work shows that FAI/iBAI, is a new and promising material combination for passivating perovskite/selective-contact interfaces.

Physical/chemical passivation of the interface between perovskite layer and HTL employing methods such as atomic layer deposition of   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   and deposition of thin layers of polymer and organic compounds have been shown to improve both stability and photovoltaic performance.  $  ^{[13,15]}  $   Passivations using organic halides, a constituent of metal halide perovskite precursor have also been investigated.  $  ^{[8,9,11,16,17]}  $   Passivation of   $  (\mathrm{FAPbI}_{3})_{0.85}(\mathrm{M}\mathrm{APbBr}_{3})_{0.15}  $   perovskite with FABr, where the latter reacts with the excess   $  PbI_{2}  $   present in the bulk layer, although improved PCE beyond 21%, could not improve the device stability under benign environmental conditions.

More recently, 2D perovskites have attracted great attention as credible moisture-resistant light absorber candidates. Bulky organic halides, including nonconjugated and conjugated aliphatic ammonium salts, mixed with metal halides would self-assemble under appropriate conditions to form pure 2D or layered quasi-2D perovskite phases.  $  ^{[18-22]}  $   In the early reports, in spite of the improvements achieved in moisture stability,

instability under various environmental conditions including UV irradiation, high temperatures, and humid environment is their major drawback, preventing the commercialization of perovskite-based optoelectronic devices.  $  ^{[3-5]}  $   Among these, humidity is particularly detrimental to perovskites due to their possible irreversible decomposition reactions with potential sublimation of the organic component.  $  ^{[4]}  $

A number of studies have shown that polycrystalline thin film perovskites possess relatively high density of bulk defects and also form trap states at the interface with the electron transport layers and hole transport layers (HTLs).  $  ^{[6-9]}  $   In perovskites, defects, in particular surface defects, are problematic not only in downgrading the device performance (e.g., by influencing its open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  )), but also for the role they play in the notorious photocurrent hysteresis in PSCs. # 2. Results and Discussion

# 2.1. Effect of Mixed Passivation Treatment on Device Performance

In order to investigate the effect of the developed MP method, solar cells with and without the passivation layer were fabricated. Compact titanium dioxide (c-TiO $ _2 $ ) and mesoporous TiO $ _2 $  (m-TiO $ _2 $ ) were deposited on fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass substrate. (FAPbI $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.85} $ (MAPbBr $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.15} $  with excess PbI $ _2 $  (5 mol%) was then spin-coated using antisolvent engineering technique. $ ^{[26]} $  The passivation layer prepared using various iBAI to FAI molar ratios was deposited onto the annealed (FAPbI $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.85} $ (MAPbBr $ _3 $ ) $ _{0.15} $  perovskite bulk (Figure 1a and Table 1). We denote MP0, MP25, MP50, MP75, and MP100 to samples with passivation treatment using precursor solution containing 0:100, 25:75, 50:50, 75:25, and 100:0 molar ratios of iBAI:FAI, respectively, while maintaining the total molarity at 60 × 10 $ ^{-3} $  M, which is equal to the

molarity of the excess   $  PbI_{2}  $  . If the total concentration of the MP precursor solutions is higher than the   $  60 \times 10^{-3}  $   M (molarity of the excess   $  PbI_{2}  $  ), device performance after MP treatment deteriorates (see Figure S1 in the Supporting Information). For the rest of the discussion, all the test structures and devices are fabricated using MP precursors with total concentration set at   $  60 \times 10^{-3}  $   M. 2,2',7,7'-Tetrakis[N,N-di(4-methoxyphenyl)amino]-9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD) was then deposited on the passivated layer. Detailed description of the device fabrication procedure is provided in the Supporting Information. Light current density-voltage (J-V) curves and device performance characteristics are presented in Figure 1b–f. External quantum efficiencies of typical devices are shown Figure S2 (Supporting Information), showing similar trends. It is clear that application of additional MP layer affects the device performance, varying with iBAI concentration in the precursor solution. With 0%, 25%, and 50% of iBAI, passivation treatment improves the device performance, whereas devices treated with 75% and 100% iBAI have similar or poorer performance compared with the reference cell which has no passivation layer between perovskite and HTL (Figure 1c). The main contributor to the improved PCEs of devices that had up to 50% iBAI is the enhancement in   $  V_{OC}  $   and to some extent fill factor (FF).   $  V_{OC}  $   is a function of the effective lifetime (i.e., affected by bulk and surface recombination lifetimes) of charge carriers which is in turn influenced by the defect density and effectiveness (e.g., capture cross-section) within the bulk and at the interfaces in a solar cell.  $  ^{[5]}  $   Improvement in   $  V_{OC}  $   then implies that the implemented passivation interlayer is able to reduce the interfacial trap density or its capture cross-section, suppressing nonradiative recombination of the photogenerated carriers (Figure 1d,f).  $  ^{[7,8,11]}  $   While   $  V_{OC}  $   of all devices with MP treatment are higher than the reference cells, the short-circuit current density (  $  J_{SC}  $  ) and FF are influenced noticeably by the iBAI to FAI ratio. Both   $  J_{SC}  $   and FF drop for devices with the highest portion of the bulky organic cation, iBA  $  ^{+}  $   (MP100). iBAI is known to form 2D perovskite phase when it is mixed with a lead halide precursor  $  ^{[18]}  $   (see the X-ray diffraction (XRD) discussion). Charge-carrier transfer can be dramatically limited in pure 2D and also in layered quasi-2D phase perovskites which do not have an optimized growth orientation of the corner-sharing octahedra layer(s) preferentially perpendicular to the charge-transporting layer surface.  $  ^{[18]}  $   We speculate that the observed deteriorations in FF and   $  J_{SC}  $   stem from inefficient charge transport characteristics of the passivation interlayer when the amount of iBAI is excessive. The improvement in the FF of MP0-, MP25-, and MP50-treated PSCs could be due to their better passivation effect (see   $  V_{OC}  $   results), modified energy band alignment at the perovskite/HTL interface facilitating hole extraction, and enhanced crystallinity of these passivation layers which will be demonstrated and discussed in the next section.

# 2.3. Optical and Electronic Changes Induced by the Passivation Treatment

Spectral PL measurement was performed to examine the charge-carrier radiative recombination properties of the perovskite films before and after MP treatment (see Figure 4a) in order to understand the reasons for better performance produced by the passivated devices. Note that the trend observed in the peak PL intensity (see Figure S8a in the Supporting Information) follows that of   $  V_{OC}  $   in the full devices (Figure 1d), indicating reduction in charge-carrier nonradiative recombination. This is also supported by the results of time-resolved PL decay measurements (Figure S8b, Supporting Information). Effective lifetimes (calculated from PL decay using Equation (S2) in the Supporting Information  $  ^{[27,31]}  $  ) significantly improve with MP treatments compared to unpassivated perovskite film (see Figure S8c in the Supporting Information). In order to obtain insights into the changes in the morphological and electrical characteristics of the perovskite films upon passivation treatment, Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) was performed to analyze spatial surface potential. Surface potential or contact potential difference (CPD) can be influenced by charge-carrier concentration, ionic defects, surface stoichiometry, and impurities.  $  ^{[32,33]}  $   In dark condition, in which the effect of photoinduced charge carriers is absent, the

collected results can be merely attributed to the as-prepared surface properties. Due to the highest performance and stability achieved by MP50 treatment in addition to their lowest hysteresis behavior among different MP treatments, which will be discussed below (Figure S12, Supporting Information), we therefore selected MP50-treated thin film and PSC for further detailed analysis alongside with the reference cell. Figure 5a shows the average CPD values for the reference and the MP50-treated structures as a function of bias voltage. The passivated sample exhibits higher CPD (  $  \approx  $  400 mV) compared to the reference in the absence of voltage bias. This indicates that the surface electronic properties (related to chemical and structural properties) are distinctly different between the reference and the passivated samples. Morphologically, the reference sample displays individual grains that are “outlined” by darker lines, indicating lower CPD at the grain boundaries compared to the grain interiors, as shown in Figure 5b. This is commonly observed in conventional perovskite films.  $  ^{[32-35]}  $   However, the passivated sample does not show “outlining” of the grains (Figure 5c) and show a smaller variation in the CPD values (note the smaller variation in the scale bar for Figure 5c compared to that for Figure 5b). This suggests that the MP treatment forms a surface layer (as a result of reacting with the remnant PbI  $  _{2}  $   (that might have been responsible for the grain “outline” in the 3D bulk film  $  ^{[36]}  $  )) with more uniform chemical and/or electrical characteristics across the grain interiors and grain boundaries.

To examine the ionic migration behavior in   $  (\mathrm{FAPbI}_{3})_{0.85}(\mathrm{MAPbBr}_{3})_{0.15}  $   film before and after MP treatment, bias voltages of +1, +2, 0, -1, -2, and 0 V were subsequently applied in dark by the KPFM tip while the FTO layer was grounded. The detailed measurement setup is described in Figure S9 (Supporting Information). We have shown previously that mobile ion migration can be induced by applying a bias at the atomic force microscopy tip and KPFM is capable of detecting the local potential set up by individual ions.  $  ^{[32]}  $   When the positive bias voltage is applied to the probe tip, negatively charged mobile ionic defects  $  ^{[37]}  $   (e.g., interstitial I  $  ^{-}  $   and Br  $  ^{-}  $   or MA  $  ^{+}  $   and FA  $  ^{+}  $   vacancies) are attracted to the surface and positively charged ions (e.g., interstitial MA  $  ^{+}  $   and FA  $  ^{+}  $   or I  $  ^{-}  $   and Br  $  ^{-}  $   vacancies) repel from the surface. A reverse response is expected under the negative bias condition. Figure 5a illustrates the average CPD values at each bias for the reference and the MP50 sample. FAI/iBAI mixture could have completed transformation of the residues such as PbI  $  _{2}  $  or other elements into perovskite at the surface and grain boundaries and also reduce the number of mobile ionic defects. We have previously demonstrated that grain boundaries can serve as preferential diffusion routes for ionic defects or mobile ions.  $  ^{[32,35]}  $  This is supported by the results of recent study showing that ionic migration behavior in 3D perovskites has been dramatically reduced in 2D and quasi-2D layered perovskite phases  $  ^{[37]}  $  present in the MP layers here.

To investigate the impact of the thin passivation layer on the optical absorption onset and energy band alignment of the combined bulk perovskite and passivation layers, optical absorption spectroscopy and ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) were conducted (see Figure S11 in the Supporting Information). Deposition of FABr on   $  (\mathrm{FAPbI}_{3})_{0.85}(\mathrm{MAPbBr}_{3})_{0.15}  $   film has been reported to form a top layer which has a larger bandgap than the bulk, mediating the interfacial recombination between perovskite and the HTL.  $  ^{[9]}  $   Yang and co-workers reported that interfacial 3D–2D graded perovskite could upshift the absorber layer work function close to the perovskite/phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) interface, prohibiting recombination of the electrons with holes.  $  ^{[24]}  $   Here, the bandgap extracted from the Tauc plots do not show bandgap increase with passivation treatment, but a slight drop (see Figure S11 in the Supporting Information). It was found from UPS spectra that conduction band energies remain similar for all MP-treated samples except MP100 which has a lower conduction band that is harmful on the hole transport characteristic. Here, we cannot see a clear relationship between energy band alignment modifications by MP treatment to explain the improvement in PSCs' performance. Therefore, we speculate that the reason for improvement is mainly due to the modification of the surface of   $  (\mathrm{FAPbI}_{3})_{0.85}(\mathrm{MAPbBr}_{3})_{0.15}  $   perovskite passivating the defects.

We also conducted stability tests by tracking the PCE changes of the devices stored in high humidity conditions (RH   $  75 \pm 20\%  $  ) over a period of 38 d (Figure S12c,d, Supporting Information). The reference cell showed quite stable performance compared to previous reports.  $  ^{[9,11]}  $   A notably greater stability was realized in MP50-passivated devices (Figure 6c), whereas other passivation treatments show less remarkable improvement in stability with respect to the reference cells under similar conditions (Figure S12c, Supporting Information). The reasons for the enhancement in moisture stability might be twofold. First, the greater hydrophobic characteristic of iBA  $  ^{+}  $  cation in the passivation interlayer, as compared with FA  $  ^{+}  $  and MA  $  ^{+}  $ , has been able to prevent the penetration of water molecules from the surface into the bulk perovskite underneath which is prone to moisture degradation through the existence of surface defects and grain boundary (Figure S14, Supporting Information).  $  ^{[11,14,41]}  $   X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy results in Figure S13a,b (Supporting Information) show the intensity change in N1s and C1s as MP treatment is varied. The changes are consistent with compositional change of iBAI and FAI in the MP solution. As the iBAI content in the MP treatment increases, the carbon to nitrogen ratio increases (Figure S13c, Supporting Information), confirming the presence of increased iBA in the passivation materials at the surface. Second, contribution to enhanced moisture stability is the improved surface coverage and morphology of the passivation layer provided by MP treatments (up to MP50), suppressing hysteresis and improving PSC long-term stability (see Figure S14 in the Supporting Information). The less improved stability for MP75- and MP100-treated cells could be due to their less uniform surfaces (Figure 2),

making them less effective as moisture barriers.  $  ^{[7,42]}  $   It appears that the desirable morphology observed in the surface layer of MP50-treated cell is important for improving device stability and reducing hysteresis.

# 3. Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed an effective passivation technique, i.e., the MP treatment, for efficient and stable perovskite solar cells. By treating the interface between   $  (\mathrm{FAPbI}_{3})_{0.85}(\mathrm{MAPbBr}_{3})_{0.15}  $   bulk perovskite and spiro-OMeTAD with an optimized composition of organic ammonium iodide solutions, that being equal molar fractions of FAI and bulky iBAI, performances close to that of the highest reported in the literature with reduced hysteresis and improved dark high-humidity stability were achieved. It was found that the role of iBAI is beneficial i) in assisting the formation of crystalline perovskite surface passivation layer during the reaction with remnant   $  PbI_{2}  $   from the bulk and ii) in controlling the morphology of the film. It was found that with the optimum amount of iBAI, the surface passivation layer is dense, uniform with even distribution of grain size. The major contributor to performance improvement of the MP0–MP50-treated devices was the increased   $  V_{OC}  $   and FF, consistent with the increase in PL response of the samples after surface treatment, which in turn imply that the passivation layer was effective in annihilating the surface traps. It was also realized that the morphology of the MP-treated surface passivation layer plays a critical role in device stability and hysteresis, as shown in the analysis of PSCs passivated by different combinations of FAI and iBAI.
