# Introduction

Organo-metal halide (OMH) perovskites have attracted significant attention in solar cell applications because of their tunable band gap, large absorption coefficient, high charge carrier mobility, and long charge carrier diffusion length.  $  ^{[1]}  $   The fast increase in their power conversion efficiency (PCE) to above 20% of has drawn substantial excitation.  $  ^{[2-3]}  $   The development of OMH perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has focused on two important issues with respect to practical applications. One is power conversion efficiency (PCE) and the other is stability.  $  ^{[4-6]}  $   The PCE has increased from 3.8% to over 22.1% in just a few years through device engineering and materials design based on the desired optoelectronic properties.  $  ^{[2,7-9]}  $   However, the fast degradation of the material impedes their large scale applications. A number of previous studies have demonstrated the vulnerability of OMH perovskites to environmental factors such as humidity (moisture), oxygen, and UV light.  $  ^{[10-12]}  $

The solar power conversion efficiency (PCE)  $ \eta $  of the pristine perovskite film and those modified with PEI  $ (\mathrm{Mw} = 600) $ , PEI  $ (\mathrm{Mw} = 10000) $  and PEI  $ (\mathrm{Mw} = 70000) $  were  $ 9.69\% $ ,  $ 6.84\% $ ,  $ 6.43\% $  and  $ 6.94\% $ , respectively. Even though the PCE is lower for the PEI-modified solar cells, their stability is substantially improved. The improved stability is attributed to  $ \mathrm{PEI - H^{+}} $  cations in the grain boundary of the perovskites, preventing them from direct contact with water molecules that otherwise cause fast degradation. This demonstrates that PEI modification is a simple and powerful strategy for stabilizing perovskite films.

In particular, water or moisture has been recognized as one of the most critical factors causing instability of the OMH perovskites.  $  ^{[13]}  $   This extreme vulnerability to moisture has spurred efforts to make the top charge-transporting layer of the device the first line of defense against moisture ingress. Several different approaches were utilized, including the employment of a thin blocking layer between the perovskite and the hole- or electron-transporting material,  $  ^{[14-21]}  $   the use of hydrophobic polymer or metal oxide hole- and electron-transporting layers,  $  ^{[22-24]}  $   and the use of hydrophobic carbon electrodes.  $  ^{[25-32]}  $   These approaches significantly improved the stability of PSCs. However, they require careful design of the charge transport materials.

Recently, some research efforts have focused on direct modification of perovskites to improve their stability.  $  ^{[33-34]}  $   e.g., using a mixture of three cations,   $  Cs^{+}/CH_{3}NH^{3+}(MA^{+})/CH(NH_{2})_{2}^{+}(FA^{+})  $  . Likewise, the preparation of 2D perovskites by the insertion of larger cations, for example, ethylammonium   $  (EA^{+})  $  ,   $  C_{6}H_{5}(CH_{2})_{2}NH_{3}^{+}(PEA^{+})  $  ,  $  ^{[33,35]}  $   and alkylammonium ion   $  CH_{3}(CH_{2})_{3}NH_{3}^{+}(BA^{+})  $   fluorinated 1,1,1-trifluoroethyl ammonium iodide (FEAl)   $  FEA^{+[36]}  $   into the 3D MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   lattice could also improve stability compared to that of the 3D MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   analogues, making them more attractive for large-scale industrial implementation. Similarly, polyethylenimine (PEI), an amorphous polymer with excellent stability, water resistance and chemical resistance, has been used to improve the stability of perovskite films.  $  ^{[37-41]}  $

# Conclusion

In summary, three highly stable MAPbl $ _3 $  perovskite films modified with PEIs of different molecular weights (Mw=600, Mw=10000 and Mw=70000) were successfully prepared and used to fabricate solar cells. The PEI substantially improved the stability of the perovskite films when studied over 20 days under ambient air conditions with a medial humidity of 60%.

SEM results indicated that introducing PEI cations facilitates crystal growth and improves film uniformity and compactness. XRD and FT-IR results show that  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  was successfully prepared and proved that PEI is indeed incorporation in perovskite films. Results of XRD, UV-Vis absorption spectra and time resolved photoluminescence show that PEI could not substitute for MA within the perovskite crystal lattice. The PCE of the pristine perovskite, PEI (Mw=600), PEI (Mw=10000) and PEI (Mw=70000) modified perovskite solar cell was  $ 9.69\% $ ,  $ 6.84\% $ ,  $ 6.43\% $  and  $ 6.94\% $ , respectively. Even though the PCE is lower for the PEI-modified solar cells, their stability is substantially improved. The improved stability is attributed to the array of  $ \mathrm{PEI - H^{+}} $  cations on the grain boundary of the perovskites that reduce or prevent direct contact with water molecules that otherwise cause fast degradation. This study demonstrates that PEI modification is a simple and powerful strategy for stabilizing perovskite films.

Then, TiO₂ scaffolds were deposited by spin-coating at 3000 rpm for 20 s using a mesoporous TiO₂ precursor solution, the TiO₂ scaffolds were gradually heated to 500 °C for 30 min in N₂. Subsequently, after cooling down to room temperature, $ ^{[47]} $  the perovskite layer was deposited into TiO₂ scaffold by spin-coating at 2000 rpm for 20 s, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 5 mins and it should be noted that TiO₂ scaffold requires a preheating procedure at 70 °C for 5 s. $ ^{[48-49]} $  The conductive carbon slurry which was used as both the hole transport layer and the counter electrode would subsequently deposited by printing the carbon paint on the perovskite layer followed by drying at 100 °C for 30 min. And finally, we got solar cells with sandwich structure of FTO/TiO₂/perovskite/Carbon. All the above operations were carried out at ambient conditions (humidity of 60%–70% and room temperature of 25–35 °C).

# Characterization

The XRD pattern was conducted using a XRD-6100 (Shimadzu, Japan). The chemical bond characteristics of the material were detected by Nicolet Nexus470 Fourier transform infrared spectrometer. SEM images were obtained from a field emission scanning electron microscope (Hitachi SU8020, Japan). Optical characterization was conducted on a UV-vis spectrophotometer (Persee TU1810, China). The steady-state PL measurements were acquired using a fluorescence spectrometer (Hitachi F-4600, Japan), and the fluorescence lifetime were detected by a fluorescence spectrometer (Edinburgh Instruments FLS920, Britain). The photocurrent density–voltage (J–V) characteristics of the perovskite solar cells were detected by Solar IV-150 (Zolix, China).
