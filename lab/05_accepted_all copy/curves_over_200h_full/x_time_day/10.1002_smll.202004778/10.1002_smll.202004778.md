# Titanium Nanopillar Arrays Functioning as Electron Transporting Layers for Efficient, Anti-Aging Perovskite Solar Cells

Jie Zhao,  $  ^{*}  $   Peng Sun, Zhongwei Wu, Jun Li, Xiaohan Wang, Ting Xiao, Lin Yang, Zijian Zheng,  $  ^{*}  $   and Zhifeng Huang  $  ^{*}  $

Electron transporting layers (ETLs), required to be optically transparent in perovskite solar cells (PSCs) having regular structures, possess a determinant effect on electron extraction and collection. Metal oxides (e.g.,   $  TiO_{2}  $  ) have overwhelmingly served as ETLs, but usually have low electron mobility (  $  \mu_{e} < 10^{-2} cm^{2} V^{-1} s^{-1}  $  ) not favorable for photovoltaic conversion. Here, metal oxides are replaced with metals (e.g., Ti with   $  \mu_{e} \approx 294 cm^{2} V^{-1} s^{-1}  $  ) that are sculptured via glancing angle deposition to be a close-packed nanopillar array (NaPA), which vertically protrudes on a transparent electrode to obtain sufficient optical transmission for light harvesting in perovskite. Ti NaPAs, whose rough surfaces are passivated with 5 nm thick   $  TiO_{2}  $   (i.e., Ti NaPAs@  $  TiO_{2}  $  ) to suppress exciton recombination, lead to the champion power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 18.89% that is superior to that of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   PSCs without Ti NaPAs@  $  TiO_{2}  $   or containing   $  TiO_{2}  $   NaPAs@  $  TiO_{2}  $  , owing to high surface wettability, high   $  \mu_{e}  $  , and relatively low work function of Ti. Furthermore, Ti NaPAs@  $  TiO_{2}  $   effectively prevents the decomposition of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   to achieve long-term shelf stability whereby 50-day aging only causes 15% PCE degradation. This work paves the way toward widening the material spectrum, from semiconductors to metals, to generate a diverse range of ETLs for producing efficient optoelectronic devices with long-term shelf stability.

# 1. Introduction

Due to outstanding optoelectronic properties and solution processability, perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have revolutionized photovoltaic applications by providing spectacular power conversion efficiency (PCE) exceeding 22% with competitive cost.  $  ^{[1]}  $   Electron transporting layers (ETLs) have been found to have a crucial effect on the extraction of photoinduced electrons in photovoltaically active layers.  $  ^{[2]}  $   ETLs in PSCs with regular structures should be optically transparent and have been dominantly made of metal oxides and conjugated polymers, even though their low electron mobility (  $  \mu_{e} < 10^{-2}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $   V  $  ^{-1}  $   s  $  ^{-1}  $  ) has been found to deteriorate photoinduced electron collection.  $  ^{[2a,3]}  $   Replacing these semiconductors with metals that generally have   $  \mu_{e} > 10  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $   V  $  ^{-1}  $   s  $  ^{-1}  $   is a potential solution to enhance electron collection. Alternatively, the porosification of metal thin films, e.g., through forming the networks of metal nanofibers/nanowires/meshes/webs/grids, has been developed to not only maintain high   $  \mu_{e}  $   but also remarkably increase optical transmittance. These porous metal networks have been fabricated through the integration of patterned meso- and nanoscale metal nanowires to form conjunction networks,  $  ^{[5]}  $   the formation of metallic networks using photolithographical etching,  $  ^{[6]}  $   the deposition of highly stretchable and transparent Au nanomesh electrodes on elastomers using grain boundary lithography,  $  ^{[7]}  $   and the production of bio-inspired Ag networks.  $  ^{[8]}  $   The porous metal networks have been regarded to be one of the most promising candidates to replace indium tin oxides (ITO) for functioning as transparent electrodes in solution-processed PSCs and organic light-emitting diodes.  $  ^{[9]}  $   However, these porous metal networks usually have rough surfaces to cause high electric resistance of the contacts with optoelectronically active layers.  $  ^{[10]}  $   In addition, it is challenging to obtain good morphology uniformity across macroscale areas that is in practical demand for optoelectronic devices. These problems inspire one to explore the possibility of generating an array of separated metal nanostructures that vertically grow on a transparent electrode to serve as ETLs. The protruding metal nanoarrays will not only facilitate light harvesting in optoelectronically active layers infiltrating in the nanoarrays, mainly owing to high optical transmission through the porous nanoarrays and the unique effect of localized surface plasmon resonance inherently occurring in metal nanostructures with sub-wavelength size,  $  ^{[11]}  $   but also function as an electric pathway to effectively extract the photoexcited electrons.  $  ^{[12]}  $

Here, we use glancing angle deposition (GLAD) to deposit a closely packed array of titanium (Ti) nanostructures protruding on ITO-coated glass.  $  ^{[13]}  $   Ti is selected due to its high melting point (1670 °C) that is suitable for GLAD, high   $  \mu_{e}  $  , high mechanical strength, light weight, chemical stability, oxidation and corrosion resistivity, and abundant storage on Earth.  $  ^{[14]}  $   The protruding Ti nanostructures are sculptured in columnar, helical, and zigzag shapes, and GLAD enables the uniform deposition of Ti nanoarrays across a macroscale area of   $  10 \times 10 \, cm^2  $  . Among diverse Ti nanostructures, Ti nanopillar arrays (NaPAs) vertically protruding on ITO-coated glass are chosen to serve as the ETL embedded in PSCs containing a perovskite layer (made of methylammonium lead triiodide or MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ), which can completely infiltrate into the intervals between vertical nanopillars to form intimate Ti-MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   contacts that is desirable for electron extraction. The Ti nanopillars with rough surfaces are passivated with 5 nm thick TiO  $  _{2}  $   using atomic layer deposition (ALD) to effectively prohibit exciton recombination. The PSCs containing the passivated Ti NaPAs have the champion PCE of 18.89%, higher than that of 15.88% in ones without Ti NaPAs and 17.85% in ones hybridized with the passivated semiconductor NaPAs made of the widely used TiO  $  _{2}  $  . Furthermore, the passivated Ti NaPAs lead to high photovoltaic antiaging stability at room temperature. The factors contributing to the Ti NaPA-induced photovoltaic

superiority are systematically investigated. It is illustrated that metal NaPAs can alternatively serve as ETLs to fabricate optoelectronic devices with efficient photovoltaic performance and high photovoltaic shelf stability.

# 2. Results and Discussion

# 2.1. GLAD of Ti Nanoarrays with Engineerable Shapes

A close-packed array of Ti nanostructures protruding on a supporting substrate was deposited by GLAD, owing to the glancing deposition-induced shadowing effect.  $  ^{[15]}  $   Ti nanostructures were sculptured in shape based on the control of substrate motion during deposition.  $  ^{[16]}  $   No substrate movement during GLAD led to the generation of tilted nanopillars (Figure 1a). Rapid substrate rotation (at a rate of, e.g., 2 degree per second or   $  {}^{\circ}s^{-1}  $  ) enabled the fabrication of vertical nanopillars (Figure 1b), while slow substrate rotation (at a rate of, e.g.,   $  0.17^{\circ}s^{-1}  $  ) resulted in the production of nanohelices (e.g., having 3.5 turns and the helical pitch of   $  \approx280  $   nm, Figure 1c).  $  ^{[17]}  $   Nanozigzags were produced through stepping a substrate back and forth in   $  180^{\circ}  $   intervals (Figure 1d). Ti columns generally appear to broaden in diameter along with their growth, i.e., the structural broadening (Figure 1c -II,d -II, and inset in Figure 1e) due to the GLAD-caused competition effect.  $  ^{[18]}  $   The closely packed arrays have an intercolumn spacing varying in the range of 50–150 nm. The as-deposited Ti nanostructures generally have rough surfaces (Figure 1e), and are polycrystalline (Figure 1f) with dominant crystal orientation direction along   $  \langle110\rangle  $   (Figure 1g). Ti nanostructures are spontaneously oxidized to mainly generate   $  TiO_{2}  $   at their surfaces (Figure 1h and Figure S1, Supporting Information).  $  ^{[19]}  $   Note that the sculptured shape has negligible effect on Ti crystallinity.

Importantly, GLAD enabled the deposition of Ti NaPAs on rigid (e.g., ITO-coated glasses, inset in Figure 2b-I and Figure S2a, Supporting Information) and flexible (e.g., ITO-coated polyethylene terephthalate or PET, Figure S2b, Supporting Information) substrates. Ti nanoarrays grew uniformly across a macroscale area of, e.g.,  $ 10 \times 10 \mathrm{~cm}^2 $  (Figure S2c,d, Supporting Information). It paves the way toward the generation of large-area PSCs, as being in urgent demand currently.

It is a prerequisite that Ti nanoarrays should be optically transparent to reduce optical loss, and perovskite layers can be completely infiltrated into the intervals of nanoarrays to form intimate Ti-perovskite contacts with low contact resistance. With respect to the four sculptured shapes as shown in Figure 1, vertical nanopillars tend to be favorable for perovskite infiltration. Hence, we measured optical properties of Ti vertical NaPAs in the wavelength range of 300–900 nm, as a function of nanopillar height. Ti NaPAs were engineered to have a height of 30, 100, 300, and 500 nm, showing that taller nanopillars appear to have rougher surfaces (Figure 2a–d). To mimic solar irradiation of the PSCs with

# 2.3. Photovoltaic Performance of Rigid PSCs Hybridized with Ti 100-NaPAs

Compared with the other Ti nanopillars in a shape of tilted nanopillars, helix, and zigzag, vertical nanopillars are favorable for

the permeation of perovskite precursors into Ti nanoarrays to form intimate Ti-perovskite contacts with desirable low contact resistance. It was monitored that photovoltaic performance of PSCs that contain Ti nanoarrays sculptured in the four different shapes. Given the nanoarray thickness of   $  \approx  $  120 nm, all the photovoltaic parameters tended to decrease in the order of vertical nanopillars, tilted nanopillars, nanozigzags, and nanohelices (Figure S3a, Supporting Information). High photovoltaic conversion of the vertical nanopillars also benefits from high optical transmittance (Figure S3b, Supporting Information). As a result, we selected Ti vertical NaPAs to serve as an ETL in PSCs and study their light-to-electricity conversion properties.

The height of Ti NaPAs was set to be 100 nm (i.e., Ti 100-NaPA), beyond which optical loss will significantly increase (Figure 1e). Although having less optical loss, vertical Ti nanopillars shorter than 100 nm are not favorable for the growth of big perovskite grains that are desired to prohibit exciton recombination in the grain boundaries. The MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   layer, having the thickness of   $  \approx  $  500 nm, was fully infiltrated into Ti 100-NaPA@TiO  $  _{2}  $   to form intimate physical contacts (Figure 3b).

It is well known that under light illumination and built-in electric field, photogenerated excitons will be separated to form free electrons and holes, which will then transport to ETL and hole transporting layer (HTL), and finally be collected by FTO and Au electrodes, respectively. Energy band structure plays an importance role in exciton separation and charge carrier extraction.[1d,9a] Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS, Figure S5 and Table S1, Supporting Information) was monitored to evaluate energy band diagram of the hybrid PSCs (Figure 3c), which was found to be suitable for the separation of excitons generated in the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  layers and the extraction of photoinduced charge carriers. The  $ 5\mathrm{nm} $  thick  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  layers (deposited by ALD) and  $ \mathrm{TiO_2CL} $  (deposited by GLAD) will prohibit hole transport toward the transparent ITO electrode.

As shown in the plots of photocurrent density (J) versus voltage (V) (Figure 3d), the PSCs only containing the   $  TiO_{2}  $   CL showed the champion PCE of 15.88%, with the open circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) of 0.962 V, short-circuit photocurrent density (  $  J_{SC}  $  ) of 21.95 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and fill factor (FF) of 0.752 (Table S2, Supporting Information). The addition of Ti 100-NaPA@TiO  $  _{2}  $   in the ETL led to an increase of the champion PCE to 18.89%

(with   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.042 V,   $  J_{SC}  $   of 22.61 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and FF of 0.802). The increase of   $  J_{SC}  $   from 21.95 to 22.61 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   is coincident with that of incident photon-to-current efficiency (IPCE) in the wavelength region of 300–750 nm (Figure 3e), given the proportion of   $  J_{SC}  $   to IPCE. Furthermore, statistical analysis of multiple cells showed that adding the Ti 100-NaPA@TiO  $  _{2}  $   resulted in raising the PCE from 14.55% ± 0.56% (mean value ± standard deviation) to 17.55% ± 0.71% (Figure 3f), accompanied with an increase of the   $  V_{OC}  $  ,   $  J_{SC}  $  , and FF values from 0.948 ± 0.010 to 1.026 ± 0.017 V, from 20.99 ± 0.80 to 21.87 ± 0.58 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , from 0.732 ± 0.017 to 0.779 ± 0.017, respectively (Figure S6, Supporting Information).

There are several contributions from the Ti 100-NaPAs@TiO  $  _{2}  $  to enhance the photovoltaic conversion. First, the nanoarrays can effectively prevent optical loss and thus promote light harvesting in the perovskite layers. Second, the TiO  $  _{2}  $  CL showed the water contact angle (WCA) of 42.4° (Figure 3g-I), and coating the Ti 100-NaPAs@TiO  $  _{2}  $  on the TiO  $  _{2}  $  CL made the WCA reduce to 17.6° (Figure 3g-II). It illustrates that the Ti 100-NaPA@TiO  $  _{2}  $  enables the enhancement in surface wettability of the ETL, leading to an increase in the single-crystal perovskite grain size from 0.049  $  \mu  $  m  $  ^{2}  $  in average area (Figure 3h-I) to 0.064  $  \mu  $  m  $  ^{2}  $  (Figure 3h-II). Coincidentally, the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  (110) peak in the X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectrum showed a decrease in full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) from 0.095° on the TiO  $  _{2}  $  CL to 0.088° on the “TiO  $  _{2}  $  CL + Ti 100-NaPA@TiO  $  _{2}  $ ” (Figure 3i). The incorporation of Ti 100-NaPA@TiO  $  _{2}  $  favors the formation of large single-crystal perovskite grains to inhibit exciton recombination, which usually occurs at the grain boundaries.  $  ^{[22]}  $

Fourth, compared with  $ \mu_{\text{e}} $  of 0.1–1 cm $ ^2 $  V $ ^{-1} $  s $ ^{-1} $  in TiO $ _2 $ ,[3] the GLAD-generated Ti nanopillars have much higher  $ \mu_{\text{e}} $  of  $ \approx $ 294 cm $ ^2 $  V $ ^{-1} $  s $ ^{-1} $  (Figure S7, Supporting Information) to significantly promote electron transfer from the MAPbI $ _3 $  layers to the transparent electrode. The formation of intimate perovskite/nanopillar contacts accounts for the decrease of  $ R_S $  and consequently the increase of FF from 0.752 to 0.802 (Table S2, Supporting Information).[24] Lastly, it is well known that  $ V_{\text{OC}} = (E_{\text{F,n}} - E_{\text{F,p}})/e $  (where  $ e $  is elementary electron charge, and  $ E_{\text{F,n}} $  and  $ E_{\text{F,p}} $  are the quasi-Fermi energy of

electrons and holes in perovskite).  $  ^{[25]}  $   Compared to the   $  E_{F}  $   value of   $  TiO_{2}  $   (-4.74 eV), low work function (-4.30 eV) of Ti NaPAs gives rise to upshifting the average   $  E_{F,n}  $   of the   $  ETL/MAPbI_{3}  $   heterojunctions and resultantly increasing the   $  V_{OC}  $   value (Figure 3c).

It has been reported on replacing the mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   with vertical   $  TiO_{2}  $   nanorods/nanopillars to serve as the ETLs for generating efficient PSCs,  $  ^{[23b,26]}  $   which are summarized in Table S3 in the Supporting Information. Here, the semiconductor   $  TiO_{2}  $   100-NaPAs@  $  TiO_{2}  $   was also grown on the planar   $  TiO_{2}  $   CL to function as the ETL (Figure S8a, Supporting Information), resulting in the champion PCE of 17.85% with the   $  V_{OC}  $   of 0.993 V,   $  J_{SC}  $   of 23.04 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and FF of 0.780 (Figure S8b and Table S2, Supporting Information). Replacing   $  TiO_{2}  $   100-NaPAs (  $  E_{F} = -4.74  $   eV) with Ti 100-NaPAs (work function of -4.30 eV) causes the   $  E_{F,n}  $   of the ETL/MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   heterojunctions to upshift, accounting for the increase of the   $  V_{OC}  $   value from 0.993 to 1.042 V. However, this replacement induced the loss of optical transmittance in 9–15% (Figure S8c, Supporting Information), accounting for the slight decrease of   $  J_{SC}  $   from 23.04 to 22.61 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  . Therefore, high   $  \mu_{e}  $   of Ti compensates the loss of light harvesting in the perovskite layers to make the PSCs more efficient than those having the ETL typically composed of the   $  TiO_{2}  $   1D nanostructures (Table S3, Supporting Information).

# 2.4. Antiaging of PSCs

It is of practical significance to achieve long-term photovoltaic shelf stability. The hybrid PSCs were stored in a  $ \mathrm{N}_2 $ -filled glovebox at room temperature to monitor the photovoltaic aging. The PSCs having only the  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  CL showed a  $ 50\% $  PCE degradation after 50 days (Figure 4a). In contrast, adding the Ti 100-NaPA@TiO $ _2 $  only caused roughly  $ 15\% $  degradation, illuminating the Ti NaPA-induced photovoltaic antiaging.

It has been found out that photovoltaic degradation is a complicated process, ascribed to the photodegradation of the ETL   $  TiO_{2}  $  ,  $  ^{[27]}  $   ion migration in perovskite,  $  ^{[28]}  $   reaction of diffused gold atoms with perovskite,  $  ^{[29]}  $   and humidity/oxygen-induced degradation of perovskite.  $  ^{[30]}  $   It has been reported that   $  Pb^{2+}  $   cations in   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   are prone to be galvanically reduced to metal   $  Pb^{0}  $   under an exposure to moisture, heating, or UV illumination.  $  ^{[31]}  $   Metal   $  Pb^{0}  $   is a primary deep defect state to severely cause the photovoltaic degradation.  $  ^{[32]}  $   As characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), metal   $  Pb^{0}  $   (at 136.50 and 141.30 eV,   $  Pb^{0}  $   4f) was emerging on the 10th aging day in PSCs with only the planar   $  TiO_{2}  $   CL (Figure 4b-I). In contrast, the occurrence of the galvanic reduction from   $  Pb^{2+}  $   to   $  Pb^{0}  $   was effectively delayed for 10 days by the Ti 100-NaPA@  $  TiO_{2}  $   (Figure 4b-II), accounting for the photovoltaic antiaging. Furthermore, the galvanic decomposition of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   resulted in the formation of voids in the perovskite layers (Figure 4e,f). A pristine   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   layer

growing on the planar   $  TiO_{2}  $   CL possessed the void area percentage (or void%) of only 0.33% (Figure 4c-I), and the 50 days aging caused an increase to 7.92% (Figure 4e-I). In contrast, the addition of Ti 100-NaPA@  $  TiO_{2}  $   in the ETL caused the void% to rise from 0.35% (Figure 4d-I) to only 2.94% after the 50 days aging (Figure 4f-I). The aging-induced voids lead to deteriorating light harvesting in MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   and destroying the intimate MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  /nanopillar contacts (Figure 4e-II vs 4f-II), and function as the exciton recombination centers. Therefore, the Ti 100-NaPA@  $  TiO_{2}  $   enables the prohibition of the galvanic reduction of Pb  $  ^{2+}  $   cations to metal Pb  $  ^{0}  $   and thus enhances the shelf stability of PSCs.

# 3. Conclusions

In summary, GLAD is used to uniformly deposit Ti nanoarrays on an electrode over an area of  $ 10 \times 10 \mathrm{~cm}^2 $ . Ti nanostructures are polycrystalline and sculptured in vertical nanopillars, tilted nanopillars, nanohelices, and nanozigzags. The Ti 100-NaPA, whose rough surfaces are conformally passivated with  $ 5 \mathrm{~nm} $  thick  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  by using ALD, vertically protrudes on the planar  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  CL to function as the ETL in the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  PSCs. The addition of Ti 100-NaPA@TiO $ _2 $  results in an increase of the champion PCE from  $ 15.88\% $  to  $ 18.89\% $ . # 4. Experimental Section

Materials: All materials purchased were used without further purification. Silicon wafers were purchased from Semiconductor Wafer Inc. (Taiwan). ITO-coated glass and PET were purchased from Yingkou You Xuan Trade Co. Ltd. Ti and   $  TiO_{2}  $   pellets (2–3 mm in diameters) were purchased from Zhongnuo Advanced Material (Beijing) Technology Co. Ltd. Methylammonium iodide (MAI, 99.5%) and 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-dip-methoxyphenylamine)-9,9-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD, 99%) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. Lead (II) iodide (  $  PbI_{2}  $  , 99.99%), dimethyl sulfoxide (99.8%), N,N'-dimethylformamide (99.8%), chlorobenzenes (99.8%, Alfa), and isopropanol (99.5%) were purchased from Alfa. Tetrakis(dimethylamido)titanium (TDMAT, 99.999%) and lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (Li-TFSI, 99.99%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

GLAD of Ti Nanostructures: Si wafers, ITO-coated glasses, and ITO-coated PET were ultrasonically cleaned with surface detergents, deionized water (DI water, 18.2 MΩ, Milli-Q reference water purification system fed with campus distilled water), acetone, and ethanol sequentially, followed by oxygen plasma treatment for 15 min. Then, the treated substrates were installed in a custom-built physical vapor deposition system (JunSun Tech Co. Ltd., Taiwan) in a high vacuum of   $  10^{-7}  $   to   $  10^{-6}  $   Torr, where Ti pellets were evaporated at a deposition angle (  $  \alpha  $  ) of   $  86^{\circ}  $   with respect to the direction normal to the substrate, using an electron-beam accelerating voltage of 8.0 kV. Ti was evaporated at a rate of   $  \approx0.2  $   nm s  $  ^{-1}  $   (as monitored by a quartz crystal microbalance (QCM) located near the substrate) using an emission current of 40–50 mA to produce Ti vertical nanopillars, and at a rate of   $  \approx0.3  $   nm s  $  ^{-1}  $   using an emission current of   $  \approx90  $   mA to generate Ti nanostructures with the other shapes. During GLAD, the substrate temperature of ITO-coated PET was controlled at   $  \approx5  $   °C using a water-cooling system, and that of Si wafers and ITO-coated glasses was not controlled. Rotating substrate at a rate of 2 and   $  0.17^{\circ}  $   s  $  ^{-1}  $   led to produce vertical nanopillars and nanohelices, respectively. No substrate movement was resulted in the generation of tilted nanopillars. To grow Ti nanozigzags, the substrate was stepped back and forth in   $  180^{\circ}  $   intervals. Ti CL was deposited at   $  \alpha  $   of   $  0^{\circ}  $   without substrate movement, to have a thickness of   $  \approx30  $   and 100 nm. In addition,   $  TiO_{2}  $   was evaporated at a rate of   $  0.04  $   nm s  $  ^{-1}  $   (monitored by QCM), using an electron-beam accelerating voltage of 8.0 kV and

emission current of 50–70 mA.   $  TiO_{2}  $   CL was deposited at   $  \alpha  $   of   $  0^{\circ}  $   without substrate movement, to have a thickness of   $  \approx15  $   nm.   $  TiO_{2}  $   nanopillars were deposited at   $  \alpha  $   of   $  86^{\circ}  $   and substrate rotation rate of   $  1.0^{\circ} s^{-1}  $  .

ALD of   $  TiO_{2}  $  : 5 nm thick   $  TiO_{2}  $   was conformally coated on the Ti and   $  TiO_{2}  $   nanopillars using ALD (Ensure NanoTech, Beijing, China), where TDMAT were used as the metal precursor and water as the oxidant. The bubbler was kept at 70 °C and the chamber was kept at 120 °C in the inlet, and a temperature gradient was maintained between the bubbler and chamber. The substrate temperature was ≈150 °C. The pulse time of TDMAT and   $  H_{2}O  $   were 0.6 and 0.4 s, respectively. The growth rate was controlled as ≈0.3 Å per cycle to deposit 5 nm thick   $  TiO_{2}  $  .

Fabrication of the PSCs: The ETLs, composed of the   $  TiO_{2}  $   CL, “  $  TiO_{2}  $   CL + Ti 100-NaPA@  $  TiO_{2}  $  ,” and “  $  TiO_{2}  $   CL +   $  TiO_{2}  $   100-NaPA@  $  TiO_{2}  $  ,” were deposited on ITO-coated glasses in an area of 7.25 mm  $  ^{2}  $  . The as-deposited transparent electrodes were treated with oxygen plasma for 3 min, and then immediately transferred to a   $  N_{2}  $  -filled glove box for fabricating PSCs. The precursor solution (anhydrous dimethylformamide:dimethylsulphoxide (4:1 volume ratio), 1.25 mol L  $  ^{-1}  $   MAI, and 1.3 mol L  $  ^{-1}  $     $  PbI_{2}  $  ) was spin-coated on the ETL-coated transparent electrodes using a two-step spin-coating program at 1000 and 6000 rpm, respectively. 200 mL chlorobenzene was applied to the spin-coated samples followed by annealing the samples at 150 °C for 10 min and then cooling the samples to room temperature, resulting in the formation of a MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   film on the ETL-coated transparent electrode. The samples were then spin-coated with hole transporting material (composed of 85 mg Spiro-OMeTAD dissolved in 1 mL chlorobenzene, 28.8 μL 4-tert-butylpyridine, and 17.8 μL acetonitrile containing 520 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   LiTFSI) at 4000 rpm for 20 s to form an HTL. Finally, an Au layer with the thickness of ≈80 nm was thermally evaporated on the HTL to form the hybrid PSCs.

Material and Device Characterization: The samples were mechanically split, leaving the freshly exposed surface for scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Oxford LEO 1530, and Hitachi SU8010) characterization. The nanostructures were scratched off the substrates and well dispersed in water via ultrasonication for  $ 30\mathrm{min} $ . Several drops of the mixture were applied to a grid with lacey carbon film (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Inc., US). The grid was dried under ambient conditions and characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Tecnai G2 20 STWIN) with selected area electron diffraction (SAED). Transmission and diffuse reflection spectra were monitored using Shimadzu spectrophotometer (mode UV2450, with an integrating sphere unit). The irradiation was incident along the normal direction of the ITO-coated glasses on the sides with no deposition of metals. Steady-state PL spectra were monitored at room temperature, using Horiba spectro Fluorometer (Fluoromax-4). An ITO-coated glass was deposited with a Ti CL (with the thickness of  $ 100\mathrm{nm} $ ) by GLAD, followed by thermal evaporation of Au. The multi-layer device was characterized with the standard four-probe van der Pauw method (HMS-3000, ECOPIA, in a magnetic field of  $ 0.51\mathrm{T} $ ) to measure the  $ \mu_{\mathrm{e}} $  of Ti. Without post-deposition treatment, the samples were characterized by AFM (Dimension-Icon, Bruker), XRD (Bruker, nonmonochromated Cu Kα X-ray with wavelength of  $ 0.15418\mathrm{nm} $ , Advance D8 multipurpose X-ray diffractometer), and XPS (Sengyang SKL-12, nonmonochromatic Mg Kα radiation of  $ 1253.6\mathrm{eV} $ , at a current of  $ 15\mathrm{mA} $ , voltage of  $ 10\mathrm{kV} $ , and takeoff angle (between the sample and detector) of  $ 90^{\circ} $ , and in a vacuum of  $ \approx 2\times 10^{-9} $  mbar). UPS was measured using the XPS equipment, under irradiation with a He discharge lamp having the emission energy of  $ 21.2\mathrm{eV} $ . Under AM1.5 G solar irradiation at  $ 100\mathrm{mWcm}^{-2} $  (SAN-EI, Class AAA solar simulator, XES-70S1), J-V plots and the  $ R_{\mathrm{S}} $  values of the PSCs were monitored using Keithley 2400 measurement system, and IPCE of the PSCs was monitored using the QE-R3018 measurement system (Enlitech Co.). 16 cells of each kind of the PSCs were characterized to statistically analyze their photovoltaic performance.
