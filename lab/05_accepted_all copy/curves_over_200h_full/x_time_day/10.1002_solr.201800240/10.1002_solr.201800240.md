# Suppressed Decomposition of Perovskite Film on ZnO Via a Self-Assembly Monolayer of Methoxysilane

Detao Liu, Yafei Wang, Zhen She, Hualin Zheng, Peng Zhang, Feng Wang, Jiang Wu, Zhi Chen, and Shibin Li*

Although ZnO is an attractive electron transport layer (ETL) for high performance perovskite solar cells (PSCs) due to its suitable energy structure, high electron mobility, and low-temperature process, perovskite films on ZnO are decomposed rapidly as the substrate is heated over 90 °C. However, the annealing temperature higher than 90 °C is mandatory to produce high quality perovskite films. Here, for the first time, the use of an ultra-thin self-assembly monolayer (SAM) of methoxysilane on ZnO ETL to suppress the decomposition of perovskite films is reported. A self-form solvent annealing (SFSA) method is also carried out to improve the crystal quality of perovskite, and the champion device of SAM modified ZnO based PSCs yields a PCE of 18.34%. All these processes are conducted at low temperature and compatible with fabrication of flexible devices.

Methoxysilane is a frequently-used material to modify the surface of the metal oxide in solar cells. This material not only forms a SAM layer on the surface of ZnO via a covalent bonding by condensation reaction between the hydrolyzed alkoxy groups and the hydroxyl groups at the surface of ZnO, but also lowers the work function of the ETL and facilitates the electron transfer from the light harvester layer.  $  ^{[24]}  $   On the surface of the oxide, there is a stable monolayer   $  SiO_{x}  $   film which can inhibit or reduce the contact between light harvester and metal oxide. There are also lower density of hydroxyl groups left after the methoxysilane function. Theoretically, methoxysilane functionalized ZnO can suppress the decomposition of perovskite as the perovskite film on ZnO is heated. Each methoxysilane molecule contains a functional organic group and engineering the functional organic group can control the deposition of upper film. Amino groups facilitate the deposition of perovskite film, so a methoxysilane material with an amino group is a promising modification material.  $  ^{[25]}  $   Until now, no publication reports that methoxysilane materials have been used as interfacial layers on ZnO to suppress the decomposition of perovskite films.

The crystallization of perovskite film also affects the photovoltaic performance of PSCs. Perovskite films with small grain size contains more grain boundary and exhibit much more surface defects, deteriorating the photovoltaic performance of PSCs.  $  ^{[26]}  $   Solvent annealing method is widely used to increase the grain size of perovskite crystal, but it is complicate due to the introduced solvent.  $  ^{[26-28]}  $   To simplify the solvent annealing process, a self-form solvent annealing (SFSA) method has been introduced here. It can also increase the grain size due to the same effect as solvent annealing, but it is simple. The SFSA method is to use a glass slide to cover the perovskite film and form a solvent-rich atmosphere between glass and perovskite film at the high-temperature annealing step.

In this work, it is the first time we employ a kind of methoxysilane to form a SAM layer on the surface of the sol-gel ZnO through interface engineering. The SAM modification on ZnO not only enhances the stability of perovskite films, but also

improves the surface morphology. A SFSA method has been introduced to increase the perovskite grain size and the champion PSC yields a PCE of 18.34%. The stability test of PSCs shows that the SAM modification also improves the moisture stability of perovskite films.

# 2. Results and Discussion

The chemical structure of our self-assembling molecule (3-(aminopropyl)triethoxysilane [APTES]) is shown in Figure 1a. In an APTES molecule, three ethyoxyl groups bond with a silicon atom, and these ethyoxyl groups are used to form a covalent bonding with ZnO surface and other APTES molecules by condensation reaction. The condensation reactions are also shown in Figure 1a according to ref.[29]. A short alkyl chain terminated with an amine group bonds connects with Si via a covalent bonding. The structure of PSCs based on SAM modified ZnO (SAM-ZnO) and SAM layer on ZnO film are represented schematically in Figure 1b according to ref. [24]. To increase the perovskite crystal quality, a SFSA method has been introduced here. The target perovskite films for devices are covered by another glass substrate at the second annealing step. The special and conventional annealing process are shown in Figure 1c. Due to the slow-rate escape of DMSO, a solvent-rich atmosphere is formed in the small space between glass and perovskite film, which functions as the solvent annealing of perovskite films and facilitates the formation of high-crystallization perovskite films.

As shown in Figure 1b, the architecture of photovoltaic devices based on normal planar structure is ITO/(ZnO or SAM-ZnO)/perovskite/PTAA/Au. Doped PTAA and gold layer are the hole transport materials (HTM) and anode. ZnO ETL modified with SAM for 1.5, 3, and 6 h is abbreviated to SAM-ZnO-1, SAM-ZnO-2, and SAM-ZnO-3, respectively. After the fabrication of devices, the photovoltaic performance were characterized under AM 1.5 G sun light (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) illumination. The current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics of the best performing planar PSCs based on ZnO and SAM-ZnO are shown in Figure 2a and the photovoltaic parameters are listed in Table 1. After the modification of the APTES on ZnO film, the photovoltaic performance of PSCs is enhanced dramatically. The device based on ZnO shows the lowest PCE of 5.30%, and the SAM-ZnO-2 based solar cell yields the best performance with a PCE of 13.85%, an open-circuit voltage of 1.00 V, a short-circuit current density of 21.53 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   and a fill factor of 64.25%. Compared with ZnO based PSCs, all the photovoltaic parameters of SAM-ZnO-2 based PSCs are improved dramatically due to the higher-quality perovskite film. The SAM-ZnO-1 based PSCs with a PCE of 10.99% exhibits a better performance compared with the ZnO based PSCs, but a worse performance compared with the PSCs based on SAM-ZnO-2 and SAM-ZnO-3. The treat time of 1.5 h may be not enough for APTES to form a compact monolayer, resulting in partial decomposition of perovskite during annealing. The partial decomposition of perovskites brings defects and bad morphology, inducing a deteriorative performance of PSCs. As the SAM treat time is more than 6 h, the performance of SAM-ZnO based PSCs also decreased. After the deposition of perovskite on ZnO with/without SAM layer, scanning electron microscope (SEM) was used to characterize the surface morphology of perovskite film on different ETLs. The photographs of perovskite films on ZnO with different SAM coverage are shown in Figure S2a, Supporting Information. The photograph of perovskite film on pristine ZnO annealing at 100 °C for 2 min is shown in the left of Figure S2a, Supporting Information. The film is rough and hoar, and the corresponding SEM image is shown in left of Figure S2b, Supporting Information. It is found that there are numerous large gaps between the crystals of the perovskite film on pristine ZnO. These gaps provide the channel for the contact between ZnO and perovskite film, resulting in serious recombination in devices. And these gaps induce the rough surface of perovskite films and are adverse to the deposition of HTL. These issues will deteriorate the photovoltaic performance of PSCs based on pristine ZnO. After SAM treatment of ZnO for 1.5 h, the perovskite on ETL become much darker and smoother, which is shown in middle of Figure S2a, Supporting Information. The morphology of perovskite on SAM-ZnO-1 is improved dramatically from SEM image (middle of Figure S2b, Supporting Information). The gaps between different crystals narrow dramatically but there are still a few of distinct pinholes in the perovskite film. The gaps between different crystals appear due to the decomposition of perovskite film. The aminomethane gas is released and the overall volume of the perovskite film shrinks when the perovskite film is decomposed. After the treatment of SAM for 3 h, the perovskite on ETL shown in right of Figure S2a, Supporting Information become dark black and mirror-like. The film is much more compact and smoother in comparison with the perovskite film on pristine ZnO. However, the average crystal grain size of perovskite film is decrease with the SAM treat time. The grain size is also affected by the surface state of substrates, so water contact angles have been measured as mentioned above. It shows that the SAM-ZnO has a larger water contact angle compared with UV-O₃ treated ZnO. The nonwetting surface can result in larger grain size and it is inconsistent with the SEM results, clarifying that the larger grain sizes is not induced by the surface state. When perovskite film on ZnO is heated, the aminomethane gas is released. According to other groups' researches, the released aminomethane gas can stitch the perovskite grain boundary and enlarge the grain size of crystals.[32]

The absorbance spectra of perovskite film with different annealing time was characterized by UV-vis-NIR spectrometer to identify the degradation process of perovskite when annealed on hotplate. As shown in Figure 5a, the absorption curves of perovskite film on pristine ZnO film show an apparent change when the  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  annealing time is increased from 1 to  $ 2\mathrm{min} $ , indicating that the perovskite has been degraded dramatically. The absorption baseline moves up due to the rougher surface of the perovskite film. In contrast, the film on SAM-ZnO-1 shows

slight change and the step height near 750 nm become smaller slightly. The perovskite film on SAM-ZnO-2 shows the strong absorption of light and negligible change in absorption due to the more compact SAM layer on the surface of ZnO. The more compact SAM layer can inhibit the contact between perovskite and ZnO layer more efficiently, so the decomposition of the perovksite film can be suppressed.

To understand the degradation process and identify the ingredient of the degraded films, X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of perovskite films on different ETLs have been characterized and results are shown in Figure 5b. There is a stronger  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  peak compared with (110) peak of perovskite in the XRD patterns of perovskite film on ZnO film, demonstrating that a lot of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  has been generated. The perovskite films on SAM-ZnO with different treat time also show different XRD patterns. There is also apparent  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  peak in the XRD pattern of perovskite on SAM-ZnO-1, though the  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  peak is much weaker than (110) peak of perovskite, indicating that  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  has been generated due to the incomplete coverage of the SAM layer on ZnO. However, no apparent peak of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  can be identified in the XRD pattern of perovskite film on SAM-ZnO-2. These results are consistent with the absorption results and further identify that the compact SAM on ZnO can mitigate the decomposition of perovskite due to the inhibited contact between perovskite and ZnO.

Although the decomposition of perovskite film has been inhibited dramatically during the annealing step, the small crystallization grain of the perovskite film (Figure 6a) is unfavorable for the high-performance PSCs. The smaller crystallization grain size results in more grain boundaries and surface traps. So special annealing method, SFSA, was introduced to improve the crystallization of perovskite. To confirm the effect of different annealing methods, various of characterizations were employed. The surface morphology of perovskite film obtained by SFSA is shown in Figure 6b. Perovskite films prepared by SFSA display much larger crystal grain size compared with that prepared by conventional method. To identify the affection of large grain size, I–V curves of electron-only devices with an architecture of ITO/ETL/perovskite/PCBM/Ag (inset of Figure S2, Supporting Information) are characterized to compare the trap-state density and the results are shown in Figure S3, Supporting Information. At low bias voltage, the curves showed linear relationship, indicating an ohmic response of the electron-only devices. Then the current increases rapidly when the bias voltage exceeded the kink point, clarifying that the trap states are filled completely and the conductivity increases. The kink point is related to the trap density according to following Equation (2).  $  ^{[33]}  $

$$
V _ {\mathrm{TFL}} = \frac {\mathrm{en} _ {t} L ^ {2}}{2 \varepsilon \varepsilon_ {0}}
$$

(2)

Where e is the elementary charge of the electron, L is the thickness of the perovskite film,   $  \varepsilon  $   is the relative dielectric constant of the

perovskite film,   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   is the vacuum permittivity, and the   $  n_{t}  $   is the trap-state density.   $  V_{TFL}  $   is decided by the kink point of the I–V curves. The   $  V_{TFL}  $   of the device based on SFSA perovskite (0.52 V) is lower than that based on CA perovskite (1.26 V), demonstrating that the SFSA perovskite film has a much lower trap-state density.

The steady-state photoluminescence and TR-PL of perovskite prepared by different annealing methods were measured to identify charges lifetime. To eliminate the effect of ETLs, these perovskite films were deposited on glass. The results of the steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and TR-PL are shown in Figure 6c,d, respectively. The PL peak intensity of perovskite prepared by SFSA method is higher than that of perovskite prepared by CA method. There is no ETL between perovskite and glass, so the charges cannot be transferred. Therefore, PL quenching results from the non-radiation recombination which is not beneficial to photovoltaic performance of PSCs. Lower PL intensity demonstrates there is more non-radiation recombination in CA perovskite film. The decay time of TR-PL can be used to confirm the lifetime of charges in perovskite. From the results of TR-PL, the perovskite film prepared by SFSA method shows obviously longer decay time, clarifying that charges in perovskite film prepared by SFSA method has a longer lifetime. The moisture stability of the perovskite on ZnO and SAM-ZnO are also studied. Perovskite films on ZnO and SAM-ZnO was placed in same box and the moisture is 50%RH at 25°C. The color changes of perovskite film with time are shown in Figure S4a, Supporting Information. Both perovskite films are black initially. Four days later, the perovskite film on ZnO shows apparent color change. However, no distinct change could be identified in the perovskite film on SAM-ZnO. After 8 days, most part of perovskite film on ZnO turns yellow, while there is still no distinct change in the perovskite film on SAM-ZnO. The above phenomena clarify that the perovskite film on SAM-ZnO shows much more stable than that on ZnO. It is because that there are the smaller gaps between the boundaries of two different grains in perovskite film on SAM-ZnO. It has been reported that most degradation of perovskite grains under humid condition starts from grain boundaries.  $  ^{[34]}  $   Smaller gaps between two grain boundaries can contain less water molecules and mitigate the invasion of water molecules, as shown in Figure S4b, Supporting Information. # 3. Conclusion

In conclusion, we have formed a SAM layer on ZnO film and suppressed the decomposition of perovskite films at the annealing temperature of   $  100^{\circ}  $  C. Although the SAM layer increases the work function of ETL slightly, it does not affect the charge transfer from the light-harvester layer. An annealing method called SFSA is used to improve the perovskite grain size and enhance the PCE of SAM based PSCs to 18.34%. Furthermore, perovskite films on SAM show an obviously improved stability in high-moisture condition, resulting in better performance stability of devices.
