Abstract: Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) show great potential for next-generation photovoltaics, due to their excellent optical and electrical properties. However, defects existing inside the perovskite film impair both the performance and stability of the device. Uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $ , uncoordinated I-, and metallic Pb  $ (\mathrm{Pb}^0) $  are the main defects occur during perovskite film preparation and device operation, due to the volatilization of organic cationic components. Passivating these defects is a desirable task, because they are non-radiative recombination centers that cause open-circuit voltage  $ (V_{\mathrm{OC}}) $  loss and degradation of the perovskite layer. Herein, the multifunctional bioactive compound dopamine (DA) is introduced for the first time to control the perovskite film formation and passivate the uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  defects via Lewis acid-base interactions. The  $ \mathrm{Pb}^0 $  and I- defects are effectively suppressed by the DA treatment. At the same time, the DA treatment results in a stronger crystal orientation along the (110) plane and upshifts the valence band of perovskite closer to the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of the hole transport layer  $ (2,2',7,7' $ -tetrakis(N,N'-di-pmethoxyphenylamine)-9,9'-spirobifluorene, spiro-OMeTAD), which is beneficial for charge separation and transport processes. Consequently, the stability of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $   $ (\mathrm{MA} = \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3) $  PSCs prepared with the DA additive (especially the

# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic hybrid halide perovskite materials have attracted much attention due to their excellent photoelectric properties, including long carrier lifetime, high charge carrier mobility, tunable band gap, and high light absorption coefficients, which can be exploited in a variety of fields   $  [1-4]  $  . Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) based on organic–inorganic hybrid perovskite materials have undergone extraordinarily rapid developments in the last decade, with their certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) increasing from 3.8% to 25.2%   $  [5-8]  $  . Although the certified PCE is constantly increasing, the poor long-term stability against moisture and high temperatures remains a huge challenge for the commercialization of PSCs. Previous studies revealed that the degradation of PSCs is mainly caused by corrosive moisture, heat, and UV illumination   $  [9-11]  $  . Strategies including additive engineering   $  [12]  $  , interfacial modification engineering   $  [13-15]  $  , and encapsulation can prolong the device lifetime by isolating these external environmental factors   $  [16,17]  $  .

Non-radiative recombination processes including defect-assisted recombination, Auger recombination, interface-induced recombination and band-tail recombination are detrimental to the performance of PSCs. Among these processes, defect-assisted recombination is inevitable for polycrystalline perovskite materials prepared by

solution processing, due to the large amounts of defects and grain boundaries. This process has negative impact on the electronic and optoelectronic properties of the perovskite layer, which limits the performance enhancements of the PSCs [18]. In addition, these defects not only serve as charge carrier recombination centers causing device performance loss, but also accelerate the decomposition process in the perovskite layer [19,20]. Uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} $ , uncoordinated I-, and metallic Pb ( $ \mathrm{Pb^0} $ ) species are considered the main deep-level trap defects existing in  $ \mathrm{MAPbI_3} $  (MA =  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3 $ ) films [21,22]. The uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} $  ions are easily oxidized to  $ \mathrm{Pb^0} $  under preparation, thermal annealing, and operation conditions. Previous studies have shown  $ \mathrm{Pb^0} $  to be the primary defect causing deterioration of device performance as well as unsatisfactory long-term durability [23,24]. At the same time, uncoordinated I-ions generally form during the thermal annealing process, due to volatilization of organic cationic components. Their migration toward the cathode to form AgI was observed to impair both the performance and long-term durability of the device [25]. Thus, passivating the defects at surfaces and grain boundaries is a desirable task to further enhance both the performance and stability of PSCs. Electron donor Lewis bases such as thiophene and pyridine were introduced to passivate uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} $  ions through coordinate bonding, resulting in decreased nonradiative carrier recombination and extended long-term stability [26,27].

In addition, the matching between energy levels influences the charge transport process. Mismatching energy levels lead to charge accumulation at the interfaces and exacerbate charge carrier recombination. 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N'-di-p methoxyphenylamine)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (spiro-OMeTAD) is commonly used as hole transport layer (HTL) in n-i-p planar devices. However, the energy mismatch between the valence band maximum (VBM) of the perovskite layer and the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of spiro-OMeTAD hinders the transfer of charge carriers and causes recombination at the perovskite/HTL interface. Thus, it is necessary to narrow the energy gap between perovskite layer and HTL.

Dopamine (DA), a bioactive small molecule with catechol structure and an active

3

Journal Pre-proofs

amino group, was previously used to modify Poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene)-polystyrenesulfonate (PEDOT:PSS) films in order to improve the hole extraction capability and passivate surface traps, leading to improved performance and stability [28]. Furthermore, DA was shown to be an effective interfacial layer between perovskite and electron transport layer (ETL,  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  and  $ \mathrm{SnO_2} $ ), which is beneficial for improving charge extraction and reducing charge recombination, thus enhancing the photovoltaic performance and stability of the modified devices [29,30]. Although these interfacial engineering approaches facilitate the development of PSCs, they require an additional spin-coating process, which is unfriendly to the commercial development of PSCs.

In this work, we developed a simple strategy for adding DA into the perovskite precursor solution, based on the following advantages: 1) the C=O bonds of DA effectively passivate the uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   defects through Lewis acid-base interactions; 2) the   $  Pb^{0}  $   defects can be effectively suppressed due to the strong reducibility of DA; 3) moderate DA amounts can narrow the mismatching band gap between perovskite and spiro-OMeTAD layers, thus facilitating charge transport; 4) the amino group of DA can passivate the I-defects. Consequently, the DA additive enhanced the device efficiency (up to 21.03%) and thermal stability, which is superior to those of the pristine devices.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1.Materials

Etching indium tin oxide (ITO) glass sheets (sheet resistance,  $  R_{\mathrm{S}} \approx 10 \Omega / \mathrm{sq}  $ ) were purchased from CSG Holding Co., Ltd. Methyl ammonium iodide (MAI, 99.5%) was purchased from Borun New Material Technology. Lead iodide ( $  \mathrm{PbI}_2  $ , 99.99%), 4-tert-butylpyridine (96%), and spiro-OMeTAD (99.8%) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. Chlorobenzene (99.9%), dimethylformamide (DMF, 99%), and lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (Li-TFSI, 99.95%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO, 99%),  $  \mathrm{SnO}_2  $  solution, and 3-hydroxytyramine hydrochloride (99%) were purchased from Alfa Aesar. The

4

Journal Pre-proofs

3-hydroxytyramine hydrochloride was dissolved in deionized water and its pH was adjusted to 7 by adding sodium hydroxide solution under nitrogen atmosphere. Then, the solution was leached with chlorobenzene for three times. Finally, chlorobenzene was removed by a rotary evaporator to obtain dopamine. Poly[bis(4-phenyl)(2,4,6-trimethylphenyl)amine] (PTAA) was purchased from Derthon Optoelectronic Materials Science Technology Co., Ltd.

# 2.2. Device fabrication

The pre-cleaned ITO substrates were treated with oxygen plasma for 10 min before use. The ETL was prepared by spin-coating the SnO $ _2 $  precursor at 4000 rpm for 30 s, follow by annealing at a temperature of 150 °C for 1 h in the air. After cooling down, the substrates were transferred into a glove box filled with nitrogen. The MAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite precursor was prepared by dissolving MAI and PbI $ _2 $  (1:1.05) into a solvent mixture of DMF and DMSO (4:1, v/v). To prepare the triple-cation perovskite, 176 mg FAI (FA = formamidinium iodide), 498 mg PbI $ _2 $ , 22 mg MABr, and 83 mg PbBr $ _2 $  were dissolved in a 1-mL solvent mixture of DMF and DMSO (4:1, v/v). Next, 50 μL of CsI solution (pre-dissolved as a 1.5 mol stock solution in DMSO) was added to the mixed solution to obtain the desired perovskite precursor solution. The perovskite precursor was then spin-coated onto the ETL at 4000 rpm for 40 s. Within the first 10 s, 200 μL chlorobenzene was dripped as antisolvent to remove the DMSO. The samples were then heated at 100 °C for 10 min to obtained black perovskite films. The as-prepared spiro-OMeTAD precursor (prepared by dissolving 72 mg spiro-OMeTAD in 1 mL chlorobenzene, followed by the addition of 29 μL 4-tert-butylpyridine and 18 μL Li-TFSI solution (520 mg/mL in acetonitrile)) was spin-coated onto the perovskite layer at 4000 rpm for 30 s to form the HTL. Finally, a 2-nm MoO $ _3 $  capping layer and an 80-nm Ag electrode were thermally evaporated onto the HTL under a base pressure of  $ 2.8 \times 10^{-4} $  Pa.

# 3. Results and discussion

The chemical structure of DA is shown in Fig. 1(a); the molecule has two hydroxyl groups and one ethyl amino group on the benzene ring. It is generally accepted that dopamine can be easily oxidized via the Michael addition reaction [31], as shown in Fig. S1. In order to explore the chemical bond between DA and  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ , the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of DA,  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ , and  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  with DA additive ( $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ :DA) are shown in Fig. 1(b). Except for the stretching vibration related to N-H bonds at approximately  $ 1500~\mathrm{cm^{-1}} $ , no other signal was detected around  $ 1700~\mathrm{cm^{-1}} $  for  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ . Two stretching vibrations ascribed to C=O bonds were detected at 1635 and  $ 1745~\mathrm{cm^{-1}} $ , corresponding to the two carbonyl groups on the benzene ring of DA. Upon adding DA to  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ , only one signal at low frequency corresponding to C=O bonds was detected, while the signal at higher frequency disappeared. This indicates the presence of DA in the prepared perovskite film and the potential Lewis acid-base interaction between DA and  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ . XPS measurements were further used to investigate the binding energy changes of I- and uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  in  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  film upon DA addition. Amino compounds are promising materials for I- passivation via hydrogen bond interactions [32]. As shown in Fig. 1(c), the I 3d

peaks of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ :DA film shifted to lower binding energies compared to those of the pristine  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  film, suggesting the occurrence of interactions between iodine ions and amino groups in DA. This interaction confirms that DA can effectively passivate I- defects in the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite film and suppress the free migration of I- towards the cathode, which is beneficial for enhancing both the device performance and the long-term stability. Fig. 1(d) shows the Pb  $ 4f $  spectra of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  and  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ :DA films. In the case of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  film, the two strong peaks observed at 138.0 and  $ 142.9\mathrm{eV} $  can be assigned to the Pb  $ 4f_{7/2} $  and Pb  $ 4f_{5/2} $  states, respectively [33]. Upon adding DA into the perovskite layer, both Pb  $ 4f $  peaks shifted to lower binding energies, confirming the Lewis acid-base interaction between C=O groups in DA and uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  ions in the perovskite layer. It has been reported that metallic lead ( $ \mathrm{Pb}^0 $ ) defects are generally produced from uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  defects under thermal annealing processes [24]. Two weaker peaks located at 136.1 and 141.0 eV, attributed to the  $ \mathrm{Pb}^0 $  species, were observed for the pristine perovskite film [34,35]. Interestingly, the peaks assigned to metallic  $ \mathrm{Pb}^0 $  disappeared after DA treatment, which corroborates the hypothesis that DA can effectively suppress the formation of  $ \mathrm{Pb}^0 $  defects by interacting with uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  [36].

The effect of the DA additive on the crystallization and surface morphology of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite was studied by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), as shown in Fig. 2(a,b). The top-view SEM images reveal that after DA treatment the pinholes disappeared and the perovskite crystals grew close together, forming a more compact surface. The pinholes and grain boundaries provide channels for water and oxygen from the external environment to move inside the perovskite layer, which impairs the device performance and long-term stability [13]. The Lewis acid-base interaction between  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  and C=O bonds in DA would increase the growth activation energy during the formation of perovskite films, which is beneficial for preparing perovskite films of higher crystallinity [37] The corresponding cross-section SEM images in Fig. S2 show that both  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  and  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ :DA films had good crystallinity. The weak peak of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  observed at  $ 12.64^{\circ} $  is attribute to the excess  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  in the perovskite precursor. In this work, the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite was modified with different DA concentrations. The  $ J - V $  curves of PSCs with different DA concentrations are shown in Fig. S3, while the relevant photovoltaic parameters calculated from 12 PSCs for each DA concentration are listed in Table S1. An obvious enhancement in photovoltaic performance was observed by introducing the DA additive in the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  perovskite precursor solution, with concentrations from 0 to  $ 20~\mathrm{mM} $ . Upon increasing the concentration above  $ 20~\mathrm{mM} $ , the performance of the device decreased. The pristine PSCs showed an average PCE of  $ 14.53\% $ , along with an average open-circuit voltage ( $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $ ) of  $ 1.03\mathrm{V} $ , a short-current density ( $ J_{\mathrm{SC}} $ ) of  $ 21.6\mathrm{mA/cm^2} $ , and a fill factor (FF) of  $ 65.3\% $ . The PSCs prepared with a  $ 20~\mathrm{mM} $  concentration of DA additive showed the best performance, with average  $ \mathrm{PCE} = 17.83\% $ ,  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} = 1.07\mathrm{V} $ ,  $ J_{\mathrm{SC}} = 22.7\mathrm{mA/cm^2} $ , and  $ \mathrm{FF} = 73.4\% $ , indicating that introducing an appropriate concentration of DA additive in the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  precursor solution can effectively improve the photovoltaic performance of PSCs. The PSCs prepared with a  $ 20~\mathrm{mM} $  concentration of DA additive exhibited the highest  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $ ,  $ J_{\mathrm{SC}} $ , and FF values, resulting in the highest PCE. Thus, they were selected for the additional analyses discussed below.

The obtained  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ :DA device was higher than that of the reference device, which is attributed to the reduced surface-induced recombination and better matching energy levels of perovskite and spiro-OMeTAD [18,43]. # 4. Conclusions

In summary, DA was introduced for the first time into the perovskite precursor solution to control the crystallization process of the perovskite film and passivate uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   defects via Lewis acid-base interactions.   $  Pb^{0}  $   defects were effectively suppressed due to the strong interaction between C=O bonds and uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $  . Uncoordinated I- defects were passivated by the amino group of DA. At the same time, the DA treatment improved the crystallinity and facilitated crystal growth along the (110) direction, which is beneficial for improving hole transport and reduce non-radiative recombination at the perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD interface. UPS measurements revealed that the VBM of the perovskite was upshifted from -5.53 to -5.22 eV, which is closer to the HOMO of spiro-OMeTAD (-5.2 eV). Consequently, the stability of devices based on   $  MAPbI_{3}:DA  $  , especially the thermal stability, was effectively improved due to the higher crystallinity and lower numbers of defect traps in the perovskite film. PSCs with the DA additive maintained about 90% of their original PCE upon annealing at a temperature of 85 °C for 120 h, which represents a clear improvement over that of the reference PSCs. The best performance

Therefore, using the bioactive DA compound to control the perovskite film formation process and passivate defects inside the film is an effective way to enhance both the performance and long-term stability of PSCs.
