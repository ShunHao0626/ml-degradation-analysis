# Post-Treating the Precursor Intermediate Film by a Cooling Stage for Fabricating Efficient Formamidinium-Based Perovskite Solar Cells

Di Liu,  $  ^{||}  $   Shubo Wang,  $  ^{||}  $   Rui Xia,  $  ^{*}  $   Yibo Xu, Leilei Gu, Ruiyi Li, Xiang Fang, Hongwei Hu, Ningyi Yuan,  $  ^{*}  $   and Jianning Ding

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Formamidinium lead iodide (FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ) is a category of perovskite material with an ideal band gap and high thermal stability, which can be efficiently prepared by two-step spin-coating. Spin-coating organic salts and transforming intermediate phase at the second step involves a components' reaction and state transition, thus playing a crucial role in the film quality formed afterward and optoelectronic properties of the fabricated perovskite solar cells (PSCs). In this paper, a cooling stage (CO) is used to post-treat the as-prepared precursor after the second spin-coating. The procedure of intermediate phase transferring to other state is found to be retarded; hence, the appearing velocity of perovskite nucleation is decreased. As a result, components react more adequately and larger perovskite grains with fewer defects are obtained; charge transport as well as carrier recombination behaviors are therefore optimized. The PSCs based on the CO process achieved a champion power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 21.51%

with enhanced stability. Moreover, CO treatment is observed to be beneficial for improving the film quality of perovskite in ea preparation, which we anticipate can be further extended to the commercialized application of PSCs.

KEYWORDS: perovskite solar cell, formamidinium lead iodide, spin-coating, cooling stage, intermediate phase

# INTRODUCTION

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) draw much attention in the photovoltaic field owing to the high power conversion efficiency (PCE) and low fabrication cost; the state-of-art certified PCE has skyrocketed from 3.8 to 25.5% in just one decade.  $  ^{1-3}  $  Highly efficient PSCs are fabricated based on a typical three-dimensional ABX  $  _{3}  $  perovskite structure, where A  $  ^{+}  $  is usually MA  $  ^{+}  $  (CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}^{+}  $ ), FA  $  ^{+}  $  (HC(NH  $  _{2}  $ )  $  _{2}^{+}  $ ), Cs  $  ^{+}  $ , or Rb  $  ^{+}  $ ; B  $  ^{2+}  $  is usually Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  or Sn  $  ^{2+}  $ ; and X  $  ^{-}  $  is usually a halide ion such as I  $  ^{-}  $ , Br  $  ^{-}  $ , and Cl  $  ^{-}  $ .  $  ^{4,5}  $  Perovskite layers, sandwiched between the charge transporting layers, play an extensive role in light absorption, exciton dissociation, and charge transmission; therefore, the constitution as well as crystallinity of perovskite films are significantly highlighted.  $  ^{6-8}  $

Formamidinium lead iodide (FAPbI $ _3 $ ) is a category of efficient perovskite material with an ideal band gap of around 1.48 eV; the relatively low band gap broadens the absorption of the solar spectrum when used as a light-harvesting layer compared with the most commonly employed and researched perovskite structure, MAPbI $ _3 $ . Therefore, the devices based on FAPbI $ _3 $  possess the potential for achieving a higher PCE. $ ^{9-11} $  However, FAPbI $ _3 $  is reported to easily convert to inefficient yellow  $ \delta $ -phase perovskite at room temperature in an ambient atmosphere while the black  $ \alpha $ -phase perovskite is practically

Both steps are quite vital for spin-coating FAPbI $ _3 $ ; the first step, determined by spin-coating speed, heating temperature, and duration of heating treatment, influences the amount and distribution of PbI $ _2 $  and PbI $ _2 $ -DMSO, which further affects the PbI $ _2 $  in the perovskite layer, which functions as interface passivation and also the reaction with organic salts. $ ^{21,22} $  For the second step of spin-coating FAIs and usually other additives such as MAIs, MABrs, and MACIs, the intermediate phases are partially formed and start converting to other patterns with the escape of solvents. $ ^{21,23} $  Procedures of crystal nucleation as well as grain growth are involved herein; hence, the precursor film at this state is so sensitive that it is supposed to be carefully modified. $ ^{24,25} $  Theoretically, the nucleation of crystals is closely related to the critical Gibbs free energy ( $ \Delta G^* $ ), which is determined mainly by the molar volume of solute particles, supersaturation of the solution, and temperature. When other influencing factors are invariable,  $ \Delta G^* $  is improved as the temperature decreases. $ ^{26-28} $  Therefore, if we put the precursor film into the atmosphere with low temperature,  $ \Delta G^* $ , regarded as the energy barrier for perovskite nucleation, is conceived to be increased, which leads to the retardation of the nucleation event and is beneficial for controlling the subsequent film growth.

Following this train of thought, a facile methodology assisted by a cooling stage is employed in this paper to optimize the two-step spin-coating process for preparing the FAPbI $ _3 $  films with a small doping amount of MA $ ^+ $ . After spin-coating organic salt solutions, including FAI, MAI, and MACl, on PbI $ _2 $  films, refer to the report of You et al., $ ^{19} $  the samples are immediately transferred to a cooling stage with a temperature around 13  $ ^{\circ} $ C for aging; this treatment, abbreviated as CO, is proved to be provided with the effect of restraining the departure of DMSO from an intermediate phase, and hence, slowing down the appearing velocity of FAPbI $ _3 $  nucleation. As a result, larger perovskite grains with fewer defects are obtained. The PSCs based on the CO process achieved a champion PCE of 21.51% with enhanced stability. This route can efficiently optimize the preparation of perovskite films and owns the potential for application in PSC fabrication in large areas.

# EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

Materials. PbI $ _2 $  (>99%), methylammonium iodide (MAI, >99%), methylammonium chloride (MACl), and formamidinium iodide (FAI, >99%) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology. N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF, >99.9%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, >99.9%), isopropanol (IPA, >99.9%), and NbCl $ _5 $  (99.9%) were obtained from Alfa Aesar. Spiro-OMeTAD was purchased from Borun New Material Technology. TiCl $ _4 $  (99.9%) was purchased from Aladdin. All of the materials were directly used without further purification. Fluorine-doped tin oxide on glass (FTO glass, square resistance: 6–8 Ω/sq, Haze: 11–17%) was obtained from Asahi Glass Co., Ltd. (AGC).

Device Fabrication. FTO glasses were ultrasonically cleaned using acetone, ethanol, and deionized water, respectively. Then, they were dried with nitrogen stream and cleaned by UVO light for 10 min immediately before using. The Nb-doped TiO  $  _{2}  $  (Nb/Ti = 0.05, mol/mol) layer (30–40 nm) was prepared by the chemical bath deposition method (0.2 M TiCl  $  _{4}  $  aqueous solution with NbCl  $  _{3}  $  hydrochloric acid (38%) solution (0.18 g/mL) at 70 °C for 76 min).

The preparation of the perovskite layer was according to a previous report;  $  ^{19}  $   basically, at the first step, 1.5 M PbI  $  _{2}  $   in a DMF/DMSO (9:1) solvent was spin-coated onto TiO  $  _{2}  $   at 1500 rpm for 30 s, and then annealed at 70 °C for 10 s and cooled to room temperature in a glovebox. At the second step, a mixed solution of FAI/MAI/MACl (90:6.39:9 mg in 1 mL IPA) was spin-coated onto PbI  $  _{2}  $   also in a

glovebox at a spin rate of 2000 rpm for 30 s. For the CO treatment, the sample was rapidly transferred onto the cooling stage after spin-coating organic salts for a definite period. For obtaining perovskite films, the samples were required to be thermally treated in ambient air with the humidity of around 40% at 150 °C for 15 min.

The hole transporting layer (HTL) was prepared by spin-coating at 4000 rpm for 30 s using a chlorobenzene solution by dissolving 90 mg of spiro-OMeTAD in 1 mL of chlorobenzene with the addition of 45   $  \mu  $  L of Li-TFSI/acetonitrile (170 mg/mL), 75   $  \mu  $  L of KF209/acetonitrile (100 mg/mL), and 10   $  \mu  $  L of tri-n-butyl phosphate (TBP). Finally, 100 nm of gold was prepared by thermal evaporation.

Characterization. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra outside a glovebox were obtained using a D/max 2500 PC X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (Rigaku Corporation). For testing, after putting the sample on a cooling stage for a specified time, we quickly took it out and started to measure the XRD spectra in 1 min. The XRD spectra inside the glovebox were obtained using a D2 PHASER X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (Bruker Corporation). The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (S-4800. Hitachi Corporation). The time-resolved photoluminescence (PL) decay was measured with a DeltaFlex lifetime system (HORIBA, IBH, GLA). A picosecond pulsed laser with a wavelength of 505 nm was used; the repetition rate was set as 100 kHz for the time window of 6.5 μs. The decay profiles were obtained at the peak emission (800 nm); UV–vis spectra were obtained by a UV-2600 spectrometer (Shimadzu Corporation). The J-V curves were measured by a Keithley 2400 source and the solar simulator with standard AM 1.5G (Newport Oriel 94043A, AM 1.5, 100 mW/cm²). The light intensity was calibrated by applying a Si reference cell (PVM 900, 2 × 2 Si BK7 window, M factor = 0.9398), and the scan step was 0.02 V; the delay time between the steps was 50 ms. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) was measured using an EnliTech EQE measurement system (QE-R3011, Enli Technology Co., Ltd.). A 3 × 3-mm² metal mask was used for both EQE and J-V measurements. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted on a frequency response analyzer (Zahner, Zennium, Germany); the cells were measured under the illumination (using a solar simulator with standard AM 1.5G, 100 mW/cm²), the frequency range was 1–10⁶ Hz, and the magnitude of the alternative signal was 10 mV.

Recently, Wang et al. also reported a method of employing low-temperature aging (30 °C) for post-treating the precursor film to improve the film quality. They explained that the optimization mechanism was attributed to the fact that   $  \alpha  $  -phase perovskite was seeded in the intermediate and the nucleation of the   $  \beta  $  -phase was prevented.  $  ^{23}  $   However, we herein proposed a different viewpoint (demonstrated in Figure 3) according to our experimental phenomena. During the fabrication of perovskite layers, the   $  PbI_{2}-DMSO  $   intermediate phase was first formed; we tested the XRD patterns of the   $  PbI_{2}  $   spin-coated films before and after heat treatment, as shown in Figure S3. It indicated that the amount of   $  PbI_{2}  $   was increased because of the conversion from   $  PbI_{2}-DMSO  $  ; therefore, the precursor film before dripping organic salts was actually a

mixture of   $  PbI_{2}  $   and   $  PbI_{2}-DMSO.^{33}  $   The bonding between   $  PbI_{2}  $   and DMSO molecule relied on van der Waals interactions. On the basis of the report of Seok's group,  $  ^{14}  $   when FAIs and MAIs were involved in the reaction, they generally replaced the internal DMSO due to the stronger ionic interactions between two salts; along with the removal of DMSO, the   $  FA_{x}MA_{1-x}PbI_{3}  $   nucleation appeared.  $  ^{38,39}  $   On the other hand,   $  PbI_{2}  $   already existing in a precursor also reacted with organic salts and formed crystalline   $  FA_{x}MA_{1-x}PbI_{3}  $ . Therefore, we speculated that the conversion from orange to wine red and then black was attributed to the formation of   $  \alpha-FA_{x}MA_{1-x}PbI_{3}  $   constantly and escape of DMSO afterward. Nevertheless,   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   was unstable at room temperature and transformed to a   $  \delta  $  -perovskite phase if preserved for too long, which explained the appearance of the films turning yellow. When the CO process was applied, the evaporation of DMSO as well as the nucleation of   $  FA_{x}MA_{1-x}PbI_{3}  $   were considered to be retarded owing to the low temperature, thus there was a little change in the color,  $  ^{40,41}  $   and also for this reason, the formation of seed crystals and its subsequent growth slowed down, resulting in the enlargement of grains as well as reduced grain boundaries, which explained the observation in SEM images.  $  ^{42-44}  $

Derived from the signals of the intermediate phase, as shown in Figure 2b, the removal of most DMSO happened at least after  $ 2\mathrm{min} $ . Moreover, the "frozen" films after CO treating, somehow, protected the intermediate state from decomposing and transferring to  $ \delta $ -perovskite during the XRD testing; hence, we could see the peaks of the intermediate phase. Steady-state photoluminescence spectra (PL) and transient PL decay of the samples with a structure of glass/perovskite excited at 460 nm were measured to study the charge transferring process to evaluate the film quality. As shown in Figure 5, the emission peaks for all of the samples in the PL measurements located at 810 nm agreed well with the absorption edge calculated by the UV–vis spectrum. When the photoelectrons are generated without transferring outside, i.e., bare perovskite layers with and without CO were directly deposited on glass, the energy is released by photoluminescence as electrons travel to the ground state and the nonradiative recombination process caused by defects in the films. As shown in Figure 5a, CO-treated samples had a higher PL intensity than the control group, indicating the existence of fewer defects in perovskite bulk.  $  ^{48,49}  $   A similar conclusion could also be learnt from the PL decay spectrum, depicting a dynamic behavior of the carriers. As shown in Figure 5b, the curves were analyzed with biexponential fits, and the fitting results are summarized in the inset. The pristine perovskite

film demonstrated a shorter PL lifetime (1.05   $  \mu  $  s) than the CO-treated sample (1.89   $  \mu  $  s), indicating the modifying function of the CO process in the suppression of defects in bulk perovskite, which led to nonradiative recombination. The ameliorated film condition contributed to the improvement of the optoelectronic performance of devices, especially the   $  V_{oc}  $   values.  $  ^{50,51}  $

The space charge limit current (SCLC) measurements were performed on the device structure of FTO/TiO $ _2 $ /perovskite/PCBM/Au to quantitatively characterize the trap densities within the perovskite films after different treatments. As shown in Figure 5e, the logarithmic  $ I-V $  curves for both of the groups showed three different regions with the increase of loaded bias voltage and they can be identified according to the different slopes: $ ^{23,56} $  region 1 (blue part, slope = 1) corresponded to the ohmic region, region 2 (green part, slope >2) represented the TFL region, and region 3 (yellow part, slope = 2) meant the SCLC region. When region 1 transited to region 2, the trap-filled limit voltage ( $ V_{\text{TFL}} $ ) could be estimated when all of the traps were filled, and the defect density ( $ N_{\text{traps}} $ ) could be calculated through the following formula

$$
N _ {\mathrm{traps}} = 2 \varepsilon \varepsilon_ {0} V _ {\mathrm{TFL}} / e L ^ {2}
$$

where e is the elementary charge,   $  \varepsilon  $   (value = 32) and   $  \varepsilon_{0}  $   (value =   $  8.85 \times 10^{-12}  $   F/m) are the relative dielectric constant of perovskite films and the vacuum permittivity, respectively, and L is the thickness of the perovskite film, which we have illustrated by the SEM image. As a result, the   $  N_{traps}  $   values for the perovskite films with no treatment as well as CO treatment were calculated to be   $  5.79 \times 10^{15}  $   and   $  3.57 \times 10^{15}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $  , respectively, inferring that the CO demonstrated a positive effect on decreasing the defects in perovskite bulks, which contributed to the increase of   $  V_{oc}  $   values for the afterward fabricated cells.  $  ^{57}  $

We further compared the stability of devices with different treatments. The uncapsulated cells, kept in the dark condition with the humidity of around 20%, were tested every 2 days; as shown in Figure 5f, the cells with CO treatment preserved around 83% of the initial PCE after 1 month, while the control cells dropped to 52%; the variation trends of   $  J_{sc}  $  ,   $  V_{o}  $  , and FF are shown in Figure S10. The attenuation of device performance mainly resulted from the decreased   $  J_{sc}  $   and FF, which could be caused by the decomposition of perovskite. Owing to CO treatment, the remaining   $  PbI_{2}  $   as well as defects in perovskite bulks were relatively reduced; hence, the charges were less possibly accumulated in the interface and the ionic migration was suppressed. As a result, the shelf stability was enhanced.  $  ^{58,59}  $

# CONCLUSIONS

In summary, we have developed a facile approach employing a cooling stage to treat the   $  FA_{x}MA_{1-x}PbI_{3}  $   precursor intermediate film formed via two-step spin-coating. The procedure of intermediate phase transferring to the other state was observed to be retarded when the CO process was applied, which resulted in a reduced nucleation velocity of perovskite and an adequate reaction for the components; thus, larger perovskite grains with fewer defects were obtained. Based on this treatment, the corresponding PSCs achieved an improved PCE of 21.51% on a small area (0.09 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ) and a module output efficiency of 13.36% with an active area of (7.2 cm  $  ^{2}  $  ), which could be attributed to the optimized charge transport as
