# 1. Introduction

Perovskites based on organic-inorganic lead halides with a formula of   $  APbX_{3}  $   (A = formamidinium (FA), methylammonium (MA); X = Cl, Br, and I) have attracted significant research interest due to their easy fabrication processes and excellent optoelectronic properties and have achieved remarkable progress to date, demonstrating their promising suitability for applications in the photovoltaic field [1–5]. Besides harvesting sun light, the perovskite active layer also is used to transport photogenerated charge carriers, therefore playing a crucial role in device performance [6,7]. However, failure to precisely control the crystallization process of perovskite often leads to poor morphological with grain boundaries, low grain size, and trap sites of the perovskite film, which leads to low efficiency and stability [8–15]. Therefore, many efforts have been made to solve the issues above.

Currently, two main measures are used to improve the crystal quality of solution method perovskite films: composition engineering and fabrication process   $  [16–29]  $  . On the one hand, compared with MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   as the standard composition of perovskite, a mixed perovskite layer can exhibit better cell performance and stability by partially substituting I  $  ^{-+}  $   ions. High power conversion efficiency (PCE) and good long-term stability of the perovskite solar cell (PSC) are two important factors on its commercial applications. Here, we propose a simple way to improve the PSC performance by adding 3,3',5,5'-azobenzene-tetracarboxylic acid (H4abtc) into the lead iodide (PbI2) precursor solution with Lewis acid-base adduct via two-step solution method. 24% enhancement of the PCE is achieved for the PSC with 2.0% H4abtc comparing with the pure PSC without H4abtc. Further research suggests that the H4abtc can passivate grain boundaries of perovskite film by reacting with the lead cation, therefore leading to good thermal stability and anti-moisture of perovskite films. The paper provides a new method to prepare a stable and efficient PSC by employing a novel Lewis base to passivate grain boundaries and gain high-quality perovskite films.

On the other hand, the fabrication processes mainly concern the perovskite nucleation and crystallization, which will strongly influence the film morphology, grain size and crystallinity   $  [23–39]  $  . Many methods have been reported to control fabrication processes, such as interface engineering, external electric-field poling, solvent engineering, passivation engineering, and the Lewis acid-base adduct   $  [26–39]  $  . In the early period of the Lewis acid-base adduct method,   $  PbI_{2}  $   is used as a Lewis acid while polar aprotic solvents of N,N-dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) acted as Lewis bases   $  [26]  $  . Afterwards, other Lewis bases including lone pairs on nitrogen, sulfur, and oxygen atoms such as thiourea, N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone, and hexamethylphosphoramide are also utilized   $  [28]  $  . For example, DMSO/urea-based perovskite solar cell (PSC) gained 20.06% of the power conversion efficiency (PCE), indicating that the introduction of Lewis bases can promote grain growth, and improve the PCE   $  [27]  $  . Guan and coworkers introduced the controlled tetraethyl orthosilicate additive into the perovskite precursor to improve trap passivation   $  [29]  $  .

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1. Materials preparation

All materials were gained from Aldrich and employed as received without other purification unless otherwise stated. The   $  TiO_{2}  $   paste was prepared according to previous papers [40]. 1.0 mol L  $  ^{-1}  $     $  PbI_{2}  $   precursor solutions were prepared by adding different amounts (0%, 1.0%, 1.5%, 2.0%, 2.5%, and 3.0% (mass ratio with respect to   $  PbI_{2}  $  )) of H  $  _{4}  $  abtc (3,3',5,5'-azobenzene-tetracarboxylic acid, Jinan Henghua Sci. & Tec. Co., Ltd.) in the mixed solvent of DMSO and DMF (volume ratio = 3 : 7). A mixed solution of 73 mg Spiro-MeOTAD, 12 mg Li-TFSI, and 34   $  \mu  $  L 4-tert-butyl-pyridine in 1 mL of chlorobenzene was utilized to prepare a hole transporting material.

# 2.2. Device fabrication

The cleaned fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) coated glass (NSG, Japan) was used as the substrate to fabricate the PSC. A compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   film was spin-coated on the FTO substrate in the air at 500 rpm for 9 s and 2500 rpm for 30 s. The film was then annealed at 450 °C for 30 min. A mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer was spin-coated on the compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   film and heated at the same speed and temperature [41]. Perovskite films were deposited by a two-step solution method. The precursor solution of   $  PbI_{2}  $   with or without additive was spin-coated and then dried for 10 min on 100 °C hot plate. Subsequently, 10 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   MAI in 2-propanol was spin-coated on top of it. The perovskite layer was then dried at 100 °C for 10 min in air. Spiro-OMeTAD hole transporting material was spin-coated

onto the perovskite layer at 2000 rpm for 20 s. Finally, 100 nm Au film was deposited to fabricate the device.

# 2.3. Characterization

The infrared spectrometric analyzer (BRUKER TENSOR 27) was used to record the Fourier transform infrared spectra (FTIR). The UV–visible spectrometer was used to measure the UV–vis absorbance spectrum. The X-ray diffraction (BRUKER D8-ADVANCE) was employed to research the crystal structure of the perovskite film. The morphology of the perovskite films was observed using JEOL-JSM-6701F field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM). The photoluminescence (PL) spectrum was tested with Edinburgh Instruments FLS980 fluorescence spectrometer. The photovoltaic performance of the PSC were measured with CHI660D under irradiation of   $  100 \, mW cm^{-2}  $   (AM 1.5). The active cell area was controlled to   $  0.09 \, cm^2  $   by applying a black mask.

# 3. Results and discussion

Fig. 1 A-F show the FESEM images of perovskite layers with different amounts of  $ \mathrm{H}_4 $  abtc. The pure  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  film consists of small grains with a size of about  $ 100\mathrm{nm} $  (Fig. 1 A). When only  $ 1.0\% $   $ \mathrm{H}_4 $  abtc was added into the  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  solution, there is no observable change in terms of the grain size (Fig. 1 B) compared to the pure  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  film. This manifests that a slight amount of  $ \mathrm{H}_4 $  abtc does not obviously change the morphology of the perovskite film in our experiment. Then we increased the amount of  $ \mathrm{H}_4 $  abtc, the large grains appeared (Fig. 1 C). However, when the  $ \mathrm{H}_4 $  abtc increased to  $ 2.0\% $ , larger grains  $ (300\mathrm{nm}) $  and significantly reduced pinholes are observed in the perovskite film (Fig. 1 D). With the addition of  $ 2.5\% $   $ \mathrm{H}_4 $  abtc (Fig. 1 E), a small number of grains become smaller. In order to get more insights on the morphological influence of  $ \mathrm{H}_4 $  abtc in the perovskite film, the amount of  $ \mathrm{H}_4 $  abtc was further increased to  $ 3.0\% $ . However, when the  $ \mathrm{H}_4 $  abtc additive increased to  $ 3.0\% $ , most of grains become smaller (Fig. 1 F). In general, comparing these amounts, we found that when the addition amount was  $ 2.0\% $ , the film demonstrated less pinholes and larger grains than those of other films. The C=O group in   $  H_{4}abtc  $   could easily bind with the Pb element in   $  PbI_{2}  $   because of the strong electronegativity of O element, and the hydroxyl group in   $  H_{4}abtc  $   could bind with I element in   $  PbI_{2}  $   because of the hydrogen-bonding interaction. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to understand the effect of the   $  H_{4}  $  abtc-additive on the crystal structure (Fig. 2). The strong Bragg peaks at 14.11, 19.92, 23.37, 24.63, 28.26, 31.92, 40.49, and 42.98° can be indexed to the (1 1 0), (1 1 2), (2 1 1), (2 0 2), (2 2 0), (3 1 0), (2 2 4), and (3 1 4) diffractions of the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   crystal [40,41]. Moreover, there were no additional peaks or peak shifts in the XRD patterns of the perovskite films with and without H  $  _{4}  $  abtc additive, which might be owing to that the additive is slightly incorporated into perovskite. However, it is interesting that the intensity of characteristic diffraction peaks increases by the incorporation of H  $  _{4}  $  abtc additive, which manifests the crystallinity of the perovskite film improves obviously. Especially, when the additive amount is 2.0%, the intensity of peak at 14.11° is the strongest. Furthermore, the peak intensity at 12.54° of PbI  $  _{2}  $   improved when the H  $  _{4}  $  abtc amount increased, which could be attributed to the incomplete reaction between MAI and PbI  $  _{2}  $  . This phenomenon indicates the H  $  _{4}  $  abtc additive can influence the reaction extent of the PbI  $  _{2}  $   with MAI by controlling the crystal growth rate [42–44]. This is consistent with the FESEM images above.

Due to the introduction of  $ \mathrm{H_4abtc} $ , the PL peak intensity of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ -2.0%  $ \mathrm{H_4abtc} $  perovskite increases while the HWHM reduces, suggesting increased crystallite size and decreased bulk defect [47]. The result shows that the non-radiative carrier recombination has been effectively retarded with the addition of the  $ \mathrm{H_4abtc} $  which is attributed to the formation of Lewis adducts can passivate the trap states of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  [48]. Moreover, when introducing  $ \mathrm{H_4abtc} $  in the perovskite, there is about  $ 3\mathrm{nm} $  red shift of the steady-state PL peak. The adjustment of energy level position and dispersion contributes to the red shift of PL peak [42,45,47]. Furthermore, the red shift of PL peak is also caused by the increased grain size of the perovskite [45,49]. Fig. 6 B shows the absorption spectra. When compared with the pristine film, the film with the  $ \mathrm{H_4abtc} $  showed stronger absorption at the long wavelength range over  $ 510\mathrm{nm} $ , which potentially attributes to the impact of  $ \mathrm{H_4abtc} $  additive on the uniform surface morphology and the improvement of crystallinity.

Fig. 7 A shows the diagram of PCE values under different aging time at  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  without encapsulation in air and at irradiation of  $ 100\mathrm{mWcm}^{-2} $  (AM 1.5). The PCE of the PSC without  $ \mathrm{H}_{4}\mathrm{abtc} $  decreases quickly from its initial PCE by around  $ 25\% $  after  $ 20\mathrm{h} $ , while the PSC with  $ 2.0\% $   $ \mathrm{H}_{4}\mathrm{abtc} $  has good stability retaining  $ 61\% $  of its initial PCE under the same conditions. In Fig. 7 B, the  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  phase intensity  $ (2\theta = 12.54^{\circ}) $  for the perovskite film with  $ 2.0\% $   $ \mathrm{H}_{4}\mathrm{abtc} $  additive increased slower than that of the perovskite film without  $ \mathrm{H}_{4}\mathrm{abtc} $ . This phenomenon indicates a greatly enhanced thermal stability of the perovskite with  $ \mathrm{H}_{4}\mathrm{abtc} $  additive, which is due to that the rigidity of azo group and benzene ring can improve the perovskite stability.

Fig. 8 shows the change of PCE values with the time increasing, which were tested in air and at irradiation of   $  100 \, mW cm^{-2}  $   (AM 1.5). The PSCs without encapsulation were stored in a dark moisture-proof box (relative humidity of 30%) in air at room temperature (  $  25^{\circ}C  $  ). The PSC without   $  H_{4}abtc  $   degrades fast to its initial PCE of about 68%, while the PSC with   $  2.0\% H_{4}abtc  $   has good stability keeping about 84% of its initial PCE under the same conditions after 30 d. It indicates that the reduced trap states might be responsible for enhancing the stability, and the hydrophobic benzene ring further protect the perovskite from reacting with water [12].

# 4. Conclusions

MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  perovskite film with H  $  _{4}  $  abtc was prepared with Lewis acid-base adduct via two-step solution method to describe the effect of H  $  _{4}  $  abtc on the morphology, grain size, and crystallinity of the perovskite.
