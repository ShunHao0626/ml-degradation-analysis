# Durable Defect Passivation of the Grain Surface in Perovskite Solar Cells with   $  \pi  $  -Conjugated Sulfamic Acid Additives

Kun Cao,  $  ^{\dagger}  $   Yue Huang,  $  ^{\dagger}  $   Mengru Ge, Fei Huang, Wenjian Shi, Yupei Wu, Yangfeng Cheng, Jie Qian, Lihui Liu, and Shufen Chen  $  ^{*}  $

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Defect passivation has shown an essential role in improving the efficiency and stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Herein, an efficient and low-cost   $  \pi  $  -conjugated sulfamic acid additive, 4-aminobenzenesulfonic acid (4-ABSA), is used to realize durable defect passivation of PSCs. The incorporation of 4-ABSA not only constructs a compact and smooth perovskite film but is also capable of passivating both negative- and positive-charged defects derived from under-coordinated lead and halogen ions. Besides, the   $  \pi  $  -conjugated system in 4-ABSA can induce preferred perovskite crystal orientation and stabilize the coordination effect between 4-ABSA and perovskite grains. As a result, the inverted planar PSC incorporated with 4-ABSA additives demonstrates an improved power conversion efficiency (PCE) from 18.25 to 20.32%. Moreover, this 4-ABSA passivation agent also enhances the stability of devices, which retains 83.5% of its initial efficiency under ambient condition at 60 °C after 27 days. This work

provides a   $  \pi  $  -conjugated sulfamic acid for durable defect passivation of perovskite optoelectronic devices.

KEYWORDS: perovskite solar cell, interface engineering, additive,   $  \pi  $  -conjugated, defect passivation

# INTRODUCTION

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have received considerable attention due to their high-performance and cost-effective preparation processes.  $  ^{1-3}  $   The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs has increased from 3.8% to over 25.5% in the past several years, offering great potential for next-generation photovoltaic technology.  $  ^{4-6}  $   However, the stability issue of PSCs remains one of the major challenges, which needs to be solved before their practical application.  $  ^{7-9}  $   This stability issue mainly arises from the easy degradation of perovskite under different conditions of moisture, light illumination, heat, and electric field.  $  ^{9,10}  $   To address these challenges, significant efforts have been devoted to compositional engineering, encapsulation, interface tuning, and additive engineering.  $  ^{11-17}  $

More specifically, several studies have demonstrated that the instability of PSCs is related to the existing defects on the perovskite surface and grain boundaries (GBs).  $  ^{18,19}  $   Because of the intrinsic low formation energy and ionic characteristics of perovskite materials, crystal defects (such as the uncoordinated cations and anions) are easily formed in the solution-processed perovskite polycrystalline films.  $  ^{20-22}  $   These defect sites will initialize the degradation process of perovskite due to their reactivity with oxygen and moisture.  $  ^{23-25}  $   Besides, the charged defects or mobile ions are not stable when the device works under illumination or bias voltage, leading to the degradation of device performance.  $  ^{26-28}  $   Therefore, defect passivation with functional additives has become a commonly used strategy to

For instance, Zhu and co-workers demonstrated the utilization of a bidentate molecule to increase anchoring strength for improving the passivation efficacy and stability of PSCs.  $  ^{46}  $   Recently, Meng and co-workers introduced small aromatic phosphine oxide molecules to form a strong -P=O-Pb coordination to promote stability of the passivation effect.  $  ^{47}  $   Yang and co-workers reported that the passivation molecules with stronger interaction energy between functional groups and crystal defects are advantageous for the stability of the passivation effect.  $  ^{45}  $   Thus, the amphiphilic amino acid molecules with Lewis base P=O and S=O groups are more attractive compared with the commonly used amino acids with C=O groups due to their strengthened interaction with perovskite.  $  ^{45}  $   So far, the zwitterion additives containing the sulfonic group, which can form a strong interaction with lead ions, have received considerable attention in passivating charged defects and regulating perovskite crystallization.  $  ^{14,48}  $   Huang and co-workers incorporated a 3-(decyldimethylammonio)-propane-sulfonate sulfonic zwitterion into the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   precursor solution, which resulted in a PCE of 21.2% and remained 88% of the initial efficiency after continuous light illumination for 480 h.  $  ^{39}  $   Yang and co-workers applied the (4-(1H-imidazol-3-ium-3-yl))-butane-1-sulfonate additive in the regular PSCs, resulting in an enhanced PCE from 18.77 to 20.84%.  $  ^{49}  $   These works have demonstrated great potential of the sulfonic zwitterion in realizing efficient and durable defect passivation of PSCs. However, as far as we know, most of the sulfonic zwitterions used in PSCs have alkyl chains, which may lead to insufficient charge transfer between the perovskite crystals compared with the passivation agent with π-conjugated functional groups.  $  ^{50-53}  $   Besides, the additive engineering of PSCs has demonstrated that the π-conjugated passivators are more easily assembled at the perovskite GBs, which may be critical for the durability of the passivation effect in PSCs.  $  ^{54}  $

In this study, we introduced a   $  \pi  $  -conjugated sulfamic acid (SA) additive, 4-aminobenzenesulfonic acid (4-ABSA), to realize durable defect passivation of perovskite grains. The passivation molecule contains an amine group (  $  -NH_{2}  $  ), a sulfonic group (-SOOOH), and a benzene ring. It was found that the functional groups of 4-ABSA not only had strong coordination capability in the perovskite precursor solution, thus constructing a compact and smooth perovskite film, but also were capable of passivating both negative- and positive-charged defects derived from under-coordinated lead and halogen ions. Moreover, the   $  \pi  $  -conjugated benzene ring in 4-ABSA could induce preferred crystal orientation and stabilize the coordination effect between 4-ABSA and perovskite grains. As a result, the inverted PSCs incorporated with 4-ABSA additives demonstrated an improved PCE from 18.25 to 20.32%. Furthermore, the 4-ABSA passivation agent also enhanced the stability of devices under moisture conditions with heat stress and light illumination.

Materials. 4-Aminobenzenesulfonic acid (4-ABSA, 99.9%), chlorobenzene (CB, 99.5%), ethyl acetate (EA, 99.8%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.9%), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%), cesium iodide (CsI, 99.9%), and bathocuproine (BCP, 99.9%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Formamidinium iodide (FAI), lead iodide (PbI₂, 99.99%), and poly(triaryl amine) (PTAA) ( $ M_{\text{B}} = 6000–15\ 000 $ ) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. Phenyl- $ \mathrm{C}_{61} $ -butyric acid methyl ester ( $ \mathrm{PC}_{61}\mathrm{BM} $ ) and  $ \mathrm{C}_{60} $  (99%) were purchased from Nano-C Tech. All chemicals were used as received.

Device Fabrication. For 4-ABSA-incorporated perovskite precursors, various amounts of 4-ABSA were added into perovskite precursor solutions. The PTAA solution (4 mg/mL in CB) was coated on a UV–ozone cleaned indium tin oxide (ITO) glass and annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. # RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Figure 1a shows the schematic illustration of perovskite crystal passivation using 4-ABSA additives. The passivation molecule contains an amine group (  $  -NH_{2}  $  ), a sulfonic group (  $  -SOOOH  $  ), and a benzene ring. The sulfonic and amine groups are able to passivate both positive- and negative-charged defects derived from the under-coordinated Pb and I ions.  $  ^{42,55}  $   Besides, this kind of   $  \pi  $  -conjugated molecule can easily assemble on GBs due to the rigid structure of the benzene ring.  $  ^{54}  $   To identify this, the molecular interactions between 4-ABSA and perovskite were first investigated. Figure 1b shows the   $  {}^{1}H  $   nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra of pure 4-

ABSA and   $  PbI_{2}  $   mixed with 4-ABSA. A slight chemical shift of the benzene ring-related hydrogen peaks was observed, indicating a strong binding state between   $  Pb^{2+}  $   and 4-ABSA in the solution.  $  ^{56}  $   The Lewis base S=O group of 4-ABSA may donate its lone electron pair to the empty orbitals of   $  Pb^{2+}  $ , thus forming strong coordination bonds after the perovskite film formation. To confirm this assumption, we further investigated the molecular interactions between 4-ABSA and   $  PbI_{2}  $   using FT-IR spectroscopy (Figure 1c). The characteristic S=O vibration peak of 4-ABSA was located at 1035 cm  $  ^{-1}  $ , which shifted to 1044 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   after being mixed with   $  PbI_{2}  $ . The increase of the S=O stretching wavenumber could be explained by the decreased spatial symmetry of the   $  SO_{3}^{-}  $  group after binding with   $  Pb^{2+}  $ , and thus the symmetric stretching vibration became harder.  $  ^{57}  $   XPS measurements of perovskite samples were also performed to characterize the coordination interactions between 4-ABSA and perovskite. Figure 1d shows that Pb 4f  $  _{7/2}  $   and Pb 4f  $  _{5/2}  $   signals for the pristine film are located at 138.7 and 143.5 eV, respectively. The inclusion of 4-ABSA into the perovskite film decreases the binding energies of Pb 4f peaks by   $  \sim0.3  $   eV, which indicates the decreased cationic charge of under-coordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $  .  $  ^{14,49}  $   The binding energies of S=O and S-O increased   $  \sim0.9  $   eV when 4-ABSA was added to the perovskite film (Figure S1). This could be attributed to the electron donation from the sulfonic group of 4-ABSA through the coordination interaction. Besides, the peaks of metallic Pb located at 141.8 and 136.8 eV in the pristine perovskite film were disappeared for the 4-ABSA-incorporated film. In addition, the I 3d and Br 3d spectra of the 4-ABSA-incorporated perovskite film shifted to lower binding energy compared with those of the pristine perovskite film (Figure S1). Meanwhile, compared with the N 1s spectra of 4-ABSA, the amine group from 4-ABSA in the perovskite sample had a clear change from 400.1 to 400.5 eV (Figure S1). These results confirmed the molecular interaction between 4-ABSA additives and the perovskite film.

The grain size of the 4-ABSA-incorporated film increases with increasing concentration of 4-ABSA additives (Figure 2e). This may be ascribed to the more 4-ABSA molecules in the precursor solution, and the more lead ions are complex with the sulfonic group in 4-ABSA, which slows down the crystallization rate and thus leads to a large grain size of perovskite. As shown in Figure S2, when the perovskite precursor solution concentration was as low as 0.2 M, larger crystals were seen in the perovskite with 4-ABSA additives and the grain size of perovskite also increased with increasing concentrations of 4-ABSA additives. The role of 4-ABSA in controlling the crystallization of the perovskite precursor was also investigated by TEM, as shown in Figure S3. A much more uniform crystal nucleus was formed in the 4-ABSA-incorporated perovskite precursor during the preparing process of the perovskite film. As a result, a compact morphology with a larger and more uniform grain size was obtained for the perovskite film with 0.1% 4-ABSA additives compared to the control film. Further increasing the 4-ABSA concentration to 0.2% could lead to an irregular morphology of perovskite films due to the disruption of the ordered perovskite structure by excess additives.  $  ^{48}  $

The effect of the 4-ABSA additive on perovskite crystallization and grain orientation was investigated by XRD measurements (Figure 3a). All samples displayed similar diffraction peaks with no obvious shift, indicating that the 4-ABSA molecules did not participate in the formation of the perovskite lattice. Besides, Figure S4 shows the XRD patterns of the perovskite films treated with the 4-ABSA supersaturated solution in isopropanol. There were also no additional peaks appeared, and the diffraction peaks were not notably shifted. These results implied that the 4-ABSA additives did not affect the phase of perovskite. In contrast, the 4-ABSA-incorporated films exhibited an enhanced diffraction intensity for peaks located at 14.12 and 28.42°, corresponding to the (110) and

(220) crystal planes, respectively. This result suggests a higher crystallinity of perovskite films with 4-ABSA additives. Moreover, the peak intensity ratios of (110)/(310) planes were compared to analyze the grain orientation of samples with different 4-ABSA additives. Figure 3b shows that the peak intensity ratios of (110)/(310) planes increased with increasing concentration of 4-ABSA additives. In the presence of 0.1% 4-ABSA additives, the calculated (110)/(310) ratio was increased from 1.74 to 2.49 compared with that of the reference film. The larger value of the calculated (110)/(310) ratio corresponds to an increased crystallinity and preferred grain growth orientation.  $  ^{39}  $   Therefore, we may conclude that the incorporation of 4-ABSA into the perovskite precursor can induce preferred crystal orientation of the (110) plane. This result can be ascribed to the   $  \pi  $  -conjugated benzene ring in 4-ABSA, which prefers to induce perovskite-orientated growth of the (110) plane.  $  ^{54,58-60}  $   For comparison, we studied the influence of sulfamic acid (  $  H_{2}N-SO_{3}H  $  , SA) without a benzene ring on XRD patterns of the perovskite films prepared at the same conditions. As shown in Figure S5, there was no significant enhancement of crystallinity in the (110) plane for the perovskite films prepared with SA additives. Therefore, we may ascribe the orientated growth of the (110) plane of perovskites with 4-ABSA additives to the   $  \pi  $  -conjugated benzene ring. The PL spectra for perovskite films without and with 0.1% 4-ABSA are presented in Figure 3c. 4-ABSA-incorporated perovskite film exhibited an enhanced PL intensity compared with the pristine one. Besides, TRPL spectra of perovskite films on a glass are displayed in Figure 3d, and the corresponding fitted results are presented in Table 1. The 4-ABSA-based sample exhibited a longer PL lifetime (107.86 ns) compared with the pristine one (59.29 ns), which was consistent with the enhanced PL intensity (Figure 3c). As shown in Figure S8, the 4-ABSA sample had a relatively larger water contact angle (64.5°) than that of the pristine film (45.7°). This could retard moisture permeation into the perovskite, thus improving stability. Figure S9 shows the photographs of perovskite films under air conditions with a relative humidity (RH) of ~80%. Obviously, the perovskite film without 4-ABSA decomposed mostly after 10 days, whereas the 4-ABSA-treated film still maintained a relatively darker color. Figure S10 shows the evolution of XRD patterns for perovskites under air condition with a RH of ~40%. For the control sample, the PbI₂ diffraction peak increased obviously, indicating serious perovskite decomposition. In contrast, only trace amount of the PbI₂ peak could be detected for the 4-ABSA-incorporated sample after over 10 days. This result was consistent with the evolution of absorption spectra in Figure S11. The 4-ABSA-modified film showed higher absorption intensity than the pristine one after 10 days aging. Thus, these results confirmed that a slower degradation of the perovskite film can be achieved using 4-ABSA additives. Figure 6a,b exhibits the long-term stability of the PSCs under aging conditions with thermal stressing and light illumination, respectively. For the unencapsulated devices aging at 60 °C in ambient condition (RH ~ 40%), the 4-ABSA device exhibited much better stability, which maintained 83.5% of its initial efficiency after aging for 27 days. In comparison, the control device only maintained 56.1% of the initial PCE. Light-soaking stability measurements (100 mW/cm²) were also studied (Figure 6b). The control device exhibited 50.5% of its initial PCE after only 50 h of measurement. In contrast, the 4-ABSA device maintained 82.3% of the initial PCE after aging for 70 h. The increased stability for both the perovskite film

and PSC should be mainly attributed to the robust defect passivation and moisture resistance of 4-ABSA additives. It is noted that the stabilities of PSCs under light were far worse than that at a temperature of 60 °C. Figure S12 shows the evolution of UV–vis absorption and XRD patterns for the perovskite films under light and a temperature of 60 °C, respectively. The degradation of the perovskite film under light was obviously faster than that at a temperature of 60 °C, which could ascribe to the more complex challenges under the illuminations of PSCs.  $  ^{65}  $

In addition to the evolution of the device efficiency, we also studied the J-V hysteresis phenomenon during the aging test at 60 °C in ambient condition (RH ~ 40%). As shown in Figure 6c, the average hysteresis index (HI) of the control PSCs was 0.029, which increased to 0.060 after aging for 9 days. In contrast, the average HI of 4-ABSA passivated devices increased only slightly from 0.010 to 0.029. Considering that the hysteresis of J-V curves in PSCs is mainly caused by ion migration, the reduced hysteresis could be attributed to the strong interaction between perovskite and 4-ABSA molecules, which can stabilize the mobile ions. To confirm this assumption, we peeled-off the Ag electrode of the aged device using scotch tape and conducted XPS measurements to detect the mobile iodide on the Ag surface (Figure 6d). Obviously, a decreased I 3d peak intensity could be found in the 4-ABSA-based sample, indicative of reduced ion migration in this kind of device. At the same time, fast device performance deterioration can also result from Ag electrode corrosion. Therefore, the addition of 4-ABSA in PSC could stable iodide ions to reduce electrode corrosion and serve as the moisture barrier in the long-term stability test.
