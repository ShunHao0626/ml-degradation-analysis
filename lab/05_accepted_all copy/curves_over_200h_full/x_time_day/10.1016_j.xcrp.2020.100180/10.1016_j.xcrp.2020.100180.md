# SUMMARY

CsPbI  $  _{3}  $  perovskite has drawn worldwide attention because of its thermal stability and outstanding photovoltaic performance. However, further improvement of CsPbI  $  _{3}  $  device performance is hindered by significant energy losses via charge-carrier recombination at the CsPbI  $  _{3}  $ /transport layer interface and moisture sensitivity. Here, a beach-chair-shaped band structure is designed to introduce energy level gradients at both sides of the perovskite layer to steer optimal energy band alignment and heterojunction interface passivation in  $  \beta  $ -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $  perovskite solar cells (PSCs), resulting in suppressed interfacial recombination, enhanced charge extraction, and more robust stability. As a result, champion solar cell efficiency is increased to as much as 17.12%, one of the highest in reported CsPbI  $  _{3}  $  PSCs, and the  $  V_{oc}  $  of 1.15 V is among the highest reported values. Furthermore, bare PSCs without encapsulation retain 90.4% of their initial cell efficiency after being stored for 1 month under ambient conditions.

# INTRODUCTION

Organic-inorganic halide perovskites have recently accomplished incredible achievements, most notably in the field of solar cells, where efficiencies skyrocketed beyond 25% in just a few years.  $  ^{1-5}  $   Nevertheless, the commonly employed organic moiety imposes a significant problem because it leads to quick degradation of the hybrid perovskite, challenging successful commercial application.  $  ^{6-9}  $   Nowadays, all-inorganic cesium (Cs)-based halide perovskites (CsPbX  $  _{3}  $  ; X = Cl, Br, and I) are attracting attention as a promising alternative that could overcome volatile organic cation-related long-term stability issues.  $  ^{10-15}  $   Considering various CsPbX  $  _{3}  $   perovskites, the cubic   $  \alpha  $  -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   phase with a suitable band gap (  $  \sim  $  1.73 eV) and high absorption coefficient can be regarded as the most promising light-harvesting material, whereas Br-containing counterparts, such as CsPbBr  $  _{3}  $  ,  $  ^{16-18}  $   CsPbI  $  _{2}  $  Br,  $  ^{19-21}  $   CsPbIBr  $  _{2}  $  ,  $  ^{22-24}  $   and other CsPbI  $  _{3-x}  $  Br  $  _{x}  $    $  ^{25}  $   have a band gap that is too large. So far,   $  \alpha  $  -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $  -based solar cells have achieved a power conversion efficiency (PCE) as high as 17.06%.  $  ^{26}  $   Interestingly, Wang et al.  $  ^{27}  $   have also successfully stabilized the   $  \beta  $  -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   perovskite phase. They were able to obtain a certified PCE of 18.3% for   $  \beta  $  -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $  -based perovskite solar cells (PSCs).  $  ^{27}  $   Then it was found that the   $  \beta  $  -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   and   $  \gamma  $  -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   phase interconverted with different amounts of dimethylammonium ions (DMA  $  ^{+}  $  ), achieving a record PCE up to 19.03%, positioning it as a potential candidate for next-generation photovoltaic applications.  $  ^{28}  $   Recently, we also successfully fabricated   $  \gamma  $  -CsPbI  $  _{3}  $   PSCs with a PCE up to 16% by adjusting the composition.  $  ^{13,29-31}  $   However, despite these exciting ongoing efforts, the efficiency achieved

The high PCE of conventional organic-inorganic PSCs comes from the wide absorption range producing a high short-circuit current (  $  J_{sc}  $  ) in combination with an open-circuit voltage of approximately 1.1 V.  $  ^{32,33}  $   Because here the band gap (  $  E_{g}  $  ) is in the range of 1.6 eV, this means that the open-circuit voltage deficit (  $  V_{oc,def}  $  ), defined as   $  E_{g}/q - V_{oc}  $   (where q is the elementary charge), is less than 0.5 eV.   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   has a larger   $  E_{g}  $   value of 1.73 eV and should therefore be able to produce higher   $  V_{oc}  $   values in solar cell devices.  $  ^{26}  $   At the same time, larger   $  E_{g}  $   values narrow the absorption range and, therefore,   $  J_{sc}  $  . Because of this well-known tradeoff, the theoretical efficiency of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   PSCs is slightly lower compared with the organic-inorganic counterpart. However, so far,   $  V_{oc,def}  $   is higher in the fully inorganic perovskite system, which is to a large degree due to charge carrier extraction issues throughout the device. To raise the efficiency of all-inorganic PSCs to the same level as that of their organic-inorganic counterparts, it is important to construct effective device structures with optimized energy level alignment. In fact, construction of composite heterostructure semiconductors can induce an electric field where a valence band (VB)/conduction band (CB) edge is prone to bend at the heterojunction interface, further promoting interfacial carrier separation and photoelectron extraction. Recently, interface engineering and optimization of energy level alignment have also been demonstrated successfully to improve PCE and device stability in a variety of solar cells.  $  ^{34,35}  $   It has been demonstrated that nonradiative recombination is mainly governed by undesirable energy levels at the perovskite film/ETL (HTL) interface.  $  ^{36}  $   Therefore, it is of great urgency to modify the heterojunction interface with the desired energy level alignment for highly efficient and stable   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   PSCs.

Based on this promising concept, in this work, we present a beach-chair-shaped band structure (hereafter denoted CSB) for   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   PSCs. This is achieved by incorporating   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   quantum dots (QDs) and phenyltrimethylammonium bromide (PMABr) films at either side of the perovskite layer to introduce the respective   $  E_{9}  $   variations. We demonstrate that a rational CSB architecture can not only enhance the charge transfer from the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film to the electrodes but also decrease recombination loss during the charge extraction process. Furthermore, the PMABr layer at the interface of   $  TiO_{2}/CsPbI_{3}  $   films can provide effective passivation, leading to decreased interface defect density. As a result, the optimized device, using the CSB structure, achieved a stabilized efficiency as high as 17.12%.

# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

# Fabrication and Characterization of a CSB Structure

To create a graded band structure in these   $  CsPbI_{3}  $  -based solar cell devices, the   $  E_{g}  $   of the active material has to be modified toward the transport layers. For perovskites, this can be achieved in one of two ways. The first approach is to partially substitute of one of the perovskite components. Most commonly, Br is introduced, which leads to an effective increase in   $  E_{g}  $  .  $  ^{19,20,22}  $   As an alternative, the dimensionality of the material can be changed (for example, by preparing nanoparticles), which increases the   $  E_{g}  $   because of confinement effects.  $  ^{37}  $   Both approaches are applied in this work. Considering I/Br mixing, it has to be kept in mind that sequential deposition of first Br- and then I-containing   $  CsPbX_{3}  $   layers is not possible because the second deposition will completely dissolve the first layer. # Advantages of the CSB Architecture

Using this CSB architecture, a solar cell structure is proposed, as shown in Figures 5A and 5B; a cross-sectional SEM image of a representative completed CSB device can be found in Figure S7. Knowing that the Br partially migrates into the  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_3 $  layer, and assuming some intermixing between QDs and the film on the two sides, we can draw an energy level diagram of our CSB device, as shown in Figure 5D. Overall, these energy levels allow the desired design of a beach-chair-shaped energy level landscape with a graded band structure, as sketched in Figure 5D. This structure combines several advantageous properties that are able to enhance the PCE of solar cells.

(1) In such a structure, an   $  E_{g}  $  -graded heterojunction is formed at the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   QD/  $  CsPbI_{3}  $   interface, leading to desirable band bending at the VB/CB edge for improved carrier transport between each layer.  $  ^{25,47}  $

(2) By inducing the exchange reaction between Br and I ions and forming a thin   $  CsPbI_{3-x}Br_{x}  $   film on the bottom surface of bulk   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   films, obtaining a higher   $  V_{oc}  $   and a more favorable energy level match can be realized.

$$

where   $  e, n_{t}, L, \varepsilon_{o}  $  , and   $  \varepsilon  $   stand for the elementary charge, the density of trap states, the film thickness between the two contacts, the vacuum permittivity, and the relative permittivity, respectively.  $  ^{49,50}  $   For electron-only devices (Figures 6E and 6F), the current increases linearly as a function of voltage up to the first kink point, indicating an ohmic response when the applied voltage is below that value (n = 0.96 or 1.05). The corresponding   $  n_{trap}  $   values are calculated to be   $  5.73 \times 10^{15}  $   and   $  2.87 \times 10^{15}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $   for the reference and CSB structure, respectively. Overall, the defect density is decreased by almost a factor of 2 in the CSB structure, which we again attribute to surface passivation by PMABr.

# Effect of the CSB Architecture on PSCs Performance

Last, the effect of the chair-shaped energy band architecture on   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   PSCs performance is investigated. Figure 5A and Figure S7 show a schematic of the device structure and a cross-section SEM image, respectively. Figure 7A presents the best-performing J-V characteristics of the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   PSCs devices with the reference or CSB structure devices; the corresponding photovoltaic parameters are summarized in the inset. The reference cell exhibits a PCE of 15.07% with   $  J_{sc}  $  ,   $  V_{oc}  $  , and fill factor (FF) values of 19.08 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , 1.06 V, and 74.5%, respectively. By introducing the CSB structure, the optimized cell was improved to a maximum PCE of 17.12% (  $  J_{sc} = 19.85  $   mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ,   $  V_{oc} = 1.15  $   V, and FF = 75.0%). Here, the   $  V_{oc}  $   and   $  J_{sc}  $   were enhanced significantly compared with the reference. The integrated   $  J_{sc}  $   from the external quantum efficiency (EQE) results are well matched with each   $  J_{sc}  $   measured from the J-V curves (Figure 7B). It is worth mentioning that the   $  V_{oc}  $   of 1.15 V is among the highest reported values for a   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   solar cell to date, the champion cell efficiency is increased to as much as 17.12%, and the   $  V_{oc}  $   deficit is 0.53 eV, which is lower than that of previous reports for   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   solar cells, indicating that charge collection is improved by constructing this CSB structure in the devices. Evidently, the increased PCE results mainly from the larger   $  V_{oc}  $   and   $  J_{sc}  $   values attained with this improved CSB structure.

To confirm the reliability of the J-V measurements, the stabilized steady-state current and PCE of the best-performing reference and CSB devices were recorded over time while the cells were biased at the maximum power point (  $  V_{MP}  $  ), as plotted in Figure 7C. The measured   $  J_{sc}  $   and PCE remained essentially constant over 600 s and are very close to the values obtained by the J-V scans. So far, we only provided the best-performing device efficiency of PSCs without adequate parallel statistics to draw a final conclusion.  $  ^{54}  $   To ensure reproducibility of our results, 120 individual devices were fabricated; their PCE distribution histograms are presented in Figures 7D and 7E. These results strongly support our previous result, showing that CSB devices have a clear advantage in terms of reliability, reproducibility, and solar cell performance.

Finally, we also tested device stability. For this, the CSB device was stored for 30 days under ambient conditions (RH = 25% ~30%, 25°C) without encapsulation. In summary,  $ \beta $ -CsPbI $ _3 $  PSCs based on a beach-chair-shaped band structure are reported here for the first time; they incorporate CsPbI $ _3 $  QDs and PMABr as the interface-regulating materials during processing of bulk  $ \beta $ -CsPbI $ _3 $  PSCs. The improved photovoltaic performance is a result of three advantages: First, the CSB architecture demonstrated an enhancement in the energy level match of CsPbI $ _3 $  at the CsPbI $ _3 $ /PTAA(TiO $ _2 $ ) interface, resulting in improved carrier transport between each layer, which is the main advantage of this designed structure. Another advantage is effective reduction of the defect density and interfacial recombination loss at the modified interface. The third advantage is that the optimized CSB structure can further improve the stability of the  $ \beta $ -CsPbI $ _3 $  layer as well as the entire device by simultaneous organic PMA cation surface passivation, gradient Br doping, and CsPbI $ _3 $  QD capping. Therefore, this work presents a promising strategy to further improve the performance of Cs-based halide perovskites, using an approach that is also applicable to other optoelectronic devices and perovskite material systems.

# Materials

$ \mathrm{Cs_2CO_3} $  (99.99%), DMF (99%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; 99%+), titanium(IV) chloride  $ (\mathrm{TiCl}_4 $ ; 99.99%), hydriodic acid (HI; 57% [w/w] aq.), and PMABr (97%) were purchased from Alfa Aesar.  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  (99.99%),  $ \mathrm{PbBr}_2 $  (99.99%),  $ \mathrm{PbCl}_2 $  (99.99%), CsI (99.99%), and Poly[bis(4-phenyl) (2,4,6-trimethylphenyl)amine] (PTAA, MN17000) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology. OA (99%), oleylamine (Oam; 70%), 1-octadecene (ODE; 90%), hexane (anhydrous, 95%), octane (anhydrous, ≥99%), and ethyl acetate (anhydrous, 99.8%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All chemicals were used as received.

# Synthesis of DMAPbI  $  _{3}  $

DMAPbI $ _3 $  (DMA = (CH $ _3 $ ) $ _2 $ NH $ _2^+ $ ) was synthesized by dissolving 2.9 g of PbI $ _2 $  and 7 mL of DMF in a 50-mL beaker at 40°C stirred for 30 min. Afterward, 5 mL of HI was added to the mixed solution and stirred magnetically for 2 h. The obtained precipitate was alternately washed with anhydrous ether and ethyl alcohol in sequence by vacuum filtration. Finally, the yellow powder was collected after drying overnight at 50°C in a vacuum oven.

# Preparation of CsPbI $ _3 $  QDs

The synthetic method of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   QDs was based on a previous report.  $  ^{[27]}  $   First, Cs-oleate was synthesized by magnetically stirring   $  Cs_{2}CO_{3}  $   (0.8 g), OA (2.5 mL), and dried ODE (30 mL) in a 100-mL three-neck flask at 120°C in a vacuum until the   $  Cs_{2}CO_{3}  $   powder was dissolved and kept with   $  N_{2}  $   flow before use. 0.84 g of   $  PbI_{2}  $  , 50 mL of ODE, 5 mL of OA, and 5 mL of OAm were mixed in a 150-mL three-neck flask and heated to 120°C for 30 min until the   $  PbI_{2}  $   was completely dissolved, followed by raising the temperature of the flask to 170°C. Then we swiftly injected 5 mL of Cs-oleate into the solution and maintained it for 7 s; then the flask was immediately immersed into an ice bath. The resulting solution and ethyl acetate were mixed with a volume ratio of 1:3 and centrifuged at 8,000 rpm to collect the precipitate. Subsequently, the precipitate was washed with hexane, and then the QDs were dispersed into 5 mL of octane; the   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   QD solution was kept at 4°C as prepared.

# SCN Capping

A small amount of dried KSCN powder was added to the  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_3 $  QD solution while actively stirring for  $ 30\mathrm{min} $ . After removing excess salt by centrifugation and vacuum filtration, the clear solution was precipitated with  $ 200~\mathrm{mL} $  of  $ \mathrm{Pb(OAc)_2EA} $ , and then centrifuged to form capped  $ \mathrm{CsPbI}_3 $  QDs.

# CsDMAPbI $ _3 $  Precursor Solution

0.7 g of DMAPbI  $  _{3}  $  mixed with 0.36 g of CsI and 0.01 g of PbCl  $  _{2}  $  was dissolved in 2 mL of DMF and 0.2 mL of DMSO. The solution was stirred for 2 h until entirely dissolved.

# HTL Precursor Solution

90 mg of PTAA, 22   $  \mu  $  L of sulfonyl imide (0Li-TFSI, 1 mL acetonitrile contains 520 mg Li-TFSI), and 36   $  \mu  $  L tert-butylpyridine (TBP) were dissolved in 1 mL chlorobenzene (CB) solution. The solution was stirred over 24 h before use.

# Device Fabrication

Glass substrate/fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass was cleared sequentially by acetone, isopropanol (IPA), and ethyl alcohol, and then dried by   $  N_{2}  $   flow. FTO glass was dipped into a 40-mM   $  TiCl_{4}  $   aqueous solution at 70°C for 60 min to form an ~50-nm-thick   $  TiO_{2}  $   blocking layer. Then 6 mg/mL PMABr solution (dissolved in IPA) was spin-coated onto a   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer at 4,000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 100°C for 2 min to remove excess IPA. The   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film was prepared by spin-coating the as-prepared   $  CsDMAPbI_{3}  $   precursor solution on a PMABr layer at 4,000 rpm for 35 s, followed by annealing at 210°C for 5 min. The   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   QD layer was fabricated onto   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   film at 1,000 rpm for 5 s and 2,000 rpm for 10 s and then swiftly immersed into ethyl acetate (EA) solution. The procedure was repeated three times to form an ~60-nm-thick QD film. Thereafter, the HTL solution was spin-coated onto the QD film at 5,000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, an ~70-nm-thick gold layer was thermally evaporated onto top of the device as the electrode.

The EQE was measured using the EnliTech (Taiwan) EQE system. The light soaking stability was tested at a power point of 0.86 V for 600 s, and long-term stability data were acquired with a humidity of 25%–35% in air.
