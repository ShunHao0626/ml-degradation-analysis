# Improving the Power Conversion Efficiency and Stability of Planar Perovskite Solar Cells via Small Molecule Doping

RANBIR SINGH, $ ^{1,2,5} $  MANISH KUMAR, $ ^{3} $  and VIVEK KUMAR SHUKLA $ ^{4,6} $

1.-Department of Energy and Materials Engineering, Dongguk University, Seoul, Korea. 2.-Department of Chemical Engineering, Pohang University of Science and Technology, Pohang, Korea. 3.-Pohang Accelerator Laboratory, Pohang University of Science and Technology, Pohang, Korea. 4.-Gautam Buddha University, Greater Noida, Gautam Budh Nagar, UP, India. 5.-e-mail: ranbir@dongguk.edu. 6.-e-mail: vkshuk@gmail.com

Pinhole-free small molecule (SM)-doped perovskite films have been synthesized to improve the power conversion efficiency (PCE) and stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Thin films of pristine perovskite and doped with SMs such as [6,6]-phenyl   $  C_{60}  $   butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) and   $  N,N'  $  -bis(1-ethylpropyl)-perylene-3,4,9,10-tetracarboxylic diimide (EP-PDI) were prepared on glass/indium-doped tin oxide (ITO)/poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):polystyrene sulfonate (PEDOT:PSS) substrate for perovskite solar cells with planar geometry. Both perovskite and doped perovskite:SM films were investigated for their optical, morphological, and photovoltaic properties. Doping with trace amount (0.1 wt.%) of PCBM improved the morphology of the perovskite films, with increased grain size and crystallinity, resulting in an increase of the PCE of the PSC by 35%. The improved morphology of the perovskite:SM system also played an important role in improving the device stability. Furthermore, temperature-dependent electron mobility measurements revealed a significant decrease in charge carrier trapping or defect states in the perovskite:SM system. Evidently, the lower activation energy (  $  E_{a}  $  ) in the perovskite:SM system explains the higher electron mobility in comparison with the reference perovskite system.

Key words: Perovskite,  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $ , grain boundary, planar heterojunction, small molecule, perylene-diimide

# INTRODUCTION

Perovskite materials have been considered as promising candidates for fabrication of solar cells due to the high power conversion efficiency (PCE)  $  ^{1,2}  $  resulting from their many attractive properties, including high absorption coefficient,  $  ^{1,3}  $  ambipolar nature,  $  ^{4,5}  $  high carrier mobilities,  $  ^{6}  $  and long charge-carrier diffusion lengths (  $  >1\mu m  $  ).  $  ^{6,7}  $  However, it is difficult to control formation of perovskite thin films with good coverage and free of pinholes, which are important characteristics to achieve high-performance perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Therefore, it

becomes essential to explore ways to improve solid-state thin-film morphology. In earlier studies, different methodologies were used to improve perovskite film morphology, including mixed solvent engineering,  $  ^{8-10}  $  use of a one-step spin-coating process followed by antisolvent coating,  $  ^{11}  $  various substrate pre- and post-heating,  $  ^{12,13}  $  solvent vapor annealing at room temperature,  $  ^{14,15}  $  use of bulk heterojunction perovskite film,  $  ^{16,17}  $  and vapor deposition of perovskite film.  $  ^{18,19}  $  Many materials combinations have been used to fabricate planar and bulk heterojunction-based PSCs. Planar heterojunction perovskite/small molecule (SM)-based solar cell devices have been successfully demonstrated,  $  ^{20}  $  but the role of SM doping in the perovskite photoactive layer must be investigated in detail.

SMs such as fullerene derivatives  $  ^{21,22}  $  and perylene-diimide derivatives  $  ^{23,24}  $  are widely used as electron acceptors in bulk heterojunction (BHJ) organic solar cells because of their high electron mobility and electron-accepting properties. In this work, perovskite material was doped with two different SMs, viz. [6,6]-phenyl C  $  _{60}  $  butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) and N,N'-bis(1-ethylpropyl)-perylene-3,4,9,10-tetracarboxylic diimide (EP-PDI). Addition of trace amount of PCBM and EP-PDI SMs to the perovskite material was applied based on the concept that it would improve the film morphology by filling pinholes and gaps between perovskite grains. Perovskite films doped with 0.1 wt.% of PCBM were found to exhibit the best optical, morphological, transport, and photovoltaic properties. The photovoltaic properties of these two SM-doped perovskite films were investigated in detail and compared, including device stability.

# Thin-Film Characterization

UV–Vis absorption and photoluminescence (PL) spectra of the perovskite films were recorded using a PerkinElmer Lambda 1050 spectrometer and Horiba Jobin–Yvon NanoLog spectrofluorimeter, respectively. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained using a Hitachi S-4800 field-emission SEM, and one-dimensional (1D) x-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were done using an x-ray diffractometer (Rigaku D/MAX-2500/PC) with Cu K $  _{x}  $  x-rays.

# Electron Mobility

Electron mobility was studied by fabricating electron-only devices (FTO/TiO  $  _{2}  $  /perovskite film/Ca/Al). The mobility values for each sample were calculated from the space-charge-limited current (SCLC) region of dark J-V characteristics.  $  ^{25}  $   Temperature-dependent electron mobility measurements were carried out using a JANIS VPF-100 cryostat connected to a temperature controller (Lake Shore, 335).

# Morphological Characterization

To evaluate the morphological changes in the SM-doped perovskite films, the morphology of the fabricated films was investigated by SEM (Fig. 3). The SEM image of the perovskite film (sample A) showed grain size ranging from 0.1   $  \mu  $  m to 0.4   $  \mu  $  m, including pinholes and gaps between perovskite grains, whereas a slight increase in grain size was noted for sample B, which might arise due to the difference in the surface energy of the bottom layer (ITO and PEDOT:PSS). Interestingly, a drastic change in the grain size of the perovskite:PCBM film was observed, being significantly increased. The other doped film, i.e., perovskite:EP-PDI, also showed a small increase in grain size over sample B, but the effect was less pronounced compared with the perovskite:PCBM film. The reason behind the different morphologies of sample C and D may be the difference in the film-forming ability of PCBM and EP-PDI. # Charge Carrier Transport Properties

To evaluate the charge carrier transport properties, the electron mobility of the perovskite and perovskite:SM films was measured after fabricating electron-only devices (see “Experimental Procedures” section). The electron mobility was extracted from the dark J-V curve by fitting the Mott-Gurney equation  $  ^{8}  $   (Supplementary Fig. S2); the resulting carrier mobility values are listed in Supplementary Table S3. The perovskite:PCBM film showed better electron mobility of   $  1.2 \times 10^{-3}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $   V  $  ^{-1}  $   s  $  ^{-1}  $   in comparison with the perovskite:EP-PDI film (  $  8.9 \times 10^{-4}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $   V  $  ^{-1}  $   s  $  ^{-1}  $  ) or pristine perovskite film (  $  7.3 \times 10^{-4}  $   cm  $  ^{2}  $   V  $  ^{-1}  $   s  $  ^{-1}  $  ), which is well supported by the improved structural order in the perovskite:SM films (see “Morphological Characterization” section).  $  ^{31,33}  $   The better electron mobility of sample C compared over sample D can also be explained by the better charge transport properties

of PCBM as compared with EP-PDI.  $  ^{22,34}  $   The better electron mobility in the doped perovskite might be a reason for the high FF and PCE of the PSC.

Further, temperature-dependent electron mobility measurements were performed to examine the effect of energetic disorder on the charge transport in the perovskite and perovskite:SM films. The temperature-dependent electron mobility was determined through dark J-V characteristics at different temperatures, in identical fashion as applied to calculate the electron mobility at room temperature. In Fig. 5c, the electron mobility values of the devices are plotted as a function of inverse temperature (1/T), in the temperature range of 140 K to 300 K, and fit with the Arrhenius equation   $  \mu(T) = \mu_{0} \exp(-E_{\mathrm{a}} / kT)  $  , where   $  E_{a}  $   corresponds to the activation energy, which is closely related to the width of the energetic disorder.  $  ^{24,35,36}  $   The extracted   $  E_{a}  $   values were 230 meV for perovskite, 85 meV for perovskite:PCBM, and 142 meV for perovskite:EP-PDI devices. The lower value of   $  E_{a}  $   for the perovskite:PCBM device suggests lesser energetic disorder for charge carrier transport, which also supports the higher electron mobility of this device. A significant decrease in the energetic disorder for the perovskite:EP-PDI-based devices was also noticed, but not as pronounced as for the perovskite:PCBM based devices. This decrease in energetic disorder also indicates a decrease in defect or charge-trapping states in the doped perovskites. This improvement may be due to better morphology, e.g., filled pinholes, reduced grain boundaries, and improved crystallinity, of the perovskite:PCBM film.

# Device Stability

To check device stability, the PCE of the solar cell devices was monitored for 21 days in a nitrogen glovebox (relative humidity   $  \sim3.2\%  $  ) (Fig. 5d). The solar cell devices fabricated using the perovskite:SM films were found to be more stable in comparison with those based on pristine perovskite (samples A and B). The PCEs of samples A and B were found to have dropped by 67% and 58% from their initial value, whereas that of sample C dropped by only 18% under similar conditions. The PCE of sample D was found to show a similar stability trend to that of sample C. It is expected that such compact perovskite:SM films will show enhanced resistance to degradation, resulting in improved device stability.  $  ^{37,38}  $   It is possible that the decrease in grain boundaries in the perovskite:SM film significantly reduces the defects at grain boundaries, which are the main source of moisture trapping and device degradation.  $  ^{39}  $   The enhanced stability of the perovskite:PCBM arises due to the larger grain size and high purity of the crystalline phase (Fig. 3).  $  ^{38,40}  $

# CONCLUSIONS

A two-step spin-coating method was successfully applied to fabricate high-quality small-molecule-doped perovskite films for use in planar PSCs. Doping with PCBM improved the perovskite film morphology by filling pinholes and gaps between grains, resulting in increased perovskite grain size. The reduced grain boundaries and better crystallinity of the doped perovskite films led to increased charge carrier mobility. The small-molecule-doped perovskite films showed stronger absorption and increased PL quenching compared with pristine perovskite film. In conclusion, the reduced defects and improved morphology, photovoltaic properties, and device stability indicate that such small-molecule-doped perovskite films have good potential for use in photovoltaic applications.
