# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been studied extensively over the past decade as a promising alternative to conventional silicon solar cells. The efficiency of PSCs has improved enormously since the pioneering studies of Miyasaka and Park et al.   $  [1-6]  $  , with a certified PCE of 25.5% recently reported   $  [7]  $  . Among perovskite materials used in PSC fabrication, formamidinium lead triiodide   $  \left(\mathrm{FAPbI}_{3}\right)  $  -based perovskite materials generally produce the most efficient solar cells due to their impressive optoelectronic properties, robust thermal stability, high extinction coefficients, and near-optimal bandgap (  $  \sim1.4~eV  $  ) for single-junction solar cells based on the Shockley-Queisser efficiency limit   $  [8,9]  $  . However, the black cubic perovskite   $  \alpha-FAPbI_{3}  $   phase tends to convert to the thermodynamically more stable, non-perovskite yellow hexagonal   $  \delta-FAPbI_{3}  $   phase during solar cell operation   $  [10,11]  $  , which is highly undesirable for photovoltaic applications.

Improving the performance and stability of   $  \alpha  $  -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   PSCs is therefore a major current research focus [12–14]. Composition engineering

The black perovskite (  $  \alpha  $  -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ) tends to spontaneously transform into the yellow non-perovskite (  $  \delta  $  -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ) under ambient conditions, which degrades the performance and stability of FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  -based perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Herein, we applied an in-situ polymerization controlled growth (IPCG) strategy for crystal grain to suppress the   $  \alpha  $  -to-  $  \delta  $   phase conversion. Polymerization of 2-(Dimethylamino) ethyl methacrylate (DMAEMA) contained in the antisolvent created polymers (denoted as Poly(D)) which acted to simultaneously increase the perovskite grain size, passivate defects, stabilize the black phase, and block moisture penetration, thus greatly improving photovoltaic performance and stability. The resulting FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  -based PSC (FTO/SnO  $  _{2}  $  /Perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD/Au) delivered a high power conversion efficiency (PCE) of   $  \sim  $   21% (cf.   $  \sim  $   16.0% PCE for the pristine FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   device). Further, the polymerization strategy delivers PSCs with remarkable stability, with 100% of the initial PCE performance being retained over 104 days under ambient conditions (room temperature, RH = 50   $  \pm  $   5%) without encapsulation of the device.

By partially substituting FA  $  ^{+}  $  cations with smaller cations such as methylammonium (MA  $  ^{+}  $  ), rubidium (Rb  $  ^{+}  $  ), or cesium (Cs  $  ^{+}  $  ), or by incorporating smaller anions such as Br  $  ^{-}  $  or Cl  $  ^{-}  $  into the lattice, significant improvements in PSC stability can be achieved [15,16]. Nevertheless, such mixed halide perovskites have some intrinsic problems, such as narrower spectral absorption, low thermal stability caused by the addition of MA  $  ^{+}  $  possessing high volatility, predisposition for phase separation caused by the presence of mixed anions [17], thus resulting in generally low current densities and modest operation stability. Recently, researchers have synthesized 2D perovskites by replacing the A-site cation with large organic cations possessing long alkylammonium chains, thereby achieving enhanced stabilization of the black   $  \alpha  $  -phase. Such 2D perovskites show higher humidity tolerance than 3D perovskites, though the PCEs of 2D PSCs lag far behind their 3D counterparts [18–20]. In addition, the use of additives and defect passivation via surface functionalization has also been shown to offer some benefits regarding efficiency and stability [21–23].

Previous literature studies have shown that Lewis bases with a lone-pair electron on oxygen, nitrogen, or sulfur can coordinate with the charged defects of perovskites   $  [24,25]  $  . However, these small molecules possess high volatility and distribution irregularity, thereby offering generally unstable shielding   $  [26]  $  . To counter the volatility problem, polymers offering functional groups with lone pairs on oxygen, nitrogen, or sulfur groups are gaining popularity to combat defects   $  [27,28]  $  . However, precipitation frequently occurs in the precursor solution due to strong interactions between the polymer and   $  PbI_{2}  $  , thus adversely impacting the crystallization of the perovskite   $  [28–30]  $  . Polymers can also be added into the precursor films during the antisolvent dripping stage to direct the growth of crystals, but such films can suffer from uneven coverage   $  [31]  $  . In summary, to increase the PCE and stability of   $  FAPbI_{3}  $  -based PSCs without light-harvesting losses, a simple strategy needs to be discovered for stabilizing the   $  \alpha  $  -phase whilst maintaining the inherent bandgap of   $  FAPbI_{3}  $  .

In this work, we demonstrate an efficient in-situ polymerization controlled growth (IPCG) strategy for crystal grain to improve the performance and stability of intrinsic FAPbI $ _3 $  PSCs. Briefly, DMAEMA monomer was dissolved in toluene (the antisolvent), then subsequently deposited onto the perovskite precursor film during the antisolvent dripping step used in perovskite film fabrication. DMAEMA interacts with undercoordinated Pb $ ^{2+} $  ions through lone pairs on its carbonyl group ( $ -\text{CO-} $ ) and dimethylamino group ( $ -\text{N(CH}_3\text{)}_2 $ ). Followed by thermal annealing, polymers Poly(D) form in-situ with the aid of initiators. This in-situ polymerization controlled grain growth (IPCG) strategy significantly reduces the charge trap density and passivates grain boundaries (GBs) through Lewis acid-base interactions, whilst also increasing the humidity stability of the perovskite layer due to the intrinsic hydrophobic properties of Poly(D). These properties act synergistically to enhance the performance and stability of the resulting PSC devices. A FAPbI $ _3 $ -based PSC (FTO/SnO $ _2 $ /Perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD/

# 2. Results and discussion

# 2.1. IPCG strategy

There are three main advantages associated with the in-situ polymerization of DMAEMA when preparing perovskite films. Firstly, the DMAEMA monomer is a sticky liquid with moderate volatility at room temperature (Fig. 1a). Hence, the monomer is unlikely to volatilize before being converting into the polymer. Secondly, the carbonyl group and tertiary amine groups in DMAEMA act as Lewis bases towards  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  (a Lewis acid), thus passivating crystal defects and meliorating perovskite grain growth, thus leading to enhanced optoelectronic properties and device efficiencies (e.g. a PCE of  $ \sim 21\% $  realized herein). Thirdly, in the presence of an initiator, the alkenyl group in DMAEMA allows polymerization to occur at GBs and film surface during the annealing process, and thus forming a compact and adherent shielding layer to protect the perovskite thin film (Fig. 1b bottom).

The polymerization of DMAEMA monomer is described in Fig. 1a. The DMAEMA monomer is initially a transparent liquid (Fig. 1a left). In the presence of the azobisisobutyronitrile (AIBN) initiator, DMAEDA polymerizes to a semitransparent colloid state (Fig. 1a middle). After annealing for 5 min, complete solidification occurs (Fig. 1a right). Herein, DMAEMA monomer and AIBN were introduced into the FAPbI $ _3 $  film during the antisolvent dripping process. During the subsequent thermal treatment of the thin film, solid Poly(D) was formed (Fig. 1b). To probe possible chemical interactions between Poly(D) and the perovskite thin film, we applied Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Fig. 1c–f). FTIR spectra were collected for FAPbI $ _3 $ -DMAEMA films before and after thermal treatment to track the in-situ polymerization of DMAEMA (Fig. 1c). The DMAEDA monomer shows characteristic =CH $ _2 $ , C=O, and C=C stretching modes at ~ 938, 1720, and 1640 cm $ ^{-1} $ , respectively [29,32,33]. Before thermal treatment, the FAPbI $ _3 $ -DMAEMA film shows a C=O stretch at 1707 cm $ ^{-1} $ , slightly shifted from the free monomer value, presumably due to the interaction of the C=O group with Pb $ ^{2+} $  and the =CH $ _2 $  or C=C stretching vibrations were still existed in the spectrum. During thermal treatment, the FAPbI $ _3 $ -DMAEMA film was transformed into a FAPbI $ _3 $ -Poly(D) film. The C=O stretching mode was largely unaffected by Poly(D) formation (1712 cm $ ^{-1} $ ). The variation of the C=O stretching was shown further in the zoom-in FTIR spectra (Fig. S1). No =CH $ _2 $  or C=C stretching vibrations were present in the spectrum of the thermally treated film, suggesting the complete polymerization of DMAEDA. The remained small peak around 1640 cm $ ^{-1} $  should be ascribed to the N–H vibration in FAPbI $ _3 $  perovskite material [34]. According to previous reports, carbonyl (C=O) groups act as a Lewis base when interacting with Pb-I frameworks [35], consistent with the small frequency shift seen here by FTIR for C=O in free DMAEMA and FAPbI $ _3 $ -Poly(D). XPS spectra for FAPbI $ _3 $  films with and without the IPCG strategy are shown in Fig. 1d–f and Fig. S2. The binding energy scale for all XPS spectra was calibrated against the C 1 s signal of adventitious hydrocarbons (284.5 eV). Pb 4f, O 1 s, and N 1 s spectra for the different films are shown in Fig. 1d–f, respectively. The pristine FAPbI $ _3 $  film shows Pb 4f $ _{7/2} $ , N 1 s, and O 1 s peaks at 137.81 eV (typical of Pb $ ^{2+} $  in FAPbI $ _3 $ ), 400.11 eV (due to N atoms in formamidinium ion), and 531.75 eV (due to absorbed oxygen gas and moisture molecules in the air), respectively. For the FAPbI $ _3 $ -Poly(D) perovskite film, the Pb 4f $ _{7/2} $ , N 1 s, and O 1 s peaks were located at slightly higher binding energy (137.94, 400.36, and 532.03 eV, respectively). For FAPbI $ _3 $ -Poly(D), the -N(CH $ _3 $ ) $ _2 $  and C=O groups of Poly(D) will also contribute to the N 1 s and

O 1 s spectra, respectively, which may account for the small shifts observed. The lone electron pairs on the N atom of the  $ -\mathrm{N}(\mathrm{CH}_3)_2 $  functional group and on the O atom of C=O groups may interact with the 6p orbits of  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  [32,36], modulating the lead cation electron density slightly and causing the Pb 4f signals for  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $ -Poly(D) to shift to higher binding energy relative to values  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  [37]. In addition, the Pb 4f spectra for both  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  and  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $ -Poly(D) films show a second set of weaker peaks at  $ 136.21\mathrm{eV} $  and  $ 141.08\mathrm{eV} $  (4:3 area ratio,  $ \mathrm{Pb}4\mathrm{f}_{7/2} $ , and  $ \mathrm{Pb}4\mathrm{f}_{5/2} $ , respectively) associated with a more oxidized Pb species. These more oxidized Pb cations likely formed at the interface as a  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  decomposition product. The IPCG strategy appeared to partially suppress the formation of such highly oxidized Pb species. Results of the FTIR and XPS studies suggest a strong interaction between the perovskite and Poly(D) through the functional groups of the polymer. To further investigate the spatial distribution of Poly(D) in the perovskite layer, we carried out an element scanning in liner mode through the cross-section of ITO/ perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD (Fig. S3). Compared to the reference, the intensity distribution of the element O (O is the characteristic element of Poly(D)) indicates an accumulation of O at the perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD interface. The blue arrow is used to mark the raised part. In addition, O is also distributed inside the perovskite and the concentration away from the ITO/perovskite interface is gradually reduced. We speculate that Poly(D) would mainly stay at the grain boundaries due to the fact that the Poly(D) could not incorporate into the lattice of perovskite. Considering that the O containing in the ITO, the dispersion of O at the grain boundaries has been further proven by the Transmission electron microscope(TEM) image. The TEM images in Fig. S4a-c confirm the morphology of the perovskite structure. To further investigate the elemental distribution of O in the  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $ -Poly(D) samples, elemental mapping analyses were characterized by high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX). The results are depicted in Fig. S4d-i, revealing that the O element is mainly distributed in the grain boundary. Fig. 1g schematically illustrates the assembly of Poly(D) on the surface and grain boundaries of  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $ .

# 2.2. Perovskite film characterization

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) were employed to study the effect of the polymerization process on the structure, crystal growth, and morphology of the perovskite films. Fig. 2a shows SEM images for perovskite films prepared without polymer and with different concentrations of Poly(D) (the concentration is the content of the DMAEMA in the antisolvent). The average size of the  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  crystals is  $ 350~\mathrm{nm} $ ,  $ 455~\mathrm{nm} $ ,  $ 588~\mathrm{nm} $ , and  $ 761~\mathrm{nm} $  with the corresponding concentrations, respectively. Fig. S5 displays grain size statistics of the  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  films containing different concentrations of Poly(D) using Nano measurer 1.2 software. The SEM images reveal that the introduction of Poly(D) results in perovskite crystals with a larger grain size than the control sample. The small bright particles are  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  on the surface of perovskite films. The proper amount of lead iodide is helpful to device performance [38]. Optical images of annealed thin films prepared with and without the IPCG strategy are shown in Fig. 2b. A step-by-step annealing process was used, involving holding films at  $ 80^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 60~s $  and then  $ 150^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 150~s $ . The control film fabricated without IPCG strategy almost immediately turned black upon raising the annealing temperature to  $ 150^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  (due to  $ \alpha $ - $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  formation), whereas a much slower conversion process was observed for films prepared using the IPCG strategy. The results suggest that the presence of DMAEDA or Poly (D) slows that crystallization speed and prolongs the crystallization time. This strategy completely suppressed the formation of the non-perovskite   $  \delta  $  -FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   phase even under   $  50 \pm 5\%  $   humidity conditions. # 4. Experimental section

Materials:   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloidal precursor (tin (IV) oxide, 15 wt% in   $  H_{2}O  $   colloidal dispersion), anhydrous N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, 99.5%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and dimethylformamide (DMF) were purchased from Aldrich. Lead (II) iodide (99.99%), methylammonium chloride (MACl), and 2,2',7,7'-Tetrakis[N,N-di(4-methoxyphenyl)amino]-9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMcTAD, 99.99%) were obtained from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. (PLT). Formamidinium iodide (FAI, 99.5%) was purchased from Great Cell Corp. 2-(Dimethylamino)ethyl methacrylate (DMAEMA, 99%) was purchased from Aladdin Corp. All of the aforementioned chemicals were used as received without any additional purification.

Solar cell fabrication: Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass slides (Advanced Election Technology CO., Ltd, 15 Ω) were washed consecutively in deionized water, acetone, isopropanol, and ethanol under ultrasonication (30 min in each solvent), then dried and treated with an oxygen plasma for 10 min. Next, a SnO₂ electron transport layer was deposited on the FTO layer by spin-coating a commercial SnO₂ colloidal dispersion diluted in deionized water (the volume ratio of SnO₂ colloidal dispersion to water is 1:6) at a speed of 3000 rpm using a Spin Coater. The coated FTO slides were then annealed at 150 °C for 30 min. Directly before the spin-coating the perovskite precursor solution, the SnO₂-coated FTO glass was subjected to UV-ozone treatment. The perovskite active layer was prepared by the modified adduct method [43]. The solution used to prepare the bare FAPbI₃ layer contained FAI, PbI₂, and N-Methyl-2-pyrrolidone in N, N-Dimethylformamide. Briefly, 172 mg of FAI, 461 mg of PbI₂, and 99 mg of NMP were dissolved in 600 mg of DMF. The perovskite precursor solution was then spin-coated onto the SnO₂-coated FTO glass at 4000 rpm for 50 s, with 300 μL of toluene being dropped on the rotating substrate ~ 10 s after the precursor's solution. The resulting film was then heated at 80 °C for 1 min, then transferred onto the hot plate at 150 °C for 10 min. For the in-situ polymerization controlled grain growth (IPCG) strategy, the perovskite precursor solution was spin-coated onto SnO₂-coated FTO glass at 4000 rpm for 50 s, with 300 μL of a 0.1% DMAEMA (Aladdin) solution containing 0.01% azobisisobutyronitrile (AIBN) in toluene being dropped onto the spinning substrate 10 s after dripping the precursor solution. The resulting film was then heated at 80 °C for 1 min, then transferred onto the hot plate at 150 °C for 10 min.

For the MACl incorporated PSC, perovskite film deposition was achieved using a one-step deposition method similar to that described by Seok et al., with slight modifications [41]. Perovskite active layers were spin-coated on  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 / \mathrm{FTO} $  using a solution containing  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  with 30 mol% methylammonium hydrochloride (MACl,  $ 98\% $  Aldrich) in a mixed solvent of DMF and DMSO (8:1 v/v). The perovskite precursor solution was spin-coated at  $ 6000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 50~s $ , with  $ 300~\mu L $  of toluene being dropped on the rotating substrate  $ \sim 10~s $  after the perovskite precursor

solution. For the MACl incorporated PSC with IPCG strategy, the process was the same as described for the bare   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   substrate, followed by the same annealing treatment.

After preparing the perovskite active layers, a hole transporting layer (HTL)/chlorobenzene (72.3 mg/1 mL) in a mixture of 28.8   $  \mu  $  L of 4-tert-Butyl pyridine (TBP) and 17.5   $  \mu  $  L of Li-bis(trifluoromethane sulfonyl) imide (Li-TFSI)/acetonitrile (520 mg/mL) was spin-coated onto the perovskite active layers. The samples were then placed in a desiccator overnight to oxidize the spiro-OMeTAD. Finally, a gold counter electrode was deposited by thermal evaporation (thickness   $  \sim  $   65–75 nm).

Solar cell Test: In a glove box filled with Argon gas, the J–V curves of the PSCs were collected in both the reverse and forward scan directions using a Keithley 2400 source. The device areas for the FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  -MACl and bare FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   systems were fixed at 0.08 and 0.053 cm  $  ^{2}  $  , respectively, using a black metal aperture to avoid light scattering. All devices were scanned at a scan rate of 0.1 V s  $  ^{-1}  $  , using a scan step size of 0.02 V. IPCE measurements were performed on a Zahner system, using a TLS03 light source (300 Hz, 100 counts) at mixed mode.

Materials Characterization: Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) measurements (excitation at 445 nm) and time-resolved photoluminescence measurements (TRPL) were performed on an FLS 980 fluorometer using a single-photon counting method. Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) data were collected on a Bruker D8 AVANCE X-ray diffractometer equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (40 kV, 40 mA) over the 2θ range 10–50°. UV–visible absorption spectrum was collected on a TU-1901 spectrophotometer (Beijing Purkinje General Instrument Co., Ltd., China). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) data were collected on a Thermo VG-Scientific, Sigma Probe spectrometer (ESCALAB250, Thermo, USA), equipped with a Cu Kα source.
