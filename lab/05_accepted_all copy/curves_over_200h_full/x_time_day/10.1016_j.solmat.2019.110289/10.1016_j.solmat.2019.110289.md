# 1. Introduction

The unprecedented rise in the perovskite device efficiency within a relatively brief period makes it a viable alternative to the existing photovoltaic technologies   $  [1,2]  $  . The low-cost solution processability leads to roll-to-roll manufacturability and, most importantly, the material abundance justifies its sustainability giving them solid contention in the technological domain   $  [3–5]  $  . However, to move from the laboratory scale devices to the commercial arena, certain severe bottlenecks need to be overcome, and feasibility needs to be validated   $  [6]  $  . Looking from a point of view where this technology stands today, the long-term stability, especially under non-ideal conditions, is one of the major hurdles to be addressed in an immediate basis   $  [7]  $  .

To enhance the extrinsic stability against the well-articulated moisture-induced degradation   $  [8,9]  $  , several pathways are demonstrated that lead to short term improvement and occasionally long-term device stability   $  [10]  $  . Considering the nucleation and growth characteristics of ALD- $ \mathrm{Al_2O_3} $ , such deposition should have only resulted in a few monolayers of  $ \mathrm{Al_2O_3} $ , ignoring the established fact that there is a chemical reaction between the tri-methyl aluminium (TMA) and the hybrid perovskite surface [22]. During last couple of years, several groups have employed ALD grown   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   on top of the processed device as encapsulate. Seo et al., demonstrated inverted architecture PSC, where 50 nm   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   was deposited on top of the PSC as an encapsulation. The devices showed excellent thermal stability of 500 h at 85 °C under continuous one sun illumination [19]. Similarly, 16 nm of   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   was deposited on top of the entire PSC by Ramos et al., to confirm full encapsulation of the cells. Both encapsulated and unencapsulated devices were measured for a period of 94 days in ambient conditions with RH around 80% with the encapsulated devices retaining 75% of original PCE [24]. In another work, Lee et al., employed alternating layers of inorganic   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   via ALD and organic poly (1,3,5-trimethyl-1,3,5-trivinyl cyclotrisiloxane) deposited by CVD [25]. The dual thin film encapsulation (TFE) showed highly reduced water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) of   $  10^{-4}  $   g m  $  ^{-2}  $   d  $  ^{-1}  $   at 38 °C and 90% RH. The devices exhibited 97% of initial PCE after retaining them for 300 h at 50 °C and 50% RH. The effort is similar to the one that was previously used for the OLEDs and the initial investigations certainly reveal good potential. However, to achieve effective gas diffusion barrier, it is important to understand the growth characteristic of the material, in particular on organic substrates where there is little thermodynamic driving force for nucleation. It also quantifies the materials to be applied and relates to its deposition condition.

In this paper, we unveil the nucleation and growth mechanism of ALD   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   on the spiro-OMeTAD substrate by in-situ QCM and FTIR. Characteristically different nucleation of ALD-  $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   that predominately occurs from the depth of the substrate followed by a subsequent linear growth is explored further and is utilized as a diffusion barrier prevailing to enhance stability. Under different stressed condition including continuous illumination and day-night cycling, the net degradation in the coated devices is substantially lower than the uncoated ones. Furthermore, the hole transport layer spiro-OMETAD is highly sensitive to moisture, and the area of the metal electrode is small relative to the substrate. In lab scale devices, this exposes most of the spiro-OMeTAD area to moisture and the ALD-  $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   can play a significant role to increase the durability of these devices. We divulge the fact that the ALD grown   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   encapsulate substantially protects the devices not only from the humidity but more importantly from the oxygen, however it is limited by the net gas/vapor transmission rate.

# 2. Materials and methods

# 2.1. Substrate preparation

Fluorine doped tin oxide (FTO - TEC-7) substrates from Dyesol were cut to  $ 3 \times 3 \mathrm{~cm}^2 $  and etched using Zn powder (Merck) and  $ 2 \mathrm{M} \mathrm{HCl} $  (Rankem) acid. The patterned substrates were then cleaned sequentially in an ultra-sonicator bath using alconox, de-ionized water, acetone and iso-propanol solvents for  $ 10 \mathrm{~min} $  each. The cleaned FTO substrates were then subjected to UV-ozone treatment for  $ 30 \mathrm{~min} $  prior to deposition of the consecutive layers.

# 2.2.  $ SnO_{2} $  ETL deposition

The   $  SnO_{2}  $   sol-gel precursor for ETL deposition was prepared by dissolving tin (IV) chloride pentahydrate (  $  SnCl_{4}\cdot5H_{2}O  $   - 98%, Sigma Aldrich) in anhydrous iso-propanol (99.5%, Sigma Aldrich) to achieve 0.05 M concentration, followed by stirring. The   $  SnO_{2}  $   precursor solution was spin coated on UV-ozone treated FTO substrates at 2000 rpm for 45 s. The substrates were then annealed at 180 °C for 45 min. An additional layer of   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL was deposited using   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloidal dispersion diluted (15% in   $  H_{2}O  $   colloidal dispersion, Alfa Aesar) using de-ionized water with volume ratio 1:4 ratio. The precursor solution was spin coated on top of previous   $  SnO_{2}  $   coated FTO substrates at 4000 rpm for 45 s followed by annealing at 150 °C for 30 min.

# 2.3. Perovskite absorber fabrication

The chemicals used for making previously reported triple cation mixed halide perovskite absorber [26],   $  (\mathrm{FA}_{0.83}\mathrm{MA}_{0.17})_{0.95}\mathrm{Cs}_{0.5}\mathrm{P}-\mathrm{bI}_{2.5}\mathrm{Br}_{0.5}  $   include formamidine hydroiodide (FAI), (low water content, Tokyo Chemical Industries), methylamine hydrobromide (MAI), (low water content, Tokyo Chemical Industries), cesium iodide (CsI), (99.999%, Tokyo Chemical Industries), lead iodide   $  (\mathrm{PbI}_{2})  $  , (99.9%, Tokyo Chemical Industries), lead bromide   $  (\mathrm{PbBr}_{2})  $  , (99.9%, Tokyo Chemical Industries) and solvents di-methylformamide (DMF), (anhydrous, 99.8%, Sigma Aldrich), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), (anhydrous, 99.9%, Sigma Aldrich), chlorobenzene (anhydrous, 99.8%, Sigma Aldrich). The precursor was prepared by dissolving 1.1 M   $  PbI_{2}  $  , 1 M FAI, 0.2 M   $  PbBr_{2}  $   and 0.2 M MABr in DMF: DMSO (4:1 vol ratio) solvent, followed by stirring. A 52   $  \mu  $  l of 1.5 M CsI prepared in DMSO solvent was mixed with 1 ml of the previous precursor solution [26]. The resultant solution was casted onto the prepared   $  SnO_{2}  $   coated FTO substrates using anti-solvent assisted spin-coating process at 2000 and 6000 rpm for 10 s and 30 s respectively. To ensure fast crystallization and good film quality, chlorobenzene – antisolvent was dropped at the center of the spinning substrates 15 s prior to the end of 30 s spinning program. The films were then annealed at 100 °C for 30 min and the entire process was carried out in nitrogen atmosphere inside a glove box.

# 2.4. Spiro-OMeTAD HTL deposition

To deposit HTL layer, 80 mg of spiro-OMeTAD (Luminescence Technology) was dissolved in 1 ml chlorobenzene. Later, 24   $  \mu  $  l of 26 mg bis (trifluoromethane) sulfonamide lithium salt (Sigma Aldrich) dissolved in acetonitrile (anhydrous, 99.8%, Sigma Aldrich) and 40   $  \mu  $  l of 4-tert-butylpyridine (Sigma Aldrich) were added to the spiro-OMeTAD solution and stirred. 80   $  \mu  $  l of spiro-OMeTAD solution was dynamically spin coated onto FTO-SnO  $  _{2}  $  -Perovskite film at 4000 rpm for 45 s and left overnight for oxidation of spiro-OMeTAD before gold evaporation in ambient condition.

# 2.5. Gold contact

The top contact on the device was deposited by thermally evaporating 80 nm gold metal (99.999%) inside a vacuum chamber with a base pressure   $  5 \times 10^{-6}  $   mbar. To achieve desired device area, a shadow mask was used resulting in an active area of   $  12 \, mm^2  $  .

# 2.6. ALD - Al $ _2 $ O $ _3 $  Encapsulant deposition

Thin films of aluminium oxide  $ (\mathrm{Al}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{3}) $  were developed with the help of custom-built viscous flow hot wall rector configuration at  $ 75^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . Trimethyl aluminium (TMA, from Gelest, Inc) was used as the metal source and HPLC grade  $ \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} $  (from Sigma Aldrich, USA) was used as the oxygen source. All the reactants are used as received without any further purification and were always stored at RT. High purity nitrogen  $ (\mathrm{N}_2, 99.999\%) $  was used as the carrier gas during the entire deposition

# 2.7. Characterization

The in-situ quartz crystal microbalance (QCM) studies were investigated to study the growth of   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   on spiro-OMeTAD coated Au crystal at 75 °C. Gold coated AT cut quartz crystal with 6 MHz resonant frequency was used as the sensor while the continuous frequency changes were recorded using SQM 160 monitor (both from Inficon, USA). A commercially available crystal drawer and retainer assembly was used to keep the crystal in place with the help of non-conducting epoxy paste. An additional 0.1 Torr flow of   $  N_{2}  $   was flowed through this assembly to further secure any precursor or by-product leakage at the back of the sensor. Sauerbrey equation was used to convert the recorded frequency changes to mass changes.

Rigaku smartlab X-ray diffractometer (XRD) equipped with a Cu-Kα source (1.54 Å) was used for studying crystal structure of thin film materials and its phase. For morphological studies, field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM) images were obtained using Carl-Zeiss Ultra 55 system. In-situ FTIR was performed using a Vertex 70 FTIR instrument from Bruker in order to study the surface chemistry during the exposures of TMA, H₂O pulses during ALD and deposition of ALD-Al₂O₃ on the spiro-OMeTAD films. The detector for the IR beam was Mercury Cadmium Telluride (MCT) cooled with liquid nitrogen.

I-V measurement was performed by using Abet (Class A) Xenon lamp with KG-5 Filter, and gold-coated probe from Holmarc. The intensity of light was calibrated at  $ 100\mathrm{mW/cm^2} $  using silicon reference cells from Newport. A light aperture of  $ 3.14\mathrm{mm}^2 $  was used during measurement and SP-150 potentiostat was used as source meter to measure the I-V characteristics. Measurements for degradation study were done under white LED light source calibrated to  $ 100\mathrm{mW/cm^2} $  on the device. The probing stage with micromanipulators were identical to the ones that were used in I-V characterization. The source meter used in this case were Keithley 2400 and 2450, with lab-view program written in-house. # 3. Results and discussion

Fig. 1a depicts the cross-section scanning electron microscope image of a representative device employing multi-cation hybrid perovskite  $ \left(\mathrm{FA}_{0.83}\mathrm{MA}_{0.17} / 0.95\mathrm{Cs}_{0.05}\mathrm{I}_{2.5}\mathrm{Br}_{0.5}\right) $  as photoactive material with a thickness of ca.  $ 500~\mathrm{nm} $ . The device architecture is schematically shown in the Fig. 1b. Here the photon harvester is sandwiched between thermally grown (thickness of ca.  $ 30~\mathrm{nm} $ ) bi-layer  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  electron transport material (ETL) and spin-coated spiro-OMeTAD (thickness of ca.  $ 150~\mathrm{nm} $ ) hole transporting material (HTL). Devices are built on FTO coated glass substrate (sheet resistance  $ 7\Omega /\mathrm{sq.} $ ) and evaporated Au (ca.  $ 80~\mathrm{nm} $ ) is employed as the top electrode. Here, we used bi-layer  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  as ETL because of enhancement in device performance as shown in the SI figure SI-1 [28-30]. Details of the device fabrication process are described in the experimental section. A characteristic photocurrent density vs. voltage (J-V) behavior under AM1.5 illumination, of a pristine device is shown in Fig. 1c (red). The device yields a current density  $ (\mathrm{J}_{\mathrm{SC}}) $  of  $ 22.7\mathrm{mA} / \mathrm{cm}^2 $ , open circuit potential  $ (\mathrm{V_{OC}}) $  of  $ 1.08\mathrm{V} $  with  $ 77.9\% $  fill factor (FF) that essentially contributes to the overall energy conversion efficiency of  $ 19.1\% $ . It is important to mention that upon depositing ALD grown  $ \mathrm{Al_2O_3} $  on top of the processed device, a reasonable improvement of the photovoltaic parametric values is observed that contributes to the marginal enhancement of the overall energy conversion efficiency of the same device. Fig. 1c (purple) depicts the J-V characteristic of the same device as indicated above but after 300 cycles of  $ \mathrm{Al_2O_3} $  coating. The  $ \mathrm{J_{SC}} $ ,  $ \mathrm{V_{OC}} $  and the FF of the  $ \mathrm{Al_2O_3} $  coated device are  $ 22.8\mathrm{mA} / \mathrm{cm}^2 $ ,  $ 1.1\mathrm{V} $  and  $ 77.4\% $  respectively resulting in an overall efficiency of  $ 19.4\% $ . It is noteworthy that the improvement of the individual parameters is not essentially large, but more significantly, it is qualitatively consistent but quantitatively differs from sample to sample. A detail statistical analysis of the parametric changes upon the  $ \mathrm{Al_2O_3} $  ALD coating over 100 devices is given in supporting information, figure SI-2. Most importantly, hysteresis index is smaller in encapsulated devices compared to Unencapsulated devices as shown in figure SI-3.

The motivation of employing ALD grown   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   here is to explore its potential as a diffusion barrier against moisture and oxygen and therefore enhancing the stability of the hybrid perovskite devices; a methodology that has been successfully explored in OLEDs [31,32] and in perovskite solar cells as well [21–25]. For best performance of an optimized barrier layer, it is important to understand the nucleation and the growth characteristic of the material on the substrate [33]. Looking at Fig. 2a, the growth of   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   by ALD can be characteristically divided into two distinct regions; (I) a linear growth regime with mass gain per cycle ranging between 48 and 50 ng/cm² (similar to the one on the polished Au coated QCM sensor - refer to the figure SI-4 and the discussion thereon) and (II) a non-linear mass-gain vs. time characteristics during the initial 20–25 cycles, often termed as nucleation regime. The subsequent exposure of   $  H_{2}O  $   (of ca. 0.3 ± 0.05 Torr) in the second half cycle leads to further mass increase as   $  \Delta m_{2} = 12-13  $   ng/cm² representing the addition of   $  -OH^{*}  $   species followed by removal of   $  CH_{4}  $   as depicted in equation (B). The exponential nature mass changes during the   $  H_{2}O  $   half cycle here is the characteristic feature of this   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   ALD on Spiro-OMeTAD surface with higher surface roughness. The water molecule is typically known to exhibit high sticking coefficients, which is exhibited by the exponential nature of mass changes indicating slow desorption of additional and un-reacted   $  H_{2}O  $   molecules from the surface. In general, hence the total mass gain in the two half cycles is observed to be   $  \Delta m = \Delta m_{1} + \Delta m_{2} = 48-50  $   ng/cm² which is correlating to the typical   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   growth on the bare Au crystal is also observed to be ca. 51–52 ng/cm².

Technically, the nucleation period in any ALD process is a combinative feature involving the chemical and physical characteristics of the starting surface and the reactivity of the precursors (TMA and   $  H_{2}O  $  , in this case) with the surface species. It is often observed that the same deposition chemistry distinctly poses different nucleation mechanisms, either surface-enhanced or surface-inhibited, that strictly depends on the chemical nature of the starting surface [36,37]. Furthermore, to understand the reactivity of the individual precursor in the nucleation regime, we observed the mass changes by employing a train of successive exposures of   $  H_{2}O  $   and the TMA separately. Fig. 3a portrays the QCM data acquired during the 50 consecutive dosages of   $  H_{2}O  $   that essentially results in negligible net mass change. Also, the symmetric mass change transient characteristics during the   $  H_{2}O  $   dosages denotes little alteration of the surface, either physically or chemically. In the contrary, Fig. 3b shows an unprecedented change in mass when a similar train of 50 cycles of TMA is dosed on spiro-OMeTAD. These continuous changes in mass during successive dosages of TMA indicate a non-self-saturating behaviour, atypical of ALD characteristics. It is also distinctly visible that the mass change transients get reformed during this process. Such characteristic component can be attributed to two possible reasons: (a) a chain-like reaction mechanism involving TMA on the spiro-OMeTAD surface and/or (b) the TMA molecules are not only reacting to the substrate but reaching to the deep trenches of the substrates. To understand the underlying chemistry, we performed in-situ FTIR spectroscopy with the similar train of pulses of the individual precursors as shown in the figure SI5. These complementary in-situ measurements undoubtedly indicate that the   $  H_{2}O  $   dosages induce no chemical modification in the substrate. Though Li-TFSI in spiro-OMeTAD can well attract moisture, within the accuracy of our measurement system we cannot detect any systematic changes upon   $  H_{2}O  $   dosages. However, the TMA exposures results in a continuous increase in the absorbance of vibrational spectra at ca. 690, 1190 and 3260 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   corresponding to   $  CH_{3}  $   rocking, Al–CH  $  _{3}  $   bending and alkenyl C–H stretch respectively. This signifies the continuous addition of Al–CH  $  _{3}  $   species on the bulk of the spiro upon TMA exposure, which is in accord with the QCM measurements wherein a similar non-saturating behaviour is observed with TMA exposures alone. Interestingly, the above characteristic is only notable during the nucleation regime. Further deposition leads to the linear growth regime classical ALD characteristic, i.e. self-limiting and surface limited chemical reaction is followed.

Our observation is unequivocally in agreement with the work previously reported by Wilson et al., where an alternate nucleation process was discussed on those substrates where specific surface-bound chemical groups are absent [40]. We hence believe that the TMA is basically trapped inside the spiro-OMeTAD film. These trapped TMA molecules are therefore available for the  $ \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} $  that is dosed in sequence. It is worth mentioning that the cross-section energy dispersive x-ray analysis (EDAX) indicates the presence of Al throughout the spiro-OMeTAD film, as shown in the figure SI-6. The film thus formed is not the geometrically surface limited but eventually is nucleated inside the bulk of the substrate that ultimately forms a continuous film. Hence the film from beyond the nucleation regime can be used as an encapsulation for the further stability measurements under different stressed condition.

To begin with, we find that the   $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $   coating provides better structural stability under ambient conditions in comparison to the pristine device. Similar to the previous measurement, figure SI-8 shows the formation of  $  PbI_{2}  $  being prominent for aged pristine sample that is suppressed in the coated device, but the striking difference from the above is the structural void formation (figure SI-9). Such structural distortions are previously reported in the literature and found detrimental in the overall device stability7. However, the similar situation does not withhold when the relative humidity rose to 75% while keeping the temperature constant at 12 °C. Under high humidity, pristine samples undergo both structural degradation both in terms of  $  PbI_{2}  $  formation and δ-FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  formation. As shown in the figure SI-10, the formation of the  $  PbI_{2}  $  is inhibited in the  $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $  coated samples but the formation of δ-FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  is not essentially suppressed. Further experiments qualitatively suggest that the formation of the δ-FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  is essentially related to magnitude of the relative humidity where temperature also plays an essential role. We henceforth believe that the phenomenon is limited by the water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) of the employed  $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $  layer [41]. In a nutshell, our observations indicate that the ALD grown  $  Al_{2}O_{3}  $  on the device serves sufficiently well against the humidity induced materials degradation (structurally) under high humidity condition (figure SI-11) that surely motivates to investigate the long-term device stability under varied ambient conditions.

# 4. Conclusion

To conclude, employing an ultrathin layer of  $ \mathrm{ALD - Al_2O_3} $  on perovskite device architecture offers enormous advantage towards its long-term stability. An in-depth study on the deposition profile of ALD-binary oxides on top of the organic hole transport layer is carried to understand the impact of the precursor species on device performance. We explain in detail the mechanism of deposition of  $ \mathrm{Al_2O_3} $  on spiro-OMeTAD and confirm that the material ingresses through the thickness of the HTL. Complete encapsulation of the cell provides protection not only from humidity, but also from oxygen. Both continuous illumination measurement, in different atmospheres, and cyclic illumination measurements confirm the robustness of encapsulated devices in comparison to the pristine cells. It is important to notice that the initial exponent-type decay, both in  $ \mathrm{J_{SC}} $  and  $ \mathrm{V_{oc}} $  persists in the encapsulated devices, though it is magnitude-wise different, which indicates different mechanistic degradation pathway. The linear characteristic of  $ \mathrm{J_{SC}} $  and  $ \mathrm{V_{OC}} $  over the time in encapsulated devices extrapolates to a considerably high  $ \mathrm{T}_{80} $  value, though there is drastic decrease in FF of pristine cells owing to the increase in parasitic resistances. Implementation of this technology to enhance the stability of devices can be an important factor for futuristic commercialization of perovskite photovoltaics.
