# $ \pi $ -Conjugated Small Molecules Modified  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  Layer for Perovskite Solar Cells with over  $ 23\% $  Efficiency

Qiang Lou, Yufang Han, Chang Liu,  $  ^{*}  $   Kanghui Zheng, Jinsheng Zhang, Xia Chen, Qing Du, Chong Chen,  $  ^{*}  $   and Ziyi Ge  $  ^{*}  $

$ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  has been universally applied as electron transporting layer (ETL) towards the fabrication of highly efficient perovskite solar cells (PSCs), owing to its unique advantages including low-temperature solution-processability, high optical, transmittance and good electrical conductivity. Uncoordinated Sn-dangling bonds on  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  surface exist as deep traps to capture the photogenerated carriers, causing hysteresis and device instability. Fullerene derivatives, though being widely utilized as the passivator for  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $ , are highly prone to self-aggregate due to their  $ \pi $ -cage structures, which hampers passivation. Herein,  $ \pi $ -conjugated n-type small molecules with better film formation ability are innovatively designed, to improve passivation effectiveness. By exploring the interplay between molecular stacking of small molecules and charge transporting/recombination dynamics at the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2/ $  perovskite interface, it is unveiled that a more compact molecular packing of the organic passivators yields superior interfacial characteristics, in terms of fewer trap states, lower charge recombination and higher electron transporting efficiency. An impressive PCE over  $ 23\% $  is achieved with the assistance of this new-type  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $ -passivator, which is among the highest reported value for triple-cation perovskite systems to date. This work offers an original concept for the design and synthesis of ETL passivators towards the development of high performance and stable PSCs.

# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite materials have attracted widespread attentions due to their outstanding optoelectronic properties, low material costs, solution processibility, etc.  $  ^{[1-5]}  $   During the past years, perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have experienced a rapid progress in power conversion efficiency (PCE) from 3.8% to 25.5%,  $  ^{[1,6-10]}  $   approaching that of the maturely

Benefiting from these advantages, PSCs based on   $  SnO_{2}  $   as ETL have reached PCE of 25.2% to date.[30] Whereas, considerable amount of oxygen vacancies on the   $  SnO_{2}  $   surface would act as deep traps to capture the photogenerated carriers, which causes hysteresis and instability of the device.[31–34] And this intrinsic defect of   $  SnO_{2}  $   needs to be resolved for a further PCE breakthrough of PSCs.

In recent years, significant attempts of defect-passivation have been made to decrease the oxygen vacancies and trap states on   $  SnO_{2}  $   surface.  $  ^{[35-38]}  $   Among them, n-type fullerene derivatives represent one of the most studied and efficacious passivator,  $  ^{[39-42]}  $   due to the ease of forming coordinate bonds between carboxylate group and   $  SnO_{2}  $   surface. In addition, fullerene derivative is a common electron acceptor in organic solar cells (OSCs),  $  ^{[43]}  $   which grants an effective electron extraction from the perovskite active layer to ETL, thus contributing to higher PSC performances. Nevertheless, it should be noticed that   $  \pi  $  -cage structures of fullerene derivatives are prone to self-aggregate,  $  ^{[44]}  $   which strongly affects the validity and

developed silicon solar cells. It shows the great potential of PSCs as the dominator of next-generation photovoltaics. Whereas, during the evolution of PSC development, the metal oxide electron transporting layer (ETL), as well as the ETL/perovskite interface,  $  ^{[11-14]}  $  has always been an issue in regard to photovoltaic efficiency and device stability.  $  ^{[8]}  $

Derived from dye-sensitized solar cells (DSSCs), the combination of compact and mesoporous   $  TiO_{2}  $   has been commonly utilized as ETLs during the early studies of PSCs.  $  ^{[15]}  $   But a high sintering temperature of   $  \approx500\ ^{\circ}C  $   is normally required for the fabrication process, which is high energy consumption and incompatible with scalable depositions on flexible substrates. Worse still,   $  TiO_{2}  $   is highly photocatalytic active under ultraviolet (UV) irradiation that severely hampers the long-term stability of PSCs under illumination.  $  ^{[16-22]}  $   Later on, a new ETL,   $  SnO_{2}  $  , has been developed as a better candidate, due to its low-temperature processability  $  ^{[23]}  $   and high sustainability under UV illumination.  $  ^{[24-27]}  $   More importantly,   $  SnO_{2}  $   film

To this end, we innovatively proposed the application of n-type conjugated small molecule with high structural flexibility towards the end of achieving desired aggregation, better film formation, and ordered molecular packing, which expect to improve the passivation effectiveness on   $  SnO_{2}  $   surface.  $  ^{[45]}  $   In particular, Y6, being prevalently used as non-fullerene electron acceptor in OSCs, has alkyl side chains attached on the central fused core at the same side, which could prevent over aggregation of the molecule while maintaining an effective intramolecular charge transport channel.  $  ^{[43,46]}  $   In a further attempt to tune the aggregation and molecular packing of organic passivators and dig into their correlations with charge transporting/recombination dynamics at the   $  SnO_{2}  $  /perovskite interfaces,  $  ^{[47]}  $   BTAC4 is designed as a Y-series derivative through side-chain engineering. 4-Phenylbutyl is attached on the benzotriazole core as a means to increase the steric hindrances and further reduce the degree of self-aggregation, which might improve the film formation ability of neat small molecules. Additionally, the linear side chains (on BTAC4) compared to the branched alkyl ones (on Y6) are likely to induce closely packed main chains.  $  ^{[48]}  $   By parallelly investigating the optoelectronic properties of the   $  SnO_{2}  $  /Y6/perovskite and   $  SnO_{2}  $  /BTAC4/perovskite interfaces, we found that a more compact and regular stacking of the small molecules gave rise to more favorable interfacial properties, in terms of lower trap states, higher electron conducting/extraction efficiency, and suppressed charge recombination. As a result, PSCs based on BTAC4 as organic passivator achieved an exceptional PCE over 23%, and an astonishing open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) of 1.252V, which is the highest reported   $  V_{OC}  $   of CsFAMA perovskite system to date.  $  ^{[49]}  $   The unencapsulated device also shows outstanding stability by retaining 90% of the initial PCE after 768 h storing under 35% humidity.

# 2. Results and Discussion

The synthetic routes for BTAC4 are shown in Figure 1a, and the experimental details have been provided in the Supporting Information. Figure 1b schematically illustrates the passivation mechanism for  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $ : the carbonyl groups on Y6 and BTAC4 form coordinate bond with  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  by sharing lone pair electrons between O and Sn atoms, which reduces O-vacancies on the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  surfaces. In an effort to uncover the effects of side-chain manipulation rules on the molecular packings of Y-series molecules, the density functional theory (DFT) calculation is performed to simulate the chain stacking patterns of Y6 and BTAC4. As shown by the results in Figure 1c, BTAC4 exhibits smaller intermolecular distance of  $ 3.58\AA $  than that of Y6 ( $ 3.70\AA $ ), verifying a more compact stacking of BTAC4. Grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWXAS) is used to explore the molecular packing orientation of Y6 and BTAC4 films. The 2D GIWAXS patterns are shown in Figure 1d, and the corresponding 1D integrated scattering along in-plane (IP) and out-of-plane (OOP) are illustrated in Figure S1

(Supporting Information). From Figure 1d, Y6 presents a random ordering of  $ \pi-\pi $  stacking with slightly stronger IP diffractions, while BTAC4 exhibits a strong (010) diffraction peaks along OOP direction, which is indicative of a highly ordered face-on orientation of  $ \pi-\pi $  stacking. From the 1D plot in Figure S1a,b (Supporting Information),  $ d $ -spacing of (010) plane can be determined to be 3.55 and 3.51 Å along IP and OOP directions for Y6, and 3.52 and 3.35 Å along the IP and OOP directions for BTAC4, respectively. The ordered face-on  $ \pi-\pi $  stacking along with the reduced intermolecular distances in BTAC4 film expect to give rise to both higher passivation effectiveness on  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  and improved electron transporting efficiencies.

The   $  SnO_{2}  $   passivation processes with small molecules are described in the Experimental Section. Figure S2 (Supporting Information) studies the optical transmission spectra of the   $  SnO_{2}  $ ,   $  SnO_{2}/Y6  $ , and   $  SnO_{2}/BTAC4  $   films. It is observed that   $  SnO_{2}/BTAC4  $   film exhibits an overall transmittance beyond 85% in the whole UV to visible region, while the transmittance of   $  SnO_{2}/Y6  $   film is slightly lower in 600–800 nm region. The high transmittance in the visible region is critical to guarantee a sufficient absorbance of the perovskite active layer. Energy level diagrams of the ETL films are then investigated by optical absorption and ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) measurements, the results are illustrated in Figure S3 (Supporting Information). Following the equations of   $  W_{s}=hv-E_{cutoff}  $   and   $  E_{v}=21.21=(E_{cutoff}-E_{onset})  $  , [50,51] LUMO levels of Y6, BTAC4 are determined to be -4.17 and -4.04 eV, respectively; and HOMO levels are calculated to be -5.55 and -5.53 eV, respectively. Based on the results, energy level alignments of ETL/perovskite can be depicted in Figure 1e. It is seen that the LUMO levels of Y6, BTAC4 films match well with that of the perovskite layer, which ensures an efficient electron transport from perovskite to ETL.

To explore the interplay between molecular packing of the conjugated small molecules and the passivation effectiveness, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurement is firstly operated to evaluate the surface chemical properties of   $  SnO_{2}  $  ,   $  SnO_{2}/Y6  $  , and   $  SnO_{2}/BTAC4  $   films, the results are shown in Figure 2a,b. Compared with   $  SnO_{2}  $  , additional peaks of   $  SnO_{2}/Y6  $   and   $  SnO_{2}/BTAC4  $   films at 487.7 eV are assigned to F 1s,  $  ^{[38]}  $   which reveals the existence of Y6 and BTAC4 on the   $  SnO_{2}  $   films. Also, Figure 2b shows that Sn 3d peak is shifted by 0.38 and 0.17 eV toward higher binding energy for   $  SnO_{2}/Y6  $   and   $  SnO_{2}/BTAC4  $  , respectively, testifying the formation of coordinate bond between carbonyl group and   $  SnO_{2}  $  , which functions to reduce the O-vacancies on   $  SnO_{2}  $   surface. The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements are then carried out to inspect the interfacial charge dynamics of ETL/perovskite.   $  SnO_{2}/perovskite  $  ,   $  SnO_{2}/Y6/perovskite  $  , and   $  SnO_{2}/BTAC4/perovskite  $   films are all prepared on glass substrates. PL spectra are recorded at an excitation wavelength of 510 nm from the glass side. To have an in-depth understanding of charge transporting/recombination dynamics at the ETL/perovskite interfaces, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) of PSCs is further studied, the tests are performed in dark.  $  ^{[63]}  $   Inset of Figure 5a gives the equivalent circuit diagram of the PSC device. EIS spectra of PSCs in Figure 5a exhibits two typical arcs, one in the high frequency region is generally dominated by the charge transfer resistance (  $  R_{tr}  $  ) and the other in the low frequency region is associated with the recombination resistance (  $  R_{rec}  $  ).  $  ^{[64]}  $   By fitting the spectra,   $  R_{rec}  $   can be determined to be 2618, 18 397, and 19 375   $  \Omega  $   cm  $  ^{-2}  $   for PSCs based on SnO  $  _{2}  $ , SnO  $  _{2}  $ /Y6, and SnO  $  _{2}  $ /BATC4, respectively. The remarkably increased   $  R_{rec}  $   for the devices with passivation indicates suppressed charge recombination at ETL/perovskite interfaces. The Mott–Schottky (M–S) test is also adopted to reveal the charge accumulation/extraction conditions at the ETL/perovskite. Figure 5b depicts the M–S diagram of devices based on SnO  $  _{2}  $ , SnO  $  _{2}  $ /Y6, and SnO  $  _{2}  $ /BTAC4 ETLs. According to the equation   $  C^{-2}=2(V_{bi}-V)/(A^{2}q\varepsilon\varepsilon_{0}N_{d})  $  ,  $  ^{[65]}  $   charge density (  $  N_{d}  $  ) of three devices can be extracted to be   $  7.70\times10^{17}  $ ,   $  6.92\times10^{17}  $ , and   $  6.72\times10^{17}  $   cm  $  ^{-3}  $   for the SnO  $  _{2}  $ /perovskite, SnO  $  _{2}  $ /Y6/perovskite, and SnO  $  _{2}  $ /BTAC4/perovskite films, respectively. The decreased   $  N_{d}  $   at SnO  $  _{2}  $ /Y6/perovskite and SnO  $  _{2}  $ /BTAC4/perovskite interfaces manifest reduced charge accumulation, which should be ascribed to the promoted charge extraction efficiency at the interface.

To further support this conclusion, it is very necessary to study the carrier recombination process of devices under working conditions. The dependence of   $  V_{OC}  $   and   $  J_{SC}  $   on various light intensities (  $  P_{light}  $  ) of PSCs is evaluated. In Figure 5c,   $  V_{OC}  $   and light intensity show a linear relationship on the natural logarithmic scale   $  V_{OC} \propto \left(\frac{nkT}{q}\right) \ln(P_{\mathrm{light}})  $  , where the slope is nkT/q, q is the fundamental charge, k is the Boltzmann constant, and T is the absolute temperature. Compared to PSCs based on pristine   $  SnO_{2}  $   (with a slope of 1.24 kT/q), Y6 and BTAC4 passivation decrease the slope to 1.17 and 1.06 kT/q, respectively. The slope value close to 1 kT/q indicates the dominant bimolecular charge recombination in the device,  $  ^{[52]}  $   which certifies a lower trap-assisted recombination at the   $  SnO_{2}/BTAC_{4}/perovskite  $   interface. Figure 5d shows the log-linear relationship between   $  J_{SC}  $   and   $  P_{light}  $   (  $  J_{SC} \propto P_{light}^{\alpha}  $  ), where the slope is presented by   $  \alpha = (\ln J_{SC})/(\ln P_{\mathrm{light}})  $  .   $  \alpha  $   of PSCs based on   $  SnO_{2}  $  ,   $  SnO_{2}/Y6  $  , and   $  SnO_{2}/BTAC4  $   are determined to be 0.93, 0.97, and 0.98, respectively, which is indicative of a fewer single molecular recombination in PSCs with Y6 and BTAC4 passivation.  $  ^{[66]}  $   These results conform to the previous trap density analysis.

Materials: The   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloid precursor was purchased from Alfa Aesar. N, N-Dimethylformamide (DMF), chlorobenzene, and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.   $  PbI_{2}  $   (99.99%), FAI (99.9%), CsI (99.9%),   $  PbBr_{2}  $   (99.9%), MAI (99.9%), bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)-imide lithium salt (Li-TFSI), Tris(2-(1H-pyrazol-1-yl)-4-tert-butylpyridine)-cobalt (KF209 Co), 4-tertbutylpyridine (TBP), and Spiro-OMeTAD (99.8%),   $  CH_{3}CH_{2}CH_{2}CH_{2}NH_{3}I  $   (BAI) were purchased from Xian Polymer Light Technology Corp.

Solar Cells Fabrication: The ITO glass was ultrasonically cleaned with deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol for 30 min. The substrate was then placed under UV-ozone for 30 min before use.   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloidal precursor was diluted with ultrapure water at a volume ratio of 1:2 to prepare the   $  SnO_{2}  $   precursor. Then KCl was dissolved in the   $  SnO_{2}  $   precursor at a concentration of 5 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  . The 100   $  \mu  $  L   $  SnO_{2}  $   precursor was spin-coated on the ITO substrate at a speed of 6500 rpm for 10 s and then annealed at 150 °C for 5 min at a temperature of 20 °C and a relative humidity (RH) of 40%. As a result, a   $  SnO_{2}  $   layer was obtained.

After 60 min of UV-ozone treatment, the substrate was transferred to the glove box. The organic interfacial layers (Y6 and BTAC4) were spin-coated onto the SnO₂ film from CB solvent with a solution concentration of 2 mg mL⁻¹. The films were then thermal annealed at 100 °C for 10 min in N₂-filled glovebox for a better interfacial contact between SnO₂ and organic layer. A 1.03 M perovskite precursor was prepared by dissolving CsI, FAI, MAI, PbI₂, and PbBr₂ in a 0.05:0.81:0.14:0.78:0.22 molar ratio in 4:1 (v:v) mixed solvent of DMF and DMSO and then stirred at 60 °C for 10 h. Filter the perovskite precursor solution through a 0.22 μm pore PTFE filter and then spin-coat 50 μL of the perovskite precursor solution on the SnO₂/ITO substrate at a high speed of 4000 rpm for 30 s. In the last 5 s, 1 mL of ether was added dropwise as an antisolvent. The perovskite film was then annealed at 120 °C for 15 min. After the perovskite/SnO₂/ITO film was cooled to room temperature, 50 μL of 5 mg mL⁻¹ BAL isopropanol solution was spin-coated on the surface of the 3D perovskite film at a speed of 6000 rpm for 25 s and then annealed at 100 °C 10 min. The spiro-OMeTAD solution was spin-coated at 5000 rpm for 15 s, which contained 72.3 mg Spiro-OMeTAD, 17.5 μL bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt (Li-TFSI) stock solution (520 mg Li-TFSI in 1 mL acetonitrile), 8 Tris(2-(1H-pyrazol-1-yl)-4-tert-butylpyridine)-cobalt (KF209 Co) stock solution (360 mg FK-209 Co dissolved in 1 mL acetonitrile), 28.8 μL 4-tert-butylpyridine, and 1 mL chlorobenzene. After that, place the equipment in a desiccator (RH ≈ 10%) for 30 h. Finally, a 100 nm Ag film was deposited as an electrode by thermal evaporation.
