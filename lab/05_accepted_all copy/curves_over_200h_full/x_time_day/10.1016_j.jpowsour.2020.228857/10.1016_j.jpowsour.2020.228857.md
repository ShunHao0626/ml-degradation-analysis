# 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have become one of the most promising next-generation photovoltaic technologies due to the low cost of device preparation and high power conversion efficiency (PCE), and have already shown the influence [1-3]. Due to the tunable band gap [4], high light absorption coefficient [5], low exciton binding energy and long carrier diffusion length [6-8], the PCE of PSCs has grown from  $ 3.8\% $  at the beginning to  $ 25.2\% $  in 2019 [9-11], which takes only ten years. Although the efficiency of PSCs has reached new heights, due to the polycrystalline structure of the light absorption layer, volatile organic components and ionic nature, there are numerous defects in the interior and surface, which will greatly affect the efficiency and stability.

The ionic nature of the perovskite material results in a great number of defects in the perovskite film which limits the efficiency and stability of perovskite solar cells. The passivation of the defects can effectively suppress the non-radiative recombination of carriers and improve the photovoltaic performance and stability of perovskite solar cells. In this paper, quinoline is introduced into the perovskite precursor solution. By adjusting the addition amount, it is found that quinoline can effectively induce the growth and orientation of perovskite crystals, consequently, improving the film quality and reduce defect density. The optimized device shows higher  $  V_{\mathrm{OC}}  $  (1.142 V) and FF (0.792), and achieved a champion power conversion efficiency of  $  20.87\%  $ . In addition, the hysteresis of the device is suppressed and the stability is improved. The results demonstrate that quinoline is an effective perovskite additive.

Lewis bases coordinate or passivate uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  or Pb clusters by providing a pair of non-bonding electrons to form a Lewis adduct [22]. In order to evaluate whether quinoline in the perovskite precursor solution could affect the crystal size, surface morphology and roughness of the perovskite film, the SEM and atomic force microscopy (AFM) images were scanning. As can be seen from Fig. 2a and b, compared with the pristine perovskite film, the film with quinoline has a larger grain size, and the content of  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  on the upper surface is significantly reduced, which is consistent with the XRD results. It suggests that quinoline can reduce the crystallization rate of perovskite crystals, thereby obtaining larger grain size and higher crystallinity. The larger grain size means that the film has fewer grain boundaries, which reduces the non-radiative recombination of carriers. Studies have shown that a smooth perovskite film surface can promote carrier transport [31,32].

To further illustrate the effect of quinoline on the growth of perovskite crystals, their surface roughness was measured and the results are shown in Fig. 2c and d. Compared with the pristine film (25.4 nm), the root-mean-square (RMS) roughness of the film with 0.4 M quinoline is only 18.1 nm, which is consistent with the increase in absorption in the UV–vis test results [33]. The reduction in roughness greatly improves the contact between the perovskite and the hole transport layer, thereby speeding up hole transport and improving the performance of PSCs.

The J-V characteristics of devices with only electron transport include three regions: ohmic region (low bias), the trap-filled limited (TFL) region (middle bias) and the trap-free space charge limited current (SCLC) region (high bias). As the bias voltage continues to increase, the trap is gradually filled until it reaches the trap-filled limit voltage   $  (V_{\mathrm{TFL}})  $   [34,35]. From the intersection of the extension lines, the   $  V_{TEL}  $   value of the quinoline passivated device is only 0.161 V, while the pristine device is as high as 0.403 V. # 4.3. Characterization

The XRD measurements of the perovskite films were carried out by using the Bruker X-ray diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiation source. PL spectra (excitation at 507 nm) were measured using a Thermo Scientific Luminescence Fluorescence Spectrometer (LF-1303003), and TRPL spectra were measured with a time correlated single photon counting method (Zolix) on Omin-λ Monochromator/Spectrograph (760 nm). The UV–vis absorption spectra were measured by using a PerkinElmer Lambda 950UV/VIS/NIR spectrometer. Top-view and cross-sectional SEM images of PSCs were obtained under the Hitachi S-4800 and the roughness of the perovskite films was obtained by AFM (Multimode-8J). The J-V curves of the PSCs were measured on Keithley 2400 source-meter under ambient conditions and the standard one sun illumination (AM 1.5G, 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) whose light intensity was calibrated by NREL-calibrated silicon solar cells equipped with KG-2 filter was

provided by Xe lamp-based solar simulator (Irvine, CA). The EQE was measured by using a QE-R Quantum Efficiency Measurement System (Enli Technology Co. LTD) and the electrochemical performance test was measured by an electrochemical workstation (CHI660E, Chenghua Co. Ltd. Shanghai). All characterizations and measurements were performed in an air environment, and no packaging process was performed.
