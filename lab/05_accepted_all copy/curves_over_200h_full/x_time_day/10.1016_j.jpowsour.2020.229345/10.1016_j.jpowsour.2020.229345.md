# GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

# ABSTRACT

Carbon based perovskite solar cells (C–PSCs) with long-term stability and low cost have been considered as a competitive candidate for further actual applications. Generally, the preparation of semiconductor oxide electron transport layer and perovskite involves high-temperature annealing procedure for abundant light absorption as well as efficient charge transport, which limits its flexible application. Herein, we develop an all room temperature processing high-efficiency planar C-PSCs that utilizes   $  SnO_{2}  $   as electron transport layer and a cheap polymer i.e. polyethylene glycol (PEG) as the interface passivation layer to reduce the defect state density of perovskite and facilitate valid charge transfer from perovskite to carbon. The resultant planar C-PSC devices achieve a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 14.5% comparable to the conventional C-PSCs with annealing process, about 50% higher than that based on bare C-PSC devices without PEG. The room-temperature processing C-PSCs exhibits excellent stability, retaining around 90% of the initial PCE after being stored in air for 20 days. Furthermore, this room-temperature process is extended to the flexible C–PSCs, achieving a PCE over 10%. This work offers a facile room-temperature approach to reliably prepare efficient C–PSCs via interface modification.

# 1. Introduction

Organic-inorganic halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been selected as one of the most promising photovoltaic devices owing to their high power conversion efficiency (PCE) and simple fabrication process. Certified efficiency has sky rocketed over 25% during the past ten years   $  [1-3]  $  . The structure of the typical PSC contains a transparent electrode, electron transport layer (ETL), perovskite absorber layer, hole transport layer (HTL) and precious metal electrode   $  [4-6]  $  , in which the use of HTL and precious metal electrode could greatly enhance the PCE of PSC devices. Up to now, the reported efficiency over 20% are all based on this typical PSC structure with HTL and precious metal electrode   $  [7,8]  $  . However, the use of HTL and precious metal electrode would result in the instability of PSC devices and the increase of device cost. For example, the most common HTL i.e. spiro-OMeTAD is expensive (216.3 per gram) and the accompany dopants (e.g. 4-tert-butylpyridine and lithium salts) are easy to decompose under air and thermal atmosphere, which leads to the severe performance degradation of devices   $  [9,10]  $  . The deposition of the precious metal (Au, Ag) electrodes onto the HTL involves the high-energy-consumption thermal evaporation under high-vacuum condition, which also increases the PSC cost   $  [11]  $  . Furthermore, when the PSCs continuously operates under illumination, the precious metal ion diffusion as well as the mobile iodide ion diffusion from perovskite to spiro-OMeTAD are also responsible for the degradation of device performance   $  [12]  $  .

Carbon-based PSCs (C-PSCs) with the structure of TCO/ETL/perovskite/carbon is an alternative to solve instability issues of the conventional PSC devices and simultaneously reduce the device cost by using carbon to replace the HTL and precious metal electrode   $  [13–15]  $  . The reported C-PSCs can be divided into three types: mesoporous, embedment and planar structures. Among these, the mesoporous structured C-PSCs with metal oxide mesoporous scaffold (such as   $  TiO_{2}  $  ) are widely studied and achieve a highest PCE over 16% as well as excellent stability (90% of initial value for about 1000 h under ambient atmosphere)   $  [16]  $  . Note that, for the mesoporous structured C-PSCs, a high-temperature annealing process (  $  \geq400\ ^{\circ}C  $  ) is often required to induce the formation of   $  TiO_{2}  $   crystalline mesoporous layer for efficient electron transfer, which is unavailable for the flexible substrates   $  [17,18]  $  . Compared with mesoporous C-PSCs, the planar C-PSCs without mesoporous metal oxide can be fabricated with a facile low-temperature process, attracting more and more attention as a promising alternative   $  [19–21]  $  . However, for planar C-PSCs, the ETL and perovskite layer still involve the annealing process (  $  \geq150\ ^{\circ}C  $  ) for high-quality functional film with better crystallization, less trap states, and more favorable morphology   $  [22]  $  . Recently, Chen and co-workers developed a low temperature (  $  <150\ ^{\circ}C  $  ) processing WB-  $  SnO_{2}  $   as ETL and achieved a PCE of 20.52% in a conventional HTL-PSC   $  [23]  $  . Dong and co-workers prepared   $  SnO_{2}  $   film at a near room-temperature and applied it to flexible HTL-PSC, achieving a PCE up to 18%   $  [24]  $  . Additionally, our previous work showed that a small bit of bromide could induce room-temperature crystallization of perovskite   $  [25]  $  . All these previous work indicates that it is feasible to develop all room temperature processing planar C-PSCs, which is capable of extending to flexible device applications.

Another challenge for planar C–PSCs is that its photovoltaic conversion performance is still low owing to inferior hole collection behavior within the perovskite/carbon interface and severe charge recombination   $  [16]  $  . If the perovskite layer is prepared at room temperature, the low-quality perovskite and rougher perovskite/carbon interface would induce formation of more detect states at grain boundary and interface   $  [26–30]  $  , which would aggravate the charge recombination in the planar C–PSCs. Thus, it is essential to passivate surface detect state of perovskite and promote hole transmission at the perovskite/carbon electrode interface for the low-temperature processing high-efficiency planar C-PSCs.

PEG is a cheap hygroscopic polymer, which shows outstanding thermodynamic property (soften at  $ \geq 60^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ ), chemical stability and low

cost [31]. PEG has been introduced into perovskite precursor solution to enhance the stability of PSCs [32–34], because PEG with C–O bond including two lone electron pairs can not only connect with uncoordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ions at perovskite film surface by the formation of a coordinate bond to passivate detect states and improve the performance of devices, but also protect perovskite film from water molecule eroding to increase the air stability of devices. Considering the dual-functionality of PEG, herein, we firstly develop a room temperature processing high-efficiency planar C–PSCs, in which   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL film,   $  MA_{0.1}Cs_{0.15}-FA_{0.75}PbI_{2.8}Bt_{0.2}  $   and carbon layers are sequentially spin-coated and doctor-bladed onto the ITO substrates, and PEG is used as a passivation layer for perovskite/carbon electrode interface to reduce the surface detect state of the room-temperature-processing perovskite and promote hole transmission at the interface, yet it is still to report. The prepared planar C–PSCs achieve a high efficiency of 14.5% and excellent air stability with 90% of the initial value after being aged for over 20 days in air atmosphere without encapsulation. Such room-temperature process is adopted to the flexible planar C–PSCs, achieving the PCE exceeding 10%.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1. Materials preparation

ITO conductive glass substrates (15   $  \Omega  $   cm  $  ^{-2}  $  ) were purchased from Shenzhen Xiangcheng Science & Technology Ltd. Lead iodide (PbI  $  _{2}  $  , 99.9985%), cesium bromide (CsBr, 99.999%), cesium iodide (CsI, 99.999%) and the SnO  $  _{2}  $   solution (tin (IV) oxide, 15 wt% in H  $  _{2}  $  O colloidal dispersion) were obtained from Alfa Aesar. Formamidine iodide (FAI, 99.999%) and Methylamine iodide (MAI, 99.999%) were acquired from Dyesol. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, >99.0%) and chlorobenzene (CB, >99.0%) were gained from J&K Chemical Ltd. The carbon paste was purchased from Guangzhou Seaside Technology Ltd.

# 2.2. Fabrication of room-temperature processing planar C-PSC devices

The ITO/glass substrates were cleaned by ultrasound carefully with deionized water, acetone and isopropanol for 10 min respectively and finally cleaned under oxygen plasma for 20 min after blow-drying with nitrogen gas. The   $  SnO_{2}  $   aqueous colloidal dispersion (2.67 wt%) was spin-coated onto ITO/glass substrates at 3000 rpm for 30 s to prepare the   $  SnO_{2}  $   thin film at room temperature (denoted as RT-  $  SnO_{2}  $  ). Subsequently, the perovskite film crystallized and grew on the coated   $  SnO_{2}  $   film on the basis of the published one in our laboratory [25]. Polyethylene glycol (PEG) was dissolved into chlorobenzene (CB) to form uniform aqueous solution of 0.1 mg/mL, 1 mg/mL and 10 mg/mL, respectively. The PEG aqueous solution was spin-coated onto the as-deposited perovskite layer at 2500 rpm for 30 s. At last, the carbon paste was spread onto the perovskite film in air and dried in vacuum ambient at room temperature to form the planar-structure carbon electrode. For comparison, the C-PSCs based on the   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETLs heated in air at 150 °C (denoted as 150-  $  SnO_{2}  $  ) and 120 °C (denoted as 120-  $  SnO_{2}  $  ) were also prepared, respectively.

# 2.3. Characterization

The S4700 field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) was used to characterize the morphology of perovskite films. RMS (root mean square roughness) was calculated by Atom force microscopy (AFM) height images characterized with a Bruker Metrology Nanoscope III-D atomic force microscope. Photoluminescence (PL) was characterized at  $ 790~\mathrm{nm} $  and Xenon lamp was excited at  $ 470~\mathrm{nm} $ . Time resolved photoluminescence (TR-PL) was measured on photoluminescence spectrometer (FLS 980, Edinburgh Instruments), followed by exciting with NIR laser transmitter at  $ 460~\mathrm{nm} $  and collected at  $ 790~\mathrm{nm} $ . # 3. Results and discussion

For PSCs, the transmittance and crystallinity of   $  SnO_{2}  $   films would affect the sunlight absorbance, the electron transport behavior and the final photovoltaic performance of devices [35]. Transmittance and X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were performed on the   $  SnO_{2}/ITO  $   prepared at room temperature, 120 °C and 150 °C, and the results are shown in Fig. S1a&b. Although the transmittance of the RT-  $  SnO_{2}  $   film is lower than that of the 120-  $  SnO_{2}  $   and 150-  $  SnO_{2}  $   films in the region of 300–400 nm, they exhibit almost identical transmittance in the visible light region (400–800 nm) that is the main sunlight absorbing region for the upper perovskite layer (Fig. S1a). Note that the XRD patterns of the three samples are almost identical and the peaks in Fig. S1b mainly originate from the ITO substrates. Because the   $  SnO_{2}  $   films are too thin in thickness, the XRD peaks of   $  SnO_{2}  $   can not be discerned. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscope (AFM) were performed to further compare surface morphology of the   $  SnO_{2}  $   films (Fig. 1). The RT-  $  SnO_{2}  $   film (RMS = 2.87 nm, Fig. 1f) exhibits a rougher surface than the 150-  $  SnO_{2}  $   film (RMS = 1.86 nm, Fig. 1d) and 120-  $  SnO_{2}  $   film (RMS = 2.36 nm, Fig. 1e). Based on above characterizations, the post treatment temperature exactly plays a positive role in improving the transmittance and surface morphology of the   $  SnO_{2}  $ , which originates from the different solvent evaporation process with and without annealing. Fig. S2 shows the schematic diagram of formation of   $  SnO_{2}  $   films at room temperature and 150 °C, respectively. Under high temperature (150 °C), the solvent in the   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloidal dispersion evaporates fast through the relatively single path and the perovskite crystallizes in a short time, hence forming a smooth and even film surface beneficial for sunlight transmission. On the contrast, the solvent under room temperature evaporates slowly through various complex paths, and the crystallization of perovskite undergoes a longer time.

PL spectrum of the room-temperature perovskite film shows an extremely low peak intensity (Fig. 3a), indicating severe non-recombination originating from a large amount of defects on the surface of the perovskite film without annealing.

To improve photovoltaic performance of C–PSCs, a layer of PEG is added between perovskite layer and carbon electrode, with the aim of passivating the trap states of the room-temperature processing perovskite layer as well as promoting the hole transfer at this interface   $  [17,35,38]  $  . A series of PEG solution with different concentrations (0.1 mg/mL, 1 mg/mL and 10 mg/mL) were spin-coated on the perovskite layer to update the interface between perovskite and carbon electrode. Obviously, the PL peak intensity of perovskite/PEG films grows with the increasing concentration of PEG (Fig. 3a). Note that the intensity of the film with 10 mg/mL PEG solution capping is over ten times higher than the reference sample (without PEG). As we know, PEG is hydrophobic, and may prevent perovskite films from water erosion. However, according to previous reports [31,40], a hydrophobic film on the hydrophilic perovskite film easily leads to a thick film (polymer/perovskite) with pinholes due to unfavorable interface interaction. Thus, a uniform coverage of PEG layer on the perovskite is prerequisite to exert its potential water resistance effect. The surface and section SEM as well as AFM images of the perovskite/PEG films are illustrated in Fig. 4. An obvious change can be found in the surface of the perovskite film in Fig. 4a–h. The average grain size of   $  \sim  $   210 nm and thickness of   $  \sim  $   370 nm are observed in the perovskite film without PEG. When the concentration increases from 0.1 mg/mL to 1.0 mg/mL, PEG gradually spreads over the entire film surface and exhibits a new 'fiber shape' interface (thickness   $  \sim  $   80 nm). However, when the concentration of PEG increases up to 10 mg/mL, the PEG layer becomes very thick and the surface changes from 'fiber shape' to "peak shape" (Fig. 4d&h). Simultaneously, as shown in Fig. 4i-l, the RMS of perovskite/PEG films increases from 14.3 nm to 31.0 nm with the increasing thickness of PEG layer. As we all know, the flatter perovskite surface can contact with the upper carbon layer more tightly [41,42], and the rough perovskite/PEG film (RMS > 30 nm) might be harmful for the hole transport and lead to the decrease in efficiency of C-PSCs. Accordingly, the contact angle of the perovskite films with PEG gradually decreases from 82.7° to 61.5° (Fig. 5a), indicating that the hygroscopic polymer film (PEG) can absorb water molecules on the surface and prevent them from further eroding the perovskite layer. Note that 1 mg/mL PEG can result in a full coverage of PEG layer spreading on the perovskite film (Fig. 4c&g). Based on the above microstructure, spectra as well as photoelectrochemical analysis on the perovskite film and C-PSC devices, PEG passivation on the perovskite film surface can effectively remedy the crystal defects of the perovskite crystallized at room-temperature. The positive effect of PEG on the room-temperature perovskite film is schematically illustrated in Fig. 7. On one hand, PEG as a hygroscopic polymer film can protect the perovskite film from water molecule attacking and improve the air-stability of the film. On the other hand, the formation of a coordinate bond between PEG and the uncoordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  of the perovskite reduces the defect state density of the perovskite film.

The aging tests of C-PSCs were performed to acquire the photo- and air-stability (Fig. 8d&e). The improved photo- and air-stability of C-PSC devices further confirm that the PEG passivation on perovskite films greatly enhances the performance of planar C-PSC devices again. With the full room temperature process and low-cost carbon electrode, the PSC devices can be easily fabricated on a flexible substrate, as illustrated in Fig. 8f. With the replacement of ITO glass with a flexible conductive substrate, we obtain a champion efficiency of C-PSCs over 10% (Table S2), which surpass the efficiency (around 6%) of other reported flexible C-PSCs with carbon/CuPc electrode   $  [17]  $  . Bending tests (200 cycles, Fig. S6) show that the performance of the flexible PEG-treated PSCs decreases significantly, retaining with only 35% of the original PCE. The obvious decrease may be due to the small cracks in the carbon electrode during the continuous bending of the carbon electrode. The future effort will be made on improving the carbon layer quality and the carbon/perovskite interface.

# 4. Conclusions

In summary, we firstly develop an facile room-temperature process to prepare high efficiency planar C–PSCs, in which PEG is adopted to passivate the perovskite surface, improve water resistance, reduce defect state density and promote facile hole extraction from perovskite layer to carbon electrode. The room-temperature processing planar C–PSCs show competitive photovoltaic performance with a PCE of 14.5% and excellent air stability, which is comparable to the planar C–PSCs with annealing process. Furthermore, such a facile all room-temperature process can also be extended to reliably prepare efficient flexible C–PSCs with PCE of over 10%. This work paves the way to develop low-cost, stable, flexible PSCs and other photovoltaic devices.
