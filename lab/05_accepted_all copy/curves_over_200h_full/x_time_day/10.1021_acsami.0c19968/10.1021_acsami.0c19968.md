# New Insight into the Lewis Basic Sites in Metal—Organic Framework-Doped Hole Transport Materials for Efficient and Stable Perovskite Solar Cells

Jiaqi Wang, Jian Zhang,  $  ^{*}  $   Yulin Yang,  $  ^{*}  $   Shuang Gai, Yayu Dong, Lele Qiu, Debin Xia, Xiao Fan, Wei Wang, Boyuan Hu, Wei Cao, and Ruiqing Fan  $  ^{*}  $

ABSTRACT: Currently, Spiro-OMeTAD is the most widely used hole transport material (HTM) in the best-performing perovskite solar cells (PSCs), resulting from its suitable energy level and facile processing. However, the intrinsic properties of organic molecules, such as low conductivity and a nonpolar contact interface, will limit the power conversion efficiency (PCE) and stability of Spiro-OMeTAD-based PSCs. Chemical doping could be an effective strategy to ameliorate the performance of Spiro-OMeTAD, and most of the dopants are designed for controllably oxidizing Spiro-OMeTAD. In this work, a highly stable metal–organic framework   $  \{[Zn(Hcbob)]\cdot(\text{solvent})\}_{n}  $   (Zn-CBOB) with rod topology and Lewis basic sites is assembled and employed as a dopant for the hole transport layer. It is found that Zn-CBOB not only controllably oxidizes Spiro-OMeTAD and improves the conductivity of the HTM but also passivates the surface traps of the perovskite film by coordinating with   $  Pb^{2+}  $  . The Zn-CBOB-doped PSCs achieved a remarkable PCE of 20.64%. In addition, the

# INTRODUCTION

In the last few years, perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted much attention from the material research community due to their impressive efficiency, low manufacturing cost, and intrinsic optoelectronic attributes.  $  ^{1-4}  $   Recently, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs has significantly increased from 3.8 to 25.5%, showing a good commercial prospect.  $  ^{5,6}  $   Commonly, a perovskite layer is sandwiched between an electron transport layer (ETL) and a hole transport layer (HTL). The perovskite layer can absorb photons and generate free electron–hole pairs, then photogenerated electrons and holes are transferred to ETL and HTL, respectively, and create a photocurrent. The HTL plays a crucial role in planar PSCs since it can enhance carrier extraction efficiency and reduce interface charge recombination by optimizing hole transport materials (HTMs).  $  ^{7-10}  $   Nowadays, Spiro-OMeTAD is still the most widely used HTM in PSCs because of its excellent solution processability and matched energy levels. However, the conductivity of Spiro-OMeTAD is confined by the nonpolar and amorphous state of the molecule itself.  $  ^{11,12}  $

Until now, some functional MOFs have been successfully applied to PSCs for improving their performance and stability.  $  ^{27-30}  $   Our previous works also presented an efficient strategy by designing novel MOF materials and utilizing them as dopants to improve the HTL performance. Two copper-based metal–organic complexes (Cu–2Cl and Cu–4Cl) were assembled and employed as efficient p-type HTL dopants.  $  ^{31}  $   Cu–2Cl not only controllably oxidize SpiroOMeTAD (major ingredient of HTMs) via its Lewis acid sites but also passivate the perovskite surface through interactions with uncoordinated lead ions. However, most of reported MOF materials suffer the limited choice of metal centers, weak stability, and rare functionalized ligands.

Here, we selected a ligand (1,3-bis((4'-carboxybenzyl)oxy)-benzoic acid) containing activity methoxyl groups and zinc ions to build a three-dimensional (3D) MOFs   $  \{[Zn(Hcbob)]\cdot(\text{solvent})\}_{n}  $   (abbreviated as Zn-CBOB) under a mild condition. As-prepared Zn-CBOB is composed of Hcbob  $  ^{2-}  $   ligands and the rod-like secondary building units (SBUs) via 3,5-connections and 3-connections, forming a robust rod topology. The initial Spiro-OMeTAD solution shows a light-yellow color which changed to reddish brown after Zn-CBOB was added, indicating that Zn-CBOB can promote the oxidation of Spiro-OMeTAD under inert environments. Controllable oxidation of Spiro-OMeTAD enhances conductivity of the hole transport function layer. In addition, Zn-CBOB provides a protective layer on the top of the perovskite layer, which reduces direct contact between the perovskite and moisture. Consequently, owing to effective inhibition of charge recombination at the perovskite/HTM interface by interacting the Lewis base with uncoordinated Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  , the PCE of the device increases from 19.14 to 20.64% after the introduction of Zn-CBOB into the HTL. More importantly, the Zn-CBOB-doped device retains beyond 90% of the initial PCE after being stored in the ambient environment (50% RH, 25 °C) for 30 days, exhibiting a promising commercial application prospect.

# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Figure 1 presents the schematic diagram of Zn-CBOB synthesis and preparation of Zn-CBOB-doped PSCs. The Zn-CBOB was synthesized based on our previous work with minor modifications.  $  ^{32}  $   Figure S1a shows that Zn-CBOB has a larger 3D conjugate network structure, which is composed of Hcbob  $  ^{2-}  $   ligands and the rod-like SBUs via 3,5-connections and 3-connections. Therefore, the Zn-CBOB can be simplified

to a rod topology (Figure S1b) and contain two kinds of pores (19.6 Å × 8.3 Å and 18.5 Å × 11.9 Å, respectively) in the structure. Specially, the van der Waals force between oxygen atom and hydrogen atom and π-π interaction force establish a strong conjugated system and facilitate charge transport in the framework.  $  ^{33}  $   In addition, many isolated carbonyl groups (C=O) with Lewis base nature in the structure make Zn-CBOB a good electron donor and may interact with uncoordinated Pb  $  ^{2+}  $  , which could also be confirmed by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Figure S2).  $  ^{34}  $   The stretching vibration mode of C=O is at 1665 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   and shifts to 1657 cm  $  ^{-1}  $   after coordination with PbI  $  _{2}  $  . The intensity of other peaks is almost unchanged, while the intensity of the C=O peak is significantly reduced, which demonstrates a strong interaction between PbI  $  _{2}  $   and C=O in Zn-CBOB. The particle size and morphology of as-synthesized Zn-CBOB were investigated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Figure S1c). The as-synthesized Zn-CBOB shows a rod-like shape with the particle size dimensions of 400–800 nm. Before the device fabrication, the collected Zn-CBOB has been first crushed into small pieces by an ultrasonic cell disruptor and dispersed into chlorobenzene by ultrasonic waves as a pretreatment process and the particle sizes are average 100–200 nm, which has been confirmed by dynamic light scattering (Figure S3). The unique topology aroused our interest in the study of structural stability of Zn-CBOB. The powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of Zn-CBOB was detected after being soaked in water for 24 h at room temperature. As shown in Figure S1d, most of the main peaks were maintained after treatment with water, indicating the robust framework of as-synthesized products. Therefore, the excellent water stability of Zn-CBOB is beneficial to the application of device assembly and supposed to a valid dopant in the HTL.

The value of   $  (\alpha h\nu)^{2}  $   as a function of photoenergy of Zn-CBOB is shown in Figure S4, and the band gap value of Zn-CBOB is 3.52 eV. In order to further evaluate the band alignment of Zn-CBOB with interface in the devices, the ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) spectra of Zn-CBOB were obtained. The cut-off binding energy (  $  E_{cut-off}  $  ) in the UPS spectra is shown in Figure 2a. According to the above results, the highest occupied molecular orbitals (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbitals (LUMO) of Zn-CBOB are -5.25 and -1.73 eV, respectively. It is worth noting that Zn-CBOB possesses a lower HOMO energy level than Spiro-OMeTAD (-5.12 eV), which indicates that it has the potential to oxidize Spiro-OMeTAD. Furthermore, Zn-CBOB is more matched with the valence band of perovskite, which is conducive to the transfer of photogenerated holes from the perovskite film with a small energy loss. We speculate that the decreased photovoltaic parameters would be ascribed to the heterogeneous film morphology and the resulting defect states. Consequently, the best PCE was obtained with a doping concentration of 0.6 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $ .

# CONCLUSIONS

In summary, the topological structure and Lewis base sites were considered to design an efficient MOF-based dopant to improve the performance of PSCs. The as-prepared Zn-CBOB shows excellent water stability due to its unique rod topology. Benefitting from the conjugate framework and Lewis base electron acceptors, the Zn-CBOB could promote the oxidation of Spiro-OMeTAD and realize the increased hole mobility and conductivity, which is confirmed by the UV–vis absorption and J-V measurements of the hole-only devices. The Zn-CBOB can passivate the undercoordinated defects (  $  Pb^{2+}  $  ) in the perovskite layer through carbonyl groups (C=O) to improve carrier extraction and suppress charge recombination. Therefore, the optimal efficiency of the PSCs increases from 19.14 to 20.64% after MOF material being doped. Additionally, the hydrophobic Zn-CBOB protects the perovskite film from moisture. The long-term stability of the Zn-CBOB-doped device significantly enhanced, retaining beyond 90% of the initial PCE after 30 days of storage (50% RH, 25 °C). This

# EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

Materials. All solvents and reagents were commercially obtained. Spiro-OMeTAD,   $  PbBr_{2}  $  , and   $  PbI_{2}  $   were purchased from TCI. Solvents were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Other chemicals were obtained from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp. All materials were used without further purification.

Construction of   $  \{[Zn(Hcbob)]\cdot(Solvent)\}_{n}  $   (Zn-CBOB). Synthetic approach of Zn-CBOB was performed by adjusting our previous work. Briefly,   $  \mathrm{Zn(OAc)_{2}\cdot2H_{2}O}  $   and   $  H_{3}bcbob  $   (1,3-bis((4'-carboxybenzyl)oxy)benzoic acid) (molar ratio: 2:1) were dissolved in a mixed solvent of DMA,   $  H_{2}O  $  , and methyl alcohol. Then, the mixed solution was transferred to a 20 mL reaction vial and heated in an oven at 85 °C for 1 day. The colorless crystals of Zn-CBOB were collected, washed with ethanol, and then dried in air. Before the device fabrication, the collected Zn-CBOB has been first crushed into small pieces by an ultrasonic cell disruptor and dispersed into chlorobenzene by ultrasonic waves as a pretreatment process and the particle sizes are average 100–200 nm, which has been confirmed by dynamic light scattering.

Fabrication of n-i-p PSCs. FTO glasses were pre-etched by zinc powder and 3 M hydrochloric acid and cleaned with detergent, deionized water, acetone, and ethanol. The glass was cleaned via ozone for 20 min. To fabricate a compact TiO $ _{2} $  (c-TiO $ _{2} $ ) film, a mixture of titanium(IV) isopropanol and 3 M hydrochloric acid in isopropanol was spin-coated on the FTO substrate with 3000 rpm for 30 s. The FTO/c-TiO $ _{2} $  substrates were heated at 500 °C for 30 min. The perovskite precursor solution was prepared by mixing formamidinium iodide (FAI) (1 M), MABr (0.2 M), PbBr $ _{2} $  (0.2 M), and PbI $ _{2} $  (1.1 M) in 1 mL of solvent of dimethylformamide and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (4:1, v/v). Then, a CsI (1.5 M in DMSO) solution was mixed to the above solution. The perovskite solution was deposited on the c-TiO $ _{2} $  using a two-step spin coating procedure set as 1000 rpm for 10 s and 6000 rpm for 22 s. Chlorobenzene (200 μL) was dripped on the substrate in the last 5 s of spin coating. The perovskite film was annealed at 100 °C for 1 h. Spiro-OMeTAD (72.3 mg), TBP (29 μL), and Li-TFSI (17.5 μL, 520 mg mL $ ^{-1} $  in acetonitrile) were dissolved into chlorobenzene (1 mL). Pretreated Zn-CBOB was dispersed into chlorobenzene to prepare HTM/Zn-CBOB solution with different concentrations. The solution was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 20 s. Au (80 nm) was deposited via evaporation (the perovskite precursor solution, the HTM solution, and cells were prepared in the glovebox).

Device Characterization. XRD patterns were obtained by Shimadzu XRD-6000 with Cu Kα radiation. FTIR spectra were obtained on a PerkinElmer Spectrum 100 FTIR spectrometer, and the sample was prepared into the KBr disc. SEM images were produced by Hitachi SU 8000HSD. The UV–vis absorption spectra were characterized by a Japan Shimadzu model UV-2250 spectrophotometer. The zeta potential measurements and dynamic light scattering were performed using a Zetasizer Nano-ZS90 (Malvern Instruments Ltd., U.K.). XPS was taken by a photoelectron spectrometer (ESCALAB 250Xi). The water contact angle measurements of HTL with and without Zn-CBOB were obtained using DSA30. The PL spectra were conducted with FLS 920. The TPV and TPC decay measurements were carried out by a diode laser (Molex 180081-4320, without light bias) with a 4 ns pulse duration and a 10 Hz pulse frequency. The   $  V_{oc}  $   and   $  J_{sc}  $   were measured via a digital oscilloscope (Tektronix DPO 4104B). Photocurrent density–voltage characteristics and EIS were measured using CHI660D electrochemical work station AM 1.5G simulated illumination. IPCE spectra were collected from a system with a 1/4 monochromator and a 300 W xenon lamp. The atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements were carried out using a Bruker Dimension ICONPT with Co/Cr tips. CV was characterized by Gamry electrochemical workstation.
