# Octadecylamine-Functionalized Single-Walled Carbon Nanotubes for Facilitating the Formation of a Monolithic Perovskite Layer and Stable Solar Cells

Vincent Tiing Tiong, Ngoc Duy Pham, Teng Wang, Tianxiang Zhu, Xinluo Zhao, Yaohong Zhang, Qing Shen, John Bell, Linhua Hu, Songyuan Dai, and Hongxia Wang*

Organic-inorganic lead halide perovskites have shown great future for application in solar cells owing to their exceptional optical and electronic properties. To achieve high-performance perovskite solar cells, a perovskite light absorbing layer with large grains is desirable in order to minimize grain boundaries and recombination during the operation of the device. Herein, a simple yet efficient approach is developed to synthesize perovskite films consisting of monolithic-like grains with micrometer size through in situ deposition of octadecylamine functionalized single-walled carbon nanotubes (ODA-SWCNTs) onto the surface of the perovskite layer. The ODA-SWCNTs form a capping layer that controls the evaporation rate of organic solvents in the perovskite film during the postthermal treatment. This favorable morphology in turn dramatically enhances the short-circuit current density of the perovskite solar cells and almost completely eliminates the hysteresis. A maximum power conversion efficiency of 16.1% is achieved with an ODA-SWCNT incorporated planar solar cell using   $  \left(\mathrm{FA}_{0.83}\mathrm{MA}_{0.17}\right)_{0.95}\mathrm{Cs}_{0.05}\mathrm{Pb}\left(\mathrm{l}_{0.83}\mathrm{Br}_{0.17}\right)_{3}  $   as light absorber. Furthermore, the perovskite solar cells with ODA-SWCNT demonstrate extraordinary stability with performance retention of 80% after 45 d stability testing under high humidity (60–90%) environment. This work opens up a new avenue for morphology manipulation of perovskite films and enhances the device stability using carbon material.

However, due to the extremely fast crystallization process, the resultant perovskite films generally consist of small grains with broad size distribution in the range of 50–600 nm and numerous grain boundaries (GBs).  $  ^{[4a,b]}  $   GBs are well known as the enrichment regions of charge traps and defects that form recombination centers of charge carrier, thus harming the device performance.  $  ^{[5]}  $   In addition, the GBs are also the channel where small molecules such as   $  H_{2}O  $   and   $  O_{2}  $   penetrate to the bulk of the film, causing degradation. As a consequence, PSCs with large grains of perovskite film have been reported to have much better stability against degradation.  $  ^{[6]}  $   Thus, it is no surprise that a lot of efforts have been made to control the morphology of perovskite films to reduce GBs. The ideal morphology of a light absorber for solar cells is to have monolithic grain of film that has no voids and minimal GBs so that the charge carriers can transport smoothly without being trapped before being collected by electrical contact.

In addition, another issue in the area of PSC is the observation of hysteresis in the J-V plots of the solar cells. The large discrepancy in the maximum power output between the forward and reverse scans and the dependence on measurement conditions, such as the scan rate, and voltage window and preillumination conditioning make it challenging to determine the actual power conversion efficiency of the solar cells. Although the exact underlying reasons for the hysteresis are still an open question, it is generally accepted that unbalanced charge carrier separation and transport at perovskite/electron transport layer and perovskite/hole transport layer and ion migrations in the bulk and grains boundaries of perovskite films are mainly responsible for the J-V hysteresis.  $  ^{[7]}  $   Therefore, large grain sizes of perovskite film and balanced charge separation rate at the electron and hole transport layer interfaces are found to help reduce the hysteresis. It is very clear that good control of the morphology of perovskite film is vital to enhance the device performance, stability, and hysteresis reduction.

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs), in particular single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs), have drawn great attention owing to their excellent electrical, optical, and mechanical properties for application such as optoelectronic devices.  $  ^{[8]}  $   Previous reports have shown that CNTs can be effective electrodes and/or hole transport layers in perovskite solar cells owing to their capability in transporting charge carrier including both electron and hole in PSCs depending on the properties of CNTs.  $  ^{[9]}  $   Our previous work showed that integration of CNTs into poly(3-hexylthiophene) (P3HT) as hole transport layer (HTM) improved both crystallinity and morphology of P3HT, leading to enhanced efficiency of perovskite solar cells.  $  ^{[9a]}  $   Other researches have also demonstrated improved device performance when CNTs were used as an intermediate layer between perovskite and spiroOMeTAD to promote the charge transport.  $  ^{[9e,f,10]}  $   Furthermore, the hydrophobic CNT stands another merit due to its capability to overcome water vulnerability of the perovskite material by suppressing diffusion of water (moisture) into the perovskite layer that causes the degradation of PSCs.  $  ^{[9b]}  $

Nevertheless, the CNT's hydrophobic nature also stands a drawback in the application in PSC device owing to its repulsiveness to polar solvent. Therefore, surface functionalization

of CNTs is very critical to manipulate their surface wettability, ensuring uniform dispersion of CNTs in solvent that is compatible to the perovskite material without causing decomposition. Moreover, it is a big challenge to generate a compact layer of CNT through a spin-coating deposition method. Failure in such will lead to direct contact between perovskite layer and gold electrode resulting in accelerated degradation. To overcome the aforementioned shortcomings, majority of previous researches adopted a transfer-press approach to incorporate CNT films in the device where a thick layer of CNTs was premade and then transferred on top of the perovskite layer to avoid the negative influence of solvents used in CNTs precursor solution on the perovskite material. The transfer-press method has several drawbacks such as (1) there is no guaranteed intimate interfacial contact between the perovskite layer and the CNTs materials, (2) there is a risk of damaging the surface morphology of perovskite layer underneath as a result of the applied pressure, and (3) the high thickness of the premade CNTs film (several micrometer) may impede the charge transport process and affect the transparency of the device. Additionally, an insulating polymer such as poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) is normally embedded with CNT to avoid direct interaction of gold electrode with perovskite and increase the moisture protection capability.[9b,11] This inevitably increases the series resistance of the whole device due to the nonconductive nature of PMMA. As a result, the performance of the solar cells such as fill factor and current density could be affected negatively.

In this work, we demonstrate a simple yet efficient approach of in situ incorporation of an ultrathin layer of functionalized SWCNTs on top of the perovskite film in a planar structure solar cell. The surface of the SWCNTs was functionalized to improve its hydrophobicity while having a good dispersion in a solvent such as chlorobenzene (CB) that is compatible to the organic-inorganic lead halide perovskite material. By dripping the functionalized SWCNT in CB solution onto the surface of wet perovskite film during a spin-coating process, it is found that the grain sizes of perovskite were dramatically enhanced. As a result, the perovskite film consisting of micrometer-sized monolithic-like grains that vertically grew on the substrate was formed. Additionally, due to the improved hydrophobicity of functionalized SWCNTs, water infiltration was simultaneously prevented, leading to much reduced degradation of perovskite layer. Planar structured perovskite solar cells containing the functionalized hydrophobic SWCNTs exhibited improved efficiency with nearly no  $ J - V $  hysteresis and superior long-term stability even at high relative humidity (RH of  $ 60 - 90\% $  ) as compared to the device without SWCNTs.

Chemical treatments were also found to affect the line shape of the G-band modes in the Raman spectrum. The two most intensive G peaks are labeled as   $  G^{+}  $  for atomic displacements along the tube axis, and   $  G^{-}  $  for atomic displacements along the circumferential direction (right inset of Figure 1a). The transformation of broad   $  G^{-}  $  peaks for as-prepared SWCNTs to clear   $  G^{-}  $  peaks for ODA-SWCNTs shows the successful conversion of the metallic tubes (1/3) among the as-prepared SWCNT to semiconducting state (ODA-SWCNTs).  $  ^{[15]}  $   Furthermore, compared with as-prepared SWCNT, both components of the split G-band for ODA-SWCNT are upshifted by about   $  3\ cm^{-1}  $   (from 1589 to   $  1592\ cm^{-1}  $  ) (Figure 1a, inset). As shown in Figure 1b, the peak at around 1560–1580 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  observed in the spectrum of both samples is ascribed to the stretching mode of C=C bond of CNT.  $  ^{[17]}  $  The broadband at around 1088–1160 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  in as-prepared SWCNT corresponds to the C–O stretching modes and the peaks that form at around 1390–1440 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  are ascribed to C–O–H bending vibration modes, which all disappears in the ODA-SWCNTs.  $  ^{[17]}  $  The band doublet at 2850 and 2919 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  stands for C–H stretching vibration of the alkyl chain. The peaks become distinct and the intensity increases dramatically in ODA-SWCNTs. Meanwhile, a weak peak located at 1620 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  associated with the stretch mode of carboxylate groups (C=O) is observed with ODA-SWCNTs.  $  ^{[13]}  $  These all suggest the successful functionalization of SWCNTs with carboxyl group and ODA.

Wettability of the SWCNT surface is a critical parameter reflecting the hydrophobicity of the material. The SWCNT-coated films were prepared by spin coating  $ 1\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1} $  SWCNT solution on a microscope slide at a rotational speed of  $ 1000~\mathrm{rpm} $  for  $ 30~s $  before annealing at  $ 150^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 10\mathrm{min} $ . The annealing temperature of  $ 150^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  was chosen to match the sintering temperature used in the preparation of the perovskite films. Figure 3a shows that the contact angle (CA) of the as-prepared SWCNTs is  $ 51.8^{\circ} \pm 1^{\circ} $  which suggests that the SWCNTs are slight hydrophilic. After functionalization, the CA is increased to  $ 108.5^{\circ} \pm 1^{\circ} $ , suggesting the transformation of surface properties of the SWCNTs to strong hydrophobicity (Figure 3b). This is in agreement with previous findings by Yao et al. who reported freestanding ODA functionalized multiwalled carbon nanotubes that demonstrated superhydrophobic properties with  $ \mathrm{CA} = 165^{\circ} $ . They ascribed the surface transformation of

carbon nanotubes to superhydrophobicity to the changes in surface free energy of the CNTs against adsorption of water droplet.  $  ^{[23]}  $   When SWCNTs are functionalized with ODA, the octadecyl groups are exposed instead of the high surface free energy of SWCNTs. This transforms the film to a state of lower surface energy that is hydrophobic against water. Compared to Yao et al., the relatively lower CA in our case should be due to the lower concentration of ODA ligands attached on the SWCNT and a lower surface coverage of ODA-SWCNTs on the glass substrate.

To better understand whether the SWCNTs were embedded in the bulk of the perovskite layer, the cross-sectional HIM images of the cells with the perovskite layers treated with different concentrations of SWCNT solutions (0.1–0.5 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) were measured (Figure 4e–h). The SWCNT integrated films have an extra 5–10 nm film thickness due to the SWCNT layer that is sandwiched between the perovskite and spiro-OMeTAD layers. The microstructural cross-sectional feature of grains in the perovskite film differs remarkably although they have similar thicknesses. The CNT-0 perovskite layer (Figure 4e) consists of relatively small grains with random orientation, revealing its nontextured nature. With the incorporation of 0.1 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  of SWCNTs into the perovskite layer (CNT-0.1), larger grains that are vertically oriented to the substrate and span the whole film thickness are observed (Figure 4f). As the

concentration of the SWCNTs solution further increased to 0.25 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  and above (Figure 4g–h), a compact monolithic perovskite layer is formed and the grains with height comparable to film thickness are uniformly aligned along the direction perpendicular to the FTO/c-SnO  $  _{2}  $  substrate. This leads to a flat, seamless interface contact with the hole transport material. It suggests that the grain growth is mainly triggered by the ODA-SWCNTs. It is worth to note that no SWCNTs are observed in the bulk perovskite layer, suggesting that the SWCNTs only exist on the surface of perovskite layer. The observed phenomenon of morphological transformation from small grains to a monolithic perovskite film with the incorporation of SWCNTs has never been reported before. This could be due to the different method used. Compared to the commonly used transferpress method of introduction of premade CNT film onto preformed perovskite films,  $  ^{[9f,10]}  $  the in situ deposition of CNTs to the perovskite film in this work proves that CNTs can promote the grain growth of perovskite material.

We believe that the significant morphology transformation of the perovskite layer is largely attributed to the effect of the functional groups attached on the surface of SWCNTs. The oxygen (O) atom in the carbonyl (C=O) functional group on the surface of SWCNTs has strong affinity toward   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ions. This promotes the adsorption of   $  Pb^{2+}  $   in the precursor solution onto the surface of the SWCNTs, leading to the formation and growth of perovskite on the SWCNTs.  $  ^{[26]}  $   Meanwhile, the long hydrocarbon chain of ODA functional group makes the surface of the SWCNTs hydrophobic, which is repulsive to polar solvent such as dimethyl formamide (DMF) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Figure S3, Supporting Information). During the deposition and following annealing process, the closely entangled SWCNT network plays a role of surface capping layer that restricts the evaporation rate of polar solvents including DMF and DMSO used in the perovskite precursor solution. To gain insight into the effect of SWCNTs on the crystal structure of the perovskite material, X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the perovskite films with and without SWCNTs were collected (Figure 6). The effect of SWCNTs loading on the light absorption properties of the perovskite films was investigated by UV–vis spectroscopy (Figure 7a). The spectra display little changes in the onset light absorbing wavelength corresponding to the bandgap of 1.63 eV of the perovskite material. Nevertheless, it is noticed that the intensity of light absorption of the perovskite layers with the SWCNTs is slightly higher and the onset absorption spectrum redshifts. This is attributed to the light scattering of the SWCNT layer on the surface of perovskite films, which leads to the enhanced light absorption over the whole spectral wavelength.

By contacting with a layer of spiro-OMeTAD, the PL intensities of all the perovskite films were strongly suppressed due to the charge transfer (Figure 7c). # 3. Conclusion

In summary, we present a facile yet efficient method to make organic-inorganic lead halide perovskite film with monolithic micrometer grains induced by in situ incorporation of SWCNTs. The efficiency of planar SWCNT-incorporated PSCs based on   $  FA_{0.85}Cs_{0.15}Pb(I_{0.85}Br_{0.15})_{3}  $   was improved by nearly 13% with the best perovskite film treated with 0.25 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   SWCNTs. Moreover, the notorious J-V hysteresis was alleviated significantly in the SWCNTs containing PSCs. Furthermore, the supreme features of SWCNTs are transformable to other types of PSCs whereby the triple cation-based SWCNT integrated PSCs   $  ((FA_{0.83}MA_{0.17})_{0.95}Cs_{0.05}Pb(I_{0.83}Br_{0.17})_{3})  $   demonstrated a PCE of 16.1% and more than six times lower HIF than the reference cell without SWCNTs. More importantly, the SWCNT-based PSCs exhibited robust resistance to moisture and possible ion migration, maintaining 81% of its initial PCE after storing for 45 d in an ambient environment with relative humidity of 60–90%. Such desired performance features are mainly attributed to the much reduced recombination and substantially improved carries transport owing to monolithic-like grains and surface smoothness in the SWCNT based PSCs. This work demonstrates the benefit of using this method to extensively improve the morphology and optoelectronic properties of perovskite films for high-energy conversion efficiency.

# 4. Experimental Section

SWCNT Functionalization: As-prepared SWCNTs were prepared by an arc plasma jet method, whereby the carbon anode containing an Ni (4 at%)–Y (1 at%) catalyst was evaporated through a DC arc discharge of 60 A in helium gas at 500 Torr.  $  ^{[44]}  $   This process yields a mixture of semiconducting (2/3) and metallic (1/3) SWCNTs. The SWCNTs were treated by refluxing the as-prepared nanotubes using concentrated nitride acid (HNO  $  _{3}  $  , 65%) for 6 h at 100 °C to remove the catalyst particles in the SWCNTs. The reaction mixture was then diluted with Milli-Q water and kept overnight for precipitation. The supernatant was decanted, and the suspension was washed with Milli-Q water (three times) and ethanol (once) to remove excess acid through centrifugation. The suspension was redispersed in water and frozen in a refrigerator overnight, and then dried in a vacuum refrigerant dryer at -50 °C and 0.1 mbar for 24 h to form carboxylic-SWCNTs (C-SWCNTs). After this, the C-SWCNTs (10 mg) and octadecylamine (ODA, 100 mg) were mixed and dissolved in 1,2-dichlorobenzene (5 mL) under vigorous ultrasonication at room temperature until uniform dispersion was formed. The dispersion was then mixed with tetrahydrofuran (THF), and the resultant solution was sonicated and centrifuged at 12 500 rpm for 10 min to separate the sediment from the supernatant. The functionalized SWCNT was washed using ethanol and THF before being dried at 60 °C in vacuum for 8 h. The functionalized SWCNT is named as ODA-SWCNT in the following. ODA-SWCNT solution with controlled amount was then dispersed in chlorobenzene. The functionalization procedure of the SWCNTS is shown in Scheme 1.

Materials Preparation: All materials were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used as received without further purification unless otherwise stated. Two types of perovskite films were prepared in this work to test the effect of functionalized SWCNTs (ODA-SWCNTs). The first was   $  FA_{0.85}Cs_{0.15}Pb(I_{0.85}Br_{0.15})_{3}  $   and the second was   $  (FA_{0.83}MA_{0.17})_{0.95}Cs_{0.05}Pb(I_{0.83}Br_{0.17})_{3}  $  . The precursor for   $  FA_{0.85}Cs_{0.15}Pb(I_{0.85}Br_{0.15})_{3}  $   perovskite was prepared in an argon-filled glovebox by dissolving 175 mg of formamidinium iodide (FAI) (Dyesol), 38 mg of cesium bromide (CsBr), 494 mg of lead(II) iodide (PbI  $  _{2}  $  ), and 66 mg of lead(II) bromide (PbBr  $  _{2}  $  ) in 1 mL of anhydrous dimethyl formamide:dimethyl sulfoxide (4:1 (v:v)) at room temperature under rigorous stirring for overnight. The prepared perovskite solution was filtered by using a syringe filter (pore size: 0.22   $  \mu  $  m) prior to film deposition. The precursor solution for   $  (FA_{0.83}MA_{0.17})_{0.95}Cs_{0.05}Pb(I_{0.83}Br_{0.17})_{3}  $   perovskite was prepared by adding 15.6 mg of cesium iodide in 156.7 FAI, 19.1 mg methylammonium bromide (Dyesol), 470.3 mg of PbI  $  _{2}  $  , and 66 mg of PbBr  $  _{2}  $   to 1 mL of DMF:DMSO (4:1 (v:v)). A solution for the hole transporting material was prepared by addition of 72.3 mg of 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis-(N,N-di-4-methoxyphenylamino)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (spiro-OMeTAD, Borun New Material), 28.8   $  \mu  $  L of 4-tert-butylpyridine, and 17.5   $  \mu  $  L of bis(trifluoromethane) sulfonimide lithium solution (52 mg of LiTFSI in acetonitrile) into 1 mL of chlorobenzene. SWCNT solutions with concentrations of 0.1, 0.25, and 0.5 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   were prepared by dispersing ODA-SWCNT in chlorobenzene.

Device Fabrication: A substrate based on Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)-coated glass (Nippon Electric Glass, 15 Ω square  $  ^{-1}  $  ) was first patterned through partial etching FTO using 35.5 wt% hydrochloric acid (HCl) and zinc powder. The substrates were then cleaned by sequential ultrasonication in 5% Decon-90 detergent, before a mixture of acetone, isopropanol, and ethanol for 15 min being blow-dried with nitrogen gas. Prior to use, the substrates were treated with ultraviolet–ozone (UV–ozone) for 30 min to fully remove organic solvent residuals. The tin dioxide (SnO  $  _{2}  $  ) electron transporting layer was deposited on the FTO-coated glass in air by spin coating a 1 M solution of SnCl  $  _{2}  $   in ethanol at 3000 rpm for 30 s. The film was then dried at 100 °C for 5 min and annealed at 185 °C for 1 h on a hotplate. The perovskite solution was spin coated on the SnO  $  _{2}  $   substrate in a two-step program: first at 2000 rpm for 10 s and then at 4000 rpm for 20 s. Characterization: The top-view and cross-sectional images of the samples were captured using a helium ion microscope (HIM, ZEISS Orion NanoFab). The cross-sectional elemental mapping of the samples was characterized by a field emission scanning electron microscope (JEOL 7001F) at an acceleration voltage of  $ 20.0\mathrm{kV} $  combined with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy. A Raman spectrometer (Renishaw) with a laser excitation wavelength of  $ 532~\mathrm{nm} $  was used to record Raman spectra of the SWCNTs at room temperature. Incident photon-to-electron conversion efficiency measurement was conducted by using a quantum efficiency system (IQE 200B, Newport) in alternative current (AC) mode. The photoluminescence spectrum was measured with a fluorescence spectrometer (Edinburgh Instruments Ltd) at room temperature. The film was photoexcited by using a laser  $ (474~\mathrm{nm}) $  with a pulse wavelength of  $ 82.4~\mathrm{ps} $ . A femtosecond transient absorption (TA) setup was used to study the photoexcited carrier dynamics of the perovskite film. The laser source was generated by a titanium/sapphire laser (CPA-2010, Clark-MXR Inc.) with a wavelength of  $ 775~\mathrm{nm} $ , a pulse width of  $ 150~\mathrm{fs} $ , and a repetition rate of  $ 1\mathrm{kHz} $ . The output of the laser was then split into two parts. One part was incident on a sapphire plate to generate white light for the probe beam. The other part was employed to pump an optical parametric amplifier (a TOAPS from Quantronix) to generate light

pulses which was used as the pump light to excite the sample. In this study, all the samples were measured using the pump light wavelength of 470 nm with a pump fluence of 2.5   $  \mu  $  J cm  $  ^{-2}  $   at room temperature. The time-resolved TA spectra ranging from 570 to 760 nm were obtained with a temporal resolution of 100 fs. Scanning probe microscope (Spectrum Instruments) was performed on the prepared perovskite films under ambient conditions. Impedance spectroscopy of the PSCs was performed in a frequency range from 1 MHz to 10 mHz using an electrochemical workstation (BioLogic) at an applied bias equivalent to the open-circuit voltage of the cell under 1 sun illumination. An AC voltage with perturbation amplitude of 20 mV was applied in the IS measurement. The open-circuit voltage decay measurements were performed by recording the open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{oc}  $  ) of PSCs as a function of time in dark.  $  ^{[45]}  $   The   $  V_{oc}  $   of the cell was generated by a white light-emitting diode that illuminated the device with an intensity equivalent to 0.15 sun for 2 s. The OCVD was recorded when the LED was turned off. The stability of the PSCs was tested by monitoring the efficiency of the PSCs which were stored at ambient environment (relative humidity 60–90%) without encapsulation. The performance of the devices was measured every third day under ambient condition.
