# Abstract

In this paper, the effects of humidity, annealing temperature and deposition method including one step method, sequential deposition rout and hot casting technique are investigated on the morphology of perovskite layer. The optimal conditions for deposition of perovskite layers are introduced. Formation and growth of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   perovskite crystals by these procedures leads to completely different crystal shapes and sizes and as a result different cell efficiency and environmental stability. Humidity and annealing temperature have great effect on the morphology of perovskite layer in one step method. It was found that in one step method by increasing the humidity from less than 10% to ~40% (during spin coating and annealing the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   perovskite) morphology of the spin coated layer evolves from mostly ribbon shape with a large number of voids to layers comprising hollow fibers. However increase of the annealing temperature from 100 to 140 °C results in decreasing the diameter of the formed hollow fibers. In hot casting technique, by controlling the casting temperature, a very compact flat layer including lotus leaf-like morph with fewer pinholes can be created. Cells fabricated using   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   cuboid crystals grown by sequential deposition rout showed the highest photocurrent and power conversion efficiency (4.8%). However deposited layers via sequential deposition rout and then the cells fabricated using them are not chemically as stable as those prepared by one step method for long time performance due to humidity induced degradation of large crystal of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   to smaller size.

Keywords: Crystal growth; Perovskite solar cell; Photovoltaic performance; Spin coating; Morphology evolution.

# Introduction

In recent years organic-inorganic hybrid perovskites which have been previously used in field-effect transistors and light emitting diodes [1,2], have found many uses in conversion of photons energy to the electricity [3-9]. Low cost and appropriate energy gap perovskite hybrid materials, especially  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbX}_3 $  (X=Cl, Br, I), have attracted a lot of attention in

the field of third-generation solar cells [10-13]. They have suitable optical and electrical properties, such as high charge carriers, mobility and diffusion length which play a vital role for fabrication of high efficiency solar cells [14-16]. In contrast with thin film solar cells, in which layer deposition is carried out by thermal evaporation in clean rooms, third-generation solar cells have a cheaper manufacturing process and comparable efficiency [17].

A lot of work has been done to improve the morphology of the sensitizing layer and then the efficiency of the perovskite solar cell [18-20]. High uniformity and surface coverage of the perovskite layer are important factors that strongly influence the efficiency of the solar cells [21]. Surface coverage and morphology of perovskite layer are extremely affected by the deposition conditions. Several methods have been introduced for thin layer of organo-lead perovskite deposition in literature [22-24]. In one step method (OSM), solution of pre-synthesized perovskite precursor is spin coated on substrate followed by annealing of the crystal grains at elevated temperatures. In sequential deposition rout (SDR)  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  is deposited by spin coating of the solution followed by immersion of the deposited layer in methyl ammonium halide for synthesis and growth of perovskite crystals. Nie et al [25] created pinhole free and thin layers of iodochloride by hot casting technique (HCT) with mm domain sizes. By this way they not only simplified the steps, but also achieved high quality layer with high mobility and low bulk defects. In HCT perovskite precursor solution is spin coated on the pre-heated substrate so the formation and growth of perovskite crystals occur at the simultaneous presence of centrifugal force rising from spinning and thermal energy of hot substrate.

Meso-porous titanium oxide layer, which is the most used substrate for perovskite deposition, promotes the rapid growth of perovskite crystals within its porous structure and increases the efficiency of solar cells [26]. Despite removing the porous layer in planar solar cells, high efficiency is achieved for this type of cells [27, 28].

Thermal energy plays an important role in the growth of perovskite crystals in all the methods used to create the perovskite layer. Thermal treatment on perovskite layer is typically carried out to serve several purposes: (1) remove residual solvent from deposited layer; (2) assist perovskite formation from its precursors, and (3) enhance grain growth and crystallization. In OSM after deposition of perovskite by spin coating, the morphology of the perovskite layer can be changed and a layer with a high surface coverage and fewer pinholes can be created with changing the annealing temperature [29]. Gratzel et al [30] investigated the effect of annealing temperature on the morphology of the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_{3 - x}\mathrm{Cl}_x $ . They found that annealing temperature higher than  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  can lead to decomposition of the perovskite to

On the other hand they reported that incomplete conversion of deposited precursor solution is severely probable as annealing the perovskite layers is performed at temperatures lower than  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  even for long time in OSM [30]. Moreover, several researcher groups have performed annealing process at temperature higher than  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for various times in OSM to convert completely precursor solution without later decomposition [31,32]. Gao et al [33] investigated the effect of humidity during annealing stage on the morphology and surface coverage of the  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_{3 - x}\mathrm{Cl}_x $  and  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  films deposited by OSM. SDR has been adopted as a suitable technique for deposition of perovskite crystals followed by annealing the perovskite layer at temperature range of  $ 70 - 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  by several groups [34-37]. Recently Wei et al [37] studied the solar cells made of perovskite layers deposited by SDR followed by annealing at  $ 85^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ . They showed that the morphology of the perovskite film is strongly affected by the immersion time in the second step of sequential method [37]. The longer the immersion time of the spin-coated  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  layer in MAI solution, the more cuboid shape crystals with grater size grow [37]. Zheng et al [21] studied the effect of casting temperature and rotation speed on the morphology of the perovskite layer deposited by HCT on the compact blocking  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  layer for fabrication of planar photovoltaic cells. It was recognized that the substrate temperature has extremely stronger effect than centrifugal force on film morphology. Very compact island shape large grains comprising large crystals of perovskite grow on compact  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  thin layer at casting temperature higher than  $ 150^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  [21].

Therefore from above mentioned examples and many other articles in literature it is found that the perovskite layer can be deposited by completely different morphologies via different solution deposition routes. Although some influencing parameters have been studied in these methods, there are important parameters that play decisive role in determining the morphology and then the efficiency and stability of fabricated solar cell that have not been studied systematically for growth of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  perovskite crystals. In the present work perovskite films are grown on two different substrates (meso-porous  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  and  $ \mathrm{Al_2O_3} $ ) by one step and hot casting routes. Effects of annealing temperature, humidity and speed of spin coating are systematically investigated on the morphology of perovskite layer and then stability and efficiency of the fabricated cells. # Experimental Section

# Synthesis of  $ CH_{3}NH_{3}I $

Methylamine iodide (CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ I) was synthesized and purified on the basis of a reported method in literature [38]. 30 ml methylamine (33%wt in ethanol, Sigma-Aldrich) and 28 ml hydroiodic acid (57% in water, Sigma-Aldrich) were mixed to react at 0.0 °C in a 250 ml round-bottomed flask placed in an ice bath for 2 h while stirring. The solvent was then removed with a rotary evaporator by heating the solution at 60 °C under reduced pressure, and the precipitate was then crystallized. The product was washed with diethyl ether three times, and then dissolved in ethanol, recrystallized using diethyl ether, and finally dried in a vacuum at 60 °C for 24 h to yield CH $ _3 $ NH $ _3 $ I.

# Preparation of   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $   Precursor Solution

1.35 mmol   $  PbI_{2}  $   was added into the solution containing 100   $  \mu  $  l of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and 1.0 ml of DMF. This solution was heated at 65 °C for 24 h to dissolve   $  PbI_{2}  $ , and then cooled down to room temperature. Subsequently, the   $  PbI_{2}  $   solution was poured into a vial containing 1.35 mmol of MAI to obtain the final solution for the precursor of perovskite. The solution was then used for deposition of perovskite layer by one-step spin coating method.

The precursor solution containing 15 wt%  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  used for HCT was prepared as follows:  $ 0.1120\mathrm{g} $   $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and  $ 0.0380\mathrm{g} $  MAI powders were added into  $ 1.0\mathrm{ml} $  DMF/DMSO (9:1 volume ratio) in a glass vial, the cap closed tightly and heated at  $ 70^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for  $ 10\mathrm{h} $  while the mixture was continuously stirred vigorously.

# Device Fabrication

After etching with zinc powder, FTO conducting glass substrates (with the sheet resistance of 14  $ \Omega/\mathrm{sq} $ ) were washed with soap, acetone, ethanol and deionized water for 15 min in each step in an ultrasonic bath followed by drying in air flow. For deposition of hole blocking layer (compact TiO $ _2 $ ) and fabricate photoanodes, a solution of titanium isopropoxide in ethanol and HCl (2.0 M) was spin-coated on the FTO glass substrate at 4000 rpm for 60 s and dried at 100 and 500  $ ^{\circ} $ C for 20 and 60 min respectively. Meso-porous TiO $ _2 $  layer (TiO $ _2 $  paste with 20 nm TiO $ _2 $  nano-particles in ethanol (1:5 in weight)) was deposited by spin coating at 4000 rpm for 30 s on compact TiO $ _2 $ /FTO substrates. Then, the photoanodes were

In HCT, the substrate was placed on a hot plate for 3 minutes at different temperatures (125, 140, 155 and 175 °C) then quickly put on the spin coating and 40   $  \mu  $  l of precursor solution was dropped on substrate and run the spin coating with 2500 and 3000 rpm speeds for 30 s (transferring, dropping and running the spin coating were done within 8 s). Finally, 55 nm Au was coated on the top of the perovskite layer by a DC sputtering system (deposited under a vacuum condition around   $  10^{-2}  $   torr). In another way we name it sequential deposition route (SDR), the solution containing 1.0 M   $  PbI_{2}  $   was spin coated at 3000 rpm for 30 s at ambient temperature and then dipped into a solution containing 50 mM MAI for 5 min followed by annealing at 70 and 85 °C for 15 min.

# Result and Discussion

Effects of humidity, annealing temperature and speed of spin coating on morphology evolution of perovskite layer

As it can be seen in Fig. 1, morphology of coated perovskite layer evolves from mostly ribbon shape fibers with a large number of voids prepared at less than 10% humidity to layers comprising hollow fibers prepared at ~40% ambient humidity. It is observed that crystalline grains have been attached side by side to each other to create ribbon or hollow fiber shapes of perovskite layers. Nano-grains (diameter less than 100 nm) are obviously observable in high magnification SEM pictures of all morphologies of perovskite layers (Fig. 1 insets). Ribbons and hollow fibers are uniform in diameter and length. The average width of the ribbons is about 1.3 μm and decreases to 1.0 μm as the annealing temperature increases from 100 °C to 110 °C (Fig. 1). With comparing the layers prepared at 100 or 110 °C in <10% or ~40% humidity, it is observed that with increasing the humidity the morphology of the perovskite layers goes to change from ribbon shape to hollow fibers. Change of ribbon-like fibers to hollow fibers due to the increase of humidity to 40% can be well explained by “rolling up” mechanism [40]. With increasing the humidity, more molecules of water diffuse into the perovskite fibers and dissolve DMF and DMSO molecules attached to the perovskite molecules or intercalated between perovskite layers and extract them from the deposited film. When the inserted DMF and DMSO molecules are extracted from grains, the   $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}-DMF  $   with interlayer vacant would roll into a hollow fiber film (Fig. 3). Extraction is carried out more rapidly and smaller diameter hollow fibers are formed especially at temperatures near the boiling point of the DMF (153°C).

In Volmer–Weber growth which is also called island growth, small clusters as the central core are formed on the substrate and grow as an island in three dimensions to cover the substrate. This happens when sum of the free energy of film-substrate interface and surface free energy of the film is less than surface free energy of the substrate. In the FVMG method, the crystal growth is two-dimensional and layer by layer. In the SKG, a central layer is formed before the formation of the three-dimensional islands.

In the HCT, as a result of an increase of temperature from  $ 140^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $ , the morphology of perovskite layer turns from the branch to lotus leaf-like island shapes. In fact, the crystalline growth of the VWG method starts from this temperature and branch-shaped crystals completely disappear.

As it was mentioned before, the perovskite film prepared by OSM at 140 °C is accompanied by degradation of perovskite and production of small amount of   $  PbI_{2}  $   impurity. However, very recently Jacobsson et al [43] indicated that the presence of an excess amount of   $  PbI_{2}  $   in perovskite samples has mutual effects on the cell performance. Although a small amount of   $  PbI_{2}  $   causes a drop in the photocurrent, it increases the   $  V_{oc}  $   to achieve a record as high as 1.20 V [43]. The drop in the photocurrent was attributed to the accumulation of organic species at grain boundaries, low charge carrier mobility, and decreased electron injection into the   $  TiO_{2}  $  . Jump in   $  V_{oc}  $   was correlated with high crystal quality, longer charge carrier lifetimes, and high photoluminescence yields [43]. They also found the ion migration to be obstructed by the presence of small excess of   $  PbI_{2}  $ , which decreased the JV hysteresis and increased the photostability [43].

Charge carrier mobility and electron diffusion length which play an important role in facile electron transfer to  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  substrate and enhancement of photocurrent are higher in large cuboid crystals of  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  [44,45]. This is while the FF and  $ \mathrm{V_{oc}} $  are relatively stable and  $ \mathrm{J_{sc}} $  and PCE both increase for devices assembled using perovskite layers deposited by OSM and HCT routes during 45 days time interval, monitoring the performance of the cells.

Significant decrease in the photocurrent and then PCE of the cells fabricated by SDR can be attributed to the distinctive cuboid perovskite morphology from those layers prepared by OSM or HCT. It is clearly observed that the perovskite layers deposited by OSM are more compact and layers spin coated via HCT are very smooth, compact and without pores and voids between crystalline grains (Fig. 1). In the case of perovskite layers deposited by SDR, the humidity and oxygen molecules deeply penetrate inside the perovskite matrix and react with  $ \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{NH}_3\mathrm{PbI}_3 $  molecules. The perovskite molecules can be decomposed to  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  or hydrated and then form smaller crystals and more grain boundaries upon prolong exposure to humidity and oxygen [46]. The more grain boundaries are formed, the more insulating organic molecules are accumulated on the boundaries leading to low charge carrier mobility, and decreased electron injection into the  $ \mathrm{TiO}_2 $ .

In this method, by increasing the temperature from 140 °C, perovskite layer gradually degrades and its compactness decreases. As a result, the performance of solar cell is also weakened.
