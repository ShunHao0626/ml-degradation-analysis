# Efficient and Stable Perovskite Solar Cells Enabled by Dicarboxylic Acid-Supported Perovskite Crystallization

Ahmed Esmail Shalan, Erdi Akman, Faranak Sadegh, and Seckin Akin*

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: Defect states at surfaces and grain boundaries as well as poor anchoring of perovskite grains hinder the charge transport ability by acting as nonradiative recombination centers, thus resulting in undesirable phenomena such as low efficiency, poor stability, and hysteresis in perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Herein, a linear dicarboxylic acid-based passivation molecule, namely, glutaric acid (GA), is introduced by a facile antisolvent additive engineering (AAE) strategy to concurrently improve the efficiency and long-term stability of the ensuing PSCs. Thanks to the two-sided carboxyl (-COOH) groups, the strong interactions between GA and under-coordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $  sites induce the crystal growth, improve the electronic properties, and minimize the charge recombination. Ultimately, champion-stabilized efficiency approaching  $ 22\% $  is achieved with negligible hysteresis for GA-assisted devices. In addition to the enhanced moisture stability of the devices, considerable operational stability is achieved after  $ 2400\mathrm{h} $  of aging under continuous illumination at maximum power point (MPP) tracking.

Among the emerging photovoltaic technologies, undoubtedly solid-state perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have received enormous attention in the recent history of photovoltaics.  $  ^{1-8}  $   The state-of-the-art PSCs employ organic–inorganic hybrid perovskite materials as light absorbers, which are not only utilized in solar cells  $  ^{9-11}  $   but also in other next-generation high-performance optoelectronic devices.  $  ^{12}  $   Recently, the lab-scale prototype perovskite cells achieved a certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 25.5% thanks to the outstanding optoelectronic properties of active materials including widely tunable band gap, long charge carrier diffusion length, low exciton binding energy, and high charge carrier mobility.  $  ^{11,13}  $   Despite the remarkable PCE, there is a lack of fundamental knowledge concerning the instability and degradation mechanisms that remains a major hurdle for the commercialization of perovskite photovoltaics.  $  ^{14-16}  $

In this context, the formation of high-quality perovskite film without defects and pinholes is a critical issue.  $  ^{[17-19]}  $   The solution-processed perovskite film results in the formation of defects mainly located at grain boundaries or on the surface of the perovskites, which leads to nonradiative recombination losses and thus diminishes carrier lifetime and device performance. Therewithal, such grain boundaries can act as the major channel of the ion migration and the pathway for moisture or the oxygen ingress. Besides this, poor anchoring of perovskite grains associated with grain boundaries hinders the charge transport ability, thus resulting in undesirable phenomena such as low efficiency, poor stability, and hysteresis. Although different approaches have been reported to obtain high-quality perovskite films by overcoming these issues, the facile antisolvent additive engineering (AAE)

Until now, few studies employing the AAE strategy have been reported with a limited number of passivation agents (Table S1). For example, an organic dye (AQ310) was employed as a passivation molecule to reduce the trap states of the perovskites and promote better stability. In addition to enhanced stability associated with reduced trap states, a high average efficiency of 19.43% was reported in the presence of AQ310 passivation.  $  ^{22}  $   Very recently, Na et al. introduced the 2-hydroxyethyl acrylate (HEA)-assisted AAE method for achieving perovskite film with larger grain size and effectively reduced defect sites.  $  ^{23}  $   This strategy demonstrated a significantly improved PCE (18.51–20.46%) accompanied with long-term stability, retaining over 61% of the initial PCE after 96 days under ambient conditions.

Inspiring these studies, we concluded that a well-designed passivation molecule should take the defect coordination and moisture stability into consideration contemporaneously. The top-view field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM) image of pristine perovskite film shows a conventional surface morphology with pinholes and unreacted parts represented by yellow circles as well as deep grain boundaries represented by red circles (Figure 2a). On the contrary, GA-assisted perovskite film demonstrates larger grains with tighter boundaries between the grains which is beneficial for high charge transfer and low recombination rate (Figure 2b). The corresponding grain size distribution histograms are presented in Figure S1 (Supplementary Note 1). To get insight into the roughness of pristine and AAE-assisted perovskite films, we also investigated the atomic force microscopy (AFM) images of the corresponding films (Figure 2c and 2d). AFM images indicate a reduced surface roughness from 28.5 to 17.7 nm in the presence of GA additive, which provides a smoother interface between the hole transporting layer (HTL) and the perovskite layer. The smoother surface can lead to a considerable improvement in the charge transport between adjacent layers.  $  ^{23}  $

The influence of GA modification on the crystal growth of the perovskite films was further examined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and optical measurements. Figure 2e denotes that both the pristine and GA-assisted perovskite films presented diffraction peaks. The XRD curves display neither shifts nor additional peaks and point out that GA molecules can be present on the surface and grain boundary of the perovskite film.  $  ^{27}  $   However, the intensity of diffraction peaks assigned to the (110) and (220) crystal planes in the AAE-assisted perovskite film significantly increased compared to those of the pristine film, indicating better crystallinity in the presence of

GA molecules.  $  ^{28}  $   The existence of an unreacted   $  PbI_{2}  $   peak in the pristine film is beneficial for the optoelectronic properties and initial efficiency in PSCs; however, it might affect the long-term photostability of ensuing devices.  $  ^{29}  $   On the other hand, GA addition suppresses the overcrystallization of   $  PbI_{2}  $ , which is usually associated with nonuniform film coverage and pinhole formation.  $  ^{30}  $   The absorption spectrum of GA-assisted perovskite film displays a gradual increase due to good-quality perovskite film formation with large grains and improved crystallinity, while no noteworthy alteration in the absorption edge is detected (Figure 2f).

Figure 3a and 3b depict the schematic view of two neighboring grain structures cross-linked by GA molecules and GA assembly on the crystal surface as well as coordination bond formation between the under-coordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ion and Lewis base GA ligand, respectively.  $  ^{31,32}  $   Figure 3c displays the X-ray photoemission spectroscopy (XPS) spectra of the Pb in the pristine and GA-assisted perovskite films. The characteristic Pb   $  4f_{7/2}  $   peak at 138.7 eV and Pb   $  4f_{5/2}  $   peak at 142.8 eV were shifted to lower binding energies for the GA-assisted perovskite which confirms the chemical interaction between the carboxyl group (-COOH) in GA and the   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ions in the perovskite material.

Moreover, the interaction between GA and   $  PbI_{2}  $   can be confirmed by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) accompanied on GA ligand and GA-  $  PbI_{2}  $   ligand–complex (Figure 3d). The fingerprint regions of GA and GA-  $  PbI_{2}  $   samples were found at 1706 and 1683 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  , respectively (Figure 3e), and the carboxyl stretching vibration band shifted to a lower wavenumber after the addition of GA, indicating a bonding interaction between O atoms of GA and under-coordinated   $  Pb^{2+}  $   ions. The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra of GA-assisted perovskite film shows perceptibly stronger peak intensity, signifying the inhibited nonradiative recombination due to the surface passivation effects and the improved film quality (Figure 5a).  $  ^{35}  $   It is also worth noting that the GA-assisted perovskite film displayed a slight blue-shift in PL peak position compared to that of pristine film, which infers a substantial passivation in the trap states near the surface or along grain boundaries. In addition, the PL measurements in the presence of the spiro-OMeTAD HTL were conducted, and the results indicated a noticeable quenching in the case of the GA-based perovskite film compared to the pristine one, which suggests a lower trap density associated with effectively healed defects on the perovskite layers. This facilitates the charge transportation process at the perovskite/HTL interface (Figure 5b).  $  ^{36-38}  $   Additionally, to settle the defect density in the cells, we accompanied space-charge-limited current (SCLC) measurements in the hole-only device structure of FTO/PTAA/Perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD/Au (Figure S5, Supplementary Note 3).

Further investigation in the carrier dynamics of GA-assisted perovskite film is detected via time-resolved photolumines-

cence (TRPL) decay measurements (Figure 5c), and fitting results are listed in Table S3. The average decay time (  $  \tau_{av}  $  ) of the GA-assisted perovskite film increases (172 ns) compared to the pristine film (106 ns), showing that the decay in the pristine sample is higher than that in the GA-based AAE film. Specifically, the slow decay process (  $  \tau_{2}  $  ) associated with trap-assisted recombination showed a slower decay (180.2 ns) than that of pristine perovskite film (112.9 ns), while the fast decay process (  $  \tau_{1}  $  ) attributed to bimolecular recombination is not obviously changed by the recombination in the perovskite film. This phenomenon designates that the charge transfer in the case of GA-based AAE film is preferable compared to that in pristine perovskite film. This discovery is in harmony with the charge transportation kinetics and enhancement in PCE and EQE values.  $  ^{39}  $

Figure 6a illustrates the transient photovoltage (TPV) decay curve of the devices to allow for the examination of the charge-carrier dynamics in the fabricated cells. The photovoltage decay time increased from 0.41  $ \mu $ s (pristine perovskite) to 0.84  $ \mu $ s (GA-assisted perovskite), designating the efficient suppression in the charge carrier recombination, in good agreement with the improved  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $ . The energetic profile of trap density of state ( $ t $ DOS) was measured by interrogating the frequency-dependent capacitance (Figure 6b). The GA-based AAE employing device showed a one order of magnitude lower trap density in both the deeper trap region (band 2), which is assigned to defects at the perovskite surface, and the shallower trap region (band 1), which corresponded to traps at grain boundaries. This again confirms that the GA molecules can impressively heal the defects originating from under-coordinated  $ \mathrm{Pb}^{2+} $ . Not only the reduced trap states on the surface and grain boundaries of the perovskite film but also the minimized gaps between the perovskite grains might restrain ion migration and thus decelerate the device degradation. In order to confirm this phenomenon, the shelf stability of three devices in each condition was monitored in ambient conditions with a relative humidity (R.H.) of 35–40% for 30 days without encapsulation (Figure 7a). The results displayed that GA-assisted cells retain almost 76% of their initial performance while control devices maintain only   $  \sim  $  35% of their initial performance, which can be ascribed to ion migration, poor film quality, and phase instability under ambient conditions.  $  ^{21,43}  $   In addition, Figure S6 demonstrates the digital images of perovskite films in fresh and aged forms under ambient conditions for 30 days, confirming the effect of GA-based AAE passivation to attain more stable devices. Moreover, the photostability of encapsulated devices under continuous illumination at MPP tracking was monitored and is illustrated in Figure 7b. The device based on GA-assisted perovskite film maintains 88% of its initial efficiency after 2400 h while the control device retains only 39% of its performance under the same conditions. The significantly improved shelf and photostability demonstrate the beneficial effects of cross-linking GA modification. Additionally, we conducted XRD measurements of perovskite films stored under ambient conditions for 10 days (Figure S7). The obtained patterns confirmed that perovskite film in the presence of GA maintains the full diffraction peaks with the same crystallinity, whereas the decomposition from perovskite to PbI  $  _{2}  $   was observed for the pristine film, indicating the degradation of perovskite film after storing.  $  ^{44}  $   These findings again affirm the bare influence of the GA molecules on the stability of assembled devices in the absence of overlayers.

In conclusion, we proposed a facile route to fabricate high-quality perovskite films with increased grain size by adding GA molecules into antisolvent. The obtained results demonstrated that GA molecules play a major role in the crystallization of perovskite, leading to the formation of uniform perovskite with larger grains, lower density of traps, and reinforced grain boundaries. As a result, we have achieved a stabilized efficiency of 21.8% for 2 mg/mL GA-assisted devices. Moreover, the triple-cation and mixed halide perovskite-based devices exhibited an outstanding long-term operational stability, retaining 88% of their initial efficiency after 2400 h under MPP tracking and one sun illumination.
