# 1. Introduction

Abundant reports indicate that the hybrid perovskite materials possess some crucial photoelectric properties, including high absorption coefficient, transport of both electrons and holes, long charge diffusion length as well as carrier lifetime (Chao et al., 2019; Green et al., 2014; Jung and Park, 2015; Zhang et al., 2019). Therefore, since the introduction of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) in 2009 (Kim et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2012), they have won extensive attention in scientific research field (Bella et al., 2018; Heo et al., 2013; Jeong et al., 2019; Saliba et al., 2017; Seok et al., 2018). Attributed to the high power conversion efficiencies (PCEs), low manufacture costs and uncomplicated production processes, PSCs have become a rising star of the photovoltaic field and reached a record (certified) PCE of  $ 25.2\% $  very recent (Best research cell efficiencies, 2019). What's more, researchers have also been actively promoting the commercialization of PSCs (Abate et al., 2018; Huan et al., 2019; Schultes et al., 2019).

Typically, a conductive glass substrate, an electron-transporting layer (ETL), a perovskite light absorbing film, a hole-transporting layer (HTL) and a counter electrode make a n-i-p PSC (Lee et al., 2017; Saliba et al., 2018), where the cooperative work between layers guarantees the device operation at high efficiency and stability. # ABSTRACT

The performance and stability of perovskite solar cell (PSC) are inseparable from the quality of perovskite film, and the solvent engineering is being seemed as an effective strategy to enhance the properties of perovskite. Acetonitrile (ACN) is often used as a solvent to dissolve bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt (LiTFSI), but ACN can corrode the perovskite film, which hinders the promotion of PSC efficiency and durability. Herein, a solvent engineering approach is implemented to search for suitable alternatives for ACN to abate the degradation of the perovskite films. The results demonstrate that isopropanol (IPA) with smaller polarity can effectively dissolve LiTFSI and slow down the degradation of the perovskite layer compared with ACN, which can result in the reduction of defects as well as the nonradiative recombination. Consequently, the devices using LiTFSI/IPA as additive achieve superior power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) with relatively less hysteresis effects and get a champion PCE of 19.43%, while the device using LiTFSI/ACN gets an inferior PCE of 17.12%.

HTL plays a critical role in hindering the recombination of photo-induced carriers at the interface, facilitating the photogenerated holes extract and charge transport (Bakr et al., 2017; Calio et al., 2016). Therefore, many efforts focus on the research on HTL to perfect the efficiency and durability of PSCs. Nowadays, 2,2,7,7-tetrakis(N,N-di-p-methoxyphenylamine)-9,9-spirobifluorene (spiro-OMeTAD) is one of the most frequently utilized hole-transporting materials (HTMs), which obtained PCEs above  $ 21\% $  (Jiang et al., 2019; Tavakoli et al., 2018). Inorganic HTMs such as CuSCN and  $ \mathrm{NiO}_x $  have also been widely used, obtaining inferior PCEs compared with spiro-OMeTAD (Azri et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2018). However, 4-tert-butylpyridine (TBP) and bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt (LiTFSI) must serve as additives to fix the comparatively poor conductivity and hole mobility of spiro-OMeTAD (Liu et al., 2019b; Qiu et al., 2019). First, spiro-OMeTAD oxidation is largely promoted with the coexistence of LiTFSI, an effective p-dopant, and  $ \mathrm{O}_2 $  (Abate et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2015). Then, TBP acts a pivotal part in controlling the morphology of spiro-OMeTAD film. The separation of LiTFSI from the spiro-OMeTAD solution is mainly inhibited in the presence of TBP, improving the homogeneity of the HTL film. Besides, it can significantly reduce the LiTFSI accumulation on the spiro-OMeTAD surface after spin-coating, which further increases the efficiency and stability of the device

Meng et al. found that the PSCs fabricated with the molar ratio 4:1 TBP:LiTFSI had better performance than that of the traditional 6:1 TBP:LiTFSI (Wang et al., 2018). However, plenty of reports propose the use of TBP and LiTFSI causing the decrease of PCE and stability (Li et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2019a; Seo et al., 2018). LiTFSI is moisture-sensitive and easy to adsorb water, resulting in the degradation of perovskite layer. Lamberti et al. found that TBP threaten the long-term conservation of spiro-OMeTAD structural and electrical properties (Lamberti et al., 2019). TBP, known as a polar solvent, can also decompose the perovskite layer, thus the

utilization of TBP challenges the efficiency and stability of device. For this reason, montmorillonite (MMT), served as a buffer layer, was introduced between the perovskite and HTL by Li et al. to alleviate the negative effects of TBP as early as 2014 (Li et al., 2014). Owing to the insolubilization of LiTFSI in chlorobenzene (CB), which is the prevalent solvent to dissolve spiro-OMeTAD, LiTFSI has to be first dissolved in acetonitrile (ACN) to make a stock solution and then added into the CB-based solution (Zhang et al., 2018). However, ACN is also a polar solvent similar to TBP, which can cause the corrosion of the perovskite layer (Bu et al., 2017; Noel et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2018), this situation calls for a re-selection of the solvent of LiTFSI to further promote the performance of PSCs.

Herein, a series of alcohols with smaller polarity, such as methanol (MT), ethanol (ET) and isopropanol (IPA), are introduced as an alternative to dissolve LiTFSI. We demonstrate that all the alcohols selected show better photovoltaic performance than ACN. # 2. Experimental details

# 2.1.Materials

$ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  (99.99%) and CsI (> 99.0%) were purchased from TCI.  $ \mathrm{PbBr}_2 $  (> 99.99%), methyl ammonium bromide (MABr, ≥ 99.5%) and formamidinium iodide (FAI, ≥ 99.5%) were bought from Xian Polymer Light Technology Corp. Dimethylformamide (DMF, > 99.5%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, ≥ 99.7%), chlorobenzene (CB, 99.8%), bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt (LiTFSI, 99%), 4-tert-butylpyridine (TBP, 99.9%) and acetonitrile (ACN, 99.9%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Methanol (MT, 99.5%), ethanol (ET, 99.5%) and isopropanol (IPA, 99.5%) were bought from Shanghai Aladdin Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Spiro-OMeTAD was obtained from Luminescence Technology Crop, Taiwan, China. Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) substrate with a sheet resistance of  $ 14\Omega \cdot sq^{-1} $  was gained from Pilkington.

# 2.2. Preparation of Spiro-OMeTAD solution

72.3 mg spiro-OMeTAD were dissolved in 1 mL CB with the addition of 17.5   $  \mu  $  L LiTFSI solution and 28.8   $  \mu  $  L TBP. LiTFSI solution was formed by dissolving 130 mg Li-TSFI in 250   $  \mu  $  L ACN, MT, ET or IPA to determine the optimal solvent.

# 2.3. Fabrication of devices

The FTOs were treated through immersing in detergent, distilled water and alcohol for about 0.5 h in an ultrasonic cleaner, respectively. After being treated by an UV ozone for 0.5 h and an oxygen plasma for 3 min, the compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   blocking layer was coated onto the FTO by spin-coating for 20 s at 4000 rpm, followed by annealing at 500 °C for 0.5 h. The preparation of the compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer was synthesized as described previously (Tu et al., 2015).

The cesium-containing triple cation perovskite precursor solution was prepared according to previous reports (Saliba et al., 2016; Tu et al., 2018). In a typical synthesis,  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  (1.15 M), FAI (1.09 M),  $ \mathrm{PbBr}_2 $  (0.20 M), MABr (0.14 M) and CsI (0.06 M) were dissolved in a mixture of anhydrous DMF and DMSO (4/1, v/v). The solution was filtrated with  $ 0.45\mu \mathrm{m} $  PTFE filter before spin-coating.  $ 20~\mu \mathrm{L} $  of the mixed precursor solution was deposited onto the compact  $ \mathrm{TiO_2} $  substrate via a two-step program: 1000 rpm for 10 s and 6000 rpm for 20 s.  $ 120~\mu \mathrm{L} $  of chlorobenzene were poured on the center of the film, which was functioned as anti-solvent to remove an excess amount of DMF/DMSO and promote crystallization of the perovskite. The as-prepared films were heated on a hot plate at  $ 100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} $  for 1 h afterwards. After cooling down to room temperature, the spiro-OMeTAD solution was spread over the perovskite layer via a single program: 4000 rpm for 20 s. The operations mentioned above were carried out in a glovebox filled with  $ \mathbf{N}_2 $ . All PSCs were placed in a dry cabinet (humidity  $ < 15\% $ ) for about  $ 12\mathrm{h} $  to fulfill spiro-OMeTAD oxidation. Eventually,  $ 80\mathrm{nm} $  thickness of Au counter electrode were evaporation-coated on the spiro-OMeTAD layer using a shadow mask under high vacuum.

# 2.4. Characterization

The current density-voltage (J-V) curves of the PSCs were measured under the white light illumination with a light intensity of 100 mW·cm  $  ^{-2}  $  (AM 1.5G) provided from a Newport solar simulator (Irvine, CA) in ambient air without further encapsulation. The light intensity was checked by a standard Si solar cell with KG-2 filter. Average photovoltaic parameters of the PSC devices were obtained from 30 devices to ensure the reliability and repeatability of data. With the same instruments, the dark J-V curve and trap state density of device were acquired by measuring FTO/TiO  $  _{2}  $  /perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD/Au and FTO/PEDOT:PSS/perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD/Au multilayer films in the dark. Surface morphologies were obtained using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; Hitachi S-8000, Japan). UV–Vis measurements were performed on an UV/Vis-NIR spectrometer (model: Lambda 950). The steady state photoluminescence (PL) spectra were characterized by a fluorescence spectrometer (Thermo Scientific Lumina). The time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectra were analyzed using Omni-  $  \lambda  $  Monochromator/Spectrograph. The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted using an electrochemical workstation (Zahner, Germany) in the dark and the measured frequencies ranged from 100 mHz to 2 MHz with 0 V bias.

# 3. Results and discussion

To confirm the damage of ACN in spiro-OMeTAD solution to the perovskite layer, we configure a mixture of CB, LiTFSI solution (ACN or IPA as the solvent to dissolve LiTFSI) and TBP, the amount of each added component was the same as that in spiro-OMeTAD solution. The two different solutions were spin-coated onto the triple A-cation perovskite films. Then, we stored the films in a dry cabinet (humidity  $ < 15\% $  ) for about  $ 12\mathrm{h} $  in the dark before characterization.

Fig. 1 shows the morphology of the perovskite films treated by the

mixed solution mentioned above. The pristine perovskite film shows a compact and pinholes-free morphology in Fig. 1a. After the treatment of the mixed solution, some pinholes are present on the films. Compared with the perovskite film treated by ACN (Fig. 1c), the film treated by IPA (Fig. 1b) shows a less-pinholes and more compact morphology, which would critically decrease the nonradiative charge carrier recombination during carrier transportation process. It can be referred that ACN can cause the corrosion of the perovskite layer, which can be slowed down by switching to a smaller polarity solvent.

In the UV–vis absorption spectra (Fig. 2a), all perovskite films present similar absorption profiles from 400 to 800 nm. Owing to the compact and pinholes-free morphology, the pristine perovskite film shows the highest absorption. The films treated by ACN or IPA possess weaker absorption, which can be ascribed to the defects induced by the treatment of the mentioned mixed solvent. Among this, due to the reduced pinholes and good compactness, the film treated by IPA shows a relatively stronger absorbance. It is obvious that the morphology accompanies effects on the photoabsorption ability, and the film treated by a smaller polarity solvent (IPA) may have a better photovoltaic performance.

We prepared spiro-OMeTAD solution using LiTFSI/ACN and LiTFSI/IPA (denoted as ACN-spiro and IPA-spiro, respectively). The PL spectra of ACN-spiro and IPA-spiro devices are presented in Fig. 2b. Each sample exhibits a strong PL emission peak at around  $ 760\mathrm{nm} $  originated from the perovskite films, and the PL intensity of the perovskite with spiro-OMeTAD is greatly diminished. Obviously, the sample of FTO/perovskite/IPA-spiro gets a larger PL quenching, which reveals that the hole separation is carried out more efficient between the perovskite and the IPA-spiro. We deduce that the pinholes-less and relatively more compact morphology might facilitate the process of hole separation. To study the charge carrier dynamics, the TRPL spectra of ACN-spiro and IPA-spiro devices are measured. As illustrated in Fig. 2c and Table 1, the FTO/perovskite/IPA-spiro device shows lower   $  \tau_{1}  $   value of 4.453 ns than that of the FTO/perovskite/ACN-spiro, implying a faster hole injection process. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) is applied to characterize three fresh-made TBP-free spiro-OMeTAD films with various ingredients combinations. As shown in Fig. 2d, a broad peak at about  $ 3600\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $ , namely the water peak (Wang et al., 2016), is present in the LiTFSI/ACN sample. However, no obvious water peak is present in the LiTFSI/IPA sample. Regarding this, we deduce that IPA could reduce the accumulation of LiTFSI in the TBP-free films, which limits the hydration of LiTFSI since it is evenly dispersed in spiro-OMeTAD film.

Fig. 3 shows the UV–vis absorption spectroscopy and ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) of ACN-spiro and IPA-spiro. Clearly, the HOMO level of IPA-spiro (-5.0 eV) is the same as the HOMO level of ACN-spiro, indicating that the replacement of ACN does not change the energy level of spiro-OMeTAD.

To investigate the trap-state density in the PSCs with IPA-spiro and ACN-spiro,  $ J $ -V plots of the devices with a structure of FTO/PEDOT:PSS/perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD/Au are measured in the dark. Fig. 4a and 4b illustrate the corresponding dark  $ J $ -V plots of hole-only PSCs. The trap-state density can be calculated by the equation:  $ n_{trap} = 2\varepsilon \varepsilon_0 V_{TFL}/\mathrm{eL}^2 $ , where  $ \varepsilon $  is the relative dielectric constant,  $ \varepsilon_0 $  is the vacuum permittivity ( $ \varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12} \mathrm{~F} \cdot \mathrm{m}^{-1} $ ),  $ V_{TFL} $  is the trap-filled limit voltage, e is the elemental charge ( $ 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \mathrm{C} $ ) and L is the thickness of the perovskite film (Cai et al., 2017; Luo et al., 2018; Tu et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2017). It is clear to see that the  $ V_{TFL} $  values of the IPA-spiro and ACN-spiro devices are  $ 0.565 \mathrm{~V} $  and  $ 0.648 \mathrm{~V} $ . In contrast to ACN-spiro device with the  $ n_{trap} $  value of  $ 8.27 \times 10^{15} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3} $ , the IPA-spiro device possesses a lower  $ n_{trap} $  value of  $ 7.21 \times 10^{15} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3} $ . Therefore, it is concluded that IPA could mitigate the degradation of the perovskite film compared with the frequently-used ACN.

To clarify the benefits of IPA over the prevalent ACN, PSCs' dark  $ J-V $  plots was measured and is shown in Fig. 4c. According to the diode law, the  $ V_{OC} $  of device can be fitted to the formula:  $ V_{OC} = \frac{nKT}{e}\ln (\frac{5c_{\mathrm{C}}}{J_0} +1) $  (Liu et al., 2019c). For the IPA-spiro device, the  $ J_0 $  and n values are calculated to be  $ 6.82\times 10^{-8}\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $  and 2.41, whereas the  $ \mathrm{J}_0 $  and n values are  $ 1.34\times 10^{-6}\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2} $  and 2.81 for the ACN-spiro device. Regarding this, we infer that the perovskite film of the IPA-spiro device can prevent the current leakage to some degree since IPA solvent exhibit less damage to the perovskite layer than ACN solvent.

Furthermore, we apply EIS measurement to study the recombination of the PSCs based on ACN-spiro and IPA-spiro. In Fig. 4d, the semicircle in the high-frequency, which usually represents the recombination resistance of perovskite/IPA-spiro (Liu et al., 2019a), is significantly larger than that of perovskite/ACN-spiro, implying the reduced recombination loss in the IPA-spiro device, is consistent with PL and TRPL analyses mentioned above.

Keeping in mind that the alternation of ACN in spiro-OMeTAD solution may lead to the mitigation of corrosion process and the reduction of nonradiative recombination losses, we select several alcohols (MT, ET and IPA) with smaller polarity as an alternative to dissolve LiTFSI. To investigate the impact of different solvents on efficiency as well as durability, we fabricated planar n-i-p devices with a configuration of FTO/TiO $ _2 $ /perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD/Au. Fig. 5a illustrates the PSCs  $ J-V $  curves with various solvents in the spiro-OMeTAD solutions and the corresponding  $ J-V $  data are illustrated in Table 2. As we can see, each device exhibits a superior photovoltaic performance with respect to that of the pristine device. Surprisingly, the device coated by IPA-spiro achieves a champion PCE of 19.43%, with a  $ V_{OC} $  of 1.12 V, a  $ J_{SC} $  of 22.73 mA cm $ ^{-2} $  and a FF of 0.76. Compared with the pristine one with a PCE of 17.12%, the improved photovoltaic performance should be ascribed to the higher  $ V_{OC} $ ,  $ J_{SC} $  and FF.

We also find that the replacement of ACN with IPA slightly relieved J-V hysteresis, Fig. 5b shows the J-V curves of ACN-spiro and IPA-spiro devices under different scan directions, and the detailed J-V data are

listed in Table 3. The enhancement of PCE and mitigation of hysteresis could be ascribed to the reduced surface defects, which result from the replacement of ACN with IPA. As we mentioned above, IPA contained in the spiro-OMeTAD solution can mitigate the corrosion of perovskite with respect to that of ACN, and the reduced surface defects can result in suppression of nonradiative recombination losses and charge accumulation. To investigate the impact of the reduced accumulation of LiTFSI on photovoltaic performance, we fabricate the devices without the addition of TBP. Fig. 5c shows the J-V curves of two kinds of TBP-free HTLs devices and the corresponding data are list in Table 4. The TBP-free IPA-spiro PSC shows much better PCE (13.39%), although it is still far from the normal HTL, than the ACN-spiro one (3.44%).

Furthermore, to evaluate the long-term stability of our PSCs, we tested the PSCs with IPA-spiro and ACN-spiro for 20 days in ambient environment (30 ± 5% RH%, 25 °C, Fig. 5d). The IPA-spiro device maintains 92.41% of its initial efficiency, whereas the ACN-spiro device retains 82.48% of its initial efficiency. We think that ACN-spiro may cause more corrosion traces on the perovskite surface, thus moisture and oxygen could easily intrude into the perovskite layer, resulting in the drop of long-term stability. In addition, the reproducibility of the PSCs is also a critical issue. To ensure the reliability and repeatability of data, average photovoltaic parameters of the each PSC device were obtained from 30 devices. The statistical distribution of the photovoltaic data are presented in Fig. 6. The device with IPA-spiro obtain a higher mean PCE of 18.76 ± 0.41%, with a mean   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.11 ± 0.009 V, a mean   $  J_{SC}  $   of 22.50 ± 0.40 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   and a mean FF of 0.7482 ± 0.0089, while the devices with ACN-spiro achieve a lower mean PCE of 16.67 ± 0.54%, with a mean   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.08 ± 0.01 V, a mean   $  J_{SC}  $   of 21.48 ± 0.56 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   and a mean FF of 0.71.23 ± 0.0154. Clearly, the devices with IPA-spiro show better photovoltaic performance and reproducibility.

# 4. Conclusions

In summary, the corrosion process of perovskite caused by ACN, which is a usual solvent to dissolve LiTFSI before adding to spiro-OMeTAD solution, has been identified and characterized using UV–vis and SEM. To address the problem, a series of alcohols with smaller polarity (MT, ET and IPA) are introduced as an alternative to dissolve LiTFSI. Among the selected alcohols, the IPA-spiro device achieves a champion PCE of 19.43%, whereas the conventional ACN-spiro one shows an inferior PCE of 17.12%. Systematic measurements indicate that the PCE rise is due to the reduced nonradiative recombination and lower trap density, which could be ascribed to the pinholes-less and relatively more compact perovskite film. Besides, along with a less hysteresis effect, the stability of the IPA-spiro device increased. Our study offers a new strategy to alleviate the negative impact of HTL on the perovskite film, which may become a facile and effective way to improve photovoltaic performance and durability of devices.
