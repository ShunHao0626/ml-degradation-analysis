# Hydrothermally Treated   $  SnO_{2}  $   as the Electron Transport Layer in High-Efficiency Flexible Perovskite Solar Cells with a Certificated Efficiency of 17.3%

Chang Liu, Luozheng Zhang, Xianyong Zhou, Jishu Gao, Wei Chen, Xingzhu Wang, and Baomin Xu*

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) are one of the most promising solar energy conversion technologies owing to their rapidly developing power conversion efficiency (PCE). Low-temperature solution processing of the perovskite layer enables the fabrication of flexible devices. However, their application has been greatly hindered due to the lack of strategies to fabricate high-quality electron transport layers (ETLs) at the low temperatures (  $  \approx  $  100 °C) that most flexible plastic substrates can withstand, leading to poor performances for flexible PSCs. In this work, through combining the spin-coating process with a hydrothermal treatment method, ligand-free and highly crystalline   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETLs are successfully fabricated at low temperature. The flexible PSCs based on this   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL exhibit an excellent PCE of 18.1% (certified 17.3%). The flexible PSCs maintained 85% of the initial PCE after 1000 bending cycles and over 90% of the initial PCE after being stored in ambient air for 30 days without encapsulation. The investigation reveals that hydrothermal treatment not only promotes the complete removal of organic surfactants coated onto the surface of the   $  SnO_{2}  $   nanoparticles by hot water vapor but also enhances crystallization through the high vapor pressure of water, leading to the formation of high-quality   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETLs.

# 1. Introduction

As an effective photovoltaic technology, perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted significant research attention due to their high power conversion efficiencies (PCEs), which have rapidly increased from 3.8% to over 23.3% in recent years.  $  ^{[1-8]}  $   In addition, the low-temperature solution processability of the perovskite absorber layer, in comparison to that of rigid

and poly(bis(4-phenyl)(2,4,6-trimethylphenyl)amine) (PTAA), can be processed at low temperatures and directly used in flexible PSCs, their high costs and low stabilities hinder the large-scale production of flexible PSCs.  $  ^{[16-18]}  $   Utilizing inorganic ETLs should be a much more effective strategy because they have greater stability than organic ETLs; however, most inorganic ETLs have to be processed at high temperatures or vacuum, which limits their utilization in flexible PSCs.  $  ^{[19-22]}  $

Recently,   $  SnO_{2}  $   has been widely studied as an excellent ETL owing to its high conductivity, suitable energy band structure, and high ultraviolet resistance.  $  ^{[23-25]}  $   A variety of technologies have been extensively developed to fabricate   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETLs, including solution spin-coating, chemical bath deposition (CBD), atomic layer deposition (ALD), and hydrothermal methods  $  ^{[26-29]}  $   Wang et al. reported a low-temperature plasma-enhanced ALD-processed   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETL, achieving a high PCE of greater than 18% on flexible indium tin oxide/PET (ITO/PET) substrates; however, the ALD process is still too expensive for mass production.  $  ^{[30,31]}  $   For the CBD method, the addition of acids that can etch the flexible conducting substrates (i.e., indium tin oxide) is required to control the hydrolysis rate of   $  SnO_{2}  $  .  $  ^{[32,33]}  $   On the other hand, solution spin-coating-processed

substrates, makes it possible to fabricate flexible solar cells, which can enable their application beyond traditional solar cells, such as silicon solar cells.  $  ^{[9-12]}  $   Thus far, low-cost and lightweight plastic substrates such as polyethylene terephthalate (PET) and polyethylene naphthalate (PEN) are typically used as conductive substrates for fabricating flexible PSCs.  $  ^{[13-15]}  $   Such flexible substrates cannot withstand temperatures greater than 150 °C. Thus, constructing the entire architecture of a perovskite device at temperatures below 100 °C, including for both the perovskite absorber layer and the interfacial layers, is a major challenge for flexible PSCs. Low-temperature processing is especially difficult for preparing the electron transport layers (ETLs) of PSCs with a standard n-i-p structure, as the ETL is the first layer deposited onto the substrate and directly contacting the substrate. Additionally, this novel process was used to fabricate   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETLs on flexible ITO/PEN substrates and

# 2. Results and Discussion

# 2.1. SnO  $  _{2}  $   ETL Fabrication Process

Scheme 1 shows the fabrication process of a SnO $ _2 $  thin film on ITO/glass substrates with either thermal annealing or hydrothermal treatment methods. An aqueous colloidal SnO $ _2 $  solution (5 wt%) with presynthesized SnO $ _2 $  nanocrystals was first spin-coated onto an ITO/PEN substrate to form the as-deposited SnO $ _2 $  film (denoted as AD-SnO $ _2 $ ) ETL followed by either thermal annealing at 150 °C for 1 h (denoted as TA-SnO $ _2 $ ) or hydrothermal treatment at 100 °C for 1 h (denoted as HT-SnO $ _2 $ ). In previous work, it was found that AD-SnO $ _2 $  has low crystallinity, leading to low electrical conductivity and poor charge transport at the interface between the ETL and the perovskite absorber layer. $ ^{[36]} $  On the other hand, it is well known that surfactants are usually added into colloidal SnO $ _2 $  solutions to maintain the dispersion of the SnO $ _2 $  nanoparticles. This surfactant coating on the surface of the SnO $ _2 $  nanoparticles normally leads to a high resistivity in the as-deposited SnO $ _2 $  film and should be removed by a suitable method. The widely used method is annealing; however, a low annealing temperature would result in the surfactants not being fully removed, and a high annealing temperature would damage the flexible substrate, both of which lead to poor performances. It is also known that most surfactants are carbonaceous organics (R), which can be decomposed into gaseous fuels by hot water vapor by the following reaction: R + H $ _2 $ O = R(O) + H $ _2 $ . $ ^{[40]} $  Therefore, water steam treatment of SnO $ _2 $  could trigger the removal of the carbonaceous surfactants. However, typical water-gas shift reactions need relatively high-temperature (>200 °C) treatment steps. Here, we have discovered that the removal of the surfactants can be fully completed at low temperature (100 °C) with the help of the high vapor

To study the trap density of the perovskite layers deposited onto the various SnO $ _2 $  films, devices with an electron-only structure (ITO/SnO $ _2 $ /perovskite/PCBM/Ag) were constructed, and the dark current-voltage ( $ I-V $ ) curves of these devices were measured (Figure 3d). As shown in Figure 3d, at a low bias voltage, the pink line shows a linear response, indicating an ohmic-type response. The bias voltage above the kink point is defined as the trap-filled limit voltage ( $ V_{\text{TFL}} $ ). The yellow line exhibits a nonlinear current increase, revealing that the trap sites are fully occupied. The trap density (Nt) can be calculated from the  $ V_{\text{TFL}} $  result using the equation:  $ N_t = 2\varepsilon_0 V_{\text{TFL}} / eL^2 $ , where  $ \varepsilon_0 $  is the vacuum permittivity,  $ \varepsilon $  is the relative dielectric constant of perovskite,  $ e $  is the electron charge, and  $ L $  is the thickness of the film. $ ^{[50]} $  The trap densities of the perovskite layers deposited onto the AD-SnO $ _2 $ , TA-SnO $ _2 $ , and HT-SnO $ _2 $  substrates are  $ 3.33 \times 10^{16} $ ,  $ 2.57 \times 10^{16} $ , and  $ 2.27 \times 10^{16} $  cm $ ^{-3} $ , respectively. The distinctly lower trap density of the perovskite film deposited onto HT-SnO $ _2 $  is attributed to the low grain boundary density and large grain size, which is in agreement with the results from Figure 2.

# 2.4. Role of the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  ETLs in Charge Carrier Transport and Recombination Suppression

To study the charge transport in PSCs, the charge carrier dynamics in pristine and ETL-containing perovskite films were monitored by using steady-state PL (SSPL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectroscopy. As shown in Figure 4a, the perovskite film on the AD-SnO  $  _{2}  $  ETL exhibited a strong PL emission at  $  \approx770  $  nm with a linewidth of 60 nm. The PL emission was partially quenched when using the TA-SnO  $  _{2}  $  ETL. The PL quantum yield of the perovskite film deposited on HT-SnO  $  _{2}  $  was greatly decreased, indicating a significantly increased charge carrier extraction efficiency with the use of HT-SnO  $  _{2}  $ . Furthermore, the dynamics of the charge carriers were quantitatively studied by TRPL measurements. As shown in Figure 4b, the long PL decay lifetime of the perovskite/AD-SnO  $  _{2}  $  film was determined to be 75 ns, showing severe recombination before the photogenerated charge could be extracted. The PL decay lifetime (40 ns) in the perovskite/HT-SnO  $  _{2}  $  film showed a significant change in the decay and was much faster than that in the perovskite/AD-SnO  $  _{2}  $  film and perovskite/TA-SnO  $  _{2}  $  film (50 ns), indicating a faster rate of charge transfer from the perovskite to the HT-SnO  $  _{2}  $  ETL.  $  ^{[51]}  $  These results demonstrate that the perovskite film deposited on HT-SnO  $  _{2}  $  has a higher charge extraction and transport efficiency than the films deposited on AD-SnO  $  _{2}  $  and TA-SnO  $  _{2}  $ , which is consistent with the results for the performances of the PSC devices. To further clarify the effects of the different SnO  $  _{2}  $  films, electrical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was used to investigate the charge recombination parameters in the PSCs.

# 2.5. Stability of the PSCs Based on the Different   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETLs

The stabilities of the PSCs, including both their environmental stabilities and continuous working stabilities, are of great important for practical applications. Therefore, the environmental stabilities of the perovskite devices were studied by measuring the photovoltaic performances of the PSCs every 3 days for 30 days. As shown in Figure 5a, it can be seen that the device using AD-SnO  $  _{2}  $  only retains 55% of its initial efficiency when stored under ambient air for 30 days. In contrast, the TA-SnO  $  _{2}  $  based PSC retains 90% of its initial efficiency under the same conditions. Furthermore, the perovskite solar cell based on HT-SnO  $  _{2}  $  showed a slight PCE decay of 8% (from 100% to 92%) after 30 days. Therefore, compared to the PSCs based on AD-SnO  $  _{2}  $  and TA-SnO  $  _{2}  $ , the device based on HT-SnO  $  _{2}  $  exhibits the best long-time stability in ambient air without encapsulation. Moreover, to investigate the continuous working stabilities of the PSCs, the PCEs were monitored under continuous irradiation at 100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $ . As shown in Figure 6b, it can be seen that the PSC based on HT-SnO  $  _{2}  $  maintains 84% of its initial efficiency after irradiation for 100 h. Unfortunately, under the same test conditions, the AD-SnO  $  _{2}  $ , and TA-SnO  $  _{2}  $  based PSCs maintain only 37% and 72% of their initial efficiencies, respectively. It is obvious that the PSC based on HT-SnO  $  _{2}  $  exhibits excellent stability, including environmental stability and continuous working stability. # 2.6. High-Efficiency Flexible PSCs Based on the HT-  $  SnO_{2}  $  ETL

Owing to its low-temperature preparation,  $ \mathrm{HT - SnO_2} $  can be applied to flexible PSC devices as the ETL. Figure 6a shows the  $ J - V $  curve of the optimized flexible PSC device (prepared on an ITO/PEN substrate) with a  $ J_{\mathrm{SC}} $  of 21.35 (21.35) mA cm $ ^{-2} $ , a  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  of 1.106 (1.081) V, and an FF of 76.57 (74.89) %, which obtained a PCE of 18.09 (17.37) % when tested under reverse (forward) scanning. Furthermore, the certified efficiency of the flexible PSC is 17.27% (Figure S8, Supporting Information), which is the highest certified efficiency reported for a flexible PSC thus far. The corresponding EQE plot of the flexible PSC is shown in Figure 6b. The integrated  $ J_{\mathrm{SC}} $  of 21.11 mA cm $ ^{-2} $  is consistent with the  $ J_{\mathrm{SC}} $  of 21.35 mA cm $ ^{-2} $  that was obtained from the  $ J - V $  curve. Owing to the electron extraction ability of the HT- $ \mathrm{SnO}_2 $  ETL, our flexible PSC device shows a relatively small degree of hysteresis (Figure 6a). To prevent overestimation of the real performance of the flexible PSC, the SOP of the flexible PSC was tested under AM 1.5G 100 mW cm $ ^{-2} $  illumination. As shown in Figure S9 in the Supporting Information, the SOP of the flexible PSC was 17.08% after 120 s with a steady-state current density of 19.98 mA cm $ ^{-2} $  at a constant bias of 0.90 V. The stable SOP of the flexible PSC results from the effective charge transfer at

the interfacial layer and the high quality of the perovskite film deposited onto  $ \mathrm{HT - SnO_2} $ , as shown in Figure S10 in the Supporting Information. In addition, as shown in Figure 6c, the air stability of the unencapsulated flexible PSC was studied. When stored in air with a relative humidity greater than  $ 30\% $ , the flexible PSC based on  $ \mathrm{HT - SnO_2} $  maintained more than  $ 75\% $  of its initial efficiency after 30 days, indicating the high stability of the flexible device. To measure the stability of the flexible PSC after bending, the flexible PSC based on  $ \mathrm{HT - SnO_2} $  was repeatedly bent with a  $ 14\mathrm{mm} $  bending radius and the evolution of its performance was recorded. As shown in Figure 6d, the performance of the unencapsulated flexible PSCs was maintained at  $ \approx 80\% $  of its initial PCE after being bent 1000 times, indicating the good stability of the flexible PSC.

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, we proposed a simple hydrothermal treatment to obtain high-quality and ligand-free   $  SnO_{2}  $   ETLs (  $  HT-SnO_{2}  $  ). The electrical conductivity and charge transport ability of   $  SnO_{2}  $ , which was deposited from a colloidal   $  SnO_{2}  $   solution, can be effectively improved by hydrothermal treatment. Furthermore, the processing temperature has been decreased to 100 °C, and the PSCs based on these   $  HT-SnO_{2}  $   ETLs exhibit a higher   $  V_{OC}  $   and FF and a lower J-V hysteresis than those based on conventional thermally annealed   $  SnO_{2}  $ . Benefiting from the low temperature of the hydrothermal treatment, the excellent flexible PSCs exhibit PCE values of 18.09% and 17.37%, as measured from reverse and forward scans, respectively. # 4. Experimental Section

Materials: Formamidinium iodide (FAI), methylammonium bromide (MABr), and lead bromide (PbBr $ _2 $ ) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Co. 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-di- $ p $ -methoxyphenylamine)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (spiro-OMeTAD) was purchased from Yingkou OPV Tech Co., Ltd. The SnO $ _2 $  solution was purchased from Alfa Aesar (tin (IV) oxide, 15 wt% colloidal dispersion in H $ _2 $ O). PbI $ _2 $  (purity >99.9985%) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. CsI (purity >99.999%) was obtained from Sigma Aldrich. All of the other salts and anhydrous solvents, including C60 pyrrolidine tris-acid (CPTA), Li-TFSI salt, N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), ethanol, isopropyl alcohol, tert-butylpyridine (tBP), chlorobenzene, acetonitrile, and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. All of the above chemical products were used directly without further purification or treatment. The ITO/Glass (7 Ohm sq $ ^{-1} $ ) and ITO/PEN (15 Ohm sq $ ^{-1} $ ) substrates were purchased from YOUXUN Technology Corp.

Fabrication of AD-SnO $ _2 $ , TA-SnO $ _2 $ , and HT-SnO $ _2 $  films: The colloidal SnO $ _2 $  aqueous dispersion (15 wt%) was diluted using deionized water to 5 wt%. This solution was stirred at room temperature for 2 h. The AD-SnO $ _2 $  ETLs were fabricated by spin-coating the above SnO $ _2 $  solution at 4000 rpm for 30 s. For the TA-SnO $ _2 $  film, the AD-SnO $ _2 $  film was annealed on a 150 °C hot plate for 60 min to remove the residual solvent and surfactant. For the HT-SnO $ _2 $ , the AD-SnO $ _2 $  film was transferred to a 25 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave with 10 mL of water and then heated to 100 °C for 1 h. For the flexible PSC device, the ITO/PEN substrate was first spin-coated with a 1 mg mL $ ^{-1} $  solution of CPTA in DMF at 4000 rpm for 30 s at ambient temperature to increase the hydrophilicity of the flexible substrate. A C60 solution prepared by dissolving C60 in CB at a concentration of 1 mg mL $ ^{-1} $  was spin-coated onto the HT-SnO $ _2 $  coated substrates to fabricate the flexible PSCs.

Device Fabrication: The perovskite layer was deposited onto different ETL substrates using a one-step solution process. A perovskite precursor solution was prepared and consisted of 172 mg of FAI, 507 mg of PbI $ _{2} $ , 22.4 mg of MABr, and 73.4 mg of PbBr $ _{2} $  dissolved in 1 mL of a mixed solvent comprising DMF and DMSO at a volume ratio of 9:1. Then, a stock solution of 1.5 M CsI in DMSO was added to the mixed perovskite precursor solution to obtain the CS $ _{0.05} $ FA $ _{0.81} $ MA $ _{0.14} $ PbI $ _{2.55} $ Br $ _{0.45} $  precursor solution. Before deposition of the perovskite absorbing layer, the SnO $ _{2} $  substrates were treated with UV–Ozone (UVO) for 10 min. The perovskite films were deposited onto the SnO $ _{2} $  substrates via a two-step spin-coating procedure. The first step was performed at 1000 rpm for 10 s with an acceleration of 500 rpm s $ ^{-1} $ . The second step was performed at 4000 rpm for 35 s with an acceleration of 2000 rpm s $ ^{-1} $ . Chlorobenzene ( $ \approx $ 100  $ \mu $ L) was quickly added onto the spinning substrate during the second spin-coating step 15 s before the end of the procedure. Afterward, the as-prepared films were heated at 100  $ ^{\circ} $ C for  $ \approx $ 1 h until their color had changed to dark red. The hole-transport layer (HTL) was prepared by spin-coating a Spiro-OMeTAD solution. The Spiro-OMeTAD solution was prepared by dissolving Spiro-OMeTAD at a concentration of 72.3 mg mL $ ^{-1} $  in a chlorobenzene solution that contained 30  $ \mu $ L mL $ ^{-1} $  tBP and 20  $ \mu $ L mL $ ^{-1} $

Li-TFSI salt (520 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  in acetonitrile). Finally, a 100 nm thick Au layer was thermally evaporated at a rate of  $  \approx  $  0.05 nm s  $  ^{-1}  $  under a pressure of  $  4 \times 10^{-5}  $  Torr to complete the device fabrication.

Measurements and Characterization: UV–vis absorption spectra were recorded using a Perkin Elmer Lambda 950 UV–vis–NIR spectrometer. The photoluminescence (PL) spectra were acquired using a Horiba Fluoromax-4 spectrofluorometer. The time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectra were measured with an Edinburgh Instrument FLS980 using 375 nm excitation derived from an Nd:YAG laser. FTIR spectral data for the devices were collected over the 700–3600 cm  $  ^{-1}  $  range by using a Nicolet NEXUS 870 FTIR spectrometer. Drop shape analysis was performed using a drop shape analyser (AST VCA Optima XE) with 18 MΩ water and a 4 μL dispersed volume. Drop shapes were measured at 6 different places on the substrate surface at room temperature (25 °C). X-ray diffraction data were collected using a Bruker D8 Discover X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (1.54 Å) operating at 40 kV and 25 mA and equipped with a Hi-Star 2D area detector. The morphologies and structures of the films were characterized by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, ZEISS Merlin) at a 5 kV acceleration voltage. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) data were recorded by an electrochemical workstation (CHI660, China) over the frequency range of 1 Hz–1 MHz, which was applied in the dark. Photocurrent density–voltage (J–V) curves were measured under AM 1.5G one-sun illumination (100 mW cm  $  ^{-2}  $ ) with a solar simulator (Enlitech SS-F7-3A) equipped with a 300 W xenon lamp and a Keithley 2400 source meter. The light intensity was adjusted by an NREL-calibrated Si solar cell. During the measurements, the cell was covered with a mask possessing an aperture of 0.1 cm  $  ^{2}  $ . The external quantum efficiency (EQE) was measured with an EQE system (Enlitech QE-R) containing a xenon lamp, monochromator, Si detector and dual-channel power meter. The bending stability of the flexible perovskite solar cell was determined by measuring the efficiency of the device after repeatedly flexing the device different amounts of times with a 14 mm bending radius.
