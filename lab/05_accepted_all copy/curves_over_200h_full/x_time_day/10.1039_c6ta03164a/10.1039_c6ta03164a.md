# High-performance flexible and air-stable perovskite solar cells with a large active area based on poly(3-hexylthiophene) nanofibrils†

Minwoo Park,  $  ^{*a}  $   Joon-Suh Park,  $  ^{b}  $   Il Ki Han  $  ^{b}  $   and Jin Young Oh  $  ^{\ddagger c}  $

Flexible metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have been considered promising wearable energy power sources. Recently, various multi-functional charge-transporting layers, with high conductivity and air stability, have been rapidly developed in order to improve device performance. The practical use of flexible PSCs requires the retention of the performance and air stability at large active areas. However, increasing the size of the active layer results in increased contact and series resistance at the interfaces of the device. This hinders the charge transport, leading to charge recombination and degradation of the device performance. Furthermore, the poor stability of organic small molecules is detrimental to long-term stability. As such, flexible PSCs based on Li-doped poly(3-hexylthiopehene) (P3HT) nanofibrils (LN-P3HT), as a hole transporting layer (HTL), are prepared by subjecting a P3HT solution to a cooling process. The nanofibrils obtained via the  $ \pi $ -conjugation of the thiophene rings result in improved conductivity and air stability, yielding high-performance, flexible, and air-stable PSCs with an active area of  $ 1\mathrm{cm}^2 $  ( $ PCE = 13.12\% $ ). This outstanding performance and mechanical stability are retained ( $ PCE = 12.96\% $ ) after 500 bending cycles at  $ r = 15\mathrm{mm} $ ; these PSCs also exhibit a 30-day PCE retention of  $ 87\% $  (relative to the initial PCE) at a relative humidity of  $ 30\% $ . Therefore, this novel and facile strategy for preparing an efficient and air-stable organic HTL is a promising method for the realization of high-performance large-area flexible PSCs.

# Introduction

The emergence of metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has spurred innovation in the development of next-generation photovoltaic devices.  $  ^{1,2}  $   Perovskites have an excellent light absorption coefficient   $  (5.7 \times 10^{4} \text{ cm}^{-1} \text{ at } 600 \text{ nm})  $  , high charge carrier mobility   $  (66 \text{ cm}^{2} \text{ V}^{-1} \text{ s}^{-1}, \text{ for a single crystal})  $  , and a tunable optical band gap; as such, these materials are used in various applications such as light-emitting diodes, field-effect transistors, and memory devices.  $  ^{3-8}  $   Several device architectures and materials have been developed to improve the photovoltaic characteristics and environmental stability of PSCs.  $  ^{9-13}  $   As a result, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs has increased significantly, to 22.1%, surpassing that of polycrystalline silicon solar cells.  $  ^{14}  $   Low-cost solution-based

processes are highly desirable for the fabrication of PSCs suitable for building integrated photovoltaic (BIPV) systems incorporated with tandem solar cells, such as perovskite/silicon and perovskite/copper-indium-gallium-selenide (CIGS).  $  ^{15-17}  $   Furthermore, low-temperature processes have enabled the fabrication of flexible architectures on polymer substrates; the resulting high-performance and mechanically flexible PSCs are promising candidates for wearable energy power sources.  $  ^{18-22}  $

The use of flexible PSCs in practical applications requires the scaling up of the constituent active areas in order to obtain a flexible PSC module with a high photocurrent output. However, the integration of large active areas has resulted in a significant decrease in the PCE, owing to the occurrence of a high contact resistance at the interfaces of the interlayers, electrodes, and perovskite.  $  ^{23,24}  $   To obtain both high PCE and a high photocurrent output in these areas, interface engineering has been considered viable for improving the extraction and transfer of charge carriers as well as reducing the contact resistance.  $  ^{23,24}  $   This may be achieved through an alignment of the energy levels and an increase in the electrical conductivity of the interlayers.  $  ^{25-27}  $   Recently, Li-doped   $  NiMnO_{x}  $   and Nb-doped   $  TiO_{x}  $   were used as a hole transporting layer (HTL) and an electron transporting layer (ETL), respectively.  $  ^{28}  $   These dopants increased the conductivity of the interlayers and facilitated

However, the protocol required high annealing temperatures, thereby preventing the fabrication of flexible PSCs. The superior flexibility of organic semiconductors, compared to that of metal oxides, and the low-cost room-temperature solution process make flexible PSCs especially attractive. Flexible PSCs based on an inverted planar structure (p-i-n) have employed poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):polystyrene sulfonate (PEDOT:PSS) as a HTL. PEDOT:PSS is ill-suited for the fabrication of large-area flexible PSCs owing to its poor air stability and high acidity (pH ∼ 1), which lead to the corrosion of the transparent conducting oxide and perovskite layer.  $  ^{29,30}  $   Hydrophobic organic HTLs should therefore be considered for the fabrication of large-area flexible PSCs. Conjugated polymers, such as poly(3-hexylthiophene) (P3HT) and poly(triarylamine) (PTAA), exhibit better air stability than PEDOT:PSS and 2,2',7,7'-tetrakis-(N,N-di-4-methoxyphenylamino)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD) and are commonly used as HTLs in PSCs.  $  ^{31}  $   From this perspective, P3HT would be a suitable HTL for the realization of flexible PSCs because the crystallinity and morphologies of the thin films can be simply modified through various methods; this leads, in turn, to improved conductivity and air stability.

As such, we report on highly efficient, flexible, and air-stable n-i-p planar PSCs, with an active area of  $ 1\mathrm{cm}^2 $ , realized by employing Li-doped P3HT nanofibrils (LN-P3HT) as a HTL. The HTL exhibited enhanced conductivity along both the vertical and parallel directions, facilitating hole extraction from the perovskite, and lowering the specific contact resistivity between the HTL and Au. This resulted in improved device performance and retention of hysteresis-less behavior with varying sizes of the active area. In addition, the highly crystalline LN-P3HT thin films exhibited enhanced air stability, thereby facilitating the successful fabrication of highly flexible and air-stable planar PSCs. These PSCs exhibited an excellent PCE of  $ 13.12\% $  at  $ 1\mathrm{cm}^2 $ , which was almost completely retained  $ (12.96\%) $  after 500 bending cycles at  $ r = 15\mathrm{mm} $ . Furthermore, a PCE of  $ 11.4\% $  was maintained for 30 days at a relative humidity of  $ 30\% $ .

# Experimental

# Materials

Anhydrous dimethylformamide (DMF) (99.8%), anhydrous dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) (99.9%), anhydrous diethyl ether (99.9%), anhydrous m-xylene (>99%), anhydrous chlorobenzene (99.8%), anhydrous acetonitrile (99.8%), anhydrous 2-methoxyethanol (99.8%), polyethyleneimine (80% ethoxylated solution) (PEIE), [6,6]-phenyl-  $  C_{61}  $  -butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) (>99%), regioregular poly(3-hexylthiophene-2,5-diyl) (P3HT), bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt (Li-TFSI) (99.95%), and 4-tert-butyl pyridine (tBP, 96%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. In addition,  $  PbI_{2}  $  (99.9985%) and methylammonium iodide (MAI, 99.98%) were purchased from Alfa Aesar and Dyesol, respectively. The  $  CH_{3}NH_{3}PbI_{3}  $  precursor solution was prepared via the Lewis-base adduct method that was outlined in a previous report; 461 mg of  $  PbI_{2}  $ , 159 mg of

MAI, and 78 mg of DMSO were mixed with 600 mg of DMF.  $  ^{10}  $   LP-P3HT and LN-P3HT solutions were prepared by dissolving 12 mg of P3HT in m-xylene at 60 °C, and then adding 12 μL of Li-TFSI/acetonitrile (170 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) and 12 μL of tBP/acetonitrile (1 : 1 volume ratio) to the solution. The LN-P3HT solution was prepared, in 10 min, from the hot stock solution at -20 °C and the yellow-orange color changed to dark purple. Furthermore, the LP-P3HT solution was kept at 60 °C.

# Solar cell fabrication

Etched ITO/glass and ITO/PENs (2.5 cm × 2.5 cm) were rinsed with 2-propanol and acetone, dried with a   $  N_{2}  $   blower, and subsequently treated with UV-ozone for 20 min. The 0.2 wt% PEIE, diluted in 2-methoxyethanol, was spin-coated at 6000 rpm for 50 s onto the substrate. The substrate was baked at 100 °C for 5 min. Furthermore, 2 wt% of PCBM was dissolved in chlorobenzene, and the resulting solution was spin-coated, for 40 s at 1500 rpm, onto ITO-PEIE. The perovskite precursor solution was spin-coated, at 4000 rpm for 25 s, onto the sample and 0.5 mL of diethyl ether was dripped, for 10 s, on the rotating sample.  $  ^{10}  $   This solution was then heated at 65 °C for 1 min and at 100 °C for 2 min. Moreover, the LP-P3HT and LN-P3HT solutions were spin-coated, at 3000 rpm for 30 s, onto the perovskite. Before spin coating, the LN-P3HT solution was sonicated for 1 min in order to untangle the nanofibrils. Afterwards, the Au electrode was deposited at a constant rate of 0.5 Å s  $  ^{-1}  $  , by using a thermal evaporator.

# Characterization

The surface morphologies of the samples were examined via field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; Hitachi S-4100) images. TEM (JEM-2010, JEOL Ltd.) images were also obtained. Moreover, UPS measurements were performed by using a scanning XPS microprobe (PHI 5000 VersaProbe, Ulvac-PHI) equipped with a HeI monochromator (21.2 eV). The structure of the perovskite and the optical properties of the P3HT thin films were determined via XRD (Rigaku Ultima IV) and UV-Vis spectroscopy (Lambda 35, PerkinElmer), respectively. The steady-state PL spectra of the perovskite films were obtained at an excitation wavelength of 550 nm, using a Fluorolog3 photoluminescence spectrometer system equipped with a monochromator (iHR320, HORIBA Scientific). In addition, time-resolved PL measurements were performed at the PL maxima of the perovskite (770 nm), using a time-correlated single photon counting module (TCSPC, MPD-PDM Series DET-40 photon counting detector and Pendulum CNT-91 frequency counter); this module was combined with a monochromator and a second-harmonic-generated 400 nm laser, derived from a 800 nm Ti:sapphire laser (Mai Tai, Spectra-Physics), which served as a detector and an excitation source, respectively. EIS measurements were performed by using an impedance analyzer (Solartron 1260), and the corresponding Nyquist plots and EIS parameters were obtained by using the Z-View software. J-V curves were obtained by using a Keithley model 2400 source measure unit. A 180 W xenon lamp in a solar simulator (Yamashita Denso YSS-50A) was used as the light source. The

# Results and discussion

Long P3HT nanofibrils are formed in solution by controlling the thermodynamic parameters, such as the concentration, solubility of P3HT in the organic solvents, and the cooling temperature. The driving force for one-dimensional (1D) growth is based on the   $  \pi  $  -conjugation of the thiophene rings formed from the maximum overlap between the   $  \pi  $  -orbitals.  $  ^{32}  $   This conjugation is most easily induced by reducing the solubility of P3HT.  $  ^{33-36}  $   The schematic in Fig. 1A shows the formation of LN-P3HT during the cooling process. The Li-doped pristine P3HT (LP-P3HT) solution was prepared at 60 °C. The solubility of P3HT in m-xylene decreased significantly with rapid cooling of the red-yellow LP-P3HT solution at -20 °C, and the color of the solution changed to dark purple with the growth of the nanofibrils.  $  ^{34-36}  $   Fig. 1B shows a transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image of the long and ultra-thin nanofibrils obtained from the spin-coating of the LN-P3HT solution. The morphologies of the nanofibrils remained the same after the addition of Li-TFSI, suggesting that Li acted as only a p-type dopant that led to an increased electrical conductivity of P3HT.  $  ^{37}  $   These   $  \sim5-20  $     $  \mu  $  m-long nanofibrils had a diameter of   $  \sim20  $   nm. The change in the photovoltaic parameters and the degree of hysteresis varied with the increasing active area of the LP-P3HT device. For example, at an active area of 1 cm², the fill factor (FF) decreased significantly from 0.716 to 0.622 during the backward sweep, while the FF of the LN-P3HT device decreased from 0.712 to 0.669. The hysteresis increased substantially with the increasing active area of LP-P3HT, whereas the LN-P3HT device exhibited hysteresis-less behavior (i.e., negligible change occurred) with changes in the active area. This difference in the hysteresis behavior was reflected in the series resistance (  $  R_{s}  $  , 25.19 Ω cm² and 10.88 Ω cm² for the LP-P3HT and LN-P3HT, respectively) of the devices. Similar trends were observed for

devices with medium-sized   $  (0.48\ \text{cm}^{2}  $   and   $  0.72\ cm^{2})  $   active areas (ESI, Fig. S3†). The LN-P3HT also improved the air- and photo-stability of the flexible PSCs. Specifically, the amorphous region and the grain boundaries of P3HT serve as the space for the penetration of oxygen species, facilitating the degradation of thiophenes,  $  ^{36,46}  $  whereas the tight packing and long persistence length in the nanofibrils may act as an energy barrier for the change in the structure of the P3HT chains, which limits the oxidation or attachment of hydroxyl groups to the thiophene rings.  $  ^{36,46}  $  The LN-P3HT effectively blocked the penetration of oxygen and water molecules into the perovskite; hence, the flexible LN-P3HT devices stored under ambient conditions (relative

humidity: 30%) for 30 days exhibited a more stable performance than the flexible LP-P3HT device. The flexible LP-P3HT and LN-P3HT devices exhibited respective PCE retentions of 87% (PCE = 11.4%) and 71% (PCE = 7.72%) relative to the initial values (Fig. 7A). The good air-stability of the flexible LN-P3HT device was comparable to that of the devices employing dopant-free organic semiconductors such as reduced graphene oxides, triphenylamine/fluorene derivatives and polymeric HTLs consisting of a random copolymer.  $  ^{47-49}  $

Fig. 7B exhibits the photo-stability of the flexible devices under constant environmental conditions (1 sun, 40 °C and relative humidity: 30%). Recently, it has been found out that the degradation of perovskites was independent of light irradiation in the absence of other main factors including   $  TiO_{2}  $  , moisture, and elevated temperature by light.  $  ^{50}  $   Therefore, the environment of our stability test may have an effect on the fast degradation of the perovskite regardless of the HTLs. After 12 h, the flexible LP-P3HT and LN-P3HT devices exhibited respective PCE retentions of 55.7% (PCE = 7.23%) and 40.6% (PCE = 4.48%) relative to the initial values. The better photo-stability of the LN-P3HT device was attributed to the robust LN-P3HT against the light irradiation.  $  ^{36,46}  $   The normalized conductivity in the LN-P3HT and LP-P3HT was found to be 0.963 and 0.885 after 24 h at 1 sun, respectively (ESI, Fig. S7†). The retention of conductivity in the LN-P3HT supported the device performance to some degree. This suggests that the LN-P3HT would have great potential as a bi-functional HTL. As such, the LN-P3HT is a promising HTL for high-performance, flexible, mechanically robust, air- and photo-stable PSC modules.

# Conclusions

In summary, LN-P3HT was prepared by employing a cooling process in order to realize highly efficient, flexible, air- and photo-stable PSCs having an active area of  $ 1\mathrm{cm}^2 $ . The abrupt decrease in the solubility of P3HT in  $ m $ -xylene, during this process, facilitated  $ \pi $ -conjugation of thiophenes, thereby yielding long P3HT nanofibrils. Compared to LP-P3HT, LN-P3HT exhibited more efficient hole extraction from the perovskite and a lower specific contact resistivity to Au. This resulted in hysteresis-less behavior and improved performance at large
