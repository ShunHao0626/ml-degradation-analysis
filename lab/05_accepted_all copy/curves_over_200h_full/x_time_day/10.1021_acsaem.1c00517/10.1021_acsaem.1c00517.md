# Enhanced Performance of Perovskite Solar Cells Loaded with Iodine-Rich   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   Quantum Dots

Qing Tian, Guozhen Ding, Yuting Cai, Zicheng Li, Xueyuan Tang,  $  ^{*}  $   Rong-Jun Xie,  $  ^{*}  $   and Peng Gao  $  ^{*}  $

ACCESS

ABSTRACT: To pursue practical applications of perovskite solar cells (PSCs), while the power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) are continuously pushed up, defect passivation of the metal halide perovskite layer is considered an efficacious method for improving the efficiency and stability of the devices. Herein, we proposed incorporating iodine-rich   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   quantum dots (QDs) in the halide perovskite film to improve the photovoltaic performance of PSCs. Using the modified QDs enabled the reduction of the nonradiative recombination centers by passivating iodide vacancies and optimization of the energy alignment near to the perovskite/hole transport material interface. Finally, high-performance planar PSCs with an efficiency exceeding 22% and higher long-term stability (over 91% of its initial PCE after 39 days) were achieved.

KEYWORDS: perovskites, solar cells, quantum dots, performance, passivation

# INTRODUCTION

Organic–inorganic hybrid halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have demonstrated their attractiveness on account of the fast-growing power conversion efficiencies (PCEs), increasing from 3.8% in 2009 to over 25% within 10 years.  $  ^{1-3}  $   Their solution-processable nature, remarkable optical performance, and comparable PCEs with commercial single-crystalline silicon solar cells render this technology promising for wholesale and inexpensive manufactured photovoltaic (PV) devices.  $  ^{3-8}  $   However, due to the solution-processed and polycrystalline nature of the perovskite, various kinds of defects tend to form at the surface or grain boundaries.  $  ^{9-13}  $   Among these defects, the illocal vacancies of halide anions and organic cations can act as charge recombination centers and result in open-circuit voltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) deficit.  $  ^{14-18}  $   Simultaneously, ion vacancy spread is usually accompanied by the removal of other anions or cations, which is harmful to the composition stability of the perovskite materials and the secular stability of devices.  $  ^{19-23}  $   Therefore, many strategies have been developed to passivate the halide anion vacancies and enhance the efficiency and stability of devices. For example, the addition of iodide ions to the precursor solution can reduce the iodide vacancy concentration in perovskites.  $  ^{24}  $   Furthermore, guanidinium, metal ions, and ammonium halides have also shown positive impacts on passivating iodide vacancies and accordingly fabricating more efficient and stable PSCs.  $  ^{16,22,25-35}  $

Halide perovskite quantum dots (PeQDs) have demonstrated high photoluminescence (PL) quantum yields, low excitation energies, high absorption coefficients, and tunable band gaps, having homogenous properties in the aspects of stoichiometry and crystal structure with their bulk counterparts.  $  ^{36-38}  $   Using PeQDs as an interface modifier in PSCs can

# EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

Materials. The   $  SnO_{2}  $   colloid precursor, FAI, and MABr were bought from Alfa Aesar.   $  PbI_{2}  $   was bought from TCI, and   $  PbBr_{2}  $   was bought from Sigma-Aldrich. Spiro-OMeTAD was bought from Derthon. N,N-Dimethylformamide, dimethyl sulfoxide, chlorobenzene, 4-tert-butylpyridine, and lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)-imide salt were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Cesium carbonate, lead(II) dioxide, oleylamine, oleic acid, 1-octadecene, TMSI, ethyl acetate, and n-hexane were bought from Aladdin Chemical Co. Ltd. (China).

Preparation of Cs-Oleate. Cs-oleate was prepared by high-temperature complexation of   $  Cs_{2}CO_{3}  $   with oleic acid. 2.5 mmol of   $  Cs_{2}CO_{3}  $   was loaded in a four-neck round-bottom flask together with 20.0 mL of ODE and 2.8 mL of OA. The mixture was then purged with nitrogen at 110 °C. After the   $  Cs_{2}CO_{3}  $   was fully dissolved, the solution was cooled in the room environment. Before usage, the solution was again heated to 80 °C.

Preparation of Pb-Oleate. Pb-oleate was also prepared by high-temperature complexation of PbO and oleic acid. About 16.0 mmol of PbO was loaded in a four-neck round-bottom flask along with 20.0 mL of ODE and 12.0 mL of OA. The mixture was then purged with nitrogen at 110 °C until the clear Pb-oleate solution was formed. After

being cooled in the room environment, the solution was put away in a glass container. Before usage, the solution was again heated to   $  80\ °C  $  .

Synthesis of TMSI-CPI QDs. 8.60 mL of ODE and 1.80 mmol OLA were put in a 100 mL round-bottomed flask. Then, 0.43 mL of pre-heated stock solution of Cs-oleate and 0.80 mL of pre-heated stock solution of Pb-oleate were injected into the reaction system in turn. Then, the reaction temperature was raised to 150 °C under   $  N_{2}  $   and 0.20 mL of TMSI was quickly injected. About 5 s after the reaction, the solution was cooled down with ice-water. Finally, 20 mL of ethyl acetate was added to the crude QD solution and transferred to a centrifuge tube for centrifugation (5 min, 10,500 rpm). The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was redispersed in n-hexane. Then, ethyl acetate of an equal volume was added, and the solution was centrifuged at 10,500 rpm for 5 min. The precipitate was redispersed in n-hexane and centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 3 min once more. The supernatant was collected and stored. The as-prepared QDs were dispersed in chlorobenzene at different concentrations before deposition.

Synthesis of Tra-CPI QDs. 0.189 mmol PbI $ _2 $  and 5 mL of ODE were put in a 100 mL round-bottomed flask. Then, 1.58 mmol OA and 1.29 mmol OLA were injected into the reaction flask. Then, the temperature was increased to 150 °C until a clear solution was formed. Subsequently, 0.215 mL of pre-heated stock solution of Cs-oleate was injected swiftly. About 5 s after the reaction, the solution was cooled down with ice-water. Finally, 15 mL of ethyl acetate was added to the crude QD solution and transferred to a centrifuge tube for centrifugation (5 min, 10,500 rpm). Device Fabrication. First, the FTO glass substrates were etched with acid, and they were cleaned with detergent, deionized water, acetone, and ethanol for 15 min, respectively. Then, after drying in a 70 °C drying cabinet, the FTO substrates were further treated with plasma cleaner for 10 min. Next, 100 μL of SnO₂ solution was spin-coated on the FTO substrate with the conditions of 3000 rpm for 30 s and annealing at 150 °C for 30 min. The (FAPbI₃)₀.₉₂(MAPbBr₃)₀.₀₈ perovskite solution (1.53 M PbI₂, 1.4 M FAI, 0.11 M MAPbBr₃, 0.5 M MACl in DMF/DMSO = 8:1) was spin-coated onto the FTO/SnO₂ substrate at 1000 rpm for 10 s and 6000 rpm for 25 s. 200 μL of chlorobenzene/QDs in chlorobenzene with different concentrations was deposited, and the FTO/SnO₂/perovskite sample was heat-treated at 150 °C for 10 min. Then, the spiro-OMeTAD solution including 72.3 mg of spiro-OMeTAD, 1 mL of chlorobenzene, 28.8 μL of 4-tert-butylpyridine, and 17.5 μL of bis-(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide lithium salt solution was deposited at 5000 rpm for 30 s. At last, the gold electrode with the thickness of 80 nm was deposited using thermal evaporation under high vacuum.

# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

TMSI-mediated   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   PeQDs were synthesized using an iodine-rich method.  $  ^{48}  $   As seen in Figure 1a, TMSI-CPI QDs have a uniform nanostructure with a mean size of 12 nm. Fringes of a well-defined lattice can be clearly observed in a

high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) (Figure 1b). The interplanar distance is 0.31 nm, perfectly matching with the (200) plane spacing. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of TMSI-CPI QDs (Figure 1c) are in line with the HRTEM measurement, which confirms that the QDs are the cubic phase of   $  CsPbI_{3}  $  . From the measured UV–vis absorption band (Figure 1d), the band gap of TMSI-CPI QDs was determined to be 1.74 eV (Figure S1). The TMSI-CPI QDs have an emission maximum of 686 nm, a full width at half-maximum (fwhm) of 35 nm, and a high QY of 89% (Figure 1d). The energy bands of Cs, Pb, and I can be identified in the high-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (HR-XPS) spectra (Figure S2), and a high I/Pb atomic ratio of up to 4.61 is calculated, indicating more complete   $  [PbI_{6}]^{4-}  $   octahedra on the surface of the TMSI-CPI QDs.  $  ^{48}  $   The excess iodide ions may benefit the compensation of the iodide vacancies in the perovskite layer of the solar cell devices.

The TMSI-CPI QDs with different concentrations were dispersed in chlorobenzene as an anti-solvent during the one-step deposition process of the FAMA perovskite film. TMSI-CPI QDs were spread with the anti-solvent and permeated into the pre-annealing “mixed” precursor film. We first investigated the solid structure of the perovskite films modified by different loadings of TMSI-CPI QDs. All the films show generally similar XRD patterns (Figure 2a), while the diffraction of (110) peaks shifts gradually to larger angles with the increase of the concentration of QDs, indicating a progressive lattice shrinkage (Figure 2b). This is due to the introduction of smaller   $  Cs^{+}  $   ions into the perovskite lattice. Also, the reduction of fwhm of the main diffraction peaks suggests better nucleation and crystal growth during the perovskite crystallization. It is supposed that the TMSI-CPI QDs introduced in the anti-solvent treatment stage can offer seed-like nucleation sites, and the crystallinity of the perovskite film is therefore improved (Figure S4). The diffraction peak of   $  PbI_{2}  $   was observed at 12.8 °C in the XRD pattern of the control perovskite film. The peak intensity was reduced after treating with QDs, suggesting that the superfluous   $  PbI_{2}  $   was decreased.

Since the device performance is profoundly affected by the concentration of TMSI-CPI QDs, the  $ 2\mathrm{mg / mL} $  QD solution was screened out to show the best performance in this work, which will be used to prepare all the TMSI-CPI QD-modified films in the following study (vide infra). Because of the tiny size of the QDs  $ (\sim 12\mathrm{nm}) $  and a relatively small loading, they are hardly seen on the top of the perovskite film by using SEM (Figure 2c,d). With the TMSI-CPI QD modification, the perovskite film was more uniform and showed a densely packed pinhole-free perovskite layer, with less unreacted bright  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ . Besides, the atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements showed that the surface roughness decreased from 34.6

to 21.2 nm after introducing TMSI-CPI QDs in the perovskite film (Figure 2e,f). The smoother film suggests that the TMSI-CPI QDs helped form a more uniform surface, which is beneficial to the closer contact with HTM. The inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry measurement shows that the content of Cs in the film is around 0.15 wt % (Table S1).

To evaluate the optoelectronic properties of the TMSI-CPI QD-modified perovskite absorber, solar cells were fabricated by using the   $  (\mathrm{FAPbI}_{3})_{0.92}(\mathrm{MAPbBr}_{3})_{0.08}  $   perovskite films loaded with different concentrations of QDs. The typical device structure of substrate/FTO/SnO  $  _{2}  $  /(FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  )  $  _{0.92}  $  (MAPbBr  $  _{3}  $  )  $  _{0.08}  $  /spiro-OMeTAD/Au is shown in the cross-sectional SEM image (Figure 3a). The statistic parameters of the PV performances are summarized in Table 1. It can be seen that the PCE begins to rise with the increase of the concentration of TMSI-CPI QDs and reaches the maximum at 2 mg/mL QDs. The increase of PCE basically results from the significant increase of   $  V_{OC}  $  , indicating the occurrence of passivation.  $  ^{49}  $   When the loading of TMSI-CPI QDs reaches 4 mg/mL, the device performance begins to decline, which could be ascribed to (i) generation of new recombination sites and (ii) reduction of the uniformity and continuity of the perovskite film (Figure S6).

In order to verify the effect of the iodine-rich properties of TMSI-CPI QDs on the device performance, we also modified the perovskite layer with traditional   $  CsPbI_{3}  $   QDs (Tra-CPI). XRD and the energy bands of Cs, Pb, and I in the HR-XPS spectra of Tra-CPI QDs are shown in Figures S2 and S3e,f. A lower I/Pb atomic ratio of 3.42 was calculated. The devices modified by 2 mg/mL TMSI-CPI QDs demonstrated considerable reproducibility with an average PCE of 21.25% and an average open-circuit photovoltage (  $  V_{OC}  $  ) of 1.09 V of more than 40 samples. However, the average PCE and   $  V_{OC}  $   of the devices without QDs were 19.99% and 1.04 V, respectively (Figures 3b,c and S5). In addition, the values of fill factor (FF) and short-circuit current densities (  $  J_{SC}  $  ) were not sacrificed with the enhanced   $  V_{OC}  $  s. Consequently, the champion devices with the TMSI-CPI treatment exhibited an excellent PCE of 22.01% with a   $  V_{OC}  $   as high as 1.12 V,   $  J_{SC}  $   of 23.91 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , FF of 82.1%, and negligible hysteresis (Figure 3d and Table S2). On the contrary, the best control device exhibited a lower   $  V_{OC}  $   of 1.06 V,   $  J_{SC}  $   of 23.87 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $  , and FF of 82.06%, corresponding to a lower PCE of 20.91%. Figure 3e shows the external quantum efficiency (EQE) of the PSCs. After TMSI-CPI QD treatment, the device showed the increased maximum EQE over 350–750 nm. The integrated photocurrent (  $  J_{EQE}  $  ) was 23.91 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   for the TMSI-CPI QD-based device and 23.33 mA cm  $  ^{-2}  $   for the control device. Both   $  J_{EQE}  $  s were consistent with   $  J_{SC}  $  s, indicating the reliability of the measured   $  J_{SC}  $  s. The temporal evolution of the maximum power outputs are given in Figure 3f. The corresponding steady-state efficiencies were 18.62 and 20.55% for the control and the TMSI-CPI QDs-treated samples, respectively (voltage at the maximum power output, 0.93 V for the former and a higher 0.95 V for the latter).

To analyze the causes for the enhanced PV performances of the QD-modified devices, the difference made by the TMSI-CPI QDs to the physical properties of the perovskite films was investigated. As shown in the UV–vis absorption spectra

recorded without the HTM layer (Figure S8a), the films showed no apparent changes after loading with different concentrations of QDs, and all films had an identical band gap of 1.58 eV (Figure S8b–e). This could be understood by the fact that when the incident light comes from the FTO side and enters the device, it will be mostly absorbed by the FAMA perovskite film with high absorption property. Then, the TMSI-CPI QDs with small quantities should have no effects on the light-absorbance of the active layer, and the enhanced device performance should be put down to the improved perovskite imperfection and/or the boosted charge carrier kinetic process across the device.

Therefore, to get further insight into the kinetics of carrier recombination, steady-state and time-resolved PL spectra were measured for the control and the TMSI-CPI QD-based perovskite films. As shown in Figure 4a, the PL intensity of the TMSI-CPI QD-modified film is rather stronger than that of the control one, indicating that the nonradiative recombination loss is reduced after QD treatment. It could be concerned with the chemical passivation of I  $  ^{-}  $  vacancy defects on the perovskite film surface. Then, we performed ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) measurements to investigate the change of energy level after the TMSI-CPI QD modification. As seen in Figure 5a, the work functions are calculated to be 4.26, 4.22, and  $ 4.33\mathrm{eV} $  for the perovskite, TMSI-CPI QDs, and QD-modified perovskite, respectively, according to eq S2. Moreover, the valence band (VB) edges of each sample wee 1.47, 1.36, and  $ 1.33\mathrm{eV} $  below the Fermi level. Thus, based on eqs S2 and S3 and the Tauc plots (Figures S1-S5), the schematic energy level diagram can be drawn (Figure S9). The transfers of photogenerated carriers between interfaces of each material are schematically illustrated in Figure 5b. The energy levels can be tuned by adding TMSI-CPI QDs to the perovskite, and an upward shifting of the VB/conduction band (CB) edge of the absorber can facilitate hole extraction leading to reduced energy loss and a faster hole transfer rate. Meanwhile, the CB offset offers an energy barrier

for electrons, preventing the recombination between carriers. In this sense, the  $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $  and PCE of the devices are enhanced.

Moreover, we looked into the dynamic charge behavior of the devices by recording the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy at 0 V and dark conditions. Figure 5c shows the fit Nyquist plots with equivalent circuits (inset) of the control and the TMSI-CPI QD-modified devices. Table S4 summarizes the series resistance   $  (R_{\mathrm{s}})  $  , charge transfer resistance   $  (R_{\mathrm{ct}})  $  , and the recombination resistance   $  (R_{\mathrm{rec}})  $   of perovskites. For PSCs modified by TMSI-CPI QDs, the decreased   $  R_{ct}  $   suggests more efficient hole extraction than the control one due to the higher VB edge-facilitated hole transfer from the perovskite film to the HTM layer. Moreover, a higher   $  R_{rec}  $   in TMSI-CPI QD-modified devices implies the effective trap passivation at the interface, resulting in reduced nonradiative recombination and, therefore, a high   $  V_{OC}  $  .

To evaluate the long-term stability improvement of the QD-modified devices, we stored the PSCs in the ambient environment of 30% relative humidity. As shown in Figure 5d, the QD-modified device maintains 91% of the original efficiency under dark after 39 days, while the control device merely retains 75% of its initial PCE. The enhanced stability could be ascribed to (i) reduced interface defects and (ii) superior uniformity and continuity of the perovskite layer.  $  ^{52,53}  $   Finally, the hydrophobicity of perovskites with and without TMSI-CPI QDs was compared (Figure S10). The water contact angle slightly increased from 82.73 to 84.74° associated with the QD modification, thus potentially improving the stability of the overall cell against moisture.

# CONCLUSIONS

Using all-inorganic PeQDs with modified structures and properties could be an interesting strategy to enhance both the PCE and stability of perovskite PVs.
