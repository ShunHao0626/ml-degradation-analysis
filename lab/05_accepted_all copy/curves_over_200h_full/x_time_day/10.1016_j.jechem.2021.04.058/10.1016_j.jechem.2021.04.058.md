# 1. Introduction

Hybrid organic-inorganic perovskite, through a decade development, has become a superstar material in photoelectric devices, such as photovoltaics   $  [1-3]  $  , light detectors   $  [4-6]  $  , light emission diodes   $  [7-9]  $  . Particularly in photovoltaics, the certified PCE of a single-junction PSC has reached 25.5%   $  [10-13]  $  . However, there is still a large gap between the champion PCE and the theoretical value by Shockley-Queisser limit, which is mainly due to low   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF caused by severe nonradiative and interface recombination losses   $  [14,15]  $  . Generally, multiple defects are inevitably formed on the surface of perovskite film during the formation process   $  [13,16]  $  , which will cause severe recombination loss at the perovskite/hole transport layer (HTL) interface (for a normal structure) and accelerate the moisture-induced degradation process of the perovskite

Interface engineering is an effective way to improve efficiency and long-term stability of perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Herein, an ionic compound tetrabutylammonium hexafluorophosphate (TP6) is adopted to passivate surface defects of the perovskite film. It is found that TP6 effectively reduced the surface defects, especially at the grain boundaries where the defects are abundant. Meanwhile, the exposed long alkyl chains and fluorine atoms in the TP6 enhanced the moisture stability of the perovskite film due to its strong hydrophobicity. In addition, the driving force of charge carrier separation and transport is increased by enlarged built-in potential. Consequently, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs is significantly improved from  $ 20.59\% $  to  $ 22.41\% $  by increased open-circuit voltage  $ (V_{\mathrm{oc}}) $  and fill factor (FF). The unencapsulated device with TP6 treatment exhibits better stability than the control device, and the PCE retains  $ \sim 80\% $  of its initial PCE after 30 days under  $ 15\% -25\% $  relative humidity in storage, while the PCE of the control device declines by more than  $ 50\% $ .

In recent years, numerous efforts have been devoted to reducing the recombination loss at the perovskite/HTL interface to improve the   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF by passivating the defects on the surface of perovskite film, and substantial progress has been made in this field [21–26]. Secondary growth is an extensively used approach to passivate the surface defects of perovskite film. A thin layer of more stable two-dimensional (2D) perovskite will be formed through excess   $  PbI_{2}  $   reacting with the ammonium-halide-containing large cation to reduce the number of defects and protect the underlying perovskite film. As a consequence, a PCE of  $ 22.09\% $  was obtained with excellent stability [25]. Later, researchers found that an ammonium-halide-containing large cation can be directly used to passivate the defects on the surface of perovskite film without any treatment and greatly enhanced the performance of a PSC, using such materials as phenethylammonium iodide (PEAI) and derivatives. For example, You and coworkers used PEAI to passivate the defects on the surface of perovskite film and showed that a thin layer of PEAI can efficiently passivate the defects and enhance the carrier transport at the perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD interface, resulting in a certified PCE of  $ 23.32\% $  with a high  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  of 1.18 V [13]. The aim of defect passivation can also be realized by other materials such as polymers, quantum dots, functionalized graphene, etc. Although these materials can passivate the defects on the surface of perovskite film and enhance the performance of PSCs, it is difficult to achieve simultaneous passivation of multiple defects using these materials [16,27,28]. Therefore, it is important to find a multifunctional material to simultaneously passivate various defects on the surface of perovskite film.

In this work, a post-treatment strategy was executed to passivate the defects on the perovskite film by using an ionic compound TP6. We found that TP6 treatment not only passivates the defects on the surface of perovskite film and reduces the recombination loss at the perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD interface but also increases the driving force of charge carrier separation and transport by increased built-in potential, resulting in enhanced  $  V_{\mathrm{oc}}  $  and FF of PSCs. In addition, the long alkyl chains and fluorine atoms in the TP6 can also improve the hydrophobicity and enhance the moisture stability of the perovskite film. Based on these merits, the PCE was significantly increased from  $  20.59\%  $  to  $  22.41\%  $  via TP6 treatment. In addition, the device retained  $  \sim 80\%  $  of their initial PCE after 30 days under  $  15\% -25\%  $  relative humidity in the dark, while the PCE of the control device declined by more than  $  50\%  $ .

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1.Materials

$ \mathrm{TiCl_4} $ , ethanol, and isopropanol were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Corporation Co., Ltd.  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $ ,  $ \mathrm{PbBr}_2 $ , formamidinium iodide (FAI), methylammonium bromide (MABr), methylammonium chloride (MACl) and FK209 were purchased from Xi'an p-OLED Co. Dimethylformamide (DMF), chlorobenzene (CB), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and acetonitrile were bought from Alfa Aesar. Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO), spiro-OMeTAD and PEDOT: PSS were purchased from Advanced Electron Technology Co., Ltd. 4-tert-butylpyridine (t-BP) and Lithium bis(trifuoromethylsulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) were bought from Sigma-Aldrich.

# 2.2. Device fabrication

The compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer was deposited on clean FTO substrate using chemical bath deposition with 0.2 M   $  TiCl_{4}  $   aqueous solution at 70 °C for 1 h in a lab oven and then rinsed in ultrapure water and ethanol and dried by nitrogen gas. The   $  FTO/TiO_{2}  $   substrate was annealed at 200 °C for 30 min and treated by ultraviolet ozone for 10 min before perovskite film deposition. 1.4 M (  $  FAPbI_{3})_{0.95}(-MAPbBr_{3})_{0.05}  $   perovskite precursor (DMF:DMSO = 8:1 v/v) solution was utilized to prepare the perovskite films. The precursor solution was dropped onto   $  FTO/TiO_{2}  $   substrate at 4000 rpm for 30 s and 200 μL CB was poured onto the spinning substrate 15 s prior to the program end. Subsequently, the films were annealed at

150 °C for 20 min. For surface treatment, 50 μL of TP6/CB precursor solution was spin-coated onto the perovskite film at 5000 rpm for 30 s, and then the film was annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. Then, the spiro-OMeTAD layer was spin-coated on the perovskite film at 5000 rpm for 30 s from a solution composed of 90 mg spiro-OMeTAD, 36 μL t-BP, 22 μL LiTFSI/acetonitrile (520 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ), 10 μL FK209/acetonitrile (375 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  ) and 1 mL CB. Finally, an 80-nm-thick Au electrode layer was deposited on the top of the spiro-OMeTAD using a thermal evaporation system.

# 2.3. Device characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were acquired on a Rigaku SmartLab diffractometer using Cu K $ _{\alpha} $  radiation ( $ \lambda $  = 1.5418 Å). Ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) absorption spectra were measured by a HITACHI 4150 spectrophotometer. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) measurements were conducted on an ESCALAB Xi $ ^{+} $  system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The surface morphologies of the perovskite films were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, SU-8020, HITACHI) and atomic force microscopy (AFM, Bruker, Dimension ICON). The composition of the perovskite film was estimated using energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, HORIBA, EMAX evolution X-Max 80/EX-270) at an acceleration voltage of 10 kV. Water contact angles were obtained using a DataPhysics OCA 20 system. Photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved PL (TRPL) measurements of the perovskite films were made using a PicoQuant FluoTime 300 with 510-nm laser excitation pulsed at a frequency of 40 MHz for PL and 50 kHz for TRPL. The photovoltaic performance of PSCs were characterized using a Keithley 2400 SourceMeter and solar simulator (Enlitech, SS-F5-3A). The light intensity was calibrated to 100 mW cm $ ^{-2} $  using an NREL-traceable KG5-filtered silicon reference cell. A metal mask with an aperture area of 0.09 cm $ ^{2} $  was applied on top of the cell. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectrum was measured using an Enlitech QE-R system. Fourier transformed infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) were acquired with a Bruker Vertex 70. The Mott-Schottky curves of PSCs were measured in the dark using a ModuLab XM CHAS08 electrochemical work station in a voltage range of  $ -0.1 $  to 1.5 V with a scan step of 10 mV and a delay time of 1 s. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was measured in the dark using a ModuLab XM CHAS08 with a frequency range of 0.1 Hz to 100 MHz.

# 3. Results and discussion

The mixed   $  (\mathrm{FAPbI}_{3})_{0.95}(\mathrm{MAPbBr}_{3})_{0.05}  $   perovskite film is prepared by using the anti-solvent approach, as shown in Fig. 1(a). Post treatment with TP6 is employed to passivate the defects on the surface of the perovskite film with TP6. The chemical structure of TP6 is displayed in Fig. S1. XRD measurements are firstly performed to determine the effect of TP6 on the crystallinity of the perovskite film. As presented in Fig. 1(b), the perovskite films have good crystallinity and grow along the (110) direction. The magnified (110) peaks are shown in Fig. 1(c). It is obvious that the (110) peak shifts to the left with the increase of TP6 concentration, which is probably due to the substitution of a small amount of I  $  ^{-}  $   (2.20 Å) ions by PF  $  _{6}^{-}  $   (2.55 Å) ions with a larger ionic radius [29]. Moreover, the peak height intensity of the (110) peak increases with the increase of TP6 concentration to 6 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $  , and then the intensity decreases (Fig. 1d). The XRD measurements indicate that 6 mg mL  $  ^{-1}  $   is the optimal concentration to improve the crystallinity of the perovskite film (and is denoted as TP6 in following discussion).

The absorption of the perovskite film is enhanced by TP6 treatment, which is due to the enhanced crystallinity of perovskite film. The bandgap of the perovskite film is almost unaffected by the TP6 treatment (Fig. 2b), but it obviously affects the absorption edge of the perovskite films. The Urbach energy  $ (E_{\mathrm{u}}) $  is closely related to the absorption edge, and the lower the  $ E_{\mathrm{u}} $  value, the higher the structural quality of the perovskite film and the smaller the voltage loss between the  $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $  and the bandgap voltage. Fig. 2(c) shows  $ \mathrm{Ln}(\alpha)-h\nu $  plots of the control and TP6-treated perovskite films, the  $ E_{\mathrm{u}} $  is calculated from the UV-Vis spectra according to the formula  $ \alpha=\alpha_{0}\exp(h\nu/E_{\mathrm{u}}) $  (where  $ \alpha $  is the absorption coefficient and  $ h\nu $  is the photon energy) [11]. The  $ E_{\mathrm{u}} $  of the control perovskite film is 59.38 meV, which is decreased to 49.75 meV through TP6 treatment. These results indicate that TP6 treatment can effectively reduce the defect density and improve the structural quality of perovskite film.

The effect of TP6 treatment on the morphology of perovskite films is further investigated by SEM characterization (Figs. 3a, b and S2). Interestingly, the grain boundaries of the perovskite film appear darkened and are more obviously darker with the increase of TP6 concentration. As the TP6 is an ionic compound that is very reactive to defects on the as-deposited perovskite surface, it is expected that the TP6 would form a passivation layer on top of the perovskite grains upon the surface passivation, leading to slightly modified morphology observed. As the carrier recombination in a perovskite film is closely related to its film quality, PL and TRPL measurements are performed on the control and TP6-treated perovskite films. Fig. 4(a) shows the PL spectra of the control and TP6-treated perovskite films deposited on glass substrates. Clearly, the PL intensity is greatly enhanced with TP6 treatment, indicating that the nonradiative recombination is apparently suppressed by TP6 treatment.

Details of the charge recombination are revealed by TRPL measurements using time-correlated single photon counting under low intensity pulsed excitation. As shown in Fig. 4(b), the decay curves of the control and TP6-treated perovskite films are fitted using the biexponential equation  $  Y = A_{1}\exp(-t/\tau_{1}) + A_{2}\exp(-t/\tau_{2})  $ , where  $  \tau_{1}  $  and  $  \tau_{2}  $  denote the fast and slow decay time and are related to the trap-assisted surface recombination and bulk recombination processes, respectively [27,34–36]. The fitting results are presented in Table S1. The carrier lifetimes of the control device are  $  \tau_{1} = 21.25  $  ns and  $  \tau_{2} = 283.7  $  ns, which are greatly increased to  $  \tau_{1} = 86.77  $  ns and  $  \tau_{2} = 798.4  $  ns via TP6 treatment. The increase of carrier lifetime is attributed to the decrease of trap-assisted nonradiative recombination. In addition, confocal TRPL mapping is performed to explore the uniformity of the control and TP6-treated perovskite films in an area of  $  50 \times 50  $  μm (Fig. 4c and d). while both the control and TP6-treated perovskite films have good uniformity, the average carrier lifetime of the control perovskite film is apparently prolonged with TP6 treatment, indicating that the nonradiative recombination of perovskite film can be remarkably suppressed by TP6 treatment. To investigate the charge carrier transport at the perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD interface, control and TP6-treated devices with the structure glass/perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD are fabricated and characterized through PL and TRPL measurements. The PL intensity is quenched significantly in the device containing the perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD interface treated by TP6 (Fig. 4e), indicating that holes are more quickly extracted into the spiro-OMeTAD layer [29]. The corresponding decay curves and fitting lines of the devices are shown in Fig. 4(f). The key parameters are summarized in Table S2. Similarly,  $  \tau_{\text{ave}}  $  of the TP6-treated sample is reduced from 95.82 to 23.29 ns owing to the accelerated hole extraction into the spiro-OMeTAD layer, which is consistent with the PL results. To quantify the effect of TP6 treatment on the trap density of perovskite film, hole-only devices with the structure FTO/PEDOT:PSS/perovskite/spiro-OMeTAD/Au are fabricated. Generally, defects in semiconductors can capture and trap free charges [32]. Therefore, the electrical properties of semiconductors will obviously change when the defects are completely filled with charges [35]. Firstly, UPS is performed to investigate the influence of TP6 treatment on the energy level of the perovskite film (Fig. 6a and b). The electron structure upshifts of the perovskite film greatly reduced the hole transport loss by reducing the energy barrier between the perovskite and spiro-OMeTAD. In addition, the work function of the TP6-treated perovskite decreases to 4.08 eV from 4.33 eV, indicating that the TP6-treated perovskite film exhibits more n-type characteristics [40]. The TP6 treatment remarkably improves the built-in potential (  $  V_{bi}  $  ) between the n-type perovskite film and p-type spiro-OMeTAD film and increases the efficiency of charge carrier collection [41]. To verify the effect of TP6 treatment on the   $  V_{bi}  $  , capacitance–voltage (C-V) measurements were conducted. Nyquist plots and the corresponding fitting parameters are presented in Fig. 6(d) and Table S5. These results indicate that TP6 treatment can effectively reduce the trap-assisted nonradiative recombination and enlarge the driving force for charge carrier separation and transport, finally improving the performance of PSCs.

# 4. Conclusions

A multifunctional ionic compound, TP6, is used to passivate the defects on the surface of the perovskite film. The nonradiative recombination of the perovskite film is effectively inhibited by TP6 treatment, leading to significantly improved   $  V_{oc}  $   and FF. Meanwhile, the long alkyl chains and fluorine atoms in TP6 show strong hydrophobicity, resulting in improved humidity stability. Additionally, the energy level and work function of perovskite film are upshifted by the TP6 treatment, which not only reduces the energy barriers between the perovskite and the spiro-OMeTAD layer, but also enlarges the built-in potential as the driving force for charge carrier separation and transport. Consequently, the PCE is significantly improved from 20.59% to 22.41%. In addition, the unencapsulated device with TP6 treatment exhibits better stability than the control device: the TP6-treated device retains ~80% of its initial PCE after 30 days under 15%–25% relative humidity in storage, while the PCE of the control device is declines by more than 50%.
