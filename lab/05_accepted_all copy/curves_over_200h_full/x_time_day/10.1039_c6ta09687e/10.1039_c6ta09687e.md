# ABSTRACT

The operational stability is one of the main obstacles that may hold back the commercialization of perovskite solar cells (PVSCs). In this paper, we provide a detailed account for the ion migration accelerated PVSCs degradation by comparatively studying perovskite materials with two different organic cations (MA $  ^{+}  $  and FA $  ^{+}  $ ). Using Time of Flight Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry (TOF-SIMS), we have uncovered the ion migration accelerated degradation of PVSCs. Not only mobile iodide (I $  ^{-}  $ ) ions from the perovskite layer out-diffused, Ag atoms/ions from the metal electrode also diffused into the perovskite layer, which resulted in severe device degradation. Besides, we identified I $  ^{-}  $  species in the hole transport material (HTM) layer for even freshly prepared PVSC devices, which was responsible for the degradation of devices kept in the inert condition. Compared with MAPbI $  _{3}  $ , the ion migration process can be slow down in FAPbI $  _{3}  $  devices which account for a better stability of FAPbI $  _{3}  $  devices. This work underscores the impact of organic cation substitution on the PVSCs degradation and provides solid evidence for the mobile ion migration in perovskite materials and the consequent degradation in specific device settings such as the n-i-p type perovskite solar cells.

# INTRODUCTION

With its booming solar cell efficiency  $  ^{1-3}  $  , organolead halide perovskite represents an promising class of light sensitizers for thin film photovoltaic applications  $  ^{4}  $  . Compared with other prevailing thin film photovoltaic materials such as silicon, CdTe, and copper indium gallium selenide (CIGS), high performance perovskite solar cells (PVSCs) can be obtained through facile solution processes at a much lower temperature, which are greatly advantageous for cost-competitive stretchable photovoltaic devices.  $  ^{5}  $   Besides, the band gap of perovskites (CH  $  _{3}  $  NH  $  _{3}  $  PbX  $  _{3}  $   (denoted as MAPbX  $  _{3}  $  ), X = I  $  ^{-}  $  , Br  $  ^{-}  $  , or Cl  $  ^{-}  $  ) can be easily tuned by varying the ratio of halide anion, this facilitate its application in tandem cell  $  ^{6-11}  $  , photodetector  $  ^{12}  $  , Light-Emitting Diode (LED)  $  ^{13}  $   and laser  $  ^{14}  $  . However, despite all their merits such as tunable band gap  $  ^{14-16}  $   and relatively high carrier motilities  $  ^{17,18}  $  , the perovskite (especially MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  ) is sensitive to moisture, so the real application of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   based devices is still an open question.

Generally, water has been regarded as the main culprit held responsible for the fast degradation of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ . Under high humidity,  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  rapidly degrades to its precursor components via intermediate and semi-reversible monohydrate phases. $ ^{19, 20} $  Besides, although PVSCs can be stable for more than  $ 200\mathrm{h} $  in a nitrogen atmosphere ( $ 0\% $  humidity) $ ^{21} $ , even an encapsulated cell may degrade significantly in less than  $ 100\mathrm{h} $  under high humidity ( $ >50\% $ ). $ ^{22} $  It should be noted that the PVSC device stability is far more complicated than the material stability. For example, an unexpected silver iodide (AgI) phase has been discovered on the surface of degraded PVSCs using silver (Ag) as the metal contact $ ^{22, 23} $ , however, the formation mechanism of AgI is not well established. Although the stability of PVSCs can be greatly improved by modifying the upper contact layer of perovskite $ ^{24-27} $  and thus partially avoiding the in-service penetration of moisture into the cell, a number of studies have shown that even encapsulated devices or devices that were kept in a glovebox also show significant degradation $ ^{21, 28, 29} $ . This can't be explained by moisture alone and Guerrero et al. suggested that other factors such as ion migration could also result in the degradation of PVSCs. $ ^{29} $  Interestingly, the stability of PVSCs was found to be significantly improved when the commonly used AM 1.5G simulated light source was replaced by a UV light

Although the stability of PVSCs has been investigated variously, previous studies have mainly focused on  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  based PVSCs.  $ \mathrm{HC(NH_2)_2PbI_3} $  donated as  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $ , with a smaller band gap and better thermal stability $ ^{36} $ , has recently drawn much interest in the PVSC community, $ ^{3,8,37} $  but the stability of  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  has seldom been touched upon. More of a concern is the fact that when addressing the stability issue of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  based PVSCs, most researchers simply focused on the perovskite material itself without paying much attention to the interfaces of the devices during the PVSCs degradation process. On the fundamental level, although ion migration accelerated PVSCs degradation has been proposed previously, among 5 of the critical issues proposed by Jinsong Huang in a review article, $ ^{38} $  we still don't have a clear image on if ion migration exist in solar grade perovskite devices or will ion migration impact the long-term stability of PVSCs. Time of flight Secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) capable of elemental mapping with nanometer resolution has been widely used in characterizing the component and element distributions of thin film solar cells $ ^{39,40} $ , however, it has been rarely used to elucidate the degradation mechanism of PVSCs. $ ^{4,32} $  In this paper, we systematically investigate the organic cation dependent degradation of PVSCs in detail. Generally, moisture, ion migration, and the worse scenario, their synergetic combination greatly contributes to the degradation of PVSCs. This ion migration is not only due to the mobile iodide (I') out-diffusion to the top metal contact but also top metal contact (Ag for instance) in-diffusion into the perovskite layer. This combined process results in the AgI formation and accelerates the degradation of PVSCs. Compared with

This suppressed ion migration can be explained by a relatively larger radius of FA $ ^{+} $  (2.53 Å compared with 2.17 Å for MA $ ^{+} $ ) and a smaller molecular dipole moment (0.21 D for FA $ ^{+} $  compared with 2.29 D in MA $ ^{+} $ ). Last but not least, using TOF-SIMS, we unexpectedly detected I $ ^{-} $  species within the hole transport material (HTM) layer in both MA and FA devices, which should be taken as direct and solid evidence for the mobile ion migration in device level PVSCs. Our work also testifies the power of TOF-SIMS in tracking ambiguously the ion migration phenomenon in PVSCs in space and time.

We refer to the ambient condition used in this work to expose our devices (films) as the outside laboratory atmosphere. Considering the humidity of Hong Kong during the time of experiments (average humidity   $  \geq70\%  $  ) (Figure S1), we regard it as an extremely harsh condition for the PVSCs stability evaluation. Besides, devices both stored in a dry box (  $  \sim20\%  $   R.H.) and kept in a standard glove box were also tested.

Performance degradation of PVSCs. In this study, the planar n-i-p device structure with fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)/compact   $  TiO_{2}  $   layer/perovskite/sprio-MeOTAD/Ag was selected due to its easy fabrication and acceptable high efficiency. Via the optimized solvent dripping method, the PCE of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   and   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   PVSCs was optimized to as high as 16.56% (  $  V_{oc}  $   1.09 V,   $  J_{sc}  $   21.1 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $   and fill factor (ff) 0.72) and 15.02% (  $  V_{oc}  $   1.05 V,   $  J_{sc}  $   22.7 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $   and ff 0.63). Here, although the   $  J_{sc}  $   of   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   devices is higher than that of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   (22.7 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $   compared with 21.1 mA/cm  $  ^{2}  $  ), the high performance of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   is mainly due to the higher ff (0.72 compared with 0.63 in   $  FAPbI_{3}  $  ). Indeed, the poor ff has been regarded as the main problem that may hinder the development of high performance phase pure   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   based devices.  $  ^{37}  $

For the stability testing, both the dark condition (devices stored in a dry box and measured once per day in the ambient condition (  $  \geq 70\%  $   R.H.)) and the operational condition (  $  40^{\circ}C  $  ,   $  \geq 70\%  $   R.H., AM 1.5G continuous illumination) were considered. Figure 1c records the dark stability profile of an unencapsulated PVSC. With this condition, the   $  V_{oc}  $  ,   $  J_{sc}  $   and ff of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   PVSCs drop rapidly and completely degrade in approximately 10 days (Figure 1c, Figure S 2, 3). Here, although the devices were kept in the dark dry box, humidity may still play an important role in the degradation of PVSCs since our J-V measurements were conducted in the ambient condition (  $  \geq 70\%  $   R.H.). According to our previous work  $  ^{41}  $  , NiO-based PVSCs lost   $  \sim 40\%  $   efficiency when kept in a glovebox and measured in the ambient condition during a 2 month storage period. So in the present work, we regard the 10 days fast degradation of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   devices as a reasonable stability benchmark after taking our harsh ambient environment into consideration. Although the degradation of FAPbI  $  _{3}  $   devices is also evident from the stability profiles of   $  J_{sc}  $   and ff (Figure 1c,

After approximately one week, more than  $ 80\% $  of the original efficiency has been still retained, manifesting a much better stability of  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  devices. The stability testing results above suggest different degradation mechanisms in the PVSCs with different organic cations, which deserve a detailed investigation as will be elucidated below.

Tracing the degradation of PVSCs down to material level. First, XRD was used to characterize the crystal structure transformation of the perovskite films in the ambient condition (Figure S4, S5). Here,  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  films could degrade by firstly forming a monohydrated  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3\cdot \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} $  phase, and then decomposing back to the  $ \mathrm{PbI}_2 $  and MAI precursors[19, 20] (Figure S4). While different from  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ , the black  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  film in  $ \alpha $  phase transforms into  $ \delta $  phase, and this  $ \delta $ - $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  film could be stable for more than a month in the humid environment (Figure S5, S6). This feature is also confirmed by our absorption spectrum since the color of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  film gradually turned from dark brown to yellow during 1 month exposure (Figure S7a), while  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  films turned from dark brown to light yellow rapidly in less than a day after exposing to the ambient environment, and remained light yellow for the rest of the month (Figure S7b). However, the paradox here is that although  $ \alpha $ - $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  fast transferred into  $ \delta $ - $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  with humidity, the dark and operational stability of  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  devices were better than that of  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $ , so it seemed that the material stability along can't explain the stability difference observed in Figure 1.

Evidence for ion migration induced degradation. Although moisture has been regarded as a dominant factor for the degradation of PVSCs, other factors may also play an important role but so far have been less clear. It can be seen from Figure 1e that the degraded  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  devices remains dark after  $ 5\mathrm{h} $  of continuous light illumination, meaning that the degradation of  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  devices under operational condition can't be simply explained by moisture. Besides, the degradation of PVSCs (especially accompanied by the AgI formation under low humidity Figure 2a) kept in a dry box is also ambiguous. In the present work, we take advantage of TOF-SIMS to debunk the mysteries of perovskite cell stability. Devices both kept in the dry box and exposed in the ambient atmosphere were tested. To compare different samples, the signal intensities have been calibrated in such a way that the  $ \mathrm{SnO}_2^- $  counts at around  $ 2500~s $  are equalized. An ambiguous feature of the SIMS spectra is that the perovskite layer of the  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  device seems slightly thicker than that of the  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  device (Figure 3a, b). However, according to our SEM images, the  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  and  $ \mathrm{MAPbI}_3 $  films share a similar thickness (Figure S14). The catch here is that the sputtering time from SIMS measurement can't be directly linked to the perovskite layer thickness, and the longer sputtering time of  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $  perovskite layer in this case can be explained by the stronger bonding in  $ \mathrm{FAPbI}_3 $ .

For the SIMS analysis, we start with degraded PVSC devices after 5 h of continuous illumination (Figure 3). A full spectrum of TOF-SIMS profile containing different elements was recorded in Figure S12, but if not stated otherwise, only the   $  Ag^{-}  $  ,   $  I^{-}  $  ,   $  PbI_{2}^{-}  $  intensity would be considered in our discussion for simplify. Strikingly, traces of   $  I^{-}  $  intensity showed up in the HTM layer, a large amount of   $  I^{-}  $  could even be traced in the metal Ag layer. This result suggests that   $  I^{-}  $  has factually penetrated throughout the HTM layer and diffused into the Ag metal contact. This feature is obvious in both   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   and   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   devices, especially in   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   devices. Besides, as shown in Figure 3 (a, b), a small amount of   $  Ag^{-}  $  intensity was detected in the   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   layer (  $  FAPbI_{3}  $   is not obvious), this can be clearly observed from our 3D elemental mapping (Figure 3(c, d)). Recently, gold migration was observed in PVSCs while using a gold back contact  $  ^{32}  $  . Considering the relatively active feature of Ag compared with Au, we believe that Ag atoms/ions

Moisture assisted ion migration. Back to the performance degradation of PVSCs, we notice that the ion migration characteristics revealed from our SIMS measurements can well explain the superb dark stability of   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   (1 month) compared with that of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   (10 days). Generally, the ion migration induced degradation mainly resulted in   $  J_{sc}  $   and   $  ff\ drop^{32}  $ , which is indeed the case for our   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   PVSCs (Figure 1, Figure S3). On the other hand, the slower decline rate of   $  J_{sc}  $   and ff in   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   devices during the dark stability evaluation can be explained by the suppressed ion migration as discovered from our TOF-SIMS measurements (Figure 3). Next, we consider the poor operational stability with devices exposed to the laboratory ambient. Figure 4 records the TOF-SIMS analysis for films exposed to the ambient condition for a week (Figure 4 b, d). Compared with devices kept in the dry box (Figure 4 a, c), the ion migration for the ambient degraded PVSCs has been greatly enhanced (Figure 4 b, d, Figure S 13), possibly due to the high humidity of the ambient condition. So the poor operational stability of PVSCs recorded in Figure 1 b is at least partially related to the ion migration. Here, this moisture enhanced ion migration can be explained as follows: according to our XRD analysis (Figure S4, S5),   $  MAPbI_{3}  $   degraded to its precursor   $  PbI_{2}  $   and MAI in the presence of moisture, and this results in an excess amount of mobile I⁻, which contributes to the ion migration process (Figure 4 e, f). Evidence for ion migration in device level PVSCs. Since a considerable amount of   $  \Gamma  $   species have been identified in the HTM layer of degraded PVSC devices, it is tempting to ask if TOF-SIMS can be used to monitor the migration of mobile ions in the PVSC devices, especially in fresh prepared PVSCs. To this end, the TOF-SIMS experiment was designed for a comparative study by fabricating two PVSCs, one with and the other without an Ag layer atop. Here, we focus on some species which are not supposed to be in the HTM layer, such as   $  \Gamma  $  , for example. After the SIMS testing, the fresh samples were kept in the glovebox for another 2 weeks (0% R.H.), and then measured again to see any changes of the unexpected species (  $  \Gamma  $  ) varied with time. Here, in-situ SIMS monitoring of the ions from the devices under an external electric field is not considered due to our technical limit. However, if ions could diffuse in (outside) the HTL layer freely without an external electric field, this ion migration process would definitely be accelerated under an external electric field.

As shown in Figure 5a, a considerable amount of  $ \Gamma $  was detected in the HTM layer of freshly prepared perovskite devices without the metal Ag contact. However, this  $ \Gamma $  intensity is not due to mobile ion redistribution under the electric field generated in the space charge region, but rather due to the interaction between Spiro-OMeTAD $ ^{+} $  and mobile  $ \Gamma $  as follows:

$$
\text { Spiro - OMeTAD } ^ {+} + \mathrm{I} \rightarrow \text { Spiro - OMeTAD - I } \quad (1) ^ {4 3}
$$

This side reaction results in a large amount of accumulated   $  \Gamma  $   species in the Spiro layer, which reduces the p-doping character of the HTL and could be the reason for the observed hysteresis in Spiro based PVSCs.  $  ^{43,44}  $   Detailed comparison on the hysteresis features between MA and FA devices will be given below. The far smaller   $  \Gamma  $   intensity with PCBM-MAPbI  $  _{3}  $   and MAPbBr  $  _{3}  $   can be explained as follows: (1) the crystal lattice of MAPbBr  $  _{3}  $   is smaller than that of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  , so a stronger bonding can be guaranteed in MAPbBr  $  _{3}  $   and the halide ions diffusion can be suppressed  $  ^{16}  $  ; (2) PCBM can passivate the grain boundaries of MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  , thus resulting in suppressed mobile ion migration  $  ^{45}  $  . Furthermore, as shown in Figure S14 and Table S1, the hysteresis is much smaller in   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   devices compared with that of   $  MAPbI_{3}  $  , due reasonably to the reduced iodine migration in   $  FAPbI_{3}  $   materials.

# CONCULSION

To conclude, organic cation dependent degradation of PVSCs has been investigated in great detail. It is confirmed that by using TOF-SIMS to track ions in the devices, besides moisture, ion migration plays an important role in the PVSCs degradation. Besides, using SIMS, we have verified the existence of mobile ions in PVSCs, although the existence of I  $  ^{-}  $  in the HTL was due to the reaction between Spiro  $  ^{+}  $  and I  $  ^{-}  $ , the intensity of this I  $  ^{-}  $  species also correlate with the mobile (less mobile) feature of the halide ions in the perovskite materials. Compared with MAPbI  $  _{3}  $ , this ion migration tendency can be greatly slowed down in FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  devices, possibly explained the improved stability and the hysteresis less feature in FAPbI  $  _{3}  $  devices. Overall, this work not only

# EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS

All materials if not stated were purchased from Sigma and Aldrich.

# Solar cell fabrication

FTO glasses were cleaned in an ultrasonic bath containing ethanol for 30 min, and treated in a UV Ozone (UVO) cleaner for 20 min. After that, a   $  TiO_{2}  $   dense layer was prepared by spin-coating a 0.15 M titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetylacetonate) solution in 1-butanol three times on the FTO substrate at 2,000 r.p.m. for 20 s. Between each coating, the FTO substrate was dried on a hot plate at 125 °C for 5 min, and finally annealed at 500 °C for 1 h.

A one step method  $  ^{34,51}  $  has been used to prepare the perovskite films. The perovskite solution was prepared by mixing 0.553 g PbI  $  _{2}  $  (99.9985%, Alfa) and 0.19g MAI or 0.206 g FAI (both purchased from Dyesol) in a 1ml solution of N, N-dimethylformamide and Dimethyl sulfoxide mixture solution with a volume ratio (3:7) and stirred at 60 °C overnight. The perovskite precursor solution was then spun casted on the FTO/TiO  $  _{2}  $  substrate at 1000 rpm for 15 s and 4000 rpm for 45 s. 0.7 ml chlorobenzene was drop-casted on the substrate during the last 20 s of the second spin-coating step. After the spin-coating process, the MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  (FAPbI  $  _{3}  $ ) films were annealed at 100 °C (150 °C) for 30 min separately. The HTM solution was prepared by dissolving spiro-MeOTAD (Lumtec) in chlorobenzene (72.3 mg/1 ml), 28.8 ul tert-butylpyridine (TBP) solution and 17.5 ul Li-bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (Li-TFSI)/acetonitrile (540 mg/1 ml). The solution was spin-coated on the prepared film at 5,000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, an 80 nm silver electrode was thermally evaporated on the Spiro-MeOTAD coated film with a base pressure of 1x10  $  ^{-4}  $  Pa. The fabrication of MAPbBr  $  _{3}  $  PVSCs follows a similar process, while for PCBM-MAPbI  $  _{3}  $ , the prepared MAPbI  $  _{3}  $  films were dipped in a chlorobeneze solution containing
